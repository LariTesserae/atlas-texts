# The Hush at Chalkmouth

You would walk past it. Nearly everyone has. From the downs above it looks like a crease in the turf, a place where the ground sighs inward and a few thorn trees lean over nothing. The drop only opens when you are standing at its lip: a pit in the white chalk, roughly round, perhaps a hundred and forty paces across and not quite forty deep. The whole of it, wall to wall and floor to rim, is a town.

## The Shape of It

Nobody planned the town. It accreted, the way a wasp's nest does. The first dwellings were probably scooped into the chalk walls at the bottom, cave-mouths with lintels of split flint. Later houses were built against those, then on top of them, then bridged across to the opposite side, until the sinkhole filled with rooms the way a hive fills with cells. There are no streets in the ordinary sense. There are *throughs*: passages that run under floors, over roofs, along the tops of walls, down ladder-shafts worn smooth as horn. A path from one side to the other might pass through six kitchens, a sleeping loft, and a granary, and nobody would object, because that is simply the way through.

The building materials are few and all local. Chalk is cut into soft blocks that harden as they weather. Flint nodules, knapped square-faced, are set in courses where strength is needed. Wattle is woven from hazel and daubed with chalk-mud. Rafters are ash and oak, blackened and polished by centuries of hands. A few doors are single slabs of greenish stone that someone, at some point, carried here from a long way off. There is almost no metal. Cooking is done in clay; cutting with flint blades kept in little moss-lined niches by every hearth; sewing with bone needles.

The town has been here a very long time, long enough that its edges have gone soft. Every corner is rounded. Door-sills are dished in the middle by feet. Steps have become ramps. Chalk walls have slumped into shoulders, and the shoulders have grown fur.

## The Green

That fur is moss, and it is everywhere.

It is the first thing and the last thing about the place. Cushion mosses swell on the roofs like sleeping animals. Feather mosses hang from the undersides of bridges in soft beards. Liverworts plate the damp walls in overlapping green scales. In the deepest throughs the moss is so thick underfoot that you sink to the ankle, and it springs back behind you as though you had never been.

Out of the moss grows everything else. Hart's-tongue ferns push from the mortar joints, glossy and strap-leaved. Polypody runs along the roof ridges. Wood sorrel grows on windowsills. Ivy covers the upper walls so densely that some houses exist only as ivy-shapes with a door in them. Small, tough yews, centuries old and no taller than a person, grow twisted out of the rim. Their roots come down through the ceilings of the top-tier rooms, and the families there hang their baskets on them. There is barely a hand's breadth of bare surface anywhere. The green has not ruined the town. It has finished it.

## The Cold and the Wet

The air at the bottom of the pit is cold, the particular deep cold of stone that has never been warmed. Breath fogs. Fingers ache after a while. At the rim there is sometimes a fringe of hoarfrost on the ivy, and it seems never to melt, though it never spreads either.

It rarely rains down here. The pit is too narrow and the overhangs too many. The water comes instead as condensation. The cold chalk pulls moisture out of the air, and every surface wears a fine skin of it, beading on fern fronds and gathering at the tips of moss. Now and then a single drop falls. It lands on moss, so it makes no sound. At the very bottom, in the oldest room, there is a round basin cut into the floor where the seep collects. It is clear, and so cold it hurts the teeth. It is always exactly as full as it was the day before.

The place is damp, then, but never flooded or muddy. The water moves too slowly to be called flowing. It is simply present, the way the cold is.

## The Light

You would expect a hole in the ground to be dim. It is the opposite.

The sky above Chalkmouth is nearly always a high, even white. It is not grey, and it is not quite cloud. That white falls into the pit and meets white walls, which throw it back. Every whitewashed chalk surface not taken by moss throws it again, and every bead of condensation holds a point of it. The result is a light that seems to come from no direction at all. There are almost no shadows. The undersides of bridges are as bright as their tops. You can read the grain in a rafter at the back of a sealed room. The green of the moss, lit from everywhere, looks faintly unreal, like the green of a leaf held up against the sun.

Visitors, the few there have been, describe feeling slightly dazzled the entire time, as though they had stepped into a very clear memory.

## The Quiet

Sound does not survive here.

It may be the moss, which drinks noise the way it drinks water. It may be the shape of the pit, or the cold, dense air. A dropped pot does not clatter; it *thuds*, once, and the thud is gone before you have turned your head. A shout carries perhaps the length of a room and then simply stops, as if it had walked into felt. You can hear your own heartbeat. You can hear the tiny tick of a snail's shell against stone, but only if the snail is on your hand.

The people who live here long ago stopped fighting this. They speak rarely and softly, and mostly with their hands. They have a whole grammar of touches, nods, and small gestures, fine enough to haggle in or to tell a joke. Children learn it before they learn words. Speech is kept for singing, and singing is done close, mouth nearly to ear, one person to another, so that each song is heard by exactly one listener and then gone.

## The Inhabitants, All of Them

The town is full, the way it is full of moss. There are people in every tier. Families sleep in lofts piled with fleeces and heather. Old women sit in doorways twisting nettle fibre into cord. Tanners work in the lowest levels, and potters by the few hearths that vent up through clay flues to the rim. A household might be four rooms stacked vertically and reached by three different ladders. Nobody owns the throughs; everyone keeps the throughs clean.

But the people are not the majority, and they know it.

Wrens nest in the ivy, in the thatch, in pots left on shelves, in the pockets of coats left hanging. There are hundreds of them. They move through the throughs exactly as the people do, and they do not sing. They only flick and bob. Bats hang in the high corners of lofts by day, in clusters like brown fruit. Bank voles run the tops of the walls on paths worn into the moss. Stoats, white-coated all year in the cold, hunt the voles and are tolerated because they keep the granaries clean. Snails are uncountable: banded snails on every fern, great grey slugs on the wet walls, tiny glassy snails in the liverwort. Their silver trails cross every surface, and in the shadowless light they shine like writing.

Pale moths rest by day under the bridges, wings flat and furred. Woodlice live in every crack. In the pool at the bottom there are small blind shrimp, translucent, which the people regard as the eldest residents and never disturb. Occasionally a roe deer finds its way down from the downs along one of the outer ramps and stands in a courtyard, blinking at the light, before somebody gently shows it back up.

No creature here seems afraid of any other. Everything shares the throughs. A person, a stoat, and a wren may pass through the same doorway in the same minute, and none of them will startle.

## What Does Not Happen

The strangest thing about Chalkmouth is not the light or the silence. It is that it does not change.

The downs above have seasons. Snow comes, larks come, the grass browns and greens. None of it reaches the bottom. Down here it is always the same hour of the same cold, bright morning, somewhere at the tail end of winter, just before anything begins. The ferns do not die back. The moss neither spreads nor recedes. The frost at the rim stays exactly where it is. The water in the basin stays at its mark. People age, slowly, and children grow, slowly, but even that seems muffled, as though time were a sound and the moss had drunk most of it.

The inhabitants do not find this sinister. To them it is simply what the place *is*: settled, finished, balanced. Their few customs all serve to keep it so. Nothing is ever built new; things are only mended. Nothing is thrown away; broken pots are set on sills for the moss to take. Nothing is carried up out of the pit except the occasional lost deer. When someone dies, they are laid in one of the oldest rooms and the room's door is left open. The moss comes in over them over the long unchanging years, until they are a soft green shape on a soft green bed, and then that room becomes a through like any other, and people walk past with a hand touched briefly to the wall.

## Arriving, and Leaving

If you climb down, by the outer ramp, past the yews, through the first ivy-house, you will be met. Not greeted, exactly. Someone will look up from their cord-twisting and make a small gesture: palm down, fingers spreading. It means something like *settle*.

And you will, almost without meaning to. Your voice will drop. Your steps will soften on the moss. After an hour you will stop noticing the cold, and after a day you will stop noticing the light, and after that you will find it hard to remember what the downs above looked like, or why anyone would want a world where things kept moving.

The people who have left say it was the silence they missed first. Up top, they say, everything is so loud, and so full of *weather*.
