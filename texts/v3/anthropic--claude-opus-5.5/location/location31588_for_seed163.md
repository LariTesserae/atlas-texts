# The Loud Country

## The Ground That Is Not Ground

Walk out onto the plateau and the first thing you learn is that there is no soil. What gives under your boot is moss, a living mat two and three meters deep, and under it the rock. Nothing lies between them.

The mat is mostly sphagnum in a dozen colors: wine-red where it sits drier on the rises, gold-green in the hollows, a green so pale it looks lit from inside where water sheets just beneath the surface. Clubmoss threads through it in branching cords. Liverworts lie flat and glossy in the wettest places, like spilled leaves of green wax. Sedges grow in scattered tufts, and dwarf willows no higher than your ankle creep sideways across the surface, rooting as they go. There is no tree taller than your knee in any direction, as far as you can see. The plants are not growing on the landscape. They are the landscape. Every contour you walk over is the shape of the moss, and the rock beneath is only rumored.

Step and the mat sinks. Water wells up around your foot, clear and so cold it aches through leather, then drains back as you lift away. Your footprint fills and softens and is gone within the hour. A person can cross this country all day and leave no trace behind them.

## The Light

The plateau sits high, above most weather. The air is thin and dry above and wet below, and the sun comes through it almost unfiltered. The light is enormous. It falls on a surface that is everywhere wet, and the wet throws it back. Every moss tip holds a bead of water. The ten thousand shallow pools scattered across the mat each hold a sky. At midday the land glitters so completely that the eye can't rest on it. The people who work here wear slitted goggles of horn or smoked glass, and their cheeks are burned brown-red even in the cool.

It is cool, always. It is rarely freezing in summer and never warm. The wind comes off the ice cap to the north, carrying the smell of old snow and cold stone. You can stand in full brilliant sunlight and see your breath. The contrast confuses the body: the light says noon in high summer, the skin says early spring.

To the north the ice itself fills the horizon, a low white wall, blue at its base, too bright to look at directly. To the south the land simply stops.

## The Young Rock

Where the moss thins on the high points, or where the ice has only lately withdrawn, the rock shows through. It is almost shockingly new.

This plateau has been out from under the ice for only a few thousand years, and at its northern edge for only decades. The rock hasn't begun to weather. It is dark and fine-grained, polished by the ice to a sheen like worn pewter and scored with long parallel grooves that all point the same way, south, toward the drop. Boulders sit where the ice set them down, perched on nothing, some the size of houses. None of them has settled. Their edges are still sharp enough to cut.

Most strangely, there are no valleys. Water has not yet had time to organize itself. It hasn't carved channels or cut gullies or found the dendritic branching that older land takes on. Instead it moves across the plateau in sheets, through and under the moss, a slow broad seep a finger's depth thick and kilometers wide. The whole plateau drains at once, everywhere, like water across a tilted tray. Where the moss is thick you cannot see it moving at all. You only feel the faint pull of it against your ankles when you stand still. The moss is a sponge that holds most of the year's melt and releases it slowly, all summer and well into the dark months.

The first rivers are only beginning. Near the southern rim the seeps gather into shallow braided runs over bare rock, then into proper streams, then into a dozen trenches no deeper than a man is tall. Those trenches are the first cuts this land has ever taken. In ten thousand years they may be canyons. Now they are raw notches at the lip of the world.

## The Sound

You hear the plateau before you see its edge. In truth you hear it the whole time, from the first hour. At first you take it for wind. Then you take it for your own blood. Eventually you understand that it is the Falls.

Along thirty kilometers of southern rim, the gathered water of the whole plateau goes over the edge. It doesn't fall in one great cataract. It pours through hundreds of separate outlets, streams and trenches and plain sheets of overflow, all plunging nearly a kilometer into the valley mist below. Each is loud. Together they make a sound that isn't really a sound anymore but a condition of the air. It is a low roar so continuous and so vast that it enters the body through the feet and chest before the ears.

Out in the middle of the plateau it is a steady ocean-hum. You can talk if you lean close. Within five kilometers of the rim you must shout. Within one kilometer speech is impossible. The roar has texture: a deep bass foundation from the largest falls, a hissing mid-register of the spray, and over that the crack and boom of water striking ledges far below. The noise never pauses. It swells a little in the long afternoons of midsummer, when melt peaks, and sinks slightly in the deep of winter, but it does not stop. There is no silence anywhere on the plateau, and no one who lives here has heard any.

The spray rises from the drop in a standing white wall and drifts back over the rim. It feeds a band of moss along the edge so thick and saturated that it hangs over the lip in dripping green cornices. The sun, shining into this constant mist, makes rainbows that don't fade. They are fixed and stacked, arcs within arcs, sitting over the drop all day while the light holds.

## The Works

Industry came here for the sound, or rather for what makes it: water falling from a great height, in a volume that never fails.

The works are few and far apart. That is part of their character. Over the whole plateau there are perhaps six structures of any size, and a person can walk a full day between them.

**The turbine halls** sit at the rim, three of them, spaced along the edge wherever the rock is soundest. They are long and low, built of dressed stone quarried from erratic boulders, with iron roofs painted a dull red that is the only warm color for a hundred kilometers. Each takes water from a stone intake at the lip of a stream and drops it through riveted iron penstocks, pipes wide enough to walk through, down the face of the cliff to the generating floor far below. The halls on the rim are control houses. The machines proper sit at the bottom, in the mist, and their hum joins the roar and disappears into it. Inside the upper houses, rows of brass-cased gauges and heavy knife-switches are tended by workers who communicate entirely by hand and by colored lamp. Many of them have gone partly deaf. They wear it without complaint, as a kind of tenure.

**The pylons** stride away southward from the halls, lattice towers of black iron carrying cables down off the plateau to wherever the power is wanted. On the plateau itself they stand on broad timber rafts laid over the moss, because there is no footing to be had except bedrock, and bedrock lies too deep. Some have tilted over the decades as the moss grew around and beneath their rafts. The linemen check their lean each spring with plumb and level.

**The railway** is a narrow-gauge line on timber trestles that crosses the plateau from the pass in the west to the turbine halls, laid directly onto the moss on long sleepers that spread the weight. A small tank engine runs it twice a week, pulling ore cars loaded with supplies and people and occasionally a crate of spare turbine parts. In the enormous open light you can watch its smoke for an hour before it arrives. The trestles sink a little every year and have to be jacked up and re-shimmed. The line is a patient war against the softness of the ground.

**The ice station** stands at the northern edge, where the ice is withdrawing. It is a single stone hut with a weather mast, a rain gauge, and a line of iron stakes driven into newly bared rock. Each stake is dated, and each one marks where the ice front stood in a given year. The line of stakes runs back south across the moss for three kilometers. The oldest are buried to their tops in the mat, their dates just legible on brass tags that someone clears once a year. The newest stand on naked, gleaming, grooved stone that hasn't yet caught a single spore.

Beyond these there are only traces: a collapsed stone cairn from before the works; a rusted rail spike driven into an erratic for no reason anyone remembers; the peeled ribs of a wooden sledge sinking slowly into the moss, half green already.

## The Rules of This Place

The people of the works live by a few rules. Some are written and all are understood.

**Do not cut the moss.** The moss is the reservoir. It holds the winter's snow and the summer's melt and lets them out slowly, so that the falls run all year and the turbines never stop. Where the moss has been cut or burned, the water rushes off in spring floods and fails in autumn. Everyone here knows this. Peat cutting is forbidden across the plateau. The rail line, the pylons, and the halls are all built to spread their weight and to disturb as little of the mat as they can.

**Walk in the light, rest in the dark.** The light is so strong that working without goggles through a long day blinds you for a night. The shift runs early morning to midday, then again from evening, with the high glare of afternoon given over to sleep behind shuttered windows.

**Speak with the hands.** Near the rim, the roar makes speech useless. The works have their own sign language, fast and flat and full of words for pressure, flow, danger, and weather. Workers born on the plateau learn it as a first tongue. Visitors are handed a card of basic signs at the pass and warned that shouting is useless and rude.

**The land is growing.** Every year the ice gives up a few more meters of bare rock. Every year the moss follows it north, first as a green film in the cracks, then a thin carpet, then within a generation a mat thick enough to walk on. The plateau is getting larger and wetter. The turbines are slowly getting stronger. No one here will see the change finish. It runs on the scale of centuries.

## The Living Things That Move

Very little moves here other than water. The plateau is a place of plants, and animals are an exception.

A single species of small brown hare lives on the mat, grazing willow and sedge. It is rarely seen and often guessed at from droppings. A pipit nests in the tussocks and rises singing in early summer. You can see its song but not hear it, because the roar swallows it whole. Its beak opens and its throat moves, and it seems to sing in silence. There are midges in the still weeks, in clouds that catch the light like dust. On the ice edge a pair of ravens has lived as long as anyone remembers, or their descendants have, picking over whatever the ice gives up.

Nothing else. No deer, no grazing herds, no fish in waters too new and too cold. The plateau's life is almost all rooted. It waits and grows and drinks the light.

## The Whole of It

Stand in the middle of the plateau at midmorning, with the goggles pushed up for a moment so you can see truly, and it all comes together. The moss stretches away in every direction, red and gold and pale green, glittering with ten thousand pools. The ice glows white along the north. A thin line of smoke marks the railway. A single iron pylon stands small in the distance, leaning slightly. The air is cold and brilliant and wet in your lungs. The ground gives and returns beneath you. The endless roar of the falls sits under everything, felt more than heard, as though the land itself were a great engine idling.

It is a young country, not yet shaped. Its rivers have not yet been decided. Its rock is still smooth from the ice. Everything that lives here is green and patient, and the only thing that hurries is the water, going over the edge.
