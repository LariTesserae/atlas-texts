# The Hammer Moor

## The Ground

The first thing to know is that there is no soil.

What you stand on is living plant, several meters thick. Sphagnum is packed over older sphagnum, which is packed over a brown compression of every moss that has ever grown here. Somewhere far down it becomes a dark jelly that was once plant and is now closer to oil. Below that, finally, is rock. You will never see the rock. You feel it only as a faint firmness when the mat under your boots is thin, the way you feel a bone through a thick sleeve.

The mat is wet all the way down. Each step squeezes water out around your feet in a rust-colored ring, and each step lifted lets it seep back. Nothing here is dry. Nothing here is not growing. The mosses are green-gold, blood-red, and a pale green like the underside of a wave. Cotton-sedge stands in loose tufts. There are sundews, and bog-rosemary no higher than your ankle. There are low heathers whose woody stems have been bent flat by wind until they grow along the surface like veins under skin. Lichens coat everything that holds still long enough. Very little holds still.

It is cold. It is not frozen, only a steady cold a few degrees above freezing, the kind that works into the joints of the hands. At the edges of the pools a skin of ice forms, breaks, forms again. Rime grows on the sedge tips and melts, grows and melts, many times an hour, following something that is not the sun.

## The Plateau

You are high, and you can tell mostly from the thinness of the air and the closeness of the cloud.

There were mountains here once. They were a whole range, sharp and young, taller than anything now standing on this continent. They have been worn down over a span of time with no useful number. The range is now a plateau so level that the tarns lie in it like spilled mercury. There is no way to tell which direction water should run, so it runs in all of them and in none. A few granite knuckles break the surface of the mat, rounded smooth as river stones though they are the size of houses. They are the last stumps of the old summits, and they are scattered miles apart. Lichen has written over them so thickly that they look upholstered.

The plateau is enormous. Standing on it, you feel it go on past sight in every direction: a slow brown-green ocean under a pewter lid of cloud. The cloud base hangs perhaps thirty meters above you, sometimes three, sometimes down around your knees. The light is dim and sourceless and even. There are no shadows. Or rather, there are none of the ordinary kind.

## The Work

The mat is doing something, and after an hour you start to feel it through your feet.

A swell comes. The ground ahead rises, a long low hump in the moss about knee-high, and travels toward you at walking speed. It passes under you and lifts you gently, and water bursts out of the moss around you in a thousand small jets. Then it moves on behind. A few seconds later you hear it: a deep thud from under the ground, like a drop-forge in a building you cannot see. Another swell follows the first, then another, in a slow, regular rhythm.

The moor is pumping. Nobody built the pump. Over some unknowable age the mosses learned to contract in waves, and they drive water across the plateau the way a gut drives food. They pull it up from the rock and push it out to the tarns, and from the tarns into the air. They run it through the whole living body of the moor. The thud is the mat slapping back down onto itself after each wave. There are many rhythms at once, coming from many directions. Some are slow as a heartbeat, some quick as a steam hammer. Where two meet, the ground bucks and throws up a spout of peat-water that hangs for a moment in the cold air before collapsing.

It gets stranger the longer you look.

Miles off, pale columns stand up from the moor and vanish into the cloud. These are steam. Where the mat is thickest, the buried peat has been rotting for so long, and so densely, that it runs hot. It is decomposing itself like a banked furnace. The heat forces boiling water up through channels in the moss, and it vents with a sound halfway between a kettle and a factory whistle. The columns are few and far apart, perhaps one every few kilometers. They lean in the wind like smokestacks. The moss around each vent grows ranker, redder, and more crowded, and it steams faintly like the flank of a horse after work.

Then there is the current. At the margins of certain tarns, where one kind of moss meets another, the air crackles. The mat is making electricity. Some chemistry between the acid water and the alkaline mosses, or between living layers and dead ones, builds a charge until it cannot hold. Blue-white threads of spark walk across the water surface, and you can smell ozone. Your hair lifts. The sedge heads nearby are all turned toward the tarn, their white cotton standing out stiff and glowing faintly at the tips. On bad nights the whole plateau hums, a low mains-frequency drone you feel more in your teeth than your ears.

The sedge is spinning. Wind combs the cotton-sedge heads and draws off long fibers, which twist together in the eddies behind the granite knuckles. Rope forms, then cable. These lengths of pale woven fiber are hundreds of meters long, and they roll across the moor in the gales, catch on the knuckles, and wrap them. Some knuckles are bound so thickly they look like bobbins. The cables are never cut, woven, or used by anyone. They are simply produced, in volume, and left.

It is a factory that no one built, running at full capacity with no product and no owner. It has been running since before there was anyone to own anything.

## The Noise

It is loud here, though not deafening. It is the steady loudness of a working district heard from the next street.

There is the thud of the swells, the shriek and hiss of the vents, and the electric drone. There is the constant slop and gurgle of water moving everywhere at once. Beneath all of it is a sound you only notice when it stops: a vast, slow grinding, like millstones. It is the mat dragging over the buried rock as the whole moor creeps, and it is where the last of the mountains are still being ground away.

Over all of that are the animals.

## The Living

For a place so empty to the eye, it is crowded with life.

Wading birds come in flocks so large they change the color of the cloud: golden plover, dunlin, and something like a curlew, with a bill so long and curved it seems impossible. The flocks turn together, flashing pale and then dark, and their calls come down as a whistling rain. They feed on the swells. When a wave passes, the jets of water throw up larvae and worms and tiny freshwater shrimp, and the birds follow each ridge across the moor like gulls behind a plough.

Snipe drum overhead. Their stiff tail feathers make a throbbing, bleating hum as they dive. Here the drumming has fallen in with the rhythm of the pumps, so every dive lands on the beat, and the air above the moor pulses in time with the ground.

Frogs fill the shallow pools in the tens of thousands. They call in short, clipped bursts, and near the charged tarns their calls take on a strange sharpness, a staccato rattle like a telegraph key. Listen long enough and you would swear they are sending something.

Shaggy grazers wander the moor in small herds. They are something like reindeer, something like musk ox, with coats matted so thick with moss that they seem to be part of the mat walking around. They move slowly and far apart, and they are always in the distance. They never startle. When a bog-burst opens near them they simply step around it.

Eels live in the tarns, black and very old. Eels are said to live here for centuries. When the tarns drain, which happens often and without warning, they slide across the open moss to the next pool. They leave glistening trails, sometimes hundreds at once, a whole migration crossing between one minute and the next.

Moths are drawn to the sparks. Clouds of them rise pale and furred at dusk around the charged tarns. They fly through the arcing current and come out glowing, and they drift away over the moor like embers from a forge. Wherever they settle, the moss lights faintly for a few minutes afterward.

The insects are too many to count. There are midges, water-beetles, and pond-skaters. There are spiders that ride the swells on sheets of silk, and slugs as long as your forearm with backs the same green-gold as the moss. The whole mat seethes when you kneel to look at it.

## The Shadows That Aren't

And here is the thing that makes this place not quite right.

The mountains are gone, but the moor still remembers them.

In the fog you will see shapes. There are huge dark slopes, ridge-lines, and summits standing up into the cloud where there is nothing but flat moss. They are not illusions in the usual sense. They cast shadow. Walk toward one and the light dims as you pass into it. The air grows colder, as it does on the north face of a real peak. The wind changes direction and funnels between slopes that are not there. Rain falls in them at an angle, as if sliding down an invisible face.

These are the ghost mountains. The plateau is so old, and settled so completely into its final shape, that the land seems to have lost track of which version of itself is current. The range that stood here a hundred million years ago still exerts itself. It keeps its shadows, its weather, and its cold. Birds fly around the ghost peaks, never through them. The flocks bend as if around stone. The grazers will not climb them, and neither, once you have tried, will you. Walking uphill on flat ground makes the heart pound, the calves burn, and the ears pop.

It goes further. The swells carry more than water. When a large one passes beneath you, for the half-second you ride its crest, you sometimes see the plateau as it was at another age. You might see the mountains standing whole and savage and white, with glaciers and bare rock and no moss anywhere. Or you see nothing at all, only a plain of dust under a strange sun. Then the wave passes and you drop back into the present, the thud arrives, and the frogs resume.

Distances do not hold. A steam column you are walking toward will be three kilometers off for hours, then suddenly at your elbow. A flock of plover passes overhead, and ten minutes later the same flock passes again: the same shape, the same calls in the same order, as if the moment had been run twice. The horizon, when the cloud lifts enough to see one, is not always flat. Sometimes it curves up at the edges like the inside of a bowl, and you stand at the bottom of the whole world. Compasses spin near the charged tarns. Elsewhere they settle confidently on a north that is not the old north, but the north the range had when it was young.

## The Weather

None of this is calm. The moor is always in motion.

Storms cross the plateau constantly, one on the heels of another, dragging gray curtains of rain and sleet. The cloud tears and heals. The light swings from dim to dimmer, never bright.

The mat ruptures. Where pressure from the pumping builds too high, the moss splits open with a sound like canvas tearing. A bog-burst pours out: a river of black liquid peat that flows for a kilometer across the moor, steaming in the cold, before it slows and stops and begins at once to green over. Within days the scar is moss again. Tarns open and close. A pool the size of a lake can drain in an hour through some channel in the deep mat, leaving the eels crossing its bed. Elsewhere water wells up through the moss in a slow flood and a new tarn spreads where there was none.

The steam columns shift. A vent chokes and dies, and another bursts open a kilometer away, scalding the mosses around it before they turn red and rank and thriving. Cables of sedge roll past in the gales, tumbling like dropped rope. Spark-storms walk across the tarns. The ghost mountains shift in the fog: a peak here one hour, a col the next, a whole range turned to face a different way.

Everything is being made and unmade at once. The moor builds itself up out of its own death and tears itself down again, over and over, in the cold and the dark and the noise. Underneath, at the slow pace of rock, the grinding goes on, taking away the last of the mountains' bones.

## Being There

If you stand long enough, you stop hearing the moor as noise and begin to hear it as a single enormous working thing. The thud of the pumps, the drone of the current, the whistle of the vents, the drumming of the snipe on the beat, the telegraph-frogs, and the grinding underneath all fold into one continuous labor.

You are very small on it and very alone. The presences are scattered: a herd in the far distance, a steam column leaning in the wind, a ghost peak's shadow falling across you from nowhere. Between them there is only moss, water, and cloud, in every direction, for longer than you can see.

It is not hostile. It simply does not notice you. It is busy. It has been busy since the mountains were young, and it will be busy long after the mountains' shadows finally forget themselves and lie down flat. Even then, you suspect, the moor will keep working, turning its own death into its own body.
