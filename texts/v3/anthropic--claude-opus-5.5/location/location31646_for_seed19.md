# The Held Plateau

You come up onto it the way you'd come up out of water, except there's no water anywhere and hasn't been for longer than anyone could say. The last of the climb is a slope so gradual you barely notice it's a slope. Then the air thins by some final degree, the sky darkens toward the zenith to the blue of a bruise's edge, and the ground in front of you stops rising. It just goes on.

This is the top of something that was once a mountain range, possibly the tallest one the world ever raised. It has been worn down for so long that its peaks are gone, and what's left is their root: a tableland of pale stone lying at an altitude where breath comes shallow and the cold is steady rather than bitter. It is still nearly the highest ground there is. The erosion finished long ago. Nothing is being taken away anymore. The plateau has reached the shape it will keep.

---

## The Ground

The stone is quartzite and old granite, and in places something darker and glassier that might once have been lava. Every surface has been polished. Wind scoured this rock for ages beyond counting, then at some point stopped. The texture it left is smooth, faintly greasy to the touch, like the inside of a shell or a doorstep worn by generations of feet. Low swells and hollows run across it in long, shallow undulations, as if a sea had frozen mid-swell and then been sanded for a million years.

In the hollows there is dust, very fine and very dry, the colour of bone meal. It doesn't blow, because there's nothing to blow it. It lies where it settled. Crusts of white mineral line some of the deeper basins, the ghosts of lakes that evaporated before anything with eyes existed to see them. Salt and gypsum form in brittle rosettes. Once in a very long while you find a single bead of frost clinging to the underside of an overhang. It doesn't melt and it doesn't grow. It may have been there since the last water in the world passed through.

The distances are enormous. Standing on one of the low swells, you can see perhaps sixty or seventy miles in several directions, with the plateau broken only by a few worn tors, rounded stacks of rock like loaves or knuckles, and by long, gentle troughs that were once valleys. The horizon doesn't curve away the way it should. It seems to lift slightly at the far edges, like the rim of a plate, so that you always feel you're standing in the bottom of something very large and very shallow.

## The Plants

Almost nothing grows. What does grow grows slowly enough to count as geology.

There are lichens: grey-green, rust-orange, and a black kind like spilled tar. They form rings on the stone, some of them yards across, and each ring's age is better measured against the erosion of the rock than against any calendar. There are cushion plants, dense domes of tiny leaves packed so tightly they're hard as a fist, clinging in the shelter of tors. A few have flowers, pinprick-small and pale violet, and the flowers are open. As far as anyone can tell, they have always been open.

Once, in a trough, there is a single low shrub with grey bark and no leaves. Its branches are polished like the rock. Its roots go down into a crack and are said never to reach anything.

## The Animals

Then you realise the swell you're standing on is covered in animals, and so is the next one, and the one beyond that, all the way out.

At first your eye reads them as stones. They're the same pale colours as the rock, the same dust-grey and bone-cream, and they're very still. Then one lowers its head. Then another, a little farther off, raises its head. Now you see them, and once you've seen them you can't stop seeing them. The whole plateau is thick with life.

There are grazers roughly the size of large goats, with heavy shoulders and thick fleeces, standing in loose herds of hundreds that shade into other herds, so there's no telling where one ends. Among their legs move smaller things: long-eared, round-bodied creatures like pikas or hares, dozens to every grazer. On the tors, birds perch in rows so dense the rock looks feathered, heavy-bodied ground birds with dun plumage. Above them, at intervals, larger birds hang in the air on outspread wings. They don't beat their wings and they don't glide anywhere. Every rock, turned over, is furred beneath with beetles and moths. The dust in the hollows, looked at closely, is partly insect: minute pale mites and springtails, millions to a handful, so the ground itself is faintly alive under your palm.

The density is overwhelming, but the quiet is stranger. Thousands of animals are within sight and you hear almost nothing: no bleating, no birdsong, no wingbeats. When you stand still long enough, what you can hear is breathing. It is one enormous, diffuse, very slow breath that seems to come from everywhere at once, in and out, perhaps once a minute. Under it there is a faint dry whisper of hooves shifting on stone and of countless small feet in dust, like sand poured very slowly from one hand into the other.

Look closer and you notice the animals are worn.

The grazers' horns have been polished down to smooth nubs, like the rock. Their faces are blurred. There are shallow depressions where eyes should be, and the suggestion of a muzzle, the way a statue looks after centuries of rain. The fleeces aren't wool so much as a kind of soft, fibrous weathering, felted and pale. The birds' beaks are rounded. The hares have no visible ears, only smooth swellings. They are alive: warm to the touch if you dare, breathing, sometimes shifting their weight. But they have been here as long as the stone and have eroded alongside it. They have undergone the same patient subtraction and have reached, like the plateau, their final, settled form.

The oldest of them are hard to tell from the tors. Some of the tors are almost certainly the oldest of them.

## The Rules

The plateau never changes, and it keeps to that without exception.

The sun stands a little past midday, low enough to cast long, clean shadows eastward and high enough to give ordinary, honest light. It doesn't move. The shadows don't lengthen. There is no night here, and no dawn. The light is neither harsh nor dim. It is the plain light of an afternoon that decided to stay.

The cold is the same everywhere and at every moment. It sits at the edge of discomfort, enough to make you want another layer but never enough to hurt.

The animals eat. The grazers lower their heads and crop at the lichen rings, and the hares nibble the cushion plants. The lichen never diminishes and the cushions never show a bite. Nothing drinks, because there's nothing to drink, and nothing seems to suffer for it.

Nothing is born and nothing dies. The number of animals is fixed. People who have tried to count a single herd report the same thing. The count comes out different every time, but it never goes up or down. It stays exactly as uncountable as it was.

Every animal moves in a loop. A grazer lowers its head, crops, raises its head, turns slightly left, and returns to where it began. A hare takes three hops forward and three back. A bird on a tor shifts from foot to foot. Watched individually, each is doing one small thing forever. Watched together, the whole plateau is a vast, patterned stillness made of motion. Across the herds these small cycles fall into phase, and waves of lowering and raising heads pass slowly across the land like wind across grass. There is no wind. The waves cross the whole visible plateau in a little under a minute, in time with the breath.

Footprints don't last. You can press your boot into the dust of a hollow and step away, and by the time you look back the dust has settled smooth again. Nothing moved it. The dust has simply always been smooth there.

## What Others Left

People have come here. Not many, and not recently, though "recently" doesn't mean much on the plateau.

There are cairns scattered at wide intervals, knee-high stacks of flat stones placed by hand. Some have a single white quartz pebble on top. There are low curved walls, windbreaks built against a wind that doesn't blow, with ash in their lee that never went cold because it was never warm. In the shelter of one tor stands a ring of upright slabs roughly man-high, with a hide roof long collapsed into dust. Inside the ring lie scattered tools: bone awls, a scraper of black glassy stone, a flint blade whose grip is polished smooth by some hand that held it for a very long time.

Scratched into the smooth faces of certain rocks are marks, shallow grooves made with a harder stone. Some are tallies, long rows of strokes that stop partway, as if the counter realised it was pointless. Some are drawings of the grazers, done in a few confident lines, with horns and eyes and faces. These are the only places on the whole plateau where the animals have faces.

In a few places the built things and the animals have become hard to tell apart. There's a cairn whose topmost stone rises and falls slightly, once a minute. In one of the windbreaks, a grazer lies curled in the exact posture of a sleeping person, and its fleece, looked at closely, has the weave of cloth. At the edge of one herd stands a figure that is taller and narrower than the others, stands upright, and holds something long in one worn-smooth hand. It might be a staff. It raises its head when the others raise theirs.

The pilgrims who came here may not all have left.

## The Breakdown

The longer you stay, the less the place holds together as a place.

Distances start to misbehave. A tor that seemed a mile off is suddenly within reach, then far again, and you haven't moved. You walk toward a herd for what feels like an hour and the herd stays the same size in your vision. Then you look down and find you're standing among them, and they're closer than you thought, pressed around you, warm and breathing and faceless, and they don't mind you at all.

The animals share something that isn't quite attention. They aren't watching you, since they have no eyes to watch with. But when you move, the waves of rising and falling heads bend slightly around you, like water around a stone, and the pattern accommodates you. Stand still long enough and your own breath falls into step with the vast slow breath of the plateau without your choosing it. After a while you notice you've been lowering your head when they lower theirs.

The ground may be breathing too. Lie flat on one of the long swells and you can feel it rise and fall very slightly, once a minute. The swells may be the backs of things so old and so worn they've become terrain. The whole plateau may be one creature, and the herds on it may be something like its thoughts, patterned and cyclical and going nowhere. Or the herds may be what's left of the mountain range, which wore down so slowly and so long that at some point the distinction between the eroding and the living simply wore away too.

Nobody knows. The plateau doesn't answer questions. It holds them, the way it holds everything, at a fixed and patient distance.

---

## Leaving

You can leave. Most people who come here do, eventually. You walk back to the edge, where the ground begins almost imperceptibly to descend, and the air thickens by a degree, and the sky's zenith fades back toward ordinary blue.

Behind you the waves of heads keep rising and falling across sixty miles of polished stone, in time with a breath you can still feel in your own chest for days afterward. The sun doesn't move. The hanging birds don't fall. The count stays uncountable.

If you look back from the last swell before the descent, you'll see the place exactly as you first saw it. It will look that way the next time too, and the time after that, until you are as worn and quiet as everything else up there.
