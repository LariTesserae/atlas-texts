# The Gullet of Seven Winds

## Approach

You come to it from below, climbing a spine of ridge so old its edges have rounded like the knuckles of a sleeping hand. The rock is a pale, close-grained stone, the colour of weak tea, and it has been sanded for so long that it feels almost warm and soft under the palm, like leather left in the sun. There is no water anywhere on this mountain. There never has been. No stream-cut runs down its flanks, no salt crust shows where a pool once dried. The stone has been shaped by wind alone, and the wind has had forever.

Near the top the ridge dips into a hollow, a hanging basin perhaps three hundred paces across, cupped between two broken horns of rock. From the lip you hear it before you see it clearly. It is a roar, but a roar with structure in it: a deep grinding bass, a thousand whistling tones layered over that, and under both a clicking, chittering, rasping noise of living things that never stops.

This is the Gullet. Seven winds are said to meet here, though no one has counted them the same way twice. What matters is that the air never settles. It pours in over the western horn hot and dry off the plateaus. It spills down from the eastern horn thinner and cooler. In the bowl it tangles, twists, and climbs, and it keeps this up forever.

## The Air

The first thing to understand is the dust. The air in the Gullet is never clear. It is thick with a fine mineral powder, ochre and rust and a strange pale green, lifted from the plateaus and carried up here, where the colliding winds slow and spin and let some of it fall. The light comes through it the way light comes through honey or old parchment, dim and golden-brown, with no real shadows. Noon here looks like late afternoon anywhere else. On the worst days it looks like dusk at every hour, and the sun is a pale coin you can stare at without blinking.

It is warm, always. The heat is dry, like an oven after the bread has come out. Your lips crack within the hour. Your eyes sting until you learn to squint the way the locals do, lids nearly closed, peering through lashes clotted with powder.

The dust is not dead. That is the second thing to understand.

## The Growth

The Gullet is lush, and nothing in it drinks.

The plants here have never known water. They run on something else: the dust itself, and the heat, and a thick amber oil that moves through their bodies the way sap moves through trees elsewhere. Their chemistry is resin and mineral and slow combustion. Break a stem and it bleeds a golden, fragrant, faintly smoking fluid that hardens to glassy beads within minutes. The whole basin smells of it: pine-pitch, myrrh, hot stone, and under those something metallic, like a struck flint.

The most common growth is what the resin-gatherers call **combers**. These are fern-like fronds, waist-high to head-high, whose leaves are split into thousands of fine hairs. They stand facing into the wind and strain the dust out of the air. Their hairs are sticky with resin, and the powder clings. Over hours a comber's fronds grow heavy and ochre-coated. Then the plant draws the dust inward, digests the minerals, and the leaves shine clean and gold again. A slope of combers ripples constantly, and it shifts colour as it goes: dun, then bright, then dun again, in waves that follow the wind.

Rising above the combers are the **spirals**. They are trees, more or less, with trunks that corkscrew upward in tight coils, sometimes three or four times a man's height. The trunks are hollow. The wind runs through them, and they sound. Each spiral has its own note, set by its length and the width of its bore. Some groan in the bass, some hum, and the youngest shriek like a boiling kettle. This is the grinding music you heard from the ridge. On a steady-wind day the Gullet plays something almost like a chord. On a turbulent day, which is most days, the chord breaks into a thousand overlapping howls that shift with every gust, so loud you have to put your mouth to someone's ear to be heard.

Low to the ground, in the lee of boulders, grow **cushions**: dense mats of tiny waxy leaves, grey-green, soft as moss. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the air, because they burn. Slowly and invisibly they oxidize their own resin, giving off heat and a faint shimmer. Travellers sleep on them. Animals den under them.

There are stranger things. There are **lanterns**: bulbous pods on thin stalks, translucent, full of resin, which catch the dim light and glow a deep orange, so the thickets look lit from inside. There are **whips**: long cords of fibre that lie coiled on the stone until a gust catches them, then lash out and snap like drovers' crops, cracking loud enough to startle birds. No one knows what the whips are for. Some say they scatter seed. Some say they are hunting.

## The Churn

Nothing in the Gullet holds still, and nothing lasts long.

This is the rule of the place, and you feel it within a day. The growth is fast, fast enough to watch. A comber frond uncoils from bud to full height between morning and midday. A young spiral lengthens a finger's width in an hour, and its note drops as it grows, so the music of the basin slides slowly downward across a season.

The destruction is just as fast. The winds are not gentle. When they come together wrong, a vortex forms in the bowl, a brown whirling column that tears across the basin floor. It rips combers out by the root and snaps the thinner spirals, flinging fragments high into the dust. Behind it lies a scoured track of bare stone, littered with resin beads and shredded gold fibre. Within a day or two the track is green again.

And there is fire. With this much dust in constant motion, the air carries a charge. On heavy days the dust-clouds crackle with dry lightning: pale blue-white forks with no rain behind them, only a smell of scorched metal. When a strike lands in a resin-rich thicket, the thicket goes up. It is not a slow burn. It is a whump of golden flame and a roaring updraft, and the spirals scream a single rising note as the fire runs through their hollows.

Then it stops. The combers are too dusty and mineral-heavy to carry flame far, and the winds pull it apart. Within an hour the burn is black stone with smoking stumps. By the next morning there are shoots. Some of the plants, the lanterns especially, seem to need the fire. Their pods burst only in heat and fling out seeds that root in the warm ash.

So the Gullet is never one picture. It is a mosaic that keeps reshuffling: a patch of gold combers, a scar of blackened stone, a fresh vortex-track going green, a thicket of lanterns glowing over the ash of last week's fire. From the rim it looks like a quilt being constantly torn and re-sewn by invisible hands.

Yet the bowl itself has not changed in a very long time. The stone is worn smooth and settled. The basin holds its shape the way an old riverbed would, if there had ever been a river. The churn happens *on* the Gullet, not *to* it. The rock underneath is patient. It has seen ten thousand thousand fires and does not care.

## The Living Things

The Gullet teems.

The air is full of **motes**. They are small insects, or something like insects, with dusty wings of pale brown, so many that on some days you cannot tell where the dust ends and the swarm begins. They feed on resin and on each other. They ride the vortices up and are flung out over the rim. More always come back.

Hunting the motes are the **scythes**: narrow-winged birds, grey and ochre, which fly with their beaks wide open, sweeping through the swarms. They never land if they can help it. They sleep on the wing, riding the updrafts above the bowl, and their thin high cries cut through the roar of the spirals like needles through cloth. A flock of scythes wheeling through a golden dust-cloud is the most beautiful thing in the Gullet, and the loudest after the storms.

On the ground there are lizards, and more lizards. The **pitch-lickers** are fat, slow, and armoured in scales that look like beads of old resin, dark and glassy. They crawl through the combers and lap sap from broken stems. They are drunk half the time, the gatherers say. The resin does something to them, and you will find them sprawled in the warm cushions, staring at nothing, their throats pulsing in time with the spirals' song. The **flickers** are the opposite: thin, rust-coloured, and impossibly quick. They are gone before you are sure you saw one, darting after motes and fallen seed.

Something lives inside the spirals, too. You hear it more than see it. It is a knocking, a tapping inside the hollow trunks, in rhythms that almost seem deliberate. The gatherers call them **drummers** and leave them alone. When a spiral burns, sometimes something small and many-legged and pale scuttles out of the top and flings itself into the wind. It spreads a membrane and glides away over the rim, and no one knows where it goes.

At the edges, where the stone is barest, there are **shell-crabs**. These are not crabs, but it is the nearest word: hand-sized things with flat bodies and many jointed legs, shelled in a crust of fused dust. After a fire they come out in their hundreds and pick over the ash. They eat the charred resin and grow their shells a little thicker, until you can see the stripes of each burning they have lived through.

None of them drink. There is nothing to drink. Their bodies run on resin and heat, like the plants. When they die they dry to husks in a day and blow away as dust, and the combers take them in.

## The Marks of People

People come here, but not many, and not to stay.

They come for the resin. Gullet amber is prized in the lowlands. It burns long, sweet, and clean. It seals pots and binds arrowheads, and it is chewed as a medicine for the lungs. The gatherers climb up in small parties, three or four at a time, carrying flint knives, bone scrapers, and baskets of woven comber fibre lined with leather. They stay a few days, tapping spirals and scraping hardened beads from the stone, and then they go down again before their lips bleed too badly and the dust settles too deep in their chests.

What they leave behind is scattered and small.

There are **cairns** around the rim, a dozen or so, stacked from the smooth tea-coloured stone. Each is hung with knots of fibre that stream and snap in the wind. They mark safe paths down into the bowl, the routes the vortices seem to avoid, though the gatherers argue about whether that is true.

There is one **shelter**, low against the eastern horn, where the stone overhangs. Its walls are unmortared rock, the gaps sealed with poured resin that has darkened over the generations to a deep red-black, so the whole wall looks like it is bleeding very slowly. Inside, the floor is spread with cut cushion-mats, warm and faintly shimmering. Soot from a thousand small resin-lamps coats the ceiling. Someone, long ago, scratched pictures into that soot, and later hands kept them fresh: spirals, scythes, a whirling column with a small figure inside it, arms raised.

Scattered through the basin are **tapping-marks**: neat V-shaped notches cut into old spirals, some fresh and weeping gold, some healed over into ridged scars. A few spirals have small clay cups tied beneath the notches with fibre cord to catch the sap. The cups are shaped by hand, thumb-marks still in the rims.

And there are the **singing-stones**. On a flat outcrop near the middle of the bowl, someone set a ring of seven upright slabs, each drilled with holes of different sizes. When the wind is right they add their own notes to the chorus, flat and stony beneath the hollow howl of the spirals. The gatherers say they were placed there to call the seven winds by name. They say that on the rare days the stones and the spirals sound together in true harmony, the dust clears for a moment and you can see the whole sky, a deep hard blue, empty, going on forever. Most gatherers have never seen it. Some have, and they do not come back up the mountain after that. No one is sure whether that is because they have what they came for, or because they are afraid of it.

## The Feel of It

Stand in the Gullet for an hour and the noise stops being noise. It becomes a kind of weather you live inside. The spirals groan and shriek. The whips crack. The scythes scream overhead. The motes whir past your face, the drummers knock inside the trunks, and the whole basin hisses with blowing grit against stone. Every now and then comes the dull whump of something catching fire, or the rising roar of a vortex building on the far side of the bowl.

The light is dim, the air is warm, and gold dust streams through everything. The ground under your feet is old and smooth and unbothered. Everything growing on it is frantic, fast, and short-lived: rising, burning, rising again.

It is not a place for staying. But it is not hostile, exactly. It simply has its own rules, and water is not among them. Stillness is not among them either.
