# The Laid Flats

It begins as a smell before it becomes a place. Warm brine, sulfur, and under both something green and faintly sweet, like cut stems left too long in a vase. Then the ground comes into focus, and the ground is the thing that matters. Nothing here was eroded into shape. Nothing was carved. Every surface, from one horizon to the next and for a thousand kilometers beyond that, was *laid down*. It was grown, precipitated, and stacked a film at a time by things too small to see.

## The ground

The land is almost perfectly flat and barely above the sea. It sits so low that "above" is a matter of tide and season, a few handspans of elevation spread across a distance where the curve of the planet matters more than any hill. From a standing height you can see perhaps five kilometers. The flats continue much farther than that. They run beyond sight for weeks of walking and spread across a coastal plain the size of a subcontinent. The ocean that feeds them is a rumor far to the west. Its water arrives here only as sheet floods, or seeping up through the ground, or falling as rain.

Underfoot, the ground is a microbial mat. It is leathery and slightly springy, and it gives like thick felt laid over wet clay. Its colors run in bands. Olive green sits at the surface, where the light-eaters live. Beneath that lies a thin line of purple, then a pinkish layer, then a black one that smells of eggs when broken. The bands are only millimeters deep. Below them the mat becomes stone. The bacteria trap grains of carbonate mud and help calcium carbonate crystallize out of the saturated water around them. Over years the lower layers harden, laminate, and turn to rock. So the whole landscape is a single slow construction site. The living layer sits on top of a building made of its own dead.

That building is not flat everywhere. In the shallow basins the mats rise into domes: stromatolites. They look like loaves of bread, or the backs of buried animals, or the tops of pillars that never finished rising. Some are the size of a fist. Some reach the height of a table, and those have been growing since before any calendar. Cut one open and you would find thousands of thin, wavy laminae, one for every tide, every day, every alternation of light and dark. They are archives nobody reads. In some basins the domes crowd together and fuse into reefs, long ridges of clotted, knobby stone called thrombolites. These ridges divide the water into rooms.

## Where the water is

The place is damp, but not wet. Water here is a condition of the ground rather than a feature of it. It comes in several ways.

**Dew.** Every dawn the flats are furred with it. The night air cools just enough over the mats for moisture to condense, and for an hour after sunrise every surface glitters. The mats drink it and swell. By mid-morning it is gone.

**Seeps.** Warm groundwater rises through fractures in the old laid-down rock, carrying dissolved carbonate from far inland. Where it reaches the surface it spreads into shallow pools, and the pools build their own rims. Carbonate precipitates at each lip where the water spills over and degasses. The result is rimstone terraces: nested, scalloped basins like stacked saucers, each one overflowing into the next. Some terrace fields are a few meters across. Others step downhill, if downhill is even the word for a gradient of centimeters per kilometer, for miles, a staircase of mirrors. The water in them is blood-warm, clear, and tinted green by the mats lining every basin.

**Lagoons.** Across the lowest parts of the plain lie hypersaline lagoons, very broad and very shallow. A person could wade across most of them without wetting a knee, if the water weren't so warm and so dense with salt. They shrink and grow with the seasons. Where they retreat they leave white margins of gypsum crystals, bladed and lattice-like and crunching. Those crystals are construction too, the lagoon building its own shoreline out of what it cannot hold.

**Storms.** These are the fourth way, and the most violent.

## The weather this place makes

The plain is large enough, flat enough, and hot enough to generate its own weather, and it does so almost every day.

By noon the air above the mats is shimmering. The temperature on the surface reaches the point where standing water becomes uncomfortable to the touch, and the air a meter up is not much cooler. Heat pours off the ground in invisible columns. Out over the lagoons, where evaporation is fiercest, the air grows thick with moisture. Somewhere around early afternoon, far out on the flats, a cumulus tower begins to boil upward. Then another forms, and another.

These are not the storms of mountain country, forced up by slopes. They are convection storms, born of the plain itself. They grow in rows along invisible lines where the sea breeze, crawling in from the distant west, collides with the stagnant heat of the interior. On clear days you can watch the sea-breeze front advance. A line of low clouds forms across the horizon, and the haze ahead of it is subtly different from the haze behind.

When the storms break, the rain comes as a wall. It is warm and heavy, and it lasts perhaps twenty minutes. The mats, which have been drying and cracking since dawn, are suddenly drowned under a skin of fresh water. Polygonal desiccation cracks fill and vanish. Raindrops leave dimples in the soft upper mud, thousands per square meter. Some of those dimples will be caught and preserved by the next day's growth, so that the rock underfoot remembers this rain too.

Then the storm moves on. The sun returns. By evening the fresh water has sheeted away into the lagoons or soaked downward, and steam rises from the flats in long, low sheets that glow orange in the late light.

## What grows

Almost nothing grows here in the way a green plant grows. The mats are the vegetation, and they are sparse in a strange way. They cover almost everything, but they are so thin, so low, so close to the substrate that from a distance the flats look barren. They read as a pastel desert of salt-white, dun, and faint green rather than a garden.

In a few places, where seeps have kept the ground fresh enough for long enough, there are clumps of something taller. Low, fleshy, salt-tolerant succulents grow in isolated patches, perhaps ten meters apart. Their stems are jointed and translucent, red at the tips, and they stand no higher than a shin. They have no competitors, so they spread in loose circles. The circles don't touch.

Along the rims of the oldest terrace fields there are also filamentous green algae, streaming in the overflow like hair in a current. That is all.

## The absence that makes it possible

There are no animals. There are no insects in the air, no crabs in the lagoons, no worms in the mud, no birds on the horizon. And that absence is the law of this place.

On other shores, grazing creatures eat mats like these as fast as they grow. Burrowers churn the laminae before they can harden, and stromatolites survive only in refuges too salty or too hot for anything else. Here, nothing has arrived to eat. So the builders build uninterrupted. Every layer that forms stays formed. Every dome keeps rising. The surface is not disturbed except by weather, and weather here is gentle to stone, because the rain is brief and the gradients are too shallow for water to gain carving speed.

This is why the flats are so *new*. Erosion has barely begun. The channels that exist were built, not cut. Where sheet floods drain toward the lagoons, the mats grow thicker along the flow margins and raise little levees, so the water ends up running between walls it helped make. There are no ravines, no scarps, no talus, no weathered faces. Cracks exist, but they are fresh and shallow, and most of them heal. The oldest surfaces are only a few centuries old at the top. Everything visible is the most recent page of a book still being written.

## What you hear

Very little.

The wind is the loudest thing, when it blows. It moves in long, low hisses across the flats, with nothing to catch on but the rims of domes and the stems of the scattered succulents. On calm days even that stops.

Then you begin to hear the small sounds. Late on bright afternoons the mats release oxygen. It gathers in bubbles under the surface film until they rise and pop, a faint, constant ticking across the lagoons, like rain that isn't falling. At the terrace rims there is the trickle of overflow, a sound so thin it disappears if you step away. In the heat of noon the drying crust crackles as it contracts, the gypsum rims especially, with a sound like cellophane being slowly unwrapped. At dusk the crust cools and ticks back.

And there is thunder, always far off. It is not quite heard so much as felt as pressure. The storms stay on the horizon most afternoons, and their sound arrives late and smoothed by distance, a long sigh from the edge of the world.

## Light

The light is ordinary sunlight, but the landscape does particular things with it. At dawn the dew-covered mats turn the plain into a field of low glitter, silver-white under a pale sky. At midday the glare off the gypsum margins and the lagoon surfaces is harsh. Colors wash out, and the horizon dissolves into a white band of heat haze where mirages float. Distant stromatolite reefs appear to hang above the ground, doubled, upside-down.

Afternoon brings the storms and their shadows, huge moving blotches of dark crossing the plain at the speed of the wind. After rain the air clears, and for an hour the colors come back: the olive and pink of the mats, the white of the gypsum, the turquoise of shallow brine over pale carbonate. Sunset is long and red on the steam. Night is dark and full of stars, reflected in every terrace pool.

## The scale of it

It is hard to hold in mind how large this is. A person crossing the flats would walk for days without meeting a feature taller than their own waist, and without the view changing much. Day by day there would be small variations. A field of fused domes would give way to open mat, a lagoon to a gypsum pan, a terrace staircase to a plain of cracked, healing crust. The same processes repeat everywhere with different emphases: dew, heat, storm, drying, growth.

At this scale, the builders are doing something geological. The flats are slowly building themselves upward and outward. Their carbonate traps the sea's dissolved stone and their layers thicken the continent's edge. Over enough time this entire plain will become a single limestone platform, kilometers thick. Its base was laid by bacteria in the shallow warm light, and its top is still being laid now, this morning, this hour, one film at a time.

For now it is unfinished. It is hot, damp, quiet, and very bright. It is made entirely of things that were made, and nothing here has yet begun to take it apart.
