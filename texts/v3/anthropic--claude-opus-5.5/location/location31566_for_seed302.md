# The Weeping Field

It sits on a tableland high enough that the sky has thinned to a hard, dark blue at the zenith, and the ground is brighter than the sky.

From any vantage the first impression is of a crowd. Pale stone columns stand shoulder to shoulder across the plateau, thousands upon thousands of them, packed so close that their flanks nearly touch. They run to the horizon in every direction the eye can follow, a stone thicket without a single tree in it. Each column is roughly the height of two people and as thick through as a barrel. Their tops are rounded and domed, like the crowns of heads bowed in the same direction. The spaces between them are narrow fissures, an arm's width at most, sometimes a hand's width, and in places they close entirely where two columns have fused along a seam.

Every one of them is weeping.

## The Water

The plateau is a sponge under pressure. Deep beneath it, water has been forced into a layer of porous rock and has nowhere to go but up, and it finds its way up through the columns themselves. Each column has a narrow throat at its core, and water wells from the crown of each dome in a slow, continuous upwelling. It is not a fountain. It is more like a vessel filled precisely to its brim and kept there. The water sheets over the dome and runs down the flanks in a film so thin and even that the stone seems lacquered rather than wet.

The film thickens as it descends. By the time it reaches the base of a column, it has gathered into ropes and braids that spill into the fissures. The fissures carry it away through a hidden network of channels no wider than gutters, which merge underground and resurface somewhere far off the plateau's edge. From above, you see none of that. You see only stone and glaze and the downward shimmer, repeated to the edge of sight.

The water is cold, a few degrees above freezing. It carries so much dissolved mineral, silica and a little calcium, that it is faintly heavy in the hand and leaves a chalky slickness on the skin as it dries. It tastes of flint and something like cold metal.

## The Stone

The columns are made of the water. Over a span of time with no meaningful beginning, mineral has settled out of the flow and built each column in layers. Because the water always runs the same way, the columns are fluted with shallow vertical grooves, the channels where the film prefers to run. The fluting gives them the look of organ pipes, or of wax that has run and set.

The stone is sinter, a dense silica rock the white of old bone, and translucent at the edges. Where the film is thinnest, near the crowns, the surface shows faint banding in cream and pearl. Lower down it is pure white. The whole surface is glassy-smooth, polished by an unbroken skin of moving water.

Here is the first of the field's strangeness: the columns neither grow nor shrink. At the crowns, where the upwelling water is freshest and most aggressive, it dissolves the stone very slightly. Along the flanks and at the base, as the water cools and slows, it deposits the same amount back. The two processes cancel exactly. Each column is a standing wave in rock, made continuously of new material and holding exactly the same shape. The fluting stays put. The seam where two columns touch stays put. A scratch made in the stone would be healed within a season and the surface returned to what it was. The field is an equilibrium so precise that it amounts to stillness, even though nothing in it stops moving.

## The Light

The brightness is total. The sun at this altitude is fierce and small, and the plateau takes that light and multiplies it. Every column is a white body under a mirror film. Every film catches the sun on its convex face and throws it back, so the field glitters in overlapping points, millions of small suns sliding down millions of flanks. Nothing in the field casts a truly dark shadow. The fissures between columns, which ought to be black, are lit from their walls by reflection upon reflection until even their bottoms glow a faint milky blue.

Above the columns hangs a permanent low haze, a spray-mist no thicker than a veil, raised by the tumbling at the columns' bases and held by the cold, still air. The mist takes the light and diffuses it, so brightness seems to come from everywhere at once, from below as much as above. Looking toward the sun is impossible. Looking away from it, you find that the mist carries a faint ring of color around your own shadow's head, a small circular rainbow that follows wherever you look. On the far edges of the field, where the haze deepens, the horizon has dissolved into white. There is no line where stone ends and sky begins.

## The Sound

The sound hits before anything else, and it never lets up.

Each column alone would make a modest noise: a soft pouring at the base, a patter where the braids strike the fissure floor. But there are too many columns to count, and all of them pour at once, and the fissures between them are resonant stone throats that take each small sound and carry it. The sounds do not add up so much as fuse. The result is a single immense roar, a deep, even, sourceless thunder that fills the air completely.

It is felt as much as heard. It hums in the breastbone and the teeth. A shout cannot be heard at arm's length; the voice simply disappears into it. The roar has no direction. It comes up from the ground, down from the mist, and in from all sides at equal weight, so there is no sense of where anything is by listening. Within an hour the mind begins to treat it as a kind of silence. It is so constant that it becomes the floor on which thought stands, a white noise pressed flat and endless. Only when you leave the field does the true silence of the high air arrive, and then it rings.

The roar does not vary. It does not rise with wind, because there is little wind. It does not change by day or night, because the upwelling never changes. It has sounded at the same pitch and volume for as long as the columns have stood.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing does. There are no plants: not a moss, not a lichen, not a green stain in any crack. The water is too cold, too fast, and too heavy with silica. Anything that settles on the stone is sealed within days under a skin of new mineral and entombed in glass. Faint dark specks, deep within the translucent crowns of some columns, may be spores or dust caught this way long ago, drowned in stone like insects in amber.

The animals that do come here are specialists, and they are few.

A small grey bird, stout and short-tailed, works the fissures. It walks straight into the falling sheets at the bases of the columns and forages beneath the water, hunting something too small to see among the braids. Because no call could carry through the roar, it speaks with its body. It bobs, rapidly and constantly, and flashes a white eyelid that shows bright against its grey face. Two of these birds meeting in a fissure will bob at one another for a long time before one gives way. Most of the time they are alone.

High swifts nest in hollows under the overhanging lips of some columns, behind the water curtain, where the stone is always wet and no predator can reach. They leave at dawn and are gone all day, feeding somewhere far below the plateau. At dusk they return in a single dark stream and vanish into the curtains without a pause.

In the few places where the fissures widen into shallow pools, there are tiny translucent crustaceans, each no bigger than a grain of rice, drifting in the cold mineral water. They are nearly invisible, betrayed only when light catches the beat of their legs.

That is all. Hours can pass in the field without a single moving thing besides the water.

## The Shape of It

The plateau tilts very slightly westward, and the columns lean with it, all bowed a few degrees toward the same edge like a congregation. Near the rim they grow smaller and more crowded, dropping to knee height and fusing into ribbed mats of sinter, until at the very edge the stone ends in a cliff. There the gathered water from every underground channel pours out in a single broad curtain and falls a long way into cloud. From the rim you can see the drop but not the bottom.

In every other direction the field goes on: white, fluted, glazed, glittering, and roaring. It runs across the tableland, into the haze, and out of sight. It is the same everywhere you stand in it. It was the same a thousand years ago and will be the same a thousand years from now, a place in perfect motion that has never, in any way that matters, changed.
