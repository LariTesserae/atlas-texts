# The Lowland of the First Crust

## The Ground

Start with the ground, because everything here comes back to it.

The land is basalt, and it is new. It is not ancient stone worn smooth, and it is not rubble settling into soil. It looks as if it cooled an hour ago and has stayed that way. It spreads across a wide, nearly level plain a few meters above an unseen sea, in the ropes and folds of pāhoehoe: coils like poured batter, lobes that swelled and skinned over, toes of rock pushed out and stopped. The surfaces keep a faint glassy sheen, gunmetal going to iridescent blue-violet where the light slants. Pressure ridges run for kilometers, low and seamed down the middle. Tumuli rise like blisters, some as tall as a person, cracked open across their crowns so you can look down into dark, warm hollows.

Nothing has been carved. There are no gullies, no stream channels, no alluvial fans, no talus. The edges of every flow lobe are as crisp as when they chilled. Rain falls, and it has always fallen, and it has not cut a single groove. Water does not run off this land. It sits in the dips and folds of the rock and seeps down through the crack networks into the warm dark underneath. The drainage is entirely vertical. The plain is a vast shallow tray of stone that drinks from above and breathes from below.

It does breathe. The rock is warm to the touch everywhere, the warmth of a sleeping animal. Through ten thousand fissures, some no wider than a fingernail, the land exhales a slow, steady steam. It never gusts or spouts. It seeps upward in a threadlike haze, and across the whole plain those threads merge into the atmosphere.

## The Air

The air is hot and saturated. It is so humid that the difference between air and water becomes a question of degree. Beads condense on every cool surface, and there are very few cool surfaces. Every leaf edge drips. The rock weeps along its fractures, and each crack wears a line of wet shine. Breathing feels like drinking something warm.

Overhead there is no sky, only a ceiling. The steam rising from the plain meets something higher up and becomes a permanent pale overcast at a height that is hard to judge. It might be a hundred meters or a thousand. The light comes through it diffuse and shadowless, a dim pewter-green glow like the underside of a leaf held against a lamp. There is no sun to locate and no direction the light comes from. It does not brighten toward noon or fade toward evening. As far as anything living here can tell, it is always the same hour. Visibility runs out not in darkness but in haze. At a few hundred meters, the far pressure ridges soften into gray-blue, and past that is only a luminous suggestion of more.

Still, the place feels open. You can sense the plain going on: ridge after ridge, pool after pool, the same low undulation reaching past sight into a region the size of a valley or a province. It feels open, but you can't see across it, which is its own kind of intimacy.

## The Green

On this raw, unweathered stone, which by every rule of geology should be bare for centuries, there is jungle.

It is jungle without soil. The plants here never learned to wait for rock to rot into earth. They grip the glass directly. Mosses come first and are everywhere, thick, spongy, and supersaturated. They pad the ropy coils of the pāhoehoe until the old flow patterns show through the green like muscle under skin. Liverworts overlap in scales across the tumuli. Clubmosses stand in dense stiff colonies along the pressure ridges.

Out of this living carpet rise the ferns, and the ferns are the true architecture. Tree ferns stand in crowds, trunks shaggy with dead fronds and wrapped in their own aerial roots, crowns unfurling eight or ten meters up into the haze. Beneath them grow sword ferns, filmy ferns so thin the dim light passes through them, and staghorns shelved on the trunks. Every surface that can hold a spore holds one. The fern trunks carry orchids, bromeliads, trailing peperomias and more moss, so each tree fern is a vertical garden.

The roots are the strangest part. They don't burrow, because there is nothing to burrow into. They go into the cracks. They follow the fissure networks down, fat and pale and sometimes as thick as an arm, chasing the warm damp exhalation below. At the crack mouths, bundles of roots vanish downward. Where a tumulus has split open, you can look inside and see the hollow webbed with roots in the dark, hung with beads of condensation, feeding on the steam. The whole forest drinks the land's breath. The water rises as vapor from below, condenses on the fronds, drips back down, seeps into the cracks, rises again. It is a closed circuit, and the forest lives inside it.

The vegetation is dense but layered, not impenetrable. Paths open between fern trunks. There are glades where a wide pool has kept the ferns back and the moss runs right to the water's edge, and there are long green aisles along the flat tops of flow lobes. The canopy is broken and open to the haze. The effect is a cathedral built of many small chapels.

## The Pools

The water sits on the land in pools, thousands of them. They fill the swales between flow lobes and pool in the collapsed roofs of old lava tubes. Some are shallow films that barely cover the rock, and some are deep sinks going down into blue darkness. They are warm, some bath-warm and a few almost too hot to touch, and they are perfectly clear, because nothing has ever eroded into them. Nothing silts them up. The glassy basalt floors show through, ropes and folds preserved underwater like fossil ripples.

The clear water is crowded. The pools are loud with life, and so is everything else.

## The Animals

The animals are what you notice first, and what you notice last, and what you never stop noticing.

There is an enormous amount of animal life. Life isn't layered onto the landscape here; the landscape is layered onto the life. Every surface moves.

**Crabs** are the foundation. There are millions of them, possibly billions across the whole plain. Small land crabs, red and violet and a dusky black that matches the basalt, pick over every square meter of moss. They cluster in the crack mouths where warm air rises. They clatter across the bare lobes in waves, clicking and scraping. Near the pools they gather so thickly that the edges of the water look like a shifting carpet of carapaces. They eat everything: fallen fronds, dead insects, algae, each other. What they leave behind is the only soil this place has, a thin dark tilth in the deepest folds, made of crab. They are how the rock gets eaten without being worn down. The land doesn't weather. It is grazed.

**Frogs** are the voice. They are small, most no bigger than a thumbnail, in every color: a translucent green that shows their organs through the skin, a clay-orange, a speckled black and gold. They live in the moss, in the water held in bromeliad cups, and on the undersides of fern fronds. There are more of them than seems possible. Their calls never stop.

**Insects** thicken the air. Mayflies rise from the pools in uneven haze and never swarm and die off in a single hatch, because there are no seasons to synchronize them. The hatch is continuous, a steady fog of new wings that never ends. Dragonflies and damselflies hunt through it, their metallic bodies catching the flat light. Beetles are everywhere: bark beetles, click beetles, huge slow scarabs trundling over the moss. Moths shelter under fronds, their wings patterned to look like lichen, basalt, or the eyes of something larger. Ants run in columns along the pressure ridges, and termites build nothing, because there is no wood to build with. Instead they live in the fern trunks and in the tangle of roots in the cracks, chewing tunnels through the vegetable matter.

**Birds** fill the middle air. There are rails and crakes in the pool edges, stilts and small herons, and flocks of little dull-green birds that move through the ferns like water poured sideways. Something larger and unseen calls from within the haze, a single rising whoop that is answered from far away, then farther. Fruit doves feed in the tree-fern crowns, where some species of fern bears a cluster of red fleshy growths that the birds eat and spread.

**Lizards** own the rock. Skinks slip through the moss, and geckos cling to the underside of every overhang, chirping. On the bare lobes, where the rock is warmest, big dark lizards lie motionless for hours. They are the color of basalt and have the same faint iridescence, so when one finally moves, it looks as though the ground itself has shifted.

**Snails and slugs** cover the damp surfaces, glossy and large and slow, leaving silver trails that crisscross the moss like a map of every route ever taken.

In the tube-caves and under the tumuli, where the roots hang down, live pale things. Blind crickets feel their way along the roots. Colorless millipedes drink condensation. Tiny white shrimp swim in the warm black water at the bottom of the lava tubes. They have never seen even this dim light and never will.

## The Sound

It is loud. It is not deafening, but it is constant and enveloping, the sound of an enormous amount of life all talking at once.

The frog chorus is the base layer. It is a high, overlapping trilling with dozens of species calling at different pitches and rhythms, so it never resolves into one note. It fills every gap. Beneath it is the clicking and rustling of crabs, a dry susurration like rain on leaves, heard everywhere, always. Over it come the insects: the thin whine of mosquitoes and midges, the heavier drone of beetles in flight, the creak and rasp of katydids. Birds punctuate it with calls, whistles, and flutelike phrases. The geckos add a sharp chuck-chuck-chuck from the overhangs.

Water adds its own noise. It isn't the rush of streams, since there are none. It is the drip. Every frond drips, every overhang drips, every crack weeps. The steady patter of condensation falling into ten thousand pools makes a soft, bright, irregular percussion under everything.

Under all of it, if you press your ear to the rock, the land has a sound of its own: a low hiss, the whisper of the steam, as constant as a pulse.

There is no silence here and there never has been. The chorus doesn't swell at dusk or fade at dawn, because there is no dusk or dawn. It is simply always going.

## The Stillness

Here is the rule of this world: nothing changes.

That isn't because nothing happens. Everything happens constantly. Frogs are born and eaten, fronds unfurl and fall, crabs molt and die and are devoured. But the sum of it is balanced to the finest degree. The heat rising from below is exactly matched by the heat lost into the haze. The water falling as drip is exactly matched by the water rising as steam. What grows is eaten, what is eaten becomes crab, and what becomes crab is returned to the moss. The rock is new and stays new, because nothing wears it down. The crabs graze the life off it, and the roots slip into cracks that were already there without widening them.

There are no seasons. There are no storms, because the atmosphere is too saturated and too even for pressure gradients to form. There is no wind beyond the faint upward drift of steam. There are no floods and no droughts. The pools never rise or fall. The temperature never shifts by a degree.

The geology is embryonic but not developing. The land is in the first moment of its existence, and the first moment has simply never ended. It is new rock, ungrooved and unweathered, carrying a jungle that would take a million years to grow anywhere else, and yet has been here, unchanged, for as long as anyone could tell.

It's possible the land is still being made. Somewhere far off, past the haze, fresh lava might still be coming up through the cracks, pushing the plain outward at the same rate the sea pushes it back. The plain would be a conveyor belt, and every part of it would be permanently young. Or the heat below might be the whole secret: a warmth that never runs out and a slow vapor that never stops, keeping everything forever at the moment of first emergence.

Either way, the result is the same. It is a warm, wet, loud, crowded, dim country of glass and fern and frog and crab, spreading across a plain that feels open and can't be seen across. It lives in a single endless afternoon without a sun, a world that has just been born and has decided to stay that way.
