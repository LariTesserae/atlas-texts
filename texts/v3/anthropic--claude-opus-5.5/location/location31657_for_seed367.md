# The Hollow at Kesh-under-Stone

You would not find it by looking. The plateau goes on for days in every direction, a high tableland worn so old that its mountains have become suggestions: low brown swells, rounded like the backs of sleeping animals, their summits ground down to gravel and polished knobs of quartzite that shine faintly at noon. The wind up here is warm and never stops. It carries nothing, no smell of rain or rot, only the mineral scent of hot stone and, sometimes, a faint sweetness you cannot place.

The sweetness is the only clue. It rises from a crack.

---

## The Opening

The crack is narrow, a dark seam between two slabs of limestone gone the color of old bone, easy to mistake for one more fissure in a land full of them. But stand at its lip in the late afternoon and you will feel the air change. It stops pushing past you and starts breathing upward, warm and thick and faintly animal, like the breath of a stable. Then the sound reaches you: a roar that is not wind, a layered churning of wings and voices and insect-drone, rising out of the ground as if the plateau had a throat and something inside it were alive.

Below the seam the rock opens. It is a doline, a sinkhole left from an age when water still moved through this stone and dissolved a chamber the size of a small valley. Most of its roof collapsed long ago. What remains is a pocket perhaps four hundred paces long and a third as wide, walled on every side by cliffs that lean inward, so that the sky above is a ragged slot of brightness. Even at midday the floor of the hollow sits in a brown-gold dusk. Light arrives secondhand, bouncing off pale walls and filtering through green, so the whole place glows the dim amber of honey held up to a lamp.

The water that carved it is gone. It may have been gone for a hundred thousand years. The stone remembers it anyway, in flowstone draperies frozen mid-pour, in rimstone terraces that once held pools and now hold dust, in scalloped ceilings where currents once turned. It is a dead river's house, and other things live in it now.

---

## The Green

Life crowds the hollow in a way that makes no sense until you understand what feeds it.

It isn't water. It's the animals.

The walls, the ledges, and the tangled floor are furred with plants built to drink almost nothing and eat almost everything. Rock figs dominate. Their pale roots pour down the cliffs like wax in cords as thick as a man's thigh, gripping every seam, splitting slabs, knotting across the open air in bridges and nets. Their trunks swell into bottle-shaped reservoirs, grey-skinned and smooth, each one a cistern the tree has filled over centuries from the rare storms that cross the plateau once in a decade. Their leaves are small and leathery and waxed against loss, and there are so many of them that the canopy closes overhead in a second, lower sky.

Beneath the figs grow cushion plants in dense green domes, hard enough to sit on, their interiors packed with dead leaves that hold the damp of breath and shade. Succulents stack across the ledges: rosettes the size of cartwheels, jointed stems like strings of green beads, fat paddles that bleed a milky sap when broken. Resurrection ferns carpet the upper walls, curled brown and dead-looking for most of the year. When the air grows even slightly heavy they uncurl overnight into a green that almost hurts to look at. Lichens cover everything the plants haven't claimed, in orange and sulfur-yellow and a grey-violet that looks like smoke pressed into the stone.

All of it grows on dung. The floor of the hollow is a deep, soft, warm bed of guano, laid down layer upon layer by the creatures overhead. It is dry as flour and rich enough to burn the roots of any plant not adapted to it. Walking on it feels like walking on a mattress stuffed with ash, and every footstep releases a small pale cloud and that sweet, sharp, ammoniac smell. The plants have learned to live on this richness, and on the moisture of so many bodies breathing in an enclosed space. The hollow runs its own slow weather. Warm exhalations rise, touch the cooler upper walls near the slot of sky, and leave a film of condensation too faint to see. The ferns and lichens up there drink from it. Everything below drinks from them.

---

## The Multitude

The fauna is overwhelming.

The upper walls belong to the swifts. There are hundreds of thousands of them, cigar-bodied and sickle-winged, their nests glued to the stone in colonies so dense the cliff looks scaled. At dawn they pour out through the slot of sky in a black torrent that takes most of an hour to empty, screaming the whole time, a high metallic shriek multiplied until it becomes a single ringing tone. At dusk they come back, diving from the heights at speeds that whistle, and for a while the hollow sounds like a forge being hammered from every side.

As the swifts return, the bats leave. They roost in the deep overhangs and the old solution-pockets of the ceiling, in clusters that hang like brown fruit and ripple when disturbed. When the light fails they unpeel from the rock and stream upward along the walls in a spiral you can hear more than see, a dry papery rushing like wind through dead corn. For a few minutes at twilight the two tides pass each other in the slot, swifts falling and bats rising, and the air becomes solid with wings.

Lower down, bees. The wild combs of the hollow are enormous, great golden curtains draped from the fig roots and the undersides of ledges, some taller than a person, humming at a pitch you feel in your teeth. The bees work the succulent flowers, which bloom in pulses of yellow and bruise-purple, and the honey they make is thick, dark, faintly bitter, and very nearly the only liquid in the place that isn't sap.

The floor seethes. Guano beetles in black, bronze, and oil-slick green trundle through the dust in such numbers that the ground seems to crawl. Roaches the length of a finger scatter from every lifted stone. Centipedes as long as a forearm hunt among them, fast and amber-banded and venomous, and in turn become prey to the geckos: dozens of kinds, from thumbnail-sized creatures that chirp like crickets to barking wall-lizards as long as a cat, pale-eyed and patient on the lichen. Rock snakes sleep in the warm crevices and eat geckos, bats, nestlings, and each other. Small tawny cats with tufted ears, three or four families of them, patrol the root-bridges and eat snakes, bats, and whatever falls.

Things fall constantly. Swift chicks tumble from overcrowded nests. Bats drop dead from old age or fall in fights. Sometimes a whole comb, grown too heavy, peels away from its root and crashes down in a slow golden avalanche, and the hollow goes briefly mad as every creature that can eat honey, wax, or bee converges on it. Nothing stays on the floor long. What the cats and snakes don't take, the beetles strip, and what the beetles leave becomes dust, and the dust becomes fig.

The hollow is never quiet. At no hour of day or night is it anything less than loud: shrieking, humming, rustling, chirping, the constant soft rain of droppings like a patter of sand, the crack of a lizard's bark, a snake's hiss, a cat's scream. It is an engine of eating, and it never stops running.

---

## Kesh

And people live here.

They call the town Kesh, or Kesh-under-Stone when they speak to outsiders, which is rarely. It climbs the southern and western walls of the hollow in tiers, a town grown into the cliff the way the figs have grown into it.

Its builders had almost no water to spare and could never make brick or mortar, so the architecture of Kesh is stacked, carved, and woven. The oldest dwellings are simply dug into the soft layers of the limestone: rooms scooped out with antler picks and stone chisels over generations, enlarged by each family in turn, until parts of the wall are honeycombed a dozen chambers deep. Their doorways are low and round-shouldered, worn smooth by uncountable hands. Newer rooms are built outward from the cliff in dry-laid stone, flat slabs fitted without mortar in walls so precise a knife blade won't slide between them. Where stone runs out, the Keshi weave. Fig-root fiber, beaten and twisted, becomes rope, matting, screens, and walls. Whole upper quarters of the town are woven structures slung between roots and ledges like enormous baskets, swaying slightly when the evening wind breathes down through the slot.

Stairs are cut into every face. Ladders of lashed wood, rare and precious and fetched from days away across the plateau, connect the tiers, and where there is no wood there are root-ladders, living fig roots trained over decades to grow into rungs. Walkways run along the ledges, some carved, some woven, some simply worn into the rock by feet. From the floor of the hollow the town looks like a cliff that has broken out in doorways: hundreds of them, dark mouths and screened windows, stacked seven and eight high, with smoke seeping from the upper ones and people moving everywhere along the ledges.

There are perhaps two thousand Keshi. They live pressed together because the hollow allows nothing else, and their town is loud with them. Grinding stones rasp in every doorway as women and men crush dried fig-fruit, beetle-meal, and succulent pith into a coarse flour. Children shout from tier to tier. Mallets thud on fiber in the weaving courts. Someone is always singing, usually the long call-and-answer songs the honey-cutters use to coordinate on the ropes, their voices climbing over the hum.

Everything in Kesh comes from the hollow's excess, and they harvest it the way the plants do.

**Guano** is their wealth. Cut in blocks from the deep floor, dried, and bundled, it is carried up and out through the crack and traded across the plateau to the herding peoples of the flats, who pay in wood, hide, flint, and salt. Guano-diggers wear woven masks and walk the floor in pairs, one digging and one watching for centipedes.

**Honey** is their drink, their medicine, and their sacrament. The honey-cutters are the town's most honored people. They descend the cliff on fiber ropes at night, smoking the combs with smoldering cushion-plant, and cut the gold free with blades of knapped chert. Most of them carry scars. A few carry more than scars, and their names are cut into the stone beside the great comb that killed them.

**Fig-water** is their life. The swollen trunks of the rock figs are tapped, carefully and never too often, with bone spigots driven into the grey bark. Each great tree belongs to a lineage, and its tapping is governed by rules older than anyone can trace. A tree that is overdrawn dies, and a family that kills its tree loses its place on the wall. In Kesh no crime is worse. The oldest trees are called grandmothers, and their spigots are sealed with wax and opened only in the worst years.

They eat beetles roasted in clay-lined pits, geckos, bats, swift chicks at the height of the nesting season, fig-fruit, succulent hearts, and the sweet milk of certain cactus-like stems. They burn dried guano and dead cushion-plant for fire, and their smoke is heavy and pungent and hangs under the canopy in blue layers that make the dim light dimmer. Their tools are stone, bone, antler, fiber, and the few copper awls and blades traded in from the far side of the plateau, hoarded and passed down and almost never used for work.

---

## Rules of the Place

Life in the hollow runs on a handful of rules, and every creature in it lives by them.

**Nothing is wasted, because nothing is plentiful but life itself.** Water is the rarest thing in the hollow and bodies are the commonest. Every creature, plant, and person has learned to convert the one into the other.

**The hollow breathes.** By day the warm air rises and flows out through the slot, and the hollow exhales its smell across the plateau. By night the cooler air of the heights sinks down through the crack. The Keshi sleep with their heads toward the walls, where the night-draft pools, and they reckon time by the hollow's breath rather than by any sun they can barely see.

**The tides of wings are the clock.** Dawn is the swift-pour, dusk the bat-rise, and the year turns on nesting and fledging, on comb-swell and comb-fall. In the seasons when the swifts are away the hollow is noticeably quieter, which is to say merely very loud, and the Keshi call it the Hush and celebrate it with a festival of long talk and storytelling, because only then can people easily hear each other.

**The rain comes once in a long while and changes everything.** Perhaps once in eight years, or twelve, a storm crosses the plateau and water pours through the crack in a brown torrent. For a day the dead river lives again: the rimstone pools fill, the flowstone runs wet, the guano floor turns to a stinking black paste, the ferns explode into green, and every creature in the hollow goes into a frenzy of drinking and breeding. The Keshi call these storms the River Remembering. They fill every vessel they own, sing all night, and date their lives by them. A child born in a rain-year is considered lucky. An old person is one who has seen five rivers.

---

## At Dusk, from the Rim

Stand at the lip of the crack at the end of the day, where you began, and look down.

The slot of sky above you has gone copper. Below, the hollow is almost dark: a deep amber gloom full of blue smoke, pricked here and there with the orange of cookfires in the doorways of Kesh. Seven tiers of little lights climb the western wall. The roar rises past you, the swifts dropping in by the thousands with their iron shrieks, the first bats unspooling upward past them. Under it all runs the steady golden hum of the combs and the rasp of a hundred grinding stones, and faintly the honey-cutters are singing as they check their ropes for the night's descent.

The warm breath of the hollow rises past your face, thick with dung and honey and smoke and fig-sap and the dry sweet dust of an ancient riverbed.

Behind you the plateau is silent under the first stars, worn flat and empty, the oldest land anyone knows, with nothing on it at all.
