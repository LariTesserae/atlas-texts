# The Grinding Flats

## The Ground

The land is so old that it has stopped arguing with itself. There were mountains here once, and before them the roots of mountains, and before that something with no name, and all of it has been worn down to a single tilted plane of stone. The plane runs slightly downhill to the east, toward a coast no one has walked to, and its fall is so slight that water can't decide which way to go. The eye reads the ground as level. The body, walking it for a day, feels the faint pull.

The stone is bare everywhere. It is dark slate-gray, banded with paler seams of quartz and something rust-green, and the bands run in long, broken curves across the flats. They trace the folded bellies of ranges that eroded away before anything had eyes to see them. The ancient folds are laid out in the ground like the grain of a split log, and in some places you can follow one pale band for miles as it bends and doubles back on itself. The surface has been polished by water, grit, and an unimaginable span of time until it is smooth, almost soapy underfoot where it is wet, and it is nearly always wet.

Here and there the flatness breaks into low whaleback ridges, never more than twice a person's height. These are the last hard knuckles of rock that the long grinding hasn't finished with yet. They rise gently and fall away, and water streams down their flanks in sheets thin as breath. From any whaleback you can see a great distance: gray on gray, sheen on sheen, the ridges scattered like sleeping animals in a fog, and the horizon dissolving into a sky the same color as the ground.

## The Water

Water doesn't fall here so much as appear. The stone weeps. It seeps from hairline joints, beads on every surface, and gathers in shallow pans no deeper than a thumb that cover whole acres, each one a mirror of the low sky. The air condenses on cold rock through the night. By morning every surface is slick and the pans have grown, and by afternoon they have shrunk again into the pores of the stone.

Beneath the flats the rock is riddled. Over ages the mineral-heavy water has dissolved passages through the old bedrock, and the whole plain sits on a honeycomb of channels, chambers, and flooded galleries that nobody has mapped. Where these break the surface they form **throats**: round, smooth-lipped shafts, some a hand's width across and some wide enough to swallow a hut, dropping into darkness. Water runs toward them in shining braids across the stone and falls in with a sound like a held note.

The water is hard and cold, and it tastes of iron and chalk. It leaves a pale crust where it evaporates and an orange-brown bloom where it lingers. This is part of why nothing green lives here. The water carries so much dissolved stone that roots, if any had ever tried, would be armored and choked within a season. The wet has been a mineral wet for as long as the land has existed.

## The Light

It is dim here, always. The sky is a single low sheet of cloud that seems to rest on the tops of the whalebacks. The sun is a paler patch that drifts across it without ever showing an edge. Midday is the color of dusk elsewhere. Mist lies in the low pans at dawn and dusk, and on still days it never lifts. Then the flats become a gray room with no walls, and the only way to tell distance is by sound.

The pans throw the dim light back up, so the brightest thing in the landscape is often the ground rather than the sky. Walking across a wet stretch at evening feels like walking on the underside of a cloud.

## The Sound

The first thing anyone learns about this place, before the cold and before the wet, is the noise.

It isn't one sound. It is layered, and every layer is loud.

The lowest layer is **the breathing**. The underground galleries are connected, somewhere far to the east, to the sea, and twice a day the tide pushes into them. As the water rises through the honeycomb, it drives the air out through every throat on the plain at once. The flats exhale. Each throat roars at its own pitch according to its width and depth: the small ones shriek, the great ones boom so low that the sound is felt in the knees and sternum more than heard. When the tide falls, the plain inhales, and air rushes back down with a long, rising, hollow moan. The breath-cycle takes hours. It never stops. There is no silence between breaths, only a shift from the roar of exhalation to the moan of intake.

Above that is **the rattle**. Uncountable small creatures live in the wet film on the stone, and at the surface of every pan, every seep, every streaming ridge-flank, they clatter. It sounds like sleet on slate, like gravel poured endlessly onto a drumskin. The rattle is everywhere and without direction. It rises when the pans swell in the morning and thins in the afternoon as the water drains.

On top of that are **the colonies**: the screaming, wheeling, quarrelling flocks that nest on the whalebacks and feed across the flats. Their cries carry over everything else in sharp, overlapping bursts.

The combined effect is overwhelming. Standing on the flats at a full exhalation, you can't hear your own shout. People who live here long enough lose the upper reaches of their hearing, and most are partly deaf by middle age. It is not considered a misfortune. It is considered becoming a local.

## The Life

Nothing grows here that anyone would call a plant. But the stone is alive.

Wherever water lingers, the rock is coated in a thin, slippery film: rust-orange in the shallow pans, olive-black in the dim throat mouths, bruise-violet on the north sides of the ridges. The film feeds on the minerals in the water and on nothing else. It is the base of everything. It spreads visibly in the wet mornings and cracks and curls into flakes as the pans dry, and where it has flaked the bare stone underneath is brighter, freshly etched, a little lower than before. The film is slowly eating the land, and the flats are, in part, its work.

Grazing on the film are **limpet-snails**: flat, cone-shelled creatures the size of a fingernail. Their shells are the same banded gray as the stone, because the shells are stone, deposited from the water they filter. There are so many that a stretch of wet rock looks pebbled until you touch it and feel the whole surface shift.

Hunting the limpets are **rattlers**. They are small, many-legged, armored things, somewhere between a woodlouse and a crab, each no longer than a thumb joint. Their backs carry loose overlapping plates, and they clatter these constantly to warn, to court, to claim a patch of film, or simply to announce they exist. A single rattler sounds like a fingernail tapping a tooth. Millions of them make the sleet-roar that covers the plain. They swarm the pans in the mornings, pour down the throats in the evenings as the water drains, and hide in the honeycomb when the air is dry.

Feeding on the rattlers are the birds. The most common, called **pan-strutters**, are long-legged waders that stalk the wet flats in loose lines, stabbing with narrow beaks. Their pale feathers are streaked orange from the film. Higher up, **throat-divers** hang in the rising air of each exhalation, riding the tide-breath out of the shafts like leaves in a chimney, then fold and plunge down after rattlers when the breath turns. They nest on the whalebacks in dense, stinking, deafening colonies, packed wing to wing on bare stone, their eggs laid directly into the smooth hollows worn by water.

Stranger things live deeper. In the dark galleries, where the breathing is loudest, there are pale, eyeless eels that are said to be as long as a person. They are hunted by nothing and seen only when the tide pushes one up a throat and strands it on the stone at the lip. And in the widest throats, at the deepest moment of the inhale, something down there answers the moan with a sound of its own: a slow, deliberate, clicking series, like knuckles on wood, repeating. Nobody knows what makes it. The locals leave offerings at the lips of the throats where it has been heard.

## The People

There aren't many of them, and they are scattered across the flats in groups of a dozen or twenty. Each group keeps to a whaleback and moves on after a few years, when the colonies shift or the film thins.

They build low. Their houses are rings of slate slabs pried from the whaleback flanks with antler and hard-stone wedges. The slabs are stacked without mortar and roofed with overlapping plates of bird-bone lashed with gut. Houses are dug slightly into the lee of the ridges and face away from the nearest large throat. Inside, the noise is only loud rather than unbearable. A family's fire is fed with dried bird dung and the oily bodies of eels, and it smokes badly. Every house is blackened inside and smells of fish oil, guano, and wet stone.

Across the flats they leave marks. **Cairns** stand on the rises: tall, careful stacks of banded stone that serve as waypoints in the mist, because in fog, with the noise, you can be lost within a hundred paces of your own door. **Weirs** of stacked stone channel the running water across the pans into narrow chutes, where woven gut-nets catch rattlers by the thousand as they pour toward the throats in the evening. **Drying racks** of bird bone stand beside the weirs, and rattlers are dried on them, crushed, and stored in stone-lined pits. Rattler meal, eggs, bird flesh, and the occasional stranded eel make up nearly everything they eat.

Their tools are stone, bone, and gut, with a little soft native copper hammered into fishhooks and awls. The copper comes from one of the green seams in the rock, worked cold. The copper seams are known, named, and walked to on long trips. The metal is precious, and it tastes, the people say, like the water.

They talk with their hands. Spoken words are useless on the flats, so the people have a full, rich sign language, fast and fluid, much of it made at chest height so it can be read in the gloom. They also talk through stone. A person pressed flat to a whaleback can feel taps made by another person on the same ridge, and there is a code of taps for warnings, for summoning, for news of an eel stranded or a child lost. Children learn to lie on the stone and listen with their bodies before they learn to walk.

When they sing, they sing into the throats at the turn of the breath. There is a brief moment when the exhale falters before the inhale begins, a few heartbeats when the roar dips. People gather at the lip of a large throat to shout or chant into it in that gap. Their voices drop into the dark and are carried away by the returning wind, pulled down into the galleries along with the air. They say this is how you speak to the dead, and to whatever it is that clicks.

## The Rules of the World

- **The land breathes.** The tide under the flats sets the rhythm of everything: when the rattlers swarm, when the birds hunt, when people sleep and when they gather, and when it is possible to be heard at all.
- **The wet is mineral.** Water here builds and dissolves stone. Shells, crusts, and film are all made of rock, and nothing soft can hold on long. What grows here grows in stone or is stone.
- **The film is slowly eating the plain.** Every flake it sheds takes a little rock with it. Over deep time the flats are still lowering, still flattening. The last whalebacks will go, and eventually everything will be a single level sheet of wet stone breathing under the clouds.
- **Sound is a place.** In the mist, you navigate by the pitch of the throats. Each large one has a known voice, and the old people can walk the flats blindfolded by listening for them.
- **Silence is a warning.** It happens only rarely, perhaps once in a generation, when some pressure far out at sea stops the tide from turning on time. The throats fall quiet, the rattlers go still, the birds settle, and for one long, awful moment the whole plain holds its breath. People describe it as the loudest thing they have ever heard. Then the breathing resumes, and everyone begins, again, to speak with their hands.
