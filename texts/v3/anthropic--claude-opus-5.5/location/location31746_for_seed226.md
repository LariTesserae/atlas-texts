# The Basin at Lathe

The first thing you know about the place is the noise. You hear it an hour before you see anything, climbing through cloud on the last switchbacks of the mountain. It isn't one waterfall. It's hundreds of small voices of water, falling short distances into stone, layered on top of each other until they merge into a single sustained roar. It sounds less like a river than like a crowd.

Then the path turns a shoulder of dark scree, the cloud thins, and the basin opens below you.

## The Stone That Is Still Arriving

Lathe sits in a hanging bowl near the top of the range, a notch in the mountain maybe four hundred paces across. The old rock of the peaks around it is black, fractured and grey-lichened. The floor of the bowl is something else: a pale, porous, honey-and-bone-coloured stone that looks poured rather than broken. It rises in terraces, rim above rim, like a staircase made of shallow cups. Every cup brims with water, and every rim is lipped with a soft white edge that is still growing.

The stone is new. That is the fact everything else rests on. A spring comes up at the head of the basin, cold, clear and heavy with dissolved lime from somewhere deep in the mountain. As it spreads into thin sheets and loses its breath to the thin high air, it lets go of what it carries. The lime settles out on every surface the water touches: moss, stems, fallen feathers, stone. The layer gains perhaps the thickness of a fingernail in a year. The whole floor of the basin, and the town built on it, is younger than some of the trees on the slopes above. The oldest terraces are a few thousand years old. The newest rims are soft enough to dent with a thumbnail in the morning and firm by evening.

Nothing here has eroded yet, because nothing has had time. There are no gullies, no rounded boulders, no weathered faces. Every edge is crisp and slightly frothy, like the edge of cooling sugar. Rock that elsewhere would be the end of a long story of wearing-down is, here, the beginning of one.

## Green on White

The basin is lush in the way only cool, wet, lime-rich places can be. Moss is the true citizen of Lathe. It grows in cushions on every rim, and the water filters through it and leaves stone behind, so the moss is always slowly being entombed and always growing upward to escape. The terraces' edges are its skeleton. Cut through any wall in the town and you'll see the fossil moss inside, grown in fine laminae like the pages of a book.

Around the moss grow liverworts in flat green scales, maidenhair ferns in every shaded seep, watercress thick in the pools, and bog violets and butterwort on the damp ledges. On the drier upper terraces grows a stand of short, twisted alders and mountain birch, their roots crusted pale where they reach down into the flow. Hart's-tongue ferns line the channels like green blades laid in rows. In the cold, the greens are deep and saturated, almost blue. Against the pale stone the whole basin looks like a carving lined with velvet.

It is always cool. Summer days reach only a sweater's warmth. Most mornings a cloud rests in the bowl and condenses on everything, so that even the stone that isn't under running water beads and weeps. You are wet here without ever quite being rained on.

## The Town

Lathe is a town. It has streets, a square, cisterns, a granary, perhaps two hundred houses, a meeting hall with a vaulted roof, stairs and aqueducts and gates. It is laid out with obvious intent: the lanes follow the contours of the terraces, the channels run beside the lanes, and each house sits where the water can reach it.

And almost no one lives in it.

At any time there are perhaps thirty or forty people in the basin. The town isn't ruined or abandoned. It is *early*. Most of its houses have never been lived in. They are waiting.

This is because the houses of Lathe are not built so much as grown. The builders work with hand tools of antler, flint and a little soft copper, and with baskets. They weave the form of a wall, a vault or a doorway out of hazel, willow and reed, the way you might weave a fish trap. Then they cut a channel from the nearest terrace and lead a thin sheet of spring water over the wicker. The water does the rest. Over years the basketry is coated, then buried, then replaced by stone that keeps its shape exactly. A house begun by a young woman will be sound enough to sleep in when she is old. It will be a good house for her grandchildren, and a fine one for theirs.

So the town is full of structures at every stage of becoming. Some are still plain wickerwork, green and supple, dripping. Some are grey-white and crusted, the weave still visible like a pattern in lace. Some are solid, heavy-walled, their surfaces grown over with moss and fern. Walking through Lathe you see the whole life of a building in a single street. The rooms are large, because the builders plan for households that don't exist yet. They are mostly empty, because those households haven't been born.

The emptiness has a particular quality. You stand in a vaulted room of pale stone the size of a barn, water sheeting quietly down one wall, ferns in the corners, a single bench and a folded blanket in the middle. Grey light comes through a round opening in the roof. There is nothing else, just air and wet stone and the sound. The spaces between presences are enormous. A person seen across the square is a small, distinct event.

## The Sound

The roar never stops. The spring is steady, the terraces are many, and every rim is a small waterfall. The town was deliberately built to amplify it.

The builders of Lathe learned long ago that the grown stone is resonant. It is porous, full of chambers, and hard as bell-metal once it matures. So they weave horns. Long, curling wicker trumpets, some twice a person's height, are set into the terrace edges where the falling water can pour across their mouths. Over decades they turn to stone and begin to *sound*. Each one drones at its own pitch, set by its length and the thickness of its walls, and the pitch drifts slowly as the stone thickens. Old horns grow deeper. The oldest, by the head spring, has a note so low it is felt in the chest more than heard.

Every street has its horns, and every house has a channel that sings in its walls. The meeting hall is essentially an instrument: a hollow vault in which a dozen water-horns converge, producing a chord so loud that speech is impossible during the wet months. Meetings there are held in silence, by gesture and by marks scratched on soft new stone.

The people of Lathe are not quiet, but they have no need to be. They speak loudly and plainly and laugh with their whole bodies. Children learn to hear a person's footsteps through the roar by the rhythm of the splashes. Visitors find the noise overwhelming for two or three days, and then find the silence of the lower valleys unbearable.

On the rare days when a hard frost locks the upper channels, the horns go quiet one by one, from the top of the basin down. The sudden hush is treated as an omen, a holy day and a holiday at once. Nobody works. People walk the silent streets and speak in whispers, because they can.

## The Few Animals

Fauna are rare in the basin, and each one is noticed. A pair of dippers, stout brown birds with white bibs, live along the main channel. They walk underwater against the current, bob on the rims, and nest in hollows of the young stone. There are always, somehow, exactly two. A heron comes some autumns and stands for days in the upper pool, and people leave it alone. Tiny snails with pale shells graze the moss, and the lime takes them too, so the terraces are flecked with their small stone spirals. Once in a generation or so, someone reports a stag at the head of the basin in the fog. Whether this is true is less important than that it is told.

There are no rats and almost no insects. The cold and the lime keep them away. Nothing large can live in so small and so clean a place. The emptiness of the town extends to the living world: space, stone, water, green, and here and there a single creature.

## The Rules of the Place

Lathe has its own laws of nature, and its people have built their customs around them.

**Anything left in the water becomes stone.** A dropped glove, a bird's nest, a fallen branch will be crusted in a month and solid in a few years. Children leave toys in the shallows and return as adults to find them turned to little white sculptures. A careless person's lost belongings become part of the town. The streets are full of these accidental objects, grown into rims and walls: a stone basket, a stone shoe, a lattice of stone twigs.

**The dead are given to the water.** When someone dies, they are laid in a woven cradle on the upper terraces, in a place set aside for it, and the spring is guided over them. In a lifetime they are covered. In three lifetimes they are part of a rim, a pale mound grown over with moss, with the water chiming across. The oldest rims of the basin are the oldest dead. People say, loosely and without much fear, that the town is built on its grandparents, and that the horns speak partly with their voices. This is literally true in the sense that the stone that sings is made partly of them.

**Nothing is ever finished.** A wall keeps growing after it is done. Doorways narrow, windows close, channels choke with their own deposit. Every house must be tended: the lime scraped from its openings, its water channels recut, its flows redirected so it doesn't seal itself shut. An untended house becomes a solid block in a century, a room that has swallowed its own air. There are a few of these in the older quarters, sealed with whatever was left inside. People do not like to guess what that was.

**Time is measured by thickness.** Lathe doesn't count years very carefully. It counts layers. A marriage is as old as the rim that has grown on the stone laid at its wedding. A child's age is the depth of the crust on the reed she was given at birth. Every household keeps a measuring rod, set in a steady flow, and the elders can read a person's life in its bands: thick layers for wet years, thin ones for dry, a fine dark line for the year of the ash from the far volcano.

## Slow

Everything in Lathe happens slowly. The stone grows at the speed of fingernails. A house takes forty years. The pitch of a horn falls over a lifetime by the distance between two notes. Moss outgrows its own burial by a hair each season. Even the sound, loud as it is, is steady: a great constant roar rising and falling only with the months of snowmelt and drought.

The people who choose to stay here have made their peace with this. They are building a city that will not really exist until long after they are gone. They weave rooms for people who are not yet born and lay out streets for crowds that may never come. Standing at the head of the basin in the evening fog, with the empty houses stepping down the terraces below you, the ferns black-green in the dusk, the whole bowl booming with water and the deep horns groaning like something very old waking up, it is possible to feel both things at once. The town is almost empty. The town is only just beginning.

Below, in the half-built square, a single lamp is lit in one window of a vast pale house. The sound of the water fills everything. The stone goes on arriving.
