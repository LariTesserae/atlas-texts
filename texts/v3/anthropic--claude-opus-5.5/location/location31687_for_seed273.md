# The Breathing Stack

## The Approach

From a distance it looks like a mountain, and people who have lived beside it all their lives still call it that. It rises out of the haze of the lowland plain in a long, slightly irregular cone, green along its flanks, pale grey at its crown, and so tall that for most of the year its upper third stands above the cloud deck like an island in milk. The sun hits the top hours before it reaches the plain.

Up close there is no mountain. There is masonry.

Every surface is made: brick courses, poured stone, riveted iron plate, glazed tile, cast-iron grating, timber gone black and hard as horn. There is no bedrock anywhere, not at the base, not in the deepest cuttings the maintenance crews have ever opened. Where old walls have been broken, the break shows more wall behind, and older brick behind that, smaller, softer, redder, laid in patterns nobody uses anymore. The structure has been added to for so long that its lower levels have compacted and settled like sediment. Doorways at the base are squashed into lozenges. Staircases lean. Arches have taken on the sagging, comfortable curves of old furniture.

This is the Stack. It is roughly seven kilometers high, though the figure depends on who is measuring and whether they count the spire.

## The Terraces

The outside of the Stack is stepped. From the base upward it climbs in terraces, each one a ledge of paved or gridded floor backed by the wall of the next tier, and each tier slightly narrower than the one below. Some terraces are a few meters deep. Some run back into the structure for half a kilometer, through vaulted halls whose outer walls fell away generations ago, so that they are open to the sky on one side and shadowed caverns on the other.

Every terrace is overgrown.

There was never any earth brought here. The soil of the Stack is made the same way the Stack is: slowly, from what accumulates. Brick dust, rust flakes, mortar crumbs, soot from the vents, the droppings of birds, the husks of insects, leaf litter, the pulp of rotted timber. It gathers in gutters and corners and in the recesses of ornamental cornices, darkens, fills, and then things root in it. On the lower terraces the soil is a meter deep in places, black and crumbly and faintly metallic, with seams of orange where iron has bled through. Higher up it thins to a skin, a crust on the grating, but the plants are there anyway.

The vegetation is lush in the way that built places become lush when water and warmth are reliable and nobody cuts anything back. Fig trees split the brickwork and grow sideways out of walls, their roots running down the face in pale braided cables for twenty meters to find a gutter. There are tree ferns in the dim back halls, where the light comes in slanted and green. Morning glories and a dozen kinds of creeper hang in curtains off the terrace edges, so dense that from below some tiers look like waterfalls of leaves. Mosses cover the north faces completely, thick enough to sink a hand into. On the sunward terraces the growth is scrubbier and brighter: flowering shrubs, grasses rooted in the cracks of paving, wild tomatoes and squash descended from gardens long abandoned, hanging clusters of small yellow orchids that live entirely on the moisture of old iron.

Almost nothing grows straight. Everything follows the architecture: along the lines of mortar, down pipes, through vents, around the curves of arches. Seen from the air, the planting of the Stack traces its structure the way frost traces the veins of a leaf.

## The Engine

The Stack is warm, and it is warm from the inside.

At its heart runs the Bore, a vertical shaft wide enough to swallow a town square, open from somewhere below the base to the vent-crown at the summit. Heat rises through it. Nobody alive knows exactly where the heat comes from. The standard answer is "down," and the maintenance guilds keep records going back centuries showing that the temperature in the lowest accessible galleries has not changed by more than a few degrees in all that time. The air in the Bore climbs in a steady, roaring column, and the Stack was built, or grew, or was made to grow, around harnessing it.

Everywhere the draught can be tapped, it is. Side passages lead off the Bore at thousands of points into turbine halls, where great iron vanes turn in the moving air and drive shafts, belts, and dynamos. The power runs outward through the walls in copper, in braided cable, in leather belting that crosses whole galleries overhead, in long rocking beams that move with the slow, patient rhythm of a sleeper's breathing. Electric lamps burn in the interior halls day and night: bare filament bulbs in wire cages, globes of milk glass, strings of them along stairways and funicular tracks. Freight hoists climb the outer faces on toothed rails. Cable cars swing between the Stack's shoulders and the smaller satellite towers that stand around it like foothills.

Much of the machinery no longer drives anything. It turns because the air turns it. There are halls where giant flywheels spin in the half-dark, disconnected from any purpose, moss on their rims and swifts nesting in their spokes, turning the way a river turns a millwheel that nobody has used in a hundred years.

## Sound

It is loud here, but it is a patterned loudness, and most residents stop hearing it within a month.

The base note is the Bore: a low, continuous roar felt more in the sternum than the ear, like standing near a waterfall that never varies. On top of that sits the mechanical layer: the knocking of rocker beams, the hiss of steam relief valves, the whine of dynamos, the clatter of hoist chains, the rhythmic slap of belting. Somewhere on every level a bell or a horn is sounding a shift, a warning, a departure. And over all of it, the animals.

## Fauna

The Stack teems.

Swifts and swallows own the upper air, thousands upon thousands of them, nesting in every weep hole and ventilation slot and riding the thermals that spill from the vents. At dawn and dusk their screaming rises over even the machinery. Pigeons of many breeds, some feral, some descended from long-lapsed message lofts, crowd the ledges and cornices, and their droppings are one of the great slow fertilizers of the terraces. Falcons hunt them, stooping down the faces of the Stack in dives that last whole kilometers.

In the planted terraces there are lizards everywhere, basking on warm iron, some no bigger than a finger, some the length of an arm, with blunt heads and skin the rusty orange of the brick they live against. Bees work the flowering creepers in such numbers that some tiers hum audibly. Their wild combs hang inside disused pipework, and the honey that leaks from cracked conduits is harvested by anyone brave enough.

There are goats, and nobody is quite sure how they got up so high. They are small and sure-footed, and they browse the terraces in loose bands, walking along cornices a hand's width wide above drops of a thousand meters. There are also monkeys: a slender, grey, long-tailed kind that lives in the fig trees of the middle tiers and raids everything. They have learned to ride the freight hoists. They sit on the counterweights and travel up and down the Stack as casually as commuters.

In the dark inner halls there are bats, cave crickets, pale spiders that string webs across unused doorways, and blind white snails that graze the condensation films on the walls. In the Bore itself, along the ledges where the draught is warm and steady, great colonies of moths live their whole lives without ever seeing the outside, carried up in the column and settling back down again in slow, grey drifts.

## Water

The Stack is not wet, but it is never dry.

There is almost no rain at the upper levels, which stand above the cloud. What there is instead is condensation. The warm, moist air from the Bore rises into the cold of the heights, and near the crown it condenses on vast iron cooling fins, on the undersides of vent hoods, on the glass of the summit galleries. It runs down in beads, in sheets, in trickles. The whole upper structure is designed, deliberately or by accretion, to catch it. Gutters, spouts, channels, and downpipes gather the water and lead it downward through the Stack in a branching network of cisterns and overflows.

So water on the Stack comes from above, reliably but not abundantly. Pipes drip. Walls sweat. Mosses stay green. Some gutters run constantly; others only after the long cycle turns. There are cisterns on the middle levels that have become ponds, with lilies and frogs and small silver fish nobody stocked. There are also stretches of terrace, on the sunward side, where the channels failed long ago and the ground is baked and cracked between shade trees. In the mornings mist clings to the terraces and burns off by midday. Everything in the Stack's ecology lives on this rhythm: moisture caught, held, and let slowly go.

## Light

Above the cloud deck, the light is enormous. The upper terraces sit under a hard, clear sky, the sun reflects off the white sea of cloud below, and the pale stone and whitewashed walls of the high tiers throw it back again. Midday up here is almost painful. The residents of the high levels wear broad hats and slitted eyeshades carved from horn. The plants grow waxy and bright, the lizards bask, and the iron of the summit fins shimmers.

Below the cloud, light is still generous. The Stack is so broad that its sunward flank catches the whole day, and the terraces are open-fronted. Only in the deep inner halls does it fade, and there the electric lamps take over, so that even the interior of the Stack is lit, warmly and unevenly, in pools and strings of amber.

## The Slow Breath

The unusual thing about the Stack is not that it is made. It is that it breathes.

The draught in the Bore is not quite constant. Over a period of about fifty-one hours, it strengthens and weakens in a slow, smooth cycle. At the height of the draught, called the Inhale though it is really the Bore exhaling, the roar deepens, the turbines run faster, the lamps burn brighter, and the vent-crown at the summit sheds a plume of vapor that can be seen from the plain as a white feather over the mountain. At the low of the cycle the roar drops to a murmur, some of the old flywheels slow almost to a stop, and condensation up top increases as the air lingers and cools. For a few hours water runs in every channel of the Stack, and gutters dry for two days start to trickle again.

Life on the Stack is tuned to this breath. The moths of the Bore rise in the Inhale and settle in the lull. Swifts time their feeding flights to the strongest thermals. Plants on the high terraces open their leaves when the condensation comes. The human residents measure time in breaths rather than days; a "long week" is three breaths. Maintenance crews go into the turbine halls only in the lull, when the vanes can be braked. Weddings are held at the turn from low to high, for luck.

Over longer spans there are other rhythms, slower still. The guild records suggest the breath itself lengthens by a few minutes every century. The Stack also settles. Measurements taken across generations show the summit sinking by a finger's width every decade as the old lower brick compresses under the weight of everything above. And the Stack is always being built upward again. Crews on the crown lay new courses, raise new cooling fins, extend the spire, so that the height stays roughly where it has always been. Nobody decided this. It is simply what is done: the top is kept up, the bottom is allowed to sink.

## The People

People live all over the Stack, though in their numbers they are outnumbered many times over by goats, to say nothing of birds.

They are mostly guild families: turbine-keepers, gutter-wardens, hoistmen, glaziers, masons, lamp-tenders. They farm the terraces in patchwork plots between the wild growth: beans on trellises of old conduit, citrus in bathtub-sized planters, rice in a few flooded cisterns. They trade with the plain by the long cable lines. They are, in general, comfortable with heights to a degree that unsettles visitors, and uncomfortable with silence. People born on the Stack who go down to live on the plain often cannot sleep for weeks, until they rig something to rattle or hum by the bed.

They have a saying: *The Stack was not built. It was kept.* It means that no one alive, and no one in the records, ever laid its foundation. Everyone has only ever added to it, repaired it, maintained it, let it grow. The structure is older than their memory of it, and it will outlast them, and their work is not to finish it but to keep it breathing.

## At Evening

At sunset, from a high terrace, you can see the whole shape of it: the cloud sea turning copper below, the plume of vapor from the crown lit gold, the swifts in their thousands pouring out of the walls in a screaming river, the lamps coming on tier by tier down the Stack's flanks until the whole mountain is laced with light through the leaves. The drone of the Bore rises and settles in its slow cycle. A goat stands on a gargoyle's head in silhouette. Somewhere below, a freight hoist groans upward with a monkey riding the counterweight. Water drips from a fern.

Everything here is made, and everything here is alive, and after long enough, those have stopped being different things.
