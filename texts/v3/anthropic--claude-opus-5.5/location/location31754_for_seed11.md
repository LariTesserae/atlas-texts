# The Blown Table

## The Climb

You meet the sound before you meet the place.

Three days below the rim, in the warm forests at the foot of the escarpment, there is a low pressure in the ears, a hum that seems to come from the ground. Birds down there sing at odd pitches, as if they have learned to fit between its notes. By the second day of climbing the hum becomes a drone. By the last switchback, where the trail narrows to a crack in pale stone, it is a roar that shakes the rock under your palms. You haul yourself over the lip, and the wind takes the breath out of your mouth, and you understand that you will not hear your own voice again until you leave.

This is the Table: a single immense plateau lifted so far above the surrounding country that the lowland forests look like moss on a floor. Its edges drop away in walls thousands of meters tall. Its top is almost perfectly flat.

## The Oldest Floor

The flatness is the first strange thing, and the reason for everything else.

This rock is older than nearly anything. It is a quartzite so ancient and so hard that it has outlasted the mountains that once stood on it. Those peaks wore down over spans of time that stop meaning anything, down to rounded stumps, then to swells, then to a surface as level as a tabletop. Then something far below, slow and patient, pushed the whole worn floor upward, and kept pushing. What stands now is a finished landscape. Its erosion is complete. It was simply lifted into the sky after it was done.

Walk it and you are walking a surface the weather has already said everything to. The stone is polished to a dull sheen where it breaks through. It is pale gray, cream, and a bruised rose where iron has bled through the grain. There are no sharp edges anywhere. The few outcrops that rise are rounded, like loaves or knuckles or the backs of sleeping animals, and even the tallest is no higher than a house. Between them the ground runs level to a horizon so wide and empty that the curve of the world seems faintly visible, the land bending away under a lid of fast, low cloud.

Water and wind have not only planed the surface. They have also hollowed it. Beneath the flat skin the quartzite is riddled with channels, tubes, and chambers: slow dissolution over unimaginable time, a sponge of stone. In places the roofs have fallen, leaving round shafts that drop into darkness, their rims fuzzed with green. In other places there are only small holes, a thumb's width or a fist's width, scattered across the rock like pores.

The Table breathes through these holes. That is where the noise comes from.

## The River of Air

The Table sits in a warm latitude, and the lowlands beneath it are hot, humid, and steaming. That heat never stops trying to rise. When it reaches the escarpment it climbs the walls in a continuous flood, a warm updraft that pours up thousands of meters of cliff and spills over the rim onto the plateau. It is a river of air that has been running for longer than there have been rivers.

This is why it is warm here, when by all rights it should be frozen. The air arriving at the top is lowland air, still carrying its heat. It is thin and it hurts the lungs, but it is warm, like the breath of a large animal. The cold only comes when the wind fails. Those rare lulls are dangerous, and the Table's few inhabitants dread them.

The wind never comes from one direction for long. The updraft arrives on whichever side the sun is heating most, so through the course of a day the wind swings around the compass. In the morning it pours in from the east rim, at noon it roars up from the south, and in the evening it floods from the west. Where the flows from two edges collide, usually somewhere in the plateau's broad middle, the air goes mad. It tears into vertical rotors and tumbling standing waves. Clouds form and dissolve in minutes. Small tornadic threads, gray with seed and spore, whip across the ground and vanish.

## The Instrument

When this wind crosses a surface pocked with ten million holes, each opening onto a tube of a different length and a different diameter, the Table sounds.

It is not one sound. It is every sound at once. The large open shafts boom in tones so low they are felt in the belly and the teeth rather than heard: long pulses that come and go as the gusts change. The mid-sized pores moan and hoot like a crowd of flutes, each on its own note, drifting in and out of harmony. The small holes shriek. Over it all is the wind's own roar through the vegetation, a vast broadband hiss like surf that never breaks.

Together it is deafening, and not in a figurative sense. Stand in the open for an hour and you will leave with ringing ears. Stay a week and you will lose the high frequencies of your hearing, perhaps for good. The few people who live here speak to each other with their hands, and they speak through the ground as well: they press palms to the stone and feel the drum-codes others beat with mallets on hollow outcrops a kilometer away.

The sound has a grammar, and it can be learned. As the wind shifts, different fields of holes come into play, and the chord of the whole Table changes. A plateau-wide shift from a howling, dissonant cluster to a clean, low octave means the wind has settled into a single steady flow from one rim. A rising, sliding whine means rotors are forming. A sudden thinning, when the booming stops and only the shriek remains, means the updraft is faltering, and it is time to find shelter before the cold drops out of the sky.

The worst sound on the Table is silence. It comes perhaps twice a year, when the lowland heat and the plateau's own chill balance exactly and the river of air simply stops. For a few minutes or a few hours, the ten million holes go quiet. People who have heard it say the stillness is unbearable, a pressure in the skull. The temperature can fall thirty degrees in an hour. Then the wind returns all at once, usually from an unexpected rim, in a front that flattens everything standing.

## The Combed Green

For all its violence, the Table is green, and more than green: lush.

The rising air carries lowland moisture with it. As it cools in the climb, the moisture condenses, so the updraft arrives at the rim as a warm, wet, fast-moving fog. It rarely rains on the plateau itself. The clouds are moving too fast, and the air has already wrung out most of its water on the cliffs. What the Table gets instead is condensation, endless and fine, beading on every surface the fog touches. The plants have learned to catch it.

Nearly everything grows low, pressed to the stone, and everything is combed. Great mats of fibrous grass cover the plateau in sweeps, each blade a long filament, silver-green on one side and wine-dark on the other. Because the wind swings through the day, the grasses swing with it, and whole fields of them turn over at once, flashing silver, then dark, then silver again, in waves that roll across the plateau for kilometers. From a high outcrop it looks as if the ground itself is flowing.

Between the grasses grows hair-moss, a dense, spongy growth like the pelt of an animal. It is thick enough to sink into to the knee, and it holds water the way stone cannot. Squeeze a handful and it runs clear and cold. This is most of the Table's water: not pools or streams, but a vast wet fur laid over dry rock, which drinks the fog and gives it back slowly. There are pockets of open water, too. Where a collapsed shaft has sealed at the bottom, a round, black pool lies at the base of a green-walled well, sheltered from the wind, perfectly still, with the roar passing overhead like weather on another world.

The shafts are where the Table's other gardens grow. Out of the wind, in the warm, wet, echoing dark, plants that cannot survive on the surface reach heights they could never manage in the open. There are tree ferns with fronds as long as a body. There are broad-leaved plants with leaves as thick as hide, which unroll only in the stillness below. There are vines that climb the shaft walls toward the light and stop abruptly a meter below the rim, where the wind begins, as if they have been cut with a blade. Looking down into one of these shafts is like looking into a jungle kept in a jar.

The surface plants have strange habits, as they must. Some cushion plants grow in rings around the smaller holes, packing their tissue into the openings until the note of the hole changes or stops. Over centuries they have altered the chord of whole regions of the plateau. Some flowers open only in the lulls and release clouds of scent into air that, for a few minutes, can hold it. Seed is everywhere. It is thrown up from the lowlands, carried across the plateau, and dropped in drifts behind every rock. In the lee of an outcrop you can find a meter-deep bank of seed, spore, husk, insect wing, and fluff, sprouting from the top and rotting from the bottom, a fertile, fermenting heap.

## The Accidental Living

Animals are rare, and most of those present did not choose to come.

The updraft is an elevator. It lifts everything light enough from the forests below: insects, spiders on their silk threads, seeds, winged beetles, sometimes small birds and bats caught in a gust and unable to fight their way out. It carries them over the rim and scatters them across the plateau. Most die. The Table is littered with the bright, fragile bodies of butterflies that have never seen it before, tangled in the grass like confetti after a festival no one attended.

A few survive and stay, and over time some have become something else. There are beetles here, descended from lowland stock, that have lost their wings and now live in the hair-moss, clinging to the fur of the ground. There are crickets with no voice at all, because in this noise there is no point in singing. There is a small, dense, ground-dwelling bird, dull brown and almost round, which never flies because the wind would take it away. It moves from shelter to shelter in quick dashes. It communicates, like the people, through the ground, by drumming its feet on the stone.

The largest animal on the Table is something the people call the listener. It is a long-bodied creature about the size of a goat, low-slung, with a pale hide and enormous, folded ears it keeps closed almost all the time, like fists. It grazes on the moss in the lee of the rocks. When the wind falters, it unfolds its ears and turns in slow circles, and the people watch it. If the listeners run for the shafts, the silence is coming.

There are very few of them. You can walk for a day and see one or none. The Table is mostly empty: grass, stone, fog, wind, and noise, with vast spaces between anything that breathes.

## The Marks of People

People come here, and a few stay. They leave little behind.

The most common marks are walls: low, curving windbreaks of fitted quartzite slabs, knee-high or waist-high, arranged in crescents to shelter a fire or a sleeping place from the prevailing flow. Because the wind swings all day, many are built as full circles or as spirals, so that there is always a lee somewhere. Some are very old. The stones are worn smooth, and the moss has grown up over them so that they are only green ridges in the ground. The people who built them must have been gone for a thousand years.

There are cairns, too, but these are not simple waymarkers. Each is stacked around one of the larger holes in the rock, built so that its base encloses the opening and funnels wind into it. The stones have been chosen and placed so that the cairn itself becomes part of the instrument, changing the tone of the hole below. Some play low, some high. A line of them across the plateau, spaced a day's walk apart, plays a descending sequence of notes. Anyone who knows the sequence can navigate by ear, even in fog: you follow the pitch down, and it leads you to the northern stair.

The people who live here now are few, perhaps a few hundred across the whole Table. They carry stone mallets, fiber cordage twisted from the grasses, and bone awls, and they dig their homes into the sheltered walls of the shallower shafts. They do not farm; nothing would survive the wind. They gather moss-water and seed, trap the round brown birds, and collect the lowland insects that rain onto the rim each morning, roasting them by the handful. Their greatest craft is the plug. From turf, stone, and a resin cooked from cushion plants, they make stoppers of exact sizes and fit them into the holes of the plateau, retuning it. A whole region can be made to sing a warning, or fall silent, or play a note that carries to the far edge. It is slow, generational work. Some families have spent centuries shaping the voice of a single valley of stone.

Nothing they make is permanent. Plugs blow out and walls fall. The seed drifts bury everything. The wind has been working on this surface for longer than there have been people anywhere, and it does not stop.

## The Rules of the Table

The Table has a few laws that everyone who lives there learns, usually from the ground up:

- **The wind always returns.** It never fails for good. When it does fail, it returns from the side you are not watching.
- **Nothing standing survives.** Anything that grows or is built above knee height will be taken, unless it is inside a shaft.
- **The sound is the weather.** If you learn the chord of the plateau, you will know what the sky is about to do. If you cannot hear it, feel it through the stone.
- **The water is in the ground's fur.** It is never in the sky and rarely in pools.
- **Silence kills.** It brings the cold and the returning front.
- **Everything arrives from below.** Seeds, insects, birds, warmth, water: all of it is carried up by the air. The Table produces almost nothing of its own. It is a place where the lowlands' breath comes to rest, briefly, before it is torn apart and thrown back into the sky.

## Evening

At dusk the wind swings west, and the sun goes down behind the western rim in a haze of gold and seed-dust. For a few minutes the updraft on that side is at its strongest, and the long, low shafts of the west begin to boom together in a sound like enormous doors being slammed, one after another, rolling across the plateau for fifty kilometers. The grasses flip dark, all at once, from rim to rim. The clouds over the Table catch fire in orange and then go gray.

Far out on the plateau, a single thin line of smoke rises from behind a spiral wall and is torn sideways almost immediately. Someone is roasting the day's insects. Someone else, in a green shaft a day's walk off, drums a message into the rock with a stone mallet. It is felt, not heard, through the soles of the feet: *wind west, holding, all well.*

Then the night wind begins, from the north, with a new and rising chord. The Table changes its song, as it has done every evening for longer than anyone can measure, and it goes on roaring.
