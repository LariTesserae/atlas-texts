# The Kiln Above the Clouds

You arrive at dawn, and the first thing you notice is that you cannot hear yourself breathe.

The air up here is so thin that sound comes out of it flattened, a quarter of what it should be. Your boots crunch on the crust, but the crunch reaches your ears late and small, as though someone a long way off were making it on your behalf. Below, in every direction, lies the cloud sea: a white floor stretching to the curve of the world, unbroken except where other summits break through it like the backs of animals. You are standing on the highest of them, a great broken caldera perhaps twelve kilometres across. Although you are nearer the cold of space than almost anywhere a person can stand, the ground under your feet is hot enough to feel through your soles.

The heat does not come from the sun, though the sun will come. It comes from below.

---

## The Floor

The caldera is a plateau of old fire that has had time to settle. Its rim is no longer jagged. Wind and slow chemistry have rounded it into a low, worn ring of rust and ochre and bone-white, slumped in places where ancient walls gave way and let the talus spill outward into the clouds. Inside the ring, the floor is not flat. It is *crowded*.

Every few metres something breaks the surface. There are fumaroles ringed with needle-crystals of sulphur, yellow so pure it looks like a painted error. There are mud pots the size of washbasins, blinking slowly with grey bubbles. There are sinter terraces, stepped and scalloped like stacked porcelain plates, where hot water once flowed and now only seeps. Small cones stand no higher than your waist, each one cracked at the top and exhaling a thread of vapour. Vents lie so close together that their crusts have fused into a single rippled skin, with fractures running through it that steam faintly in the cold of the early hour. There is no stretch of open ground here in the sense you expect. The land is fully occupied by its own processes, shoulder to shoulder, each feature pressed against the next.

Between them lie the pools.

The water here is not abundant, and it is not absent. It exists as pockets: hundreds of shallow basins, some no wider than a hand, some as broad as a courtyard. Each is fed by its own seep and each is a different colour, depending on its temperature and what lives in it. The hottest are clear and deep blue, with nothing living in them at all. Slightly cooler pools are rimmed with bands of orange, then green, then a brown the colour of tea, as mats of heat-loving microbes arrange themselves by tolerance. The coolest pools, the ones merely too hot to bathe in, are pink. They are pink because they are full of shrimp.

---

## The Colony

At first light, the rim looks encrusted with pale stone. Then the sun clears the cloud sea and the stone begins to move.

They are birds, or something near enough. Locals, when there were locals, called them *kiln-herons*. They are long-legged and narrow-bodied, with plumage the grey-white of sinter. Only the undersides of their wings carry a flush of the same pink as the shrimp they eat. They nest on every ledge, every boulder, every flat surface of the rim, and many that are not flat. Their nests are mounded cups of dried mud and mineral paste, built so close together that neighbours touch. Seen from above, the whole inner wall of the caldera is tiled with them, tens of thousands of cups set edge to edge like the cells of a comb.

They are silent. That is the strangest thing about them, until you understand it. In this air, a cry does not carry. A shriek that would travel a kilometre at sea level dies within a few dozen metres here. Over generations the kiln-herons have given up on voice almost entirely. They signal by posture, by the flash of pink underwing, by slow bobbing dances performed in lines along the terrace edges. A colony of perhaps half a million animals goes about its morning with no more sound than the rustle of feathers, the tick of cooling rock, and the soft *plock* of mud pots.

As the sun climbs, they descend in waves to the pools. They wade the pink basins in dense ranks, heads down, filtering, and the shrimp scatter and re-gather around their legs. The birds work the pools systematically, one basin after another, leaving each one clouded and slowly settling behind them. By mid-morning the floor of the caldera is as packed with bodies as the rim was: grey-white shapes standing among the steaming vents, so many and so close that from the rim they look like a second, living crust laid over the first.

---

## What Else Lives Here

Nothing here is lush. Plants are few and hard. A mat of dark green cushion-moss grows in the lee of the cones, where vapour condenses and drips. There are grey rosettes of a succulent with leaves thick as thumbs, which has learned to root in the cracks at the margins of warm ground, close enough to the heat to survive the nights and far enough to avoid being cooked. Lichens in rust and lime patch the older boulders. None of this adds up to much. You could walk a hundred paces without stepping on anything green.

But the animals are everywhere, because the food web does not run on plants. It runs on heat and chemistry. The microbial mats feed the shrimp and the brine flies. The flies rise from the warmer pools in dense, low clouds that drift across the terraces like smoke. The flies feed a small, quick, lizard-like thing with sulphur-yellow flanks, which basks on the warm crust in numbers that make the ground look speckled. When you step near, dozens flicker away at once into cracks too narrow to follow.

The shrimp feed the herons. The herons feed their chicks, and the chicks feed the predators.

There are two of those. The first is a raptor, dark and heavy-shouldered, that rides the thermals rising from the vents. It circles the caldera in the late morning and drops without warning into the colony, and the herons' only alarm is a ripple of flashing pink that spreads outward from the strike like a stone's rings in water. The second predator is on the ground. It is a lean, low-slung carnivore, something between a fox and a mongoose, its paws calloused thick against the hot crust. It hunts the rim at dusk, working along the rows of nests. The colony's silence works against the herons here. They cannot raise a shared alarm, so each nest defends itself alone, and each night the rim is a little emptier by morning.

None of this is loud. Predation here happens in near-silence: the thump of a strike, a scatter of feathers, a hiss of steam through a crack as a body is dragged across it.

## The Works

On the eastern side of the caldera, where the rim has slumped lowest, there is a building.

It is the only large structure here, and it is not large. A turbine hall of corrugated iron, rust-streaked and patched, squats over a bored well. Thick insulated pipe runs from the well into the hall. A second pipe runs out of it and down the flank, disappearing into the cloud. From inside comes the one continuous mechanical sound in the whole caldera, a low, steady hum. You feel it in the ground before you hear it.

It is still running. Someone built it to tap the steam and drive a generator, and the steam has not stopped. The copper windings still turn. Power still flows out along a line of lattice pylons that marches across the caldera floor and over the rim.

The pylons carry power to the ropeway. The ropeway descends from a squat concrete terminal on the rim, a single steel cable strung pylon to pylon, down through the cloud sea to somewhere you cannot see. Iron buckets hang from it at intervals, and they move, slowly and constantly, down into the white and back up out of it. The buckets coming up are empty. The buckets going down carry sulphur. A small crushing works stands beside the main fumarole field. Its conveyors are electrified and its grinder is automated, and it still scrapes the yellow crystals from the vents, crushes them, and tips the powder into the passing buckets. The machines lack the judgment to stop. Downhill, someone is presumably still receiving it. Or no one is, and somewhere far below there is a mountain of sulphur growing in the rain.

The rest of the human presence is scattered and small. There is a stone hut with a collapsed roof. Iron survey stakes stand here and there, their paint long gone. A rail spur ends in nothing. A single pressure gauge on a pipe near the well has a needle that still trembles. There is a toolbox rusted shut, and a boot sole fused into a crust of sinter as though the ground had tried to swallow it and given up.

The kiln-herons have made use of all of it. They nest on the pylons, in the rafters of the crushing shed, and along the warm top of the steam pipe. Their mud cups are stacked down its length like beads on a string. And some of them, the young ones mostly, have learned to ride the ropeway. They step into an empty returning bucket at the terminal and are carried slowly out over the edge and down into the cloud. Then they tip out, spread their wings, and ride the rising warm air back up to the rim. No one knows why they do it. They do it every day.

## The Cycle

The caldera lives by a daily rhythm as regular as the turbine.

**Morning** is clear and sharp. The cloud sea lies flat below, the air is still, and the vapour threads rise straight up from every vent. The herons descend to feed, and the lizards emerge to bask.

**Midday** is brutal. The sun at this altitude is savage, with little atmosphere to soften it, and the heat from above meets the heat from below. The whole caldera shimmers. Sinter terraces glare white enough to blind. The shallowest pools shrink visibly and leave rings of salt. Most animals retreat to shade: under ledges, into cracks, beneath the eaves of the turbine hall. The colony stands motionless with wings half-spread to shade its chicks. The only movement is the raptor overhead and the buckets on the cable.

**Afternoon** brings the clouds up. As the lower air warms, the cloud sea swells and climbs the mountain's flanks until its upper edge pours over the lowest part of the rim like slow surf. At the same time the vents, their vapour no longer burned off by direct sun, begin to billow. The rising steam and the incoming cloud meet over the caldera floor. For an hour or two the whole place is fog. It is warm, sulphurous, and dripping, and visibility drops to a few metres. Everything is wet. The moss swells. The pools refill. Condensation beads on every surface and runs off the iron in rusty tears.

**Dusk** clears again. The cloud drains back down the flanks and the air chills fast, and the steam columns stand tall and pink in the last light. The fox-thing comes out along the rim.

**Night** is very cold in the air and very warm at the ground. The caldera floor becomes a patchwork of warmth. Animals of every kind crowd onto the hottest crust they can tolerate. Lizards, herons, and even the predators lie within a metre of each other on the same warm terrace. The truce holds because none of them can afford to leave the heat. The stars at this altitude do not twinkle. They burn steady and enormous, and against them the steam columns rise, faintly lit from below by the orange glow of the one working lamp above the turbine hall door.

---

## The Rule of the Place

Everything here is borrowed from below. The heat, the water, the food, and the power all rise out of the mountain's deep interior and are spent in the thin cold air. It is a place that should be dead, too high, too hot, too poor in soil. Instead it is saturated with life. Every ledge is claimed, every pool is grazed, and every warm stone is occupied. It has been this way for a very long time. The terraces took thousands of years to grow, and the rim took longer to wear down. The machines are the newest thing here. They have already begun to be absorbed, colonised, encrusted, and folded into the pattern.

It is a busy place, and a quiet one. Half a million birds, a steam turbine, a hundred boiling springs, and a ropeway carrying sulphur into the clouds, and when you stand still on the rim in the hour before the fog, you can hear the faint tick of your own watch.
