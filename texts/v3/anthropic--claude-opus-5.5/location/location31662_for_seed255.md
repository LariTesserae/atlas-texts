# The Coursing

## First, the sound

You hear it before you understand there is anywhere to be. It comes out of the dark as a slow, uneven pounding, stone on stone, many blows overlapping, near ones sharp and far ones soft, so that the whole lowland seems to have a pulse that can't settle on a rhythm. Under the pounding is water: a constant hiss and trickle, the gulp of a sump filling, a gutter overflowing its lip and slapping onto pavers below. Under that, closer to the body than the ear, is a low grinding. Something heavy is being turned against something heavier, somewhere, all the time.

It is dark, but not completely. Your eyes take a long while to believe what they're given. There is a horizon, faintly, made of shapes blacker than the sky. There are orange points scattered across it at every distance: kiln-mouths, dozens of them, some the size of a coal and some no bigger than a spark. They give almost no light to anything but themselves. The sky above is a starless charcoal. It isn't cloud, exactly. It is a darkness with texture, like the underside of a wet slate.

The air is cool and tastes of lime and wet brick and faintly of smoke.

## The ground is not ground

Put your hand down. It lands on a paver: a square of fired clay, slightly damp, its edges so fresh and sharp they could open your palm if you pressed. Beside it is another, and another. They are laid in courses, fitted tight, with mortar lines no wider than a fingernail. The mortar is still slightly soft in places. It gives under a thumbnail like old cheese.

Nothing here is worn. That is the first rule of the Coursing, and everything else follows from it. No step has been dished by feet. No corner has been rounded by weather. The arrises of every block are knife-true. Stairs still have the chisel marks in their treads. Walls stand with their faces flat as still water. The whole country looks as if it was finished an hour ago, though parts of it clearly are not finished at all.

If you pry up a paver, which is hard, because they are fitted and heavy, there is no soil beneath it. There is a bed of crushed brick, and under that, another paved floor. It is laid at a slightly different angle, in a slightly different bond, with pavers of a slightly different red. Under that is another. The masons say no one has ever reached the bottom. Some of them say there is no bottom, only floors, the way there is no bottom to a year. Nothing in the Coursing was ever natural. It is made all the way down, and every layer is almost as new as the one above it.

## The shape of the basin

The Coursing is a lowland, a wide shallow bowl perhaps a day's walk across, and in the dark it's hard to tell where its rim is. The rim may be a real rise of built terrace, or it may only be where the sound thins out. Within it, the made world is packed close. Walls stand a forearm apart, so you move sideways between them. Towers rise so near each other that their corbels nearly touch overhead, and a person could cross from one to another at the third course without using a bridge. There are covered lanes and open lanes, lanes that pass through the lower rooms of buildings, and buildings whose ground floors are simply a lane with a ceiling.

It is dense the way a honeycomb is dense. Every structure leans on the next. A cistern's wall is also the back wall of a kiln-shed, which is also the footing of a stair, which climbs to a gallery that is the roof of a gutter. Very little stands alone.

Yet it is open, too. The builders never roofed the basin as a whole. The stacked, jostling mass is broken by long straight channels and wide sunken courts, and from the lip of any court you can see across a great deal of the Coursing: tiers and ridges of masonry going off into the dark, pricked with kiln-light. From above, if anyone could see it from above, it would look like a clay model of a mountain range that someone pressed flat with the heel of a hand.

## Water, which is the maker

It rarely rains in the Coursing, and when it does, the rain is fine and cold and brief, and every surface sings with it. Most of the water comes another way. The stone is always a little colder than the air, so the air gives up its moisture to it constantly. Every wall wears a skin of condensation. It beads on the undersides of lintels, gathers in the fine grooves the masons cut for exactly this purpose, and runs.

Everything here is shaped to catch this seep. Drip-lines are cut under every coping. Walls carry little scuppers of fired clay, each spilling into the one below. Streets are canted a finger's width so that water finds their centers and flows. Thin runnels become gutters. Gutters join into channels, and channels cross the basin in straight stone-lined cuts, dark, fast, and loud, feeding down into the great sumps at the basin's low points.

Water is also what keeps the Coursing new. It dissolves the soft fresh mortar, a little at a time, and carries away lime and brick-dust as a pale silt. The silt settles in the sumps in fine grey layers. The masons dredge it out with long-handled scoops of fired clay, knead it, shape it in wooden-less molds of stone, and fire it in the kilns. It becomes pavers, blocks, pipes, and roof-tiles. Those are laid, the condensation begins to dissolve them, and the silt runs down to the sumps again.

So the whole basin is a slow circulation. It is not erosion, since nothing is ever allowed to wear down; the masons pull a softened block and set a fresh one long before any edge can round. It is closer to breathing. The Coursing is always coming into being, and it never grows old.

## The machinery

The pounding is the trip-hammers. There are hundreds of them. Each is a heavy stone head on a long arm of fitted stone segments, pinned with pegs of hard-fired clay and lashed with a grey mineral cord the masons tease out of certain veined stones and braid by hand. It is stiff, fibrous, fireproof, and it squeaks. Water from the channels drops into a clay bucket at the short end of each arm. The bucket fills, the arm tips, the bucket spills, and the arm crashes back. The hammer falls onto a bed where crushed brick is being pounded into the fine aggregate that goes into mortar.

Each hammer beats at the rate its trickle allows. Since no two trickles are the same, no two hammers keep time. Near a hammer, the blow is a physical thing. It comes up through your feet and into your jaw, and dust jumps on the stone. Across a court it is a hard clack. Across the basin it is part of the general thudding surf.

The grinding comes from the millstones: great rings of basalt-hard fired stone turned by water-wheels whose paddles are clay slabs. They crush old blocks that have gone soft into the grit that begins the cycle again. Some of the wheels are enormous and turn once in the time it takes to say a long sentence. Their groan is the lowest sound in the place.

There are no metal tools here. A few masons carry chisels of a dense black glassy stone they chip to an edge. Everything else is stone on stone, fired clay, braided mineral cord, and water. It is primitive, and it has been refined almost beyond imagining within its limits.

## The masons

There are people, not many, fewer than the scale of the work would suggest. The masons of the Coursing are pale, broad-handed, and quiet among the noise. They work almost entirely by ear and touch. A mason can tell a sound block from a softening one by tapping it with a knuckle and listening. They find their way through the lanes by the hammers: each hammer has its own voice, a slightly different pitch and timing, and the masons know the basin as a map of sounds. *Turn at the cracked-bell hammer. Follow the double-knock down to the third sump.*

They sing, sometimes, pitched to cut through the pounding. It isn't music so much as signaling: long single tones sent across the courts that say *here*, *carry*, *fire's ready*, *mind the gutter*. At the kilns they work by the orange glow, and their faces show only there, lit from below, before they turn away and vanish back into the dark.

No mason claims to have started the Coursing. Asked who laid the first floor, they tend to rap the pavers under their feet and say, "Someone did." Asked what they are building, some say *the floor*, some say *the next floor*, and a few, the oldest, say they are not building anything. They are only keeping the building from ending.

## The animals

Life is rare here, and all of it has come to terms with the stone.

In the sumps live the silt-eels: pale, eyeless, as long as an arm, soft as wet clay. They sift the grey silt through their gills for something too small to see. The masons consider them a good sign. A sump with eels makes finer brick, they say. They're probably right; the eels leave the silt finer than they found it.

In the dry upper galleries, under the corbels, roost small leathery fliers the masons call *knocks*. They hunt by the hammers. In the moment after each blow, the echo comes back off every wall and stair, and the knocks read the whole architecture in that instant and dart through it. They eat the lime-moths: tiny, dusty, grey-white insects that feed on fresh mortar and lay their eggs in its seams. A mortar joint that feels furry is moth-bitten and must be raked out and repointed. The knocks are welcome for this. At certain times, for reasons no one knows, the moths rise all at once out of the walls of a district in a pale soundless cloud, and the knocks go wild in it, and the masons stop work and stand still until it passes.

There is no plant life anywhere. Nothing green, nothing rooted. The closest thing is the lime-bloom, a soft white crystalline fur that grows on the damp faces of very cold walls. It spreads, branches, and even seems to reach toward drafts, though it is only mineral, only water leaving salt behind. The masons scrape it off. It crunches underfoot, and on still nights you can hear it growing, a faint crackle in the corners.

## What is strange

It is strange that there are floors beneath floors forever. It is strange that there is no weather to speak of, no sun, no season, yet the Coursing keeps a kind of time: the long cycle of the sumps filling and being dredged, the thicker condensation that comes some weeks and thins in others, the moth-rises. The masons measure their lives in firings.

Strangest, though, is this: the Coursing is slightly larger than it was. No one decided to extend it, but a mason who walks to the rim of the basin and listens will hear hammers beyond the last ones they knew. Out there, past where the sound thins, new courses have been laid in the dark. They are sharp-edged, mortar still soft, a paver-width at a time, in a bond none of the masons use. Sometimes a trickle of water has already found its way into a fresh-cut gutter there and begun running toward the center of the bowl, carrying its first grains of silt downhill to be made into something.

The masons don't go out to see who is laying those courses. They say it would be rude to interrupt. They say the Coursing is being built from both sides, and that someday the two works will meet. Then, they say, it will finally be finished, and then it will begin to wear.

Nobody sounds eager for that day. The hammers keep falling. The water keeps running. In the orange mouth of a kiln, a fresh block is slowly going from grey to red.
