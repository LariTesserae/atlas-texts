# The Walled Shelf

## The Water First

It is water before it is anything else, cold enough that the stones never lose their chill. It isn't the black of the abyss. It is shallow, twenty or thirty meters at most over most of its extent, but it lies under ice for most of the year, under a sky that for months does not bring the sun above the horizon. Its darkness is the darkness of a closed room at the bottom of the world, not of great depth. When you first arrive there is nothing to see at all, only the pressure of cold water on every surface and a faint sense that the dark has corners.

It does have corners. They are everywhere.

## The Walls

The floor of this sea is not a floor. It is paving: slabs of split slate and flat fieldstone fitted edge to edge, laid without mortar, stretching away in every direction you can reach and far beyond. Below the top course is another course. Where a slab has tipped or been pried up by roots, you find more stone under it, packed rubble and then more fitted slabs. Nobody has found where the made ground ends and something older begins. As far as anyone can tell, it doesn't.

Walls rise from the paving, and they are the defining fact of the place. They are dry-stone walls, stacked by hand, stone on stone, each one chosen and turned until it sat right. They are rarely higher than a person and often lower. They run close together, a few paces apart, sometimes closer. They divide the ground into enclosures, and the enclosures are divided again, and those again, so the shelf is a honeycomb of small stone rooms, open to the water above, going on for a distance that would take a lifetime to walk. Some enclosures are square and some are long and narrow, like old field strips. Others are round, with the stones leaning inward in rough corbelling, closing into low beehive chambers you could crawl into. Many small cells sit inside larger rings. Some walls run dead straight for kilometers. Others wander, following a contour that no longer exists.

Everything is very old. No stone has kept a sharp edge. The slate has weathered into soft-shouldered loaves, and the fieldstone is rounded like river cobbles, though it was never in a river. The walls have slumped into long ridges, their tops fallen in, their bases spread out in aprons of tumbled stone. But they have kept their lines. Even where a wall has settled into a shallow hump on the paving, you can follow it, and it still turns where it turned and meets its neighbor where it always met it. The pattern has outlasted the structure. Any tool marks are gone, smoothed away by slow water over a span of time that is hard to imagine.

Among the rubble are small made things, weathered into near-anonymity: saddle-querns with the grinding hollow still faintly dished, disc-shaped stones with holes bored through their centers (loom weights, or net sinkers, or something else), lintels spanning gaps that were once doorways, and stone posts set upright in rows. None of it is metal. If metal was ever here, it went long ago. What remains was shaped by hands with other stones, and it is all that was made.

## The Channels

Running through the honeycomb, at intervals of a few hundred meters, are channels. They are stone-lined, a meter or two wide, cut down into the paving and walled on both sides, with slab-covered culverts where they pass under the enclosures. They branch and rejoin. Some carry sluice-slots in their walls, vertical grooves where boards or stones once slid down to stop the flow. They were clearly built to move water across dry ground.

They still move water, just differently. Every winter the sea surface freezes, and as the ice forms it pushes its salt out below, so cold, heavy brine sinks and pools on the paving. It creeps downhill along the gentle slope of the shelf. The shelf is low and nearly flat, but not perfectly, and the brine finds the channels. The builders' gradients still govern where it goes, so the densest, coldest water in this sea travels in the same courses that were cut for rain and meltwater, when there was an above.

This is the first rule of the place: the old design still decides things. Water goes where it was told to go, and so does everything that depends on water.

## The Forest

The channels are where the forest is thickest.

The shelf is lush, surprisingly so for somewhere this dark. It is kelp above all: long, leathery brown straps rooted in the stonework, their holdfasts gripping the joints between slabs, working down into the gaps in the walls, clasping lintels and posts. The stipes rise several meters and the blades stretch out above them, so in the growing season each enclosure is filled to its rim with kelp, and the walls become lines of darker shadow under a continuous brown canopy. Along the channels, where brine and upwelling nutrients flow, the kelp grows longest, in ropes that lie with the current in long combed lines. Seen from above through thin ice, the channels would show as darker veins across the shelf.

Under the kelp, every surface is crusted. Coralline algae coat the stones in pink-grey plaques thick as plaster, filling the old cracks and rounding the edges further, so some walls are now as much algae as rock. Bladderwrack hangs off the wall tops in tangled olive curtains. In the low places, where silt has drifted into courtyards and settled over the paving, there are meadows: eelgrass and fine red weed standing in soft beds within four stone walls, like gardens that are still, in a sense, being kept.

It shouldn't grow this well. It manages through patience and storage. For the few weeks when the sun rises and the ice thins, a dim greenish light gets through, and the kelp grows as fast as it can, building up reserves in its tissues. Through the long dark it keeps growing slowly on those stores, pushing out new blade in total darkness, ready for light that is months away. Kelp can do this. It grows in the dark on what it saved.

## The Absence

The other reason the forest grows so thickly is that nothing eats it.

There are no animals here. There are no fish in the kelp, no crabs in the rubble, no urchins on the stones, no snails on the blades, no worms in the silt, no shrimp. The water holds nothing that swims or crawls. In every other cold sea, grazers keep the forest in check. Here the forest has never been checked, so it grows until light and space run out, fills every enclosure, and spills over the walls.

Without grazers, the dead plants just pile up. Storms under the ice, or old age, break blades from the stipes, and they drift and gather against the walls in the corners of the enclosures, in dark heaps that rot very slowly in the cold, attended only by bacteria. Over centuries these drifts pack down into brown-black peaty deposits that fill the lower rooms. In some places whole enclosures are full to the brim with compressed kelp, and the walls exist only as ridges above a spongy dark floor. The forest is slowly burying the structure that holds it. It is doing this so slowly that the structure is winning, for now.

## Sound

It is quiet, but not silent.

In most seas the background noise is the crackle of countless small creatures. Here it is missing, and the quiet is an absence you can feel. What remains are the sounds of the place itself:

- **The ice.** In winter it groans and cracks overhead as it thickens and shifts. The sound carries far through water, a low boom, then a long wavering creak. Sometimes it comes from very far away, from ice over parts of the shelf no one will ever reach.
- **The stones.** Every so often a stone settles as a holdfast finally levers it loose, or a slumped wall gives a little more. It makes a dull knock with a gritty trickle after it. Then nothing for a long time.
- **The kelp.** When the current rises, the stipes lean together and their gas-bladders and hard stems touch and rub, making a soft woody creaking that moves across the forest in waves.

That is all. Long stretches of dark with only the far-off ice.

## Weather

The shelf is big enough to have weather, and it happens in the water.

Brine season is winter's weather. Cold streams run downslope through the channels, pooling in the lowest enclosures and spilling from one to the next, with the kelp combed flat along their paths. The pools stratify: the heaviest, saltiest water lies on the paving, and fresher water sits above it, with a sharp boundary between them. The kelp's lower blades hang in one water and its upper blades in another.

Then there are internal waves. The tides are small, but they push against those layers and set slow, enormous undulations moving along the boundary. A wave might take an hour to pass, a smooth rise and fall of the layer line meters high. You can't see it, but the forest shows it. Across the shelf, whole districts of kelp lift together, lean, and settle, as the wave rolls under them over thousands of enclosures. It is the closest thing this place has to wind.

In the brief bright season there is melt. The ice thins and breaks, fresh water runs out over the surface, the light shifts to its green-grey, and the whole forest grows fast. After the few storms that break the ice, fine silt is stirred off the courtyard floors and moves across the shelf in slow clouds, muddying the water for days until it settles into new, finer layers in the low rooms.

## The Scale of It

It goes on. That is the hardest thing to hold in mind.

The enclosures don't cluster into anything like a village and then give way to open ground. There is no open ground. The honeycomb continues past the reach of any light that ever penetrates this water, across a continental shelf of made stone, one small walled room after another, all shaped by hand. The walls are close together everywhere; the density never thins. You can move a day in any direction and still be in the lattice, and it will look much the same: low rounded walls, crusted with pink algae, filled with brown kelp, with a channel running somewhere nearby.

Nobody knows who built it or why they needed to divide a whole continent into rooms small enough to wade across. All that is clear is that they built everything. They made the ground, the walls, the water's paths, and even the gradient the brine follows. Then the sea came up, gradually, over the lowest land there was, and closed over it. After that the kelp arrived and took over the rooms.

## Its Final Form

This place has almost finished changing. The walls are down to their final rounded ridges and will slump no further than a few more stones. The channels are set. The forest has filled every niche it can and runs the same cycle every year: dark growth on stored reserves, a burst of growth in the green season, blades lost, drifts building, stone knocking loose, brine flowing downslope. With no animals, no flow of energy moves up through a food web, and no competition pushes anything to change. Each year is one full turn, almost identical to the last.

If it is changing at all, it is only in the slow burial: kelp compressing into peat, rooms filling, the gap between wall top and floor narrowing year by year. Eventually, over a span so long it hardly matters, the honeycomb will be buried in its own forest, and the shelf will be a smooth brown plain with the walls hidden inside it.

For now the walls still show, and the water still follows the channels. In the dark, far off, the ice creaks, and a stone settles into place somewhere in the lattice.
