# The Held Water

From the rim it looks like nothing much. A plateau of wind-scoured grey sits high enough that the air is thin and tastes of iron, and in its middle lies a lake the colour of pewter, wide enough that its far shore is only a darker line under the cloud. Ice fringes the edges in brittle plates. The surface is never still. Katabatic wind comes down off the surrounding heights and drags the water into short, steep, bitter chop. There are no reeds, no moss on the stones, no lichen. Nothing green has ever grown here. The shoreline has the rounded, exhausted look of something that stopped being sharp a very long time ago.

The surface is not where this place is. The place is underneath.

---

## The Masonry

Go down through the first few metres, where the chop churns the water into a cold grey fizz, and the light thins quickly to a dusk that never finishes. Below that the water clears somewhat, though it is never clean, because it carries a permanent suspended haze of fine rock flour. And below that is the floor.

The floor is built, all of it. As far as the dim light reaches, and as far as anyone could swim in any direction, there is no natural rock at the bottom of the lake. What lies there is Masonry: grit and silt and ground stone, cemented grain by grain into walls, domes, ridges, and long low causeways. The builders are still here. They are small, about the length of a thumb, segmented and pale, somewhere between a caddis larva and a shrimp. They have no eyes worth mentioning, and two broad forelimbs are shaped almost exactly like trowels. Call them wallers.

A waller spends its life doing three things. It gathers grains from the water, secretes a grey mucilage that hardens on contact with the cold, and presses grain into mucilage onto whatever surface it clings to. Then it moves a body's length along and does it again. It never stops.

There are millions of them, possibly billions. Wherever you look closely, a surface that seemed to be stone turns out to be crawling faintly, a shimmer of pale backs moving in slow, overlapping arcs. They are too many to count and too small to single out. Look away and they become the wall again.

---

## Age

The Masonry is old in a way that is hard to hold in the mind.

Whatever the wallers once built, it is no longer the shape it was. The lake has been working on it for longer than the wallers have been working on it, and the lake is winning slowly and patiently. Towers that may once have stood tall are now smooth stumps, rounded like old teeth. Walls have slumped into broad, gentle mounds. Chambers have collapsed and been filled and re-roofed and collapsed again so many times that their interiors are laminated in thousands of thin layers. Break one open, and a mason-stumps do break, you would see bands as fine as pages, grey on grey, each one a season of cement.

Everything has the softness of deep age. There are no corners and no edges. Where the ancient structures still show a pattern, it is visible only from a distance and only in the faint light: long parallel ridges running for kilometres, all oriented the same way, like the grain in wood or the ribs of sand under a tide. Nobody drew these lines. They are the memory of the current, written into stone by creatures that build facing into the flow, generation after generation, until the whole lake floor is combed.

Between the ridges lie broad, open plains of smooth Masonry. Here the old works have been ground nearly flat, and the wallers keep a thin, living skin over the surface, patching and patching. The ridges themselves stand far apart. Swimming across the floor, you would pass a long rounded wall, then nothing for a quarter of an hour but grey expanse and haze, then another wall dimly ahead. The place is vast and sparse. Its features are spaced out like the bones of something very large lying at rest.

---

## The Water Does Not Rest

For all its age, the lake is violent.

It is fed from the north by a river that emerges from beneath the ice fields. This is not a surface stream but a subglacial torrent that bursts up through the lake floor at a place called the Throat. The meltwater is colder than the lake and heavy with rock flour, and dense water sinks. So the Throat does not fountain upward. It pours along the bottom as an avalanche of cold, silt-laden water. Great slow billows of grey roll down the long slopes of the Masonry, swallowing the ridges, scouring the plains, and tearing off anything loose.

These turbidity currents come in pulses. Some days the Throat breathes gently and the underflow is a creeping haze. On other days, after a melt-surge in the ice, the floor itself seems to boil. A wall of grey moves across the plain faster than anything could swim. It does not roar, since sound goes strange down here, but it presses: a dull, enveloping pressure you feel in your ribs before it arrives. When it passes, the landscape behind it has changed. Ridges are notched and gouged. Domes are decapitated. Whole sections of causeway lie slumped and smoothed.

Above, the wind works too. The katabatic gales pile the water against the southern shore, and when they drop the whole lake sloshes back in a long, slow seiche. Every current reverses. The haze shifts direction as though some great body had rolled over in its sleep. Internal waves ripple along the boundary between the cold deep water and the slightly warmer water above, and you can see that boundary as a shimmering, wavering ceiling where the grey changes texture.

So the water is always moving: pouring down from the north, sloshing north and south, rippling at its layers, and carrying silt that settles, lifts, and settles again. Nothing is ever finished.

---

## The Rebuilding

This is where the wallers become astonishing.

When an underflow tears through a ridge, the wallers do not flee. Many are killed, crushed or swept away into the dark. But the ones clinging to the edges of the breach begin immediately to rebuild. The same flood that destroyed the wall is carrying exactly the material they need, rock flour and grit and sand, and they pull it from the water and press it into place.

Within hours, a new skin of fresh cement lines every broken surface. Within days, the wound has started to close. You can watch it happen, if you have patience and a light. The broken end of a ridge seethes with pale bodies, packed so densely that the wall seems to be growing like a living thing, which in a sense it is.

Everywhere in the lake, at every moment, something is breaking and something is being mended. The place has the energy of an anthill that has just been kicked, sustained forever. The Masonry is never a ruin, because ruins are finished, and it is never finished either. It is an argument that has been going on since before anyone was here to hear it.

---

## Other Life

The wallers are not alone. The lake is full of animals, though you have to learn how to see them.

There is no plant life, and no light strong enough to feed any. The whole food web rests on two things. The first is the organic dust washed in from the glaciers, ancient pollen and freeze-dried microbes from ice that fell as snow ten thousand years ago. The second is the bacterial films that grow on the fresh cement of the wallers' works. These films are the only thing here that could loosely be called vegetation. They form pale, faintly slimy fur on the newer walls, grey-white, spreading in patches wherever the wallers have recently been.

Grazers move over these films. There are flat, soft, many-legged creatures the size of a hand that glide over the Masonry and leave clean, polished trails. There are clouds of tiny translucent swimmers that hang in the haze and filter it, dense enough in places to make the water glitter faintly in a beam of light. There are long, eel-like predators, colourless and nearly blind, that follow the currents and snap up whatever the underflows dislodge. After a big turbidity surge, the scavengers arrive in hundreds, drawn to the ribbon of broken bodies left in the wake.

Strangest of all are the drifters. These are large jellylike creatures, as wide as a person is tall, that ride the seiche as it sloshes the lake back and forth. They trail long fine threads through the water and seem to feed on the plankton, but they also do something no one quite understands. Where a drifter passes low over the Masonry, the wallers beneath it stop working for a moment, all at once, as though listening. Then they resume. No one knows whether the drifters are giving some signal, or taking something, or whether the wallers are only reacting to a shadow. It is the one thing here that feels less like ecology and more like a custom.

---

## Sound

It is mostly quiet down here, but not silent.

Up close to a wall, there is a fine, dry, constant clicking: the sound of countless trowel-limbs pressing grit into cement. It is like rain on dry leaves heard from far off, or someone crumpling paper in another room. Move away from the walls and it fades into a faint hiss that seems to come from everywhere.

Beneath that runs the low, continuous murmur of moving water, too deep to hear as sound. You feel it as a hum in the skull. When a turbidity current is coming, the hum changes pitch, sliding down until it is more pressure than sound.

Collapses make a noise, though not a crash. When a section of old wall finally gives way under the current, there is a long, soft grinding, and then a thud that arrives in the chest rather than the ears. Afterward, briefly, the clicking stops across a wide area, as though every waller in range paused. Then it starts again, louder than before.

The drifters make no sound. When they pass overhead, the silence under them feels deeper than the silence elsewhere.

---

## The South Wall

It took the longest to understand the whole of the lake, because the answer is so large.

The lake drains to the south, but not over any spillway or through any river. The plateau is riddled with ancient solution channels, and water leaks down into the mountain through the rock, which is not rock at all. The entire southern end of the lake, from the lake floor up to within a few metres of the surface, is a single structure: the South Wall.

It is Masonry, like everything else, and older than anything else. It is a dam kilometres long and hundreds of metres high, built grain by grain by uncountable generations of wallers. It is so old and so eroded that from the surface it is indistinguishable from a natural shoreline, a long low grey bank sloping into the water.

The lake exists because the South Wall exists. Without it, the water would drain away down into the mountain and the plateau would be a dry basin of grey dust. The wallers built the lake that they live in.

The South Wall is always leaking. The water presses against it with the whole weight of the lake, and seeps through, and finds every flaw. Along the entire length of the wall the water trickles, streams, and in places pours into dark cracks. And along the entire length of the wall the wallers are packed more densely than anywhere else in the lake, a pale seething crust sometimes several bodies deep, patching the leaks as fast as the water finds them.

This is why the lake is turbulent. Some of the current is the Throat pouring in from the north, and some is the wind. But underneath all of it is the slow, relentless pull toward the South Wall. The whole lake is trying to leave, and the whole floor is a machine, built by creatures with no tools and no plan, for keeping it here.

The wallers do not know this. They cannot know it. They only feel water moving against their bodies and press grit into the place where it flows. But from above, if you could see the whole lake at once, it would look like a held breath: an enormous cold weight of water cupped in a pair of grey hands, and the hands constantly, silently, forever closing their fingers against the leaking.

---

## At the Surface Again

Come back up through the haze and into the chop. The wind is still coming off the heights, the ice plates are still grinding at the edges, and the lake is still a sheet of pewter under low cloud.

Nothing from up here gives any hint of the ridges combed across the floor, the boiling underflows, the drifters riding the slosh, or the endless clicking of mending. The shore is grey and rounded and old. The water is grey and cold and restless.

But now you know that the stone under your boots is not stone, and the shore you stand on was made. And somewhere below you, at this moment, a wall is breaking and being closed again, and has been, and will be, for as long as the water wants to go and the small pale builders keep refusing to let it.
