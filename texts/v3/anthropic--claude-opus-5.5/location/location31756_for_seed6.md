# The Fourth Condenser Hall

## Approach

It sits at the top of a stepped concrete stack, above the level where clouds usually form, so the first thing you see is a long glass roof floating over a white floor of weather. The roof is ridged like a greenhouse, iron mullions painted a factory green that has not yet begun to flake. Under the glass there is no room. There is water, filled to within an arm's length of the panes, and the water is so full of things that from above it looks less like a tank than a single dense green object pressed against a window.

The hall is small, perhaps forty paces long and fifteen wide. You could walk its perimeter along the gantry in a couple of minutes. What makes it feel large is how much is inside it.

## The Air Gap

Between the water's surface and the glass there is a narrow band of air, warm and wet and still. Condensation beads on the underside of the panes, gathers along the mullions, and falls back in slow single drops, each one striking the surface with a small tick that can be heard the length of the hall. This is almost the only sound. It rains here, in a way, but the rain is the hall breathing on its own ceiling and taking the breath back.

The air smells of hot iron and cut stems, the smell of a greenhouse built inside an engine room.

## Below the Surface

Put your face into the water and it is warm, close to blood heat, and perfectly clear. The clarity is the first strange thing. Nothing floats in it: no silt, no specks, no drifting bits of leaf. It is water that has been made and kept, never visited. Daylight comes down through the glass and the surface without scattering and lays itself across everything in bright green-gold panels. You can see to the far wall, or you could if anything along the way let you.

Nothing does. The hall is packed floor to surface. Great horizontal cylinders lie in rows like boilers or condensers, their riveted skins still sharp at the seams. Between and above them runs a lattice of pipework: copper, cast iron, thin bundles of brass tubing, junction boxes, valve wheels, pressure gauges with their needles resting. Drive shafts cross the room at three heights, carried on bearings bolted to cast-iron columns. Belts loop from pulleys to flywheels. There is no open water anywhere. Every gap a hand could fit into is taken, and most of what takes it is green.

## The Growth

The vegetation grows out of the machinery. It does not cling to it or root in it. It comes out of it.

Along the flanks of the big cylinders are rows of small nozzles, brass-lipped, like the spinnerets of a textile mill. From each one a ribbon of leaf emerges: flat, bright, translucent green, a hand's width across, veined in a fine regular grid. The ribbons rise and sway very slightly in the convection off the warm cylinders. They branch where the grid of veins tells them to. They grow long enough to reach the glass, then fold and spread along the underside of the surface in a canopy, then turn down again and weave themselves through the pipework on the way back to the floor.

There are other kinds. Tall stems come up in segments from floor sockets, each segment ending in a crisp node, a joint that looks botanical from a distance and turns out on inspection to be a machined collar. Rosettes sprout from the valve wheels, their leaves arranged at exact angles, as if cut from a template. Fine filaments, almost hair, bloom from the junction boxes and fill the smaller gaps with soft green haze. In the spaces behind the gauges, something like moss grows in a dense regular felt with the texture of worsted.

Up close, the leaves are faintly printed. Along the midrib of every ribbon runs a line of tiny marks, pale and regular, like the lot numbers stamped on sacking. They are not legible as any alphabet. They repeat, but not exactly. Two ribbons from neighbouring nozzles carry nearly the same sequence, drifting apart as the ribbons grow, the way two copies of a song drift apart when sung from memory.

## Newness

Everything in the hall is new. That is the second strange thing, and the longer you look, the stranger it gets.

The rivet heads are bright. Brass is gold, not brown. Paint lines on the cylinders are crisp. The cast-iron flanges still carry their mould flash, burrs sharp enough to open a thumb. Nothing is worn smooth. Nothing is pitted. The leaves have no torn edges, no brown tips, no holes. There is no decay anywhere in the water, because there is nothing here to cause it. No fish, no snails, no bacterial film, no creature of any size. Nothing eats. Nothing rots. The water holds no life of its own except what the machinery makes.

The newness is not because the hall was recently finished. It is because the hall is not finished. It is still being made.

Watch a single place long enough, which here means days, and you see it. At the tip of a copper pipe, a fresh collar of metal appears, pale pink and unoxidised. The pipe is a finger's width longer than it was. A new rivet sits in a seam that had none. A flange has budded from the side of a manifold, and from the flange, a week later, a short stub of pipe, and from the stub a nozzle, and from the nozzle the first curl of green. The building grows the way its leaves grow: from tips, from nodes, in segments, according to a grid it seems to carry inside itself.

After a while you can no longer reliably tell pipe from stem. A segmented copper tube with collars at regular intervals and a green shoot at its end: is that plumbing or bamboo? A ribbon of leaf hardening along one edge into something with the sheen of sheet metal: is that foliage or ducting? The hall has two products and is gradually forgetting which is which.

## The Engine

At the east end, half hidden by canopy, is the flywheel. It is taller than a person, cast in one piece, its spokes thick as thighs. It turns. You have to take a bearing on a spoke against a column and come back later to be sure, because it makes about one revolution per day. Its motion goes out along the shafts, through the belts, into the cylinders, and the cylinders stay warm, and the nozzles keep extruding, and the building keeps budding.

Nobody knows what drives the flywheel. No furnace is visible, no inlet, no cable coming in from outside the stack. The warmth of the water may drive it, or it may drive the warmth; the relationship could run either way, or both. The gauges show pressure, though the needles move so slowly that each one seems painted in place. One of them, near the flywheel, has a hand-lettered card tucked behind its glass: NOT ABOVE THE RED. The needle sits a hair's width below the red line, where it has sat for as long as anyone has looked. Somewhere under the glass, very slowly, it is climbing.

## Sound

Underwater the hall is nearly silent. Warm, still water deadens everything. Press your ear to a cylinder and you hear, at long intervals, a single deep tock, the flywheel's motion passing through some escapement or ratchet. You feel it more than hear it. Between the tocks there is a faint high singing in the pipework, water moving through narrow bore at walking pace. Now and then comes a very small crisp sound, like a fingernail on paper: a new leaf uncreasing, or a new rivet seating itself.

Above the surface there are only the drops falling from the glass. It is a place with the loudness turned almost all the way down, an industrial hall running at the volume of a library.

## Rules of the Place

Some things hold true here and seem to be law:

- **Nothing is removed.** Cut a ribbon of leaf and within a day the nozzle has pushed out a replacement. The cut piece does not decay. It sinks slowly and lodges somewhere in the pipework, where it will still be green and whole a year later. Over time the lowest levels of the hall have filled with a soft, pressed, perfect sediment of cuttings, packed like paper in a press.
- **Nothing is empty.** Any space large enough to hold something will, in time, hold something. If you clear an area, it closes over at the pace of the flywheel.
- **Everything is young.** Old things are not preserved; they are overgrown by newer versions of themselves. The original iron of the hall is surely still in there, deep inside the lattice, but every surface you can see is recent.
- **The level rises.** The water gets closer to the glass by the thickness of a leaf each year, as the volume of made things displaces it. The gauges are climbing toward their red lines at about the same pace. What happens when the water meets the glass, when the air gap closes and the rain stops, nothing in the hall says.

## Light

The light is ordinary daylight, which is itself strange given everything else. Mornings come in low and gold through the eastern panes and turn the canopy into stained glass. Midday is bright green and even, with sharp shadows under the shafts. Evening reddens the brass. Clouds pass below the stack, not above it, so the light is almost never interrupted. It falls into the hall steadily, day after day, and the hall takes it in and grows.

## Standing at the Gantry

From the narrow iron walkway along the west wall, just above the water, you can see the whole place at once: forty paces of green-gold density, glass overhead, cloud far below, the flywheel dark at the far end. Nothing in it is alive in the way animals are alive, and nothing in it is dead. It is a factory that has turned into a garden without stopping being a factory, a garden with no gardener and no pests, which makes more of itself, very quietly, and more of the green that grows from it, until the two have nearly converged.

A drop falls from the glass. Somewhere in the pipes, the flywheel ticks once. A nozzle you had not noticed yesterday has opened, and a curl of new green, stamped with its faint lot number, is beginning to rise.
