# The Masonry Under the Warm Dark

It begins with pressure, and with warmth. The water here is warm like the inside of a mouth. It is thick, faintly mineral, still enough that a hand held out would feel no current, only a slow, even heat on every side. There is no surface you could swim up to in any useful time. Above is only more water, and above that more still, until the idea of "up" stops mattering.

There is no light. There is almost no light. The eye adjusts for a long time and finds, eventually, a few things: a faint grey-green smear where bacteria have filmed a rock, a pinprick drifting past that is some animal's lure or lantern, the dim blue flare of disturbed plankton where something has turned sharply. The darkness is not total, and that is worse, or better. It has texture. The place shows itself in fragments, a lit edge, a moving spark, and the rest you build from touch, from the pressure-shadows of things you nearly brush against.

What you nearly brush against, again and again, are walls.

---

## The Basin

The land here, if it can be called land, is a wide, low depression, a sunken plain far below any shoreline. It is the bottom of an old inland sea that the world has been slowly lowering for a very long time. The floor is soft. It is made of silt so fine it hangs in the water for an hour after being disturbed. Beneath the silt is a harder bed of pale, pitted stone, and beneath that, somewhere, heat.

The warmth comes up through the floor in a thousand small places. There are no roaring black smokers, no violent vents. There are seeps: slow, shimmering exhalations where warmer water rises through cracks. Around each seep the silt is stained rust-orange and sulfur-yellow, and the water wavers like air over a summer road. The seeps breathe. Over a span of days each one swells and fades, swells and fades, and the whole basin has a faint tidal pulse of heat that has nothing to do with the moon.

This is what makes everything else possible.

---

## The Builders

No hand made this city, and no tool touched it. Every stone in it was carried in a mouth.

The builders are fish, broad-headed and heavy-jawed, about the length of a forearm. They are dull bronze where the faint light catches them, and their eyes are large and filmed silver. In the dark they find each other by the lateral lines along their flanks, by pressure, and by a low vocabulary of clicks made by grinding their pharyngeal teeth. They are related, distantly, to the bower-building fish of warm lakes, the kind that heap sand into craters and cones to court a mate. Here that impulse has had an unimaginable span of time to compound.

A male builder spends his life carrying things. He carries shells, mostly: the empty coils of the grazing snails that crawl everywhere on the seep-films. He also carries pebbles of the pale basin stone, chips of older walls that have fallen, and the calcified tubes of dead worms. He sets each piece with a small twisting push of the jaw, then presses it in place and holds it. From his gills and mouth he exudes a mucus that, in this mineral-heavy water, slowly hardens into a chalky mortar. One fish lays perhaps a few hundred stones in a season. There are a great many fish, and there have been a great many seasons.

---

## The City

It is urban in every sense that matters: organized, layered, dense, and sprawling past what any one sense can hold.

The city fills the basin floor for what might be miles. It is hard to know, because nothing can see more than an arm's length ahead. It is laid out, loosely, around the seeps. Every seep is a hearth, and every hearth has a district.

**The towers** rise in the warm zones directly above the seeps. They are hollow cones and chimneys of mortared shell, some barely the height of a person and some five or six times that. The warm water rises through them and draws a slow draft up their throats, so every tower hums faintly with convection. Female builders lay their eggs inside, on ledges where the temperature is exactly right. The tallest towers are ancient. Their lower courses are made of shells from snail species that no longer exist in the basin, pale spiraled forms with ridges and spines. Each generation of builders adds to the top and never takes from the bottom. A tower is a column of time. You could read it, if you had light and patience: coarse bands where a season was poor and the builders used gravel, and fine, pearlescent bands where the snails were plentiful.

**The streets** are not deliberate. They are the spaces the fish leave between their holdings, but they have become fixed by use. Every territory is walled with low courses of stone. The walls follow the boundaries that males once fought over and later simply inherited. Where two walls run parallel, a lane forms. Where many lanes cross, there is a small open plaza of swept silt. Over thousands of years the lanes have deepened, because the constant passage of fins keeps the silt moving and the floor slowly scours away. Now some of the oldest streets are sunken channels, a body's depth below the city around them, their walls grown tall on either side. Swimming down one is like walking a canyon of stacked shell, close enough on both sides to touch.

**The old quarters** lie where seeps have died. A seep can go cold over a few centuries. When it does, the builders drift away to warmer hearths, and the district they leave behind begins to silt in. You can find these places by the chill in the water and by the silence. The towers stand hollow and dark, their mortar softened and furred with pale bacterial film. Some have slumped and leaned into their neighbors, and some have fallen entirely, spilling a fan of shells across the lanes. Silt fills the lower streets until only the tops of walls show, like the rims of buried rooms. The city is not only built. It is also sinking, crumbling, and being buried, and the old quarters are where you see that it has always been doing this. The living city stands on top of an older city, which stands on an older one still. Dig anywhere through the silt and you find mortared shell.

## The Other Inhabitants

The builders are the architects, but they are far from alone. A city makes niches, and every niche has filled.

**The grazers** are the snails, first: countless small coiled mollusks, creeping across the bacterial films on every warm wall. They are the city's economy. They eat the films, and the builders eat the snails and use their shells. On the warmest walls they are so dense that a hand pressed to the stone would come away covered. When a seep swells to the top of its cycle, the films bloom and the snails spawn, and the water fills briefly with their drifting larvae, a haze that sparks blue when anything moves through it.

**The lodgers** have taken up in the walls. Tiny pale shrimp live in the gaps between stones, their antennae trembling out into the lanes. Worms have bored into the older courses of mortar and built their own small tubes out from it, so the ancient towers bristle with them. Small gobies with enormous eyes perch in doorways the builders never meant to make, holes where a stone fell out and was never replaced, and dart out at anything the size of a grain of rice.

**The hunters** move through the streets. Long, slender eels, the color of the dark itself, thread the sunken lanes as if the city were made for them. It nearly was. The deep narrow channels are perfect hunting runs. An eel lies still in a gap in a wall with only its head showing, and it waits for a builder heavy with a stone to come past. The builders have learned to travel the lanes in loose groups, clicking. You can hear it: a traveling group of clicks moving down a channel, and then one sharp, broken-off click, and then quiet.

There are larger things in the basin, too, out past the edges of the city in the open silt. Nothing in the city talks about them, but the builders all turn and freeze at the same moment, and sometimes a long slow pressure-wave rolls down the streets, the displacement of something very big passing overhead.

**The lights.** The dark is not empty of light, only poor in it. Some of the lodger-shrimp glow faintly at the tips of their antennae. A drifting jelly-like creature, no larger than a thumbnail, pulses a slow cold green as it rides the convection up the throat of a tower and spills out the top. Up there, in the warm rising plume above the busiest hearths, these small lights gather in their hundreds. Seen from the streets, if you could see, each tower would wear a faint, shifting halo. It is the brightest thing in the basin, and it is barely brighter than nothing.

---

## Vegetation

There are almost no plants here, because there is no sun. What stands in for them is pale and sparse.

Around the seeps grow bacterial mats: white, rust, and sulfur-yellow, soft as felt and thin as paper. In the hottest cracks there are stranger growths, stringy filaments that stream upward in the rising water like hair, and pale lumpy colonies that the snails will not touch. At the margins of a few seeps, where the warm water mixes with cold, there are small fields of something like weed: long, colorless, translucent ribbons anchored in the silt. They feed on the chemistry of the water rather than on light. They are rare enough that the builders treat them as landmarks, and they never build over them.

---

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. That is the first thing and the last thing.

Water carries sound well, but there is little here to make it. The seeps make none. The convection through the towers makes only a faint, subsonic breathing, more felt in the chest than heard. The loudest sustained sound in the city is the builders' clicking, and it is small, dry, and intermittent: the tick of teeth, then the softer tock of a stone pressed into mortar. In a busy district at the peak of a building season it becomes a crackle, like light rain on a roof. In the old quarters there is nothing at all.

Now and then, and not often, there is a longer sound. A tower settles. Deep in its old lower courses something gives way, and there is a groan and a slow pattering as shells cascade inward. Then everything in the district goes still. The clicking stops. For a long while no one moves. Then, one by one, the builders start again.

---

## Cycles and Rules

The city lives by rhythms that have nothing to do with day and night, because there are none.

**The seep-breath** is the shortest cycle. Each hearth warms and cools over several days. At the warm peak the films bloom, the snails feed, the shrimp swarm, and the builders work frantically. At the cool trough everything slows, the clicking falls away, and the eels grow bold. The districts do not breathe in unison, so somewhere in the city it is always a busy time and somewhere it is always a quiet one.

**The building season** is longer. Some deep slow change in the basin's chemistry, over a span like a year, brings the builders into breeding color. That means a faint iridescence visible only by the scattered light of the jelly-lanterns. It is when the most building happens. Males compete by the height and finish of their towers. A tower laid with fine, well-matched, pearlescent shell is more attractive than a rough one. Old towers are prized over new, because a male who inherits a tall ancient chimney has only to add a few good courses to its top. This is why the towers are never torn down: an old tower is worth more than any number of new ones.

**The drift of hearths** is the longest cycle. Over centuries, seeps fade and new ones open. The city moves with them, slowly, like a shadow following a sun that no one has ever seen. The living districts creep across the basin, leaving the dead quarters behind to silt in and become the foundation for whatever comes next.

There are rules that no one wrote down, because no one here writes:

- Nothing is ever removed from a wall except by collapse.
- Fallen stones may be reused, and nearly always are. The newest towers are often built from the rubble of the oldest, so ancient shells climb back up into the warm water.
- The weed-fields are not built over.
- When a builder dies, its body is taken by the eels, the shrimp, the snails. Its skull and the hard plates of its jaw are eventually found by another builder and set into a wall like any other stone. The towers contain their own builders, generation upon generation of them, mortared in among the shells.

---

## The Whole of It

Drift slowly above the city, if you can. Imagine the lights of the towers spread out below you in the dark: faint green halos over the hearths, clustered thick where the seeps are strong and thinning out into black where the old quarters lie cold. Between them, unseen, run the deep lanes where the eels wait. Beneath everything lie the buried cities, and beneath those the stone, and beneath that the patient heat that has fed all of this for longer than there have been builders.

It is warm, and dark, and nearly silent, and it is full. Everywhere, in the walls and the lanes and the rising water, something is living, eating, hiding, or carrying a single stone to set on top of all the others. It is a city with no hands and no tools, no light and no memory except what is laid down in shell. It has been building itself for so long that it no longer seems to be built at all. It seems to have grown, or settled, the way silt settles: layer on layer, slow and certain, in its own warm dark.
