# The Kettle Shelf

## First, the Ground

The land is new, and it is still arriving.

It begins as a plateau so broad that its far edges fall below the curve of the world. From anywhere on it you see only more of it, rising in long shelves and broken terraces toward a center no one has reached. The rock is basalt, still black and so young that it hasn't dulled. It breaks in clean planes and hexagonal columns, and it snaps into flakes thin enough to cut. Nothing here has been rounded yet. Ridges keep their knife-edges, and fallen slabs lie where they cracked, their faces bright as split coal. Wind and frost have hardly begun their work. In places the stone still shows the ropy wrinkle of the flow that made it, frozen mid-pour, as if the lava stopped a moment ago to listen.

The whole shelf is rising. This is too slow to see and too large to doubt. Somewhere under the continent a pressure pushes the plateau upward about a fingerbreadth a year. The creaking this produces comes as deep tectonic groans that arrive every few days and roll across the land like far-off artillery. After each one, new fractures open across the terraces, only a hand's width apart and hair-thin at first. They widen over decades into crevasses, and those crevasses become the channels everything else depends on.

The air is thin, cold, and very dry, except where it isn't.

## The Water That Comes Up Instead of Down

It almost never rains on the Kettle Shelf. The water comes from below.

The fractures breathe. The rock beneath is still hot, and from ten thousand cracks rises a warm, mineral-heavy vapor that smells of struck flint and pennies. When it meets the freezing air it condenses at once. Fog pours out of each crevasse like milk and settles low over the stone. Where it touches anything, it freezes into rime. The ground is furred with it: frost feathers long as a forearm, all pointing into the wind, growing a little each night and shedding in the brief afternoon warmth.

So the land is damp without being wet. There are no lakes, and no rivers except thin meltwater threads that run for a few hours at midday in the bottoms of the crevasses before freezing again. The moisture lives in the air, in the rime, and in the dense living carpet that has learned to drink fog. Water here is a condition of the atmosphere, and everything alive holds it close.

## The Light

The sun here does not rise and set. At this latitude, in this long season, it circles the horizon. It stays low and enormous, swollen and white-gold, and completes one slow rotation around the sky over each long day. The shadows are immense. A boulder casts a shadow a quarter-mile long, and the shadow swings around it like a clock hand.

It is still overwhelmingly bright. The thin air hardly softens the sunlight, and the land throws it back from everything. The rime fields glitter in drifts miles wide. The fresh-broken rock faces shine like mirrors. The fog banks blaze where the sun catches their tops. And the vegetation itself carries light, as I'll describe. Looking toward the sun is like looking into molten metal. Looking away from it is not much better. Sun-dogs hang beside it most of the time, and halos ring it in triple arcs, because the air is full of ice crystals.

Nothing here has true darkness. Even in the deepest crevasse shadows, the rime catches reflected glare and holds a blue-white glow, like light trapped in the bottom of a glass.

## The Carpet

Nothing tall grows on the Kettle Shelf. Yet it is lush, densely and almost indecently so, because everything that grows stays low and grows together.

The main growth is a mat people might call *coppermoss*, if people were here. It covers nearly every surface the rime doesn't claim, and often grows through the rime. It is dense and cushioned, from ankle-deep to waist-deep, and packed so tightly that you could not push a finger into it. Its colors are unexpected: verdigris green, oxidized blue, the orange of fresh copper, and deep plum-bronze where it is oldest. The coppermoss mines. Its rootlets hold acids that dissolve the young basalt, and it draws out iron, copper, and manganese, concentrating them in its tissues until the mat is partly metal. Tear a clump free and it rings faintly. Burn it, and it leaves a bead of slag.

Inside the coppermoss grow a hundred other things, each crowding the others. There are cushion plants like green bread loaves, each one hundreds of years old and only knee-high. There are frost-lilies with translucent petals that are often mistaken for rime until they move. There are fern-analogues whose fronds uncoil in spirals and stay half-coiled for years. There are lichens in concentric rings that cover whole cliff faces in targets of rust and cream. Along the fog-breathing fractures the growth is thickest of all. It spills over the crevasse lips in tiered, dripping curtains, fed by the warm mineral vapor. These are the richest places on the Shelf, green-gold terraces stepping down into white fog.

The carpet leaves no gaps. Every square inch of it is contested and occupied.

## The Current

This is where the Kettle Shelf stops resembling anywhere else.

The coppermoss is conductive. Its metal-rich filaments reach across the plateau in a single continuous web, possibly one organism and possibly millions touching. The friction of ceaseless wind, the thermal gradient between hot rock and freezing air, and some slow biological process all build charge in it. The whole mat carries current. The plateau is electrified, gently everywhere and violently in places.

You feel it before you understand it. Your hair lifts. A tingle rises through the soles of your feet. Tiny sparks jump from the frost-lilies to anything that passes them. At dusk, which here is only the stretch when the sun circles lowest, the charge becomes visible. Pale violet St. Elmo's fire runs along the ridgelines, and ball-lightning drifts over the fog banks and pops. Discharges crawl across the moss in branching lines of blue light that last a second, then fade, then return in the same shape.

Where the charge concentrates, usually at the tips of basalt spires or the lips of the largest fissures, it grounds itself in sustained arcs. These are standing columns of electric light, several feet tall. They crackle continuously for days or weeks, fuse the rock beneath them into green glass, and fill the air with ozone. They are the plateau's hearths. Life gathers around them.

The Kettle Shelf is an industrial landscape with no industry, because the ecology itself does the work industry would do. It refines and conducts and produces. Its mills and foundries are alive.

## The Animals

There are too many of them to count.

The first thing a visitor would notice is the **herd**. It is a single migratory mass of grazers whose edges nobody has seen at the same time. The animals are about ox-sized and low-built, with long shaggy coats like rusted wire and broad shovel-faces for scraping coppermoss off the rock. Their breath steams in huge white plumes. Their digestion is essentially smelting: the metal in the moss is too much to excrete cleanly, so they concentrate it. They pass a heavy, glittering, iron-rich dung that cools into slag cakes. After centuries, the herd's migration routes are paved with this slag. These are dark, gleaming roads a mile wide, the only surfaces on the Shelf that look manufactured, though no one made them.

The herd moves slowly, and it takes most of a year to cross any given terrace. Its noise is tremendous. Millions of hooves strike fresh basalt, a sound like rain on sheet metal played at the volume of a waterfall. Millions of animals exhale at once in a deep, rhythmic chuffing that reads, from a distance, as a single enormous engine idling. Their calls are low and brassy. They sound like foghorns or like steam whistles an octave down.

Around the herd, and everywhere else, are the smaller lives, and it is the smaller lives that make the fauna overwhelming.

**Wirebeetles** cover the moss so densely in some seasons that the ground shimmers. They are thumb-long and metallic, and they feed on the electrical charge itself. They clamp their mandibles onto the moss-filaments and draw current, and their shells glow faintly with what they've swallowed. When disturbed they discharge in unison, a sheet of sparks rolling outward like a shockwave. They gather by the billions at the standing arcs, where they build towering, glittering mounds of fused metal and chitin. These mounds are nearly the only structures on the plateau, and they hum like transformers.

**Frostmoths** rise from the fog in clouds that blot out the circling sun. They have pale, dusty wings and phosphorescent bodies. Their mass emergences take days, and the air becomes a slow, soft blizzard of wings that settle on everything and freeze there, so that a whole slope may turn white and feathered and faintly glowing.

**Anvil-birds** are heavy and black, with thick hooked beaks. They crack wirebeetle shells on basalt spires, and they do it constantly and rhythmically. Colonies of thousands hammer at once, filling the cold air with a metallic clatter like a forge-hall working at full shift. The sound carries for miles and never quite stops.

**Crevasse-eels** are long, pale, blind animals that live in the warm, dripping moss-curtains of the fractures. They swim through the fog, which is dense enough and wet enough that they can almost swim through air. They hunt by electroreception, reading the currents in the moss like a map. Now and then they surface into the open air above the fissures, coil once in the sunlight like a living filament, and drop back into the white.

Then there are the **engines**, which is the only word for them. They are rare, and nobody knows whether they are one species or several. They are huge and slow, larger than the grazers, armored in plates of their own concentrated metal, and they move with a grinding, pistoning gait. They lie on the standing arcs as a cat lies in sun, absorbing the current for days. Then they rise and walk, radiating heat so intense that the rime evaporates in a wide circle around them and the moss blackens underfoot. Wherever they go they leave warm ground behind, and the growth that returns there comes back richer. When one of them calls, the sound is a long mechanical drone, like a turbine spinning up across an entire valley. The herd goes silent for as long as it lasts.

## The Density

Nothing here is alone. The Kettle Shelf is full in a way that is hard to convey.

Every surface is covered. Every niche is taken. The moss grows on the rock, the lichen grows on the moss, the beetles crawl on the lichen, and the moths settle on the beetles. Birds perch on the grazers' backs, eels coil in the fog beneath them, and parasites swarm in the eels. The air is full of wings and spores and ice crystals and sparks. When the herd passes it doesn't clear the land. It churns it, then the carpet flows back in behind it within days. Standing still for an hour, a visitor would be colonized. Frost would grow on their shoulders, moths would settle in their hair, and wirebeetles would climb their boots, attracted to the static of their body.

It should feel suffocating, and it doesn't quite. The plateau is too vast for that. The crowding is intense up close, and from a height it looks like texture. The whole continent-sized shelf reads like a single living surface, dense as fur and glittering, humming under the low circling sun.

## The Sound, All Together

The Kettle Shelf is never quiet.

Beneath everything is the electric hum of the moss-web, a steady low tone near the edge of hearing, felt in the teeth. Over it are the crackle of the standing arcs, the chuffing roar of the herd like engines idling, and the forge-clatter of the anvil-birds. Frost feathers break underfoot with high glassy tinkles. The fog makes its soft hiss where it pours from the fractures. Now and then the tectonic groan lands under all of it, so deep it is more pressure than sound, and for a moment every animal on the plateau goes still before the noise resumes.

It sounds, more than anything, like a factory district heard from a hill, a great mechanized city at work, except that there is no city. There are only rock and fog and moss and animals, all running, all producing, all loud.

## The Rules

Some things seem to be true here.

**Metal moves upward.** The young basalt holds metal loosely, and the moss draws it out and concentrates it. The grazers eat the moss and concentrate it again, and the beetles and birds and engines concentrate it further. Over time the plateau's metal rises through the food web and is laid back down on the surface in slag roads, beetle mounds, and the armor of engines. The Shelf is refining itself.

**Current is a nutrient.** Life grows thickest where the charge runs strongest. Lightning-struck ground turns green within weeks. The standing arcs are the plateau's oases, and life crowds toward electricity the way it crowds toward water elsewhere.

**Warmth comes from below and from within.** No heat arrives from the sky. The sun gives only light. Every bit of warmth on the Shelf comes up through the fractures or out of the bodies of animals. To be warm, you stand near a vent or near a beast.

**Everything is slow except the sparks.** The plateau rises by a fingerbreadth a year and the herd takes a year to cross it. Cushion plants grow a finger's width in a lifetime, and the sun takes all day to circle once. Yet across this patient landscape, discharges flicker constantly, in fractions of a second. Two clocks run at once here: one of centuries and one of instants.

## At the Edge

At the Shelf's outer margins, where the terraces break off and drop in columnar cliffs thousands of feet toward the cloud-sea below, the whole system spills over. Fog pours off the rim in slow white cataracts. Moss curtains hang from the cliff faces in bronze-green sheets hundreds of feet long, crackling with current. At the brink, the herds turn back along the edge in endless slow procession, their calls echoing off the cliffs.

Below, weather gathers. The plateau is large enough to make its own. The heat rising from its fractures and the cold falling from its heights churn up storms that circle its base. The storms are charged, and they throw lightning upward into the moss-web, feeding it. The Shelf and its storms make one engine that runs the other.

From the rim, looking back inland, you would see an endless rising land. It is black-edged and verdigris-furred, dusted white with rime and moths, glittering under a sun that will not set. It is loud with animals and with electricity, and it is full to its edges and still growing. It holds nothing anyone built, and it is working as hard as anything ever built.
