# The Terraces Above the Cloud Line

## The Ground

The land is new, and it looks it. Some centuries ago, which is recent for rock, a long fissure opened across the shoulder of a mountain range. Lava poured out, cooled, and cracked into a high plateau of black basalt about a day's walk across, tilted slightly to the west. Weather has barely touched it. The edges of the flows are still sharp enough to cut a palm. The columns have not rounded. Where the lava folded over itself as it cooled, the ropes and wrinkles of its skin remain, crisp as the day it set. Nothing has been ground to sand. No gravel fans spread at the cliff feet, no soft slopes of scree. The ground breaks cleanly or not at all.

At the plateau's eastern edge the rock drops in a single sheer wall, several hundred meters of stacked hexagonal columns like organ pipes. Below that wall lies cloud, not sky.

## The Cloud

The plateau sits just above a standing layer of cloud that fills the lowlands in every direction. It is not fog that comes and goes. It is a sea, level and white, with its surface a few dozen meters below the eastern rim and lapping higher against the western slopes where the tilt brings the land down to meet it. It does not seem to move. From the edge you can watch it for an hour and see no swell or tearing, only a slow, faint breathing at its margins, where it touches the rock and condenses.

The water on the plateau comes from that condensation. It almost never rains here in the ordinary sense. The warm, saturated air rises out of the cloud-sea, meets the cooler black stone, and gives up its moisture. Every surface weeps. The columns along the rim run with thin sheets of water, down every face, so the whole eastern cliff has a dark, lacquered shine. Where the joints between columns meet, droplets gather and fall in beads. Hollows in the flow-tops hold shallow pools, clear to the bottom, some no bigger than a basin and some wide enough to wade. Nothing drains them fast, because the young rock is unfractured and nearly watertight. Nothing dries them either, because the air is always at the edge of saturation. The pools stay as they are.

The temperature matches the water. It is warm, but mildly: a steady, humid mildness that doesn't change from morning to night by more than a few degrees. The cloud below works like a blanket. Heat rises from the lowlands, the stone holds some of it, and the air settles into a permanent early-summer afternoon that has never had to recover from a storm.

## What Grows

Life here is green but not crowded. Young basalt has no soil, so every plant has had to find its own foothold, and the plateau shows exactly where the footholds are.

Moss came first and is everywhere the rock holds a film of water. It forms thick, spongy cushions in shades from nearly black-green to a vivid yellow-lime. Behind the moss came ferns, clustered wherever a crack or a pool edge has caught enough organic matter to root in. There are small ferns that hang in fringes from the dripping column-faces, and larger ones, some shoulder height, their fronds unrolling from tight coils and holding beads of condensation along every edge.

Trees are rarer. There are a few kinds, low and broad, with leathery leaves and roots that run across the surface of the stone like poured wax before they find a fissure and dive. They grow singly or in small stands of three or four, far apart, each one an event in the landscape. Between them are long open stretches of bare rock, wet and shining, with only the moss marking the drainage lines. Seen from a height, the vegetation traces the plateau's water in green: the rims of pools, the edges of flows, the channels where the weeping cliffs drain. The rest is black stone and sky.

Some of the trees have flowers, pale and waxy and scentless, and they seem to stay open indefinitely. Nothing comes to them.

## The Absence

There are no animals. No birds cross the plateau or nest in the columns. No insects move in the moss, over the pools, or around the flowers. The pools hold no larvae or tadpoles, nothing that darts or wriggles. Perhaps the plateau is too young and too isolated, a stone island above a cloud-sea that nothing winged has ever crossed. Perhaps something else explains it. Either way, the place is empty of every motion but water and the people who live here.

That absence is what makes the quiet so complete.

## The Sound

Mostly there is none. No wind to speak of; the air rests on the cloud and barely stirs. No birdsong, no insect hum, no rustle in the ferns. What remains is water, and only the gentlest kinds: drips from the column-faces, each one distinct, falling into pools at irregular, unhurried intervals. Somewhere a thin runnel slips over a lip of rock with a sound like breath through the teeth. Standing still, you can pick out single drops fifty paces away.

Human sounds carry and then die quickly into the stone and moss. A voice sounds loud. A footstep on bare basalt rings faintly, a slight glassy note, because the young rock is still dense and hard and hasn't been softened by weathering.

## The Town

There is a town here, spread across most of the plateau, though at first you may not see it as one.

It is built of the plateau's own basalt, and the builders have worked with the columns. Where the flows cooled into hexagonal pillars, they found stone that is already shaped. The columns split along natural joints into long prisms, and the builders have quarried them with wedges and levers, stone hammers and simple bronze chisels, and stacked them into walls, platforms, and stairs. There is no mortar. The hexagons fit well enough against one another that little is needed, and the constant damp keeps everything close and snug. The town is laid out in terraces that follow the plateau's gentle westward tilt, each level a broad platform of fitted stone, stepped down to the next with wide, shallow stairs.

It is a real town, organized and deliberate, with streets and channels and the platforms of what may be public buildings. But it is spacious to the point of emptiness. Houses stand far apart, each on its own terrace or at the edge of its own pool, with long stretches of bare rock and moss between them. The streets are wide paths of laid columns running between widely spaced structures, and they may run for a long time without passing a doorway. It feels less like a crowded city than a city drawn at a larger scale, as if the plateau had been measured and divided generously, with room left for every building to stand alone.

The houses are low, thick-walled, and roofed with overlapping slabs of split basalt, laid at a pitch so condensation runs off into carved gutters. The gutters are the core of the town's design. Everything here is shaped around water: catching it, moving it, holding it. Channels cut into the stone run the length of the streets, carrying the drip from the rooftops and the weeping walls down from terrace to terrace. They fill cisterns built from hollowed-out sections of flow, and from the cisterns they run on to the next level, all the way down to the western edge, where the town's lowest terraces meet the cloud and the water slips over the rim and back into the white.

The people are few, and they move slowly and quietly. You see one sitting at the edge of a pool, one crossing a far terrace, one mending a channel with a chisel. Their tools are simple: hand-shaped stone, a little bronze, woven fiber from the tougher ferns, wooden handles from the rare trees. Nothing is powered and nothing is loud. They work stone by hand and they are unhurried. There is no reason to rush on a plateau where nothing changes.

## The Stillness

Nothing changes, and that is the deepest fact of this place.

The cloud-sea holds its level. The temperature holds within a few degrees. The water condenses at the same slow rate it always has, drips at the same unhurried intervals, fills the pools to the same levels and no higher. The moss grows, but so slowly that no one alive has seen it spread. The flowers stay open. The young stone stays sharp, because nothing here is violent enough to wear it down: no frost to split it, no floods to scour it, no wind to sandblast it. The rock is young, and it will be young for a very long time.

The light is even and clear. The sun passes overhead, but the cloud-sea below reflects it back up, so shadows are faint and the whole plateau is lit softly from beneath as well as above. The wet stone shines. The pools reflect the pale sky. Everything has the clean, bright, slightly glossy look of a landscape that has just been rinsed, except that it is always just rinsed, and the rinsing never stops.

Standing at the eastern rim, looking out over the white, you can feel how finely balanced the place is: cloud held against stone, warmth held against height, water held in rock that will not let it go. The town sits in that balance quietly, built of the same stone and drinking from the same condensation, and it does not disturb anything. It has found the one way to live here, which is to fit into the stillness and let it be.
