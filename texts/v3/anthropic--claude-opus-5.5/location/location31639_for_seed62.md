# The Blanched Steps

## First, the light

Arrival is a matter of light before anything else. You come up out of the cloud bank that lies against the lower slopes most mornings, and the world goes from grey wool to a whiteness so complete that for the first minute you see nothing at all. You stand with your eyes shut and the brightness still coming through the lids, pink and then gold, and when you open them again the place assembles itself slowly, as if it were being developed on a plate.

The sun here is small and hard, a pinhole in a sky so thin that its blue is almost violet at the zenith and fades to a pale enamel at the horizon. There is little atmosphere above you to soften anything. Shadows are not grey but black, with edges sharp enough to cut. Everything that is not in shadow shines, because everything is wet.

## The rock

The ground is new. It is geologically newborn: a shelf of pale grey-white andesite that rose within the last few thousand years and has not yet been worn into anything like a slope. It stands in steps and terraces and broken blades, and it fractures in straight lines. Walk it and you are always stepping up or down some sheer little ledge knee-high or head-high, its lip still crisp, its faces flat as dressed stone. In places the rock split into columns as it cooled, and the columns stand in clusters like organ pipes, some snapped off at different heights so that the tops form irregular staircases leading nowhere.

There are no rounded boulders and no sand. The scree at the foot of each face is made of shards, some as long as a forearm, and they ring faintly if you knock them together. That is one of the few sounds you will make.

The shelf runs for miles. From any high step you can see it going on in every direction, a vast broken stair descending gently eastward toward the cloud sea and rising westward toward a line of raw peaks that still smoke a little in their upper cracks. Between them lies the valley proper: a basin perhaps six miles across, crossed by fault lines, cupped and tilted, the whole thing faintly luminous under the sun.

## The water, which is mostly not there

It rarely rains. The rain that does fall comes as a brief hiss in the late afternoon and is gone within minutes. There are no rivers, and the rock is too young and too fractured to hold a stream for long. Water finds its way into the fault lines and disappears downward.

What the Steps have instead is the cloud. Each night the cloud sea rises. It climbs the shelf in the dark and fills the basin to a depth of a few hundred feet, cold and silent, and it touches every surface. By dawn the whole valley is beaded. Then the cloud withdraws downslope as the sun strikes, and for the first hours of the day everything drips. The dripping is too soft to hear. You see it: a slow glittering rain falling off every leaf onto the leaves below.

By noon the upper surfaces are dry, but the undersides and the crevices and the moss beds stay damp all day, and the rock beneath the vegetation is always cool and slick. In a few places the water pools: small basins in the columns' snapped-off tops, each holding a cupful of perfectly clear water, each reflecting a disc of violet sky. There are thousands of them. From a height they look like a scattering of coins.

## The growth

Out of this damp comes an impossible lushness. Nothing here grows tall. The wind and the altitude forbid it. But everything grows close.

The rock faces are upholstered. A dense silver-green moss covers every ledge and step to a depth of a hand's width, so thick that walking on it is like walking on a mattress, and so full of held water that a footprint slowly fills with a clear seep. Out of the moss rise cushion plants, domed and hard as stone, packed with thousands of tiny rosettes, some of them centuries old and as big as sleeping cattle. In the cracks between columns grow tree ferns no taller than a person, their fronds uncurling at a pace you could almost watch. Where the fault lines run there are thickets of small-leaved shrubs, twisted and interlocking, with bark hung in shaggy lichen and every branch crusted with epiphytes. Orchids the size of a thumbnail, bromeliads holding their own private cupfuls of water, and rootless trailing things that live on nothing but fog.

The leaves have adapted to the light. Nearly every plant here is silvered in some way. Some are furred with fine white hairs that scatter the sun. Some are waxed to a mirror gloss. Some are faintly metallic, their surfaces holding a thin film of something that throws back the light in a blue-bronze sheen. Walking through a thicket at noon is like walking through a heap of polished cutlery. The glare comes from every direction at once, from below as much as above, and after an hour your eyes ache even behind smoked glass.

The density is total. There is no bare ground anywhere except the newest scree and the freshest fracture faces. A single square yard might hold forty species layered one on top of another: moss on rock, cushion on moss, fern rooted in cushion, orchid clinging to fern, lichen crusting the orchid's roots. It feels less like a landscape covered in plants than a landscape made of them, with the rock as a frame.

## The machines

They stand at wide intervals across the basin, far enough apart that you see perhaps three or four from any point, and each is solitary.

The largest is a tower of riveted iron, eight stories high, built on the tallest of the columns in the valley's center. Its upper half is a lattice frame hung with sheets of fine metal mesh, dozens of them, arranged like the sails of a ship becalmed. They are fog nets. Each night, when the cloud fills the basin, the mesh catches it, and the water gathers and runs down into gutters and from the gutters into pipes. The pipes are iron too, as thick as a man's waist. They leave the tower and cross the valley on trestles and on the ground, running for miles over the broken steps, and at intervals they end in simple open spouts that drip onto the moss.

This is the first strange thing. The machines water the valley. The pipes do not lead to a reservoir or a town. They lead to the plants. Along every pipe the vegetation grows thicker and taller than anywhere else, and where the pipes end the thickets become almost jungle. The lushness of the Steps, you come to understand, is partly made.

There are other machines. A long low shed of corrugated metal, its roof entirely buried in cushion plants, contains a bank of generators turning so slowly that you must watch a single rivet head for a full minute to be sure it moves. Pylons march across the valley in a straight line, carrying cables that sag between them in long parabolas, and the cables carry current. You can feel it if you stand beneath them. The hairs on your arms lift, and your teeth have a faint metallic taste. Near the western edge a narrow-gauge railway runs on a trestle over the steps, and on it sits a single ore car, loaded with grey rock. The car is moving. It moves about a yard in a day. There are scratched marks on the trestle posts, dates perhaps, and by them the car has travelled a little under half a mile in a lifetime.

Nothing here seems to have been abandoned. There are no rust stains running down the metal, no broken panes, no collapsed roofs. The machines are clean, even bright. The iron has a dark gunmetal patina that does not flake. Somewhere, someone or something is keeping them. But you will not meet anyone.

## The quiet

The Steps are almost silent. It is not the silence of an empty place, the way a desert is silent. It is the silence of absorption. The moss drinks sound the way it drinks fog. A shout carries perhaps twenty yards before it simply stops, swallowed. Footsteps make no sound at all on the upholstered ledges. The generators, turning at their glacial pace, make none. The fog nets make none. The current in the cables makes none that a human ear can find.

After a few days here you start hearing your own body: the blood in your ears, the click of your jaw, the faint crackle of your breath in the thin air. When you knock two shards of scree together the small clear ring is almost shocking, a bright bell in a padded room, and it fades within a second.

The wind, when there is wind, does not howl. It passes over the silvered leaves and turns them, and a wave of changing light runs across the valley, flashing and dimming, utterly silent. It is like watching sound with the sound taken out.

## The animals, which are few

Fauna are rare. In a day you might see nothing that moves of its own will.

There is one bird, small and dun-colored, that lives in the thickets along the pipe ends. It does not sing. It flies only in short hops between branches, and it feeds on something that lives in the bromeliad cups. You may see three or four in a day.

There are insects, a few: a pale moth that rises from the moss at dusk just before the cloud comes, a beetle with a carapace polished like the leaves. There are no flies. There is nothing that bites.

And there is the grazer, which some visitors see and many do not. It is a slender, goat-sized thing with a coat so pale it is nearly invisible against the glare, and long ears it keeps folded flat. It moves along the ledges with a slow, deliberate gait, eating cushion plants a rosette at a time. It is unafraid. It will come within a few yards of you and look at you steadily with large dark eyes, and then it will turn away and continue eating. It makes no sound. No one knows where it goes when the cloud rises.

## The rules

Stay long enough and you begin to notice things about the valley that are not quite natural.

The plants grow toward the cables. Under each span of the pylon line there is a band of vegetation leaning inward from both sides, the cushions elongated, the ferns bent, as if the current were a second, invisible sun. Where a cable passes low over a thicket, the leaves directly beneath have taken on the strongest of the metallic sheens, blue-bronze and almost iridescent, and they are warm to the touch.

The fog nets grow. Or rather, something grows on them. Each morning, after the cloud withdraws, fine threads of pale green are visible along the mesh, and by evening they are gone. No one has ever seen them removed.

The machines turn at a pace tied to something. The generators speed up, very slightly, in the season when the cloud is thickest. The ore car moves a little faster after the brief afternoon rain. They seem to be responding to the valley rather than driving it: patient engines, slowly metabolizing the fog and the light.

And the rock is still rising. You can see it in the scree. Every year there are new fracture faces, clean and white, where the shelf has split as it lifts. Plants colonize them within a season. The pipes are fitted with sliding joints to accommodate the movement, and the joints have been extended many times, each extension slightly newer than the last, like growth rings.

## The shape of a day

Dawn is the white-out: the cloud retreating downslope, the sun striking, the whole valley igniting at once into a field of wet silver. The dripping begins.

Mid-morning the colors come. The silvered leaves dry a little and their true greens show through, dozens of greens: grey-green, blue-green, bronze, a green so dark it is almost black in the deep cushions.

Noon is the hardest light. There is no softness anywhere. Shadows are pits. The pools in the column tops hold dots of sun so bright they hurt.

Afternoon brings the brief rain, if it comes: a few minutes of fine grey slant, the plants lifting and gleaming, then gone.

Evening turns the whole valley gold, then rose, then a long lilac dusk. The moths rise. The grazer, if it was here, is gone. The cables begin to show a faint blue corona along their length, barely visible, the only light in the valley besides the stars, which here are very many and very sharp.

And then, from below, silently, the cloud begins to climb. It fills the basin step by step, covering the thickets, the cushions, the ore car, the generator shed, the first two stories of the tower. The fog nets begin to fill. The pipes begin, very slowly, to run. By midnight only the top of the tower and the highest pylons stand above a white sea under a black sky. Everything below is drinking.

You lie awake in the silence and feel the valley growing around you, a thousandth of an inch at a time. Rock, plant, and machine are all rising together, patiently and without hurry, into the light.
