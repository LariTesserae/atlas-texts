# The Stacks at Hallow Col

## Arrival

You come to it from above, because there is no other way in. The road over the col ends at a concrete apron where the lorries unload, and from the edge of that apron you look down into the valley and see no valley. It is roofs all the way down, and walls, and more roofs.

The valley is perhaps six kilometres long and two across at its widest. It is a glacial trough, steep-sided, hung between two ridges of dark mountain at an altitude where the air already thins in the chest. Its floor, its walls, and a good part of its upper shoulders have been built over so completely that the mountain's own rock shows only in a few high scarps where even the builders gave up. Everything else is made. Terraces are stacked on terraces, mills are built on the roofs of older mills, stairways are bolted to the faces of warehouses, and the warehouses are propped on the vaults of culverts that swallowed the original river two hundred years ago. There is no ground here in any sense you would recognise. There are only levels, and each level is the roof of the one below.

The first thing you notice is that it is moving. The second is how little noise it makes.

## The Weather That Lives In It

Hallow Col makes its own weather, or at least it concentrates the weather that arrives. Cold wet air comes up from the lowlands to the west and is funnelled into the trough. It climbs, cools, and condenses into cloud that pours up the valley like a river running backwards. On most days the cloud moves fast enough to watch. It tears around chimneys and unravels over the terrace edges. It fills a street to the second storey and empties it again inside a minute. Visibility changes constantly. You can stand on a stair landing and see four kilometres of stacked roofline, then see nothing past your own hand, then see it all again, with the light shifted.

The light is never strong. The sun reaches the valley floor for perhaps two hours at midday in summer and not at all in winter. The rest of the time the town lives in a grey half-light, thickened by cloud and filtered through the lattice of cables, gantries, and walkways strung across every open space. Electric lamps burn at all hours in the lower levels: sodium-yellow in the mills, bluish in the tenement stairwells. In the moving fog these lamps make shifting, soft-edged globes, so the whole town seems to breathe light in and out.

It is cool here, never truly cold except in the deep of winter, and rarely warm. The air sits a few degrees above what would be comfortable, and it is always damp. Wool is the native fabric. Everything smells faintly of wet stone, hot metal, and green growth.

## Water

Water is the town's power, its enemy, and its law.

It falls as rain perhaps two days in three. It condenses out of the moving cloud onto every surface, so that on a day with no rain at all the walls still run with it. It seeps from the mountain behind the upper terraces in a thousand weeping faces, and the builders have learned to channel it rather than fight it. Every wall in Hallow Col has a gutter at its foot. Every roof drains to a runnel, every runnel to a leat, every leat to one of the great stone-lined drops that carry the water down through the stacks to the buried river.

You hear it more than you see it: a continuous low hiss under everything, the sound of water moving through stone channels in the dark beneath your feet. Grates in the stairways breathe cold air and spray. On the steepest terraces open chutes send white water down between buildings in long, smooth, roaring ribbons. These are the loudest things in the town, and even they are muffled by the fog into something more like breath than thunder.

At the bottom of it all is the river, enclosed. It runs in a vaulted culvert under the valley floor, sometimes four levels deep, and at the valley's mouth it passes through the turbine halls. That is where the town's electricity is made. The whole place exists because a high valley with this much falling water could spin generators, and the generators could run smelters and wire-drawing mills and rolling works. Everything else accreted around that fact.

The drainage law is the oldest law in Hallow Col, older than the property laws, older than the guild charters. *Water must be let through.* You may build anything, anywhere, on anything, provided you do not block a channel. A man who blocks a leat is liable for every wall that fails downstream of him. Since walls fail constantly, the law is enforced with real ferocity.

## The Growing

You would expect a place this built to be barren. It is the opposite.

Hallow Col is green in the way only a cool, wet, high place can be green: thick, soft, layered, relentless. Moss covers everything that holds still for more than a season. It lies in deep cushions on the terrace walls and in bright velvet on the north faces of the mills. On old roofs it grows in heavy felted sheets that hold water like sponges and have to be cut back from the gutters. Ferns grow from every crack in the masonry: hart's-tongue, polypody, spleenwort, and in the lower, wetter levels great arching shuttlecock ferns taller than a man, rooted in the rot of old mortar. Liverworts slick the stairways. Lichen crusts the ironwork in grey-green and rust-orange.

Trees grow too, though there is no soil for them. Birch and rowan seed themselves on roofs and ledges and send roots down through slate and brick in search of the ever-present water. Some of the oldest terraces carry birch woods on their roofs, thin pale trunks crowded together, rooted in a hundred years of accumulated moss and fallen leaves. The roots grip the buildings beneath them like hands. The townspeople have a complicated relationship with this growth. It breaks buildings, but it also holds them. More than one old terrace has survived only because the roots of the trees on its roof have knit its stones together tighter than any mortar. There is a saying, *don't cut what's carrying you*, and it applies to more than trees.

Deliberate growing happens too. Every flat surface that can take a planter has one. Kale, leeks, cabbages, and potatoes grow in boxes on roofs and balconies and in the gutters of the higher terraces, where the water is cleaner. Mine-drainage and smelter runoff make the low water poisonous, and nothing edible grows below the fourth level. In the lower town the green is ornamental or feral: ferns and moss and pale, leggy shade-plants reaching toward lamps.

From a distance, from the col road, the town reads as a dark mass of slate and iron blurred with green, as if the valley were slowly being reclaimed by forest. It isn't. The town has simply been here long enough that the forest has moved in with it.

## Materials

The oldest parts are dark slate and grey granite, quarried from the valley itself. That is why the valley walls, where they show at all, are stepped and scarred with old workings. Over this lies a century of brick, reddish-black with soot and weather. Over the brick, cast iron: columns, brackets, trusses, stairways, all of it long since painted and repainted green and rusted through and patched again. Then poured concrete, which in this climate stains within a year and grows moss within five. Then corrugated steel and sheet copper, the copper gone blue-green, streaking everything beneath it with verdigris.

Nothing is new for long. Nothing is truly old either, because nothing is left alone. The place has the texture of something continuously repaired. Every wall is a palimpsest of patches, every roof a quilt of three or four materials. Everything is weathered to the same soft, dim, damp-darkened palette, so a building erected ten years ago is already hard to tell from one erected eighty years ago. The erosion here is not dramatic. It is patient and total and has reached a kind of balance with the builders. The town wears away at about the rate it is rebuilt.

## Density

There is no empty space in Hallow Col. None.

Streets are wide enough for one handcart, or two people turned sideways. Buildings touch on all sides. Where the terraces step down the valley walls, each building's roof is the next one's yard. Where they meet the valley floor, they are packed so tight that light reaches the bottom of the alleys only as a grey rumour. Above the streets run walkways, and above the walkways cables: power lines by the thousand, sagging in heavy black catenaries between gantries, humming faintly in wet weather. Above the cables run the ropeways, and the ropeways are the thing you watch.

## Motion

It never stops.

The ropeways carry ore down from the upper workings and coal and goods up from the col. Hundreds of iron buckets travel on endless steel cables from gantry to gantry across the valley, swaying slightly, appearing and vanishing into the fog in unbroken lines. Below them, funicular cars grind up and down the steepest faces on toothed rails. Narrow-gauge electric trams run on the few level streets, their wheels rubber-rimmed by old ordinance against the noise. Conveyor belts run in covered galleries between the mills and the smelters, and from the gallery windows you glimpse ingot and wire and coil sliding past in the yellow light.

The mills run on continuous shifts. The shift changes are the town's tides, three times a day. At each one tens of thousands of people pour out of the mill gates and up and down the stairways, and for half an hour every stair and bridge and landing is packed shoulder to shoulder with bodies in grey wool, moving. Then the doors close and the streets empty, as fast as the fog clears. The pattern repeats all day and all night.

Building never stops either. Somewhere in the valley on any given day a terrace is failing. Water has undermined a footing, or a root has split a vault, or someone above has added one storey too many. Somewhere else a crew is shoring, and somewhere else a crew is demolishing, and somewhere else, often on the rubble of the last collapse, a crew is building. Scaffolding is a permanent feature of the skyline. Cranes stand on rooftops like herons. When a building comes down it comes down fast, in a slump of slate and brick and wet moss. The dust is gone into the fog almost at once. Within a week there is timber framing on the site, and within a month something new stands there, already starting to darken.

Landslips happen too, the real kind. Every few years a section of the valley wall, undermined by two centuries of building and draining, lets go. It takes a whole slope of terraces with it, and the town absorbs the loss and rebuilds. The shape of Hallow Col from above has changed within living memory. Old people describe neighbourhoods and stairways that are simply gone, rubbled over and built across. There is no reliable map of the town and never has been. The guild of surveyors maintains one, but it is always a year behind.

## The Quiet

For all this motion, Hallow Col is a hushed place.

Part of it is the fog, which eats sound. Part of it is the moss, which lies thick on every surface and absorbs sound the way it absorbs water. Part of it is the electricity. The town's machines are driven by motors, not steam, and a motor mostly hums. The mills produce a deep, steady drone that you feel more than hear. After a day you stop noticing it, and then when a shift ends and the machines spool down, the silence is enormous.

And part of it is the people, who have learned to be quiet. Voices carry badly in the fog and echo strangely off the stacked walls, so conversation happens close and low. Mill crews communicate by hand signs, and the signs have leaked out into the streets. You will see two people on a crowded stairway exchange a whole conversation in gestures, without a word. Children learn the signs before they can speak properly.

So what you hear in Hallow Col is: the hiss of water in the channels, the hum of the mills, the creak and tick of the ropeway cables and the gentle knock of buckets passing over the gantry wheels, footsteps on wet stone, a tram's low whine, and every so often, far away and dulled by the fog, the slow roar of a building coming down. Then the hiss and hum again.

## Animals

Very few. Hallow Col is too cold, too wet, too crowded, and too thoroughly built for most animals.

There are snails and slugs in great numbers, grazing the moss. On wet nights the stairways shine with their trails. There are ravens, a few pairs, who nest on the high scarps above the town and come down to pick through the refuse heaps at the col. There are herons at the reservoir above the turbine intakes, grey and patient. Their own greyness makes them nearly invisible in the fog, and the reservoir is the only open water in the valley.

Rats are rarer than you'd think. The cold and the wet are hard on them, and the townspeople are vigilant about food stores. A few feral cats live in the warm spaces around the smelter flues. Mountain goats are sometimes seen on the old dam wall, which is the one piece of human construction in the valley that looks, from a distance, like cliff.

That's nearly all. The absence of birdsong is one of the things visitors notice, if Hallow Col ever has visitors. The green of the place suggests a forest, and a forest should sing. This one doesn't.

## Rules

A few customs hold the place together:

**Water must be let through.** Already covered, but it bears repeating, because nearly everything else follows from it.

**You own your level, not your ground.** There is no ground to own. Property is held by level and by footprint. Whoever builds above you owns your sky, and has to bear the load on your roof, and is responsible for your drainage. The legal arrangements this generates are baroque, and the surveyors' guild and the water courts exist mostly to adjudicate them.

**Don't cut what's carrying you.** Trees and roots that bind a structure may not be removed without survey. Moss on a load-bearing roof is to be cleared from gutters but not from the slope. This is folk wisdom that became law.

**The ropeway doesn't stop.** It stops for nothing but a snapped cable. A funeral procession waits for the buckets to pass. So does a crowd at a shift change. So does a man pinned under a collapse. This is a hard rule and the town knows it.

**Low voices.** Unwritten, but universal.

## The Whole

Stand on the col in the late afternoon, if afternoon means anything here, and look down the valley.

Cloud streams up from the west in long grey braids, tearing on chimneys, flowing over terraces, filling and emptying the streets. Beneath it lies the town. It is six kilometres of stacked slate and iron and weeping concrete, furred with moss and bristling with birch, lit by thousands of yellow and blue lamps that swell and fade as the fog passes over them. The ropeways run in long dotted lines through it all, their buckets appearing and vanishing. A funicular crawls up a face you had taken for a single wall. Somewhere a crane swings. Somewhere, far down, a plume of dust rises, the dust of something falling, and the fog takes it.

Beneath it all you hear the hiss of water, the hum of motors, and an immense damp quiet.

It has been like this for two hundred years. It is falling down and building itself up at exactly the same rate. It looks, in its dimness and greenness and constant motion, almost exactly the way it looked when the oldest people in it were young, and almost none of it is the same.
