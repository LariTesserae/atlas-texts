# The Pans of Hesk

## The Approach

You come up onto the plateau from below, through a gorge where the rock sweats. By the last switchback the air has thickened into something you can almost lean on, hot and white and wet. Then the gorge opens and the land lies flat under a sky so bright it seems to hum. It doesn't. Nothing here hums. That is the first thing you notice, before the heat, before the glare: the silence. Your boots on the path make no sound you can hear. You clap your hands and feel the sting in your palms, but the noise dies an arm's length away, as if the air had caught it and folded it up.

The plateau is enormous, a shelf of pale stone nearly three thousand meters up, perhaps forty kilometers across, tilted slightly toward the south. It is terraced from edge to edge. The terraces aren't cut. They grew, lip by lip, over tens of thousands of years, as mineral-heavy water spilled from vents and left its load behind. The whole landscape is a slow staircase of travertine and sinter, white where it is new, bone-yellow and ochre where it is old, streaked rust and verdigris where iron and copper have bled through. The terrace rims are rounded and soft-edged, weathered like old teeth. Nothing is jagged. Everything has had time to settle into the shape the water wanted.

Water is everywhere, but rarely deep. It lies in the terrace pools a hand's depth, a knee's depth, perfectly clear and faintly blue-green. It slides over rims in sheets thin as varnish. It beads on every surface, condenses on your skin and sleeves, and creeps up porous rock faces by capillary pull, so that some cliffs look wet from the top down, weeping upward. The ground never quite dries. After noon the whole plateau steams.

## Heat and Light

It is hot, relentlessly hot. The air sits at the temperature of blood or a little above, and it is saturated, so sweat does nothing. The stone underfoot is hotter still in places, fed from below. Some terrace pools are pleasantly warm. Some would scald. Some, in the old vent-fields to the north, boil quietly: no roar, just a steady rising of fat, soundless bubbles and a shimmer above them.

The light is the other half of the place. The sun at this altitude is fierce, and the plateau is built to throw it back. White sinter, white salt crust, white vapor, mirror-flat water. There are no shadows to rest in except the ones people make. At midday the glare is so total that the terraces seem to float, their edges dissolving into the steam, the horizon gone to a bright seam where land and sky have agreed to stop distinguishing themselves. People here squint permanently. Their faces are creased around the eyes from infancy, and they wear slit-visors of dark glass or smoked horn.

## The Hush

The silence has a name locally: the Hush. Nobody fully understands it. The best explanation the engineers offer is that the vapor over the plateau is unusual, a fine, dense, mineral-charged mist whose droplets are exactly the wrong size for sound. It swallows noise the way deep snow does, only more thoroughly. A shout carries perhaps five meters. A dropped iron bar gives a dull pat. The boiling vents make no noise. The falling water makes none. Rain, when it comes, arrives with only a pressure on the skin.

It isn't perfectly uniform. The Hush thins at dawn, when the air is coolest and the mist has settled into the low pools. For an hour or so after sunrise you can hear, faintly, the tick of cooling stone, the drip of condensation, the scratch of something walking on salt crust. Old residents call this the Hour of Edges and take it seriously. Children are told stories then. Debts are spoken aloud then, so that they count. The rest of the day, life is conducted by sight and touch.

So the people of Hesk speak with their hands, a fluent, compact sign-language worked up over generations. They mark surfaces with soft chalk and grease pencil. Doors carry slate panels where messages accumulate and are wiped. Warnings are colored: red flags on scalding pools, yellow rope around thin crust. Machinery is monitored by vibration. Engineers keep a palm on a housing or a bare foot on a floor plate and read the thrum in their bones. Everyone here knows the feel of a turbine running true and the feel of one about to fail.

## The Town

Hesk is a city, though it doesn't look like one from any single point. It is spread thin across the plateau: clusters of buildings on the higher, cooler terraces, a kilometer or two apart, linked by pipes and walkways and cable. Seen from the rim of the gorge in the brief clarity of morning, it looks like a scattering of pale boxes on a white staircase, threaded together with dark lines. Between the clusters lie long stretches of nothing but terrace and water and light.

The buildings are made of the plateau itself. Sinter is quarried, sawn wet, and set in blocks that cure harder with each rain as dissolved minerals knit them together. Old walls have fused into single masses, seamless, their mortar lines gone. Roofs are shallow and white. Windows are small, set deep, glazed with a milky glass made locally from silica sand, and they glow rather than show. Doors are heavy and set low, so that indoors, where the mist is thinner, a little sound is possible. Homes have one quiet-room where the air is dried by salt beds, and there families can speak, sing, and hear each other breathe. These rooms are small and close, and intimacy in Hesk is bound up with them.

The city is layered. Older buildings stand on terraces that were active when they were built. Water has since found them, and they are now half-sheathed in new travertine, their lower walls crusted and swollen, their doorways narrowed. Some old districts have been abandoned to the rock and are slowly being swallowed: rooms filling with pale stone, shapes of chairs and stairs furred over, recognizable for a century and then not. The people don't fight this much. They build uphill and leave the old places to be absorbed. There is an attitude here that stone is patient and humans are temporary tenants in its process.

## Industry

Hesk exists because of what the water carries. The springs come up from deep, and they are rich: lithium, boron, sulfur, potash, rare salts that fetch high prices in the lowland cities. The plateau is a vast chemical works, and most of it is silent.

Evaporation pans cover the southern terraces for kilometers. They are diked pools where spring water is trapped and baked, each pan a slightly different color according to what has precipitated: pink, lemon-yellow, blue-white, a startling pale violet where some manganese salt concentrates. Workers walk the dikes in broad hats and pale wrappings, raking crystals into windrows with long wooden rakes. The rakes make no sound. Carts on narrow rails carry the harvest to the refineries, and the rails are laid on felt to spare the ground vibration.

The refineries sit at the heart of each cluster: long low sheds, chimneys that exhale not smoke but white vapor, which merges with the Hush and vanishes. Inside, vats and crystallizers and centrifuges are driven by geothermal power. Every refinery has its own bore, a shaft sunk into the heat below, capped with ceramic heads and piped into turbines. The turbines are sealed in thick ceramic housings, partly for heat and partly because engineers learned long ago that the Hush amplifies vibration through solid material even as it kills sound in air. A badly mounted engine can shake a building apart without anyone hearing it coming.

Electricity runs everywhere, on cables strung between insulated sinter posts. At night, and the nights are short and hot, the town is lit by lamps of salt-glass that throw a soft pale glow, and the vapor catches the light so that each cluster sits inside its own faint luminous dome. From a distance, at night, the plateau looks like a scatter of lanterns underwater.

There are condensing towers too: tall latticed frames of ceramic fins, open to the air, where the plateau's constant humidity is drawn out as drinking water. The spring water is too mineral to drink, and a cup of it would stone your kidneys in a season. So Hesk drinks what the sky gives up, gathered drop by drop on the fins and fed down to cisterns. The towers stand alone on the terraces, pale and spidery, beaded with water, dripping steadily into their basins without a sound.

## Life Without Plants

Nothing green grows here. The heat, the minerals, and the altitude forbid it. No moss, no grass, no lichen on the rocks. Anything seeded by a traveler's boot dies within days, stunted and bleached. The only primary life is microbial, and it is everywhere.

Microbial mats line every pool and spillway, graded by temperature. In the hottest water near the vents they are almost invisible, a faint yellowish film. Cooler, they thicken into orange, then rust-red, then leathery brown and olive and deep maroon at the terrace edges. They form ripples, knobs, and pillars. In some pools they build slow domes like soft stromatolites. They give the plateau its only warmth of color beyond the pans. Seen from above, the terraced springfields are banded like agate, concentric rings of sulfur, apricot, and blood.

On this living scum, an abundance of animals depends.

The first and most numerous are the flies, small, dark, heat-loving brine flies that breed in the mats in unimaginable numbers. They rise in the afternoons in low drifting clouds so dense they shade the water, and they settle in black carpets along the pool rims, thick enough to make the stone look burned. They don't bite. They barely fly. When you walk the dikes they part before your feet in soft dark ripples, perfectly silent.

Beneath the surface, pale shrimp the length of a fingernail graze the mats in the cooler pools. They are translucent pink and swarm in slow swirling clouds that turn together like shoals of fish. In certain pans they are so numerous that the water turns faintly rose.

Snails, low and ridged, shells crusted with mineral, crawl along the wet rims and leave glistening trails. Over years their shells thicken with travertine until the old ones can barely move, and they end as small white stones indistinguishable from the terrace itself. Locals collect the most perfectly encrusted as keepsakes. There are beds of them near the old vents where generations have stacked, so that the ground is partly made of snail.

Then the predators.

Spiders web the condensing towers. They are small, pale, long-legged things that string silk between the ceramic fins and catch drifting flies. On bright mornings the towers glitter with their webs, each strand beaded with condensation, and the engineers leave them alone, believing they help the towers drip.

Lizards bask on the pipes. They are heat-mad, fast, sand-colored or salt-white, with bulging dark eyes against the glare. The pipes are always warm, and in the brief cool of dawn the lizards cluster on them in ranks, heads all pointing the same way, waiting. By midday they have spread out over the terraces, darting at fly-mats and snatching mouthfuls.

And the birds. The birds are the plateau's great spectacle. They are waders, tall and thin-legged, feathered in pale pink and ash-grey. They stand in the warm pools by the thousands, heads down, filtering shrimp and fly larvae through curved bills. They move in slow processions across the terraces, stepping deliberately, stirring the mats with their feet. When they fly, whole flocks lift together in a single soft exhale of wings, a rose-colored cloud that wheels over the bright water and settles again a kilometer off. They make no calls that anyone has ever heard. Whether they are mute, or whether their voices are simply swallowed by the Hush, nobody knows. Some ornithologists from the lowlands claim the birds sing constantly and at great volume, and that the Hush has hidden it for all of history. Hesk's residents find this idea disturbing and don't like to discuss it.

There are also stranger creatures, less often seen. There are pale, eyeless, eel-like things in the deepest vent throats, glimpsed occasionally curling up through boiling water that should kill anything alive. There are small burrowing mammals, like shrews with salt-crusted fur, that live in the dry crust between pools and come out only at the Hour of Edges. And there are the "glass moths," which aren't moths at all but some kind of winged insect with wings so clear and thin that you see them only as flickering distortions in the bright air, as if the light itself were stuttering.

## Weather and Cycle

The plateau runs on a daily rhythm as regular as a pulse.

At dawn the air is at its coolest, still hotter than a lowland summer noon. Mist lies flat in the pools, the Hush thins, and faint sounds return. The lizards line up on the pipes. The birds stand motionless with their heads tucked. Workers go out to the pans in the grey-pink light, and for an hour Hesk talks aloud.

By mid-morning the sun has climbed and the light becomes overwhelming. The mist rises off the pools and hangs a few meters up, a luminous ceiling. The Hush closes in. Workers go quiet and switch to signs. The flies begin to wake.

At midday the plateau is almost unbearable, a white furnace. Most outdoor work stops. People retreat into thick-walled buildings and quiet-rooms. Only the birds keep working, wading slowly through pools that would burn human feet.

In the afternoon, the rain. It is born of the plateau's own vapor. As the day's heat drives more and more moisture into the air, the mist ceiling thickens, darkens slightly from white to pearl, and then simply lets go. The rain falls straight down, warm, large-dropped, heavy, for twenty minutes or an hour. It carries dissolved salts from the steam, and when it dries it leaves a fine crystalline frost on every surface, so that the whole town glitters in the late light. This, more than anything, is what builds Hesk's walls ever thicker. Each afternoon's rain lays another invisible layer of mineral over everything.

Evening. The rain clears, the sun sinks toward the gorge, and the long light turns the terraces gold and copper and flame. The flies rise in their clouds. The birds feed frantically. The spiders repair their webs. The lamps come on in the clusters, each inside its glowing dome of vapor.

Night is short, brilliant with stars at the zenith and hazed at the edges. The springs keep flowing. Nothing freezes; nothing cools very much. The turbines keep turning under their ceramic hoods, and the town sleeps with bare feet on the floors, half-listening, through the skin, for the rhythm of the machines.

## The Long Time

Everything here is slow, and everything here is moving. The terraces grow a finger's width in a lifetime. Springs wander. A vent that fed a district for three centuries goes dry, and a new one opens two kilometers off, and the town gradually follows it, building up against the new water and leaving the old stones to cure. Refineries are abandoned when their bores cool, and each old one becomes a hill of pale mineral with ceramic chimneys poking out like the stems of drowned trees. In some places you can see several generations of Hesk stacked atop each other, a city growing like its terraces, upward and outward, layer by layer, following the heat.

The people accept this. Their dead are not buried but laid in the spillways of particular springs, on stone biers, and within a few decades they are sheathed in travertine, pale smooth forms that become part of the terrace, part of the staircase, part of the ground the living walk on. Families visit these places at the Hour of Edges and speak aloud to them, while speech can still carry.

Stand on the rim of the gorge at dawn and look out: the white staircase of the plateau, the colored pans, the rings of living scum around the vents, the pink flocks, the scattered clusters of pale buildings threaded with dark cable, the condensing towers dripping, the steam lifting slowly as the light grows. All of it sits in a bright, total, wordless stillness that is somehow full of motion. Then the sun clears the horizon, and the Hush comes down, and it all goes on without a sound.
