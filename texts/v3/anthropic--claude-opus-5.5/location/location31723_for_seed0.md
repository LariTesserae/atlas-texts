# The Hotbed

## The ground

It starts with the rock, and the rock is new.

Forty years ago, perhaps sixty, the ground here split along a line several miles long and bled. The basin it filled is shallow, a low bowl barely above the sea that lies somewhere off in the dark to the east. The flows cooled into black ropes and plates. Nothing has worn them down yet. Everywhere the edges are raw: pressure ridges folded up like shoved blankets, gas-bubble crusts that break underfoot into shards, glassy rinds on every surface. The rock cuts. Anything that moves here quickly learns to move carefully, or it bleeds.

The rock has not finished cooling. A few feet down it is still warm, and in places it is hot. The heat rises slowly and constantly through the whole basin, so the ground feels less like earth than like the flank of a large sleeping animal. At night the air above it stays warm. In the day, when there is a day, it gets close to unbearable.

## The dark

There is rarely much of a day.

The fissure still breathes. Small vents along the old split lift a steady grey haze that the warm air carries up and spreads into a lid across the basin. From outside it looks like a permanent low storm cloud parked over the lowland. From inside, noon is a brown dusk and every other hour is simply dark. The sun is a rumour: a paler smear, sometimes, if the wind shifts.

Under the haze is the canopy, and under the canopy it is black.

## The growth

Warmth, darkness, a constant slow drip of moisture, and a floor of fresh mineral rock have produced an absurd forest.

The first colonists were ferns and mosses, which rooted in cracks and caught the settling ash. Behind them came tree ferns, and behind those came the figs. The figs are the true masters here. They start as seeds dropped by bats onto the heads of tree ferns, send down roots that find the warm cracks, and grow at a speed that looks frantic. A fig root does not only find a crack. It pries it open. The basalt is young and brittle and full of flaws, and the roots split it the way a wedge splits wood. In places you can hear the rock cracking in the canopy's dark: a sharp report, then a trickle of falling grit.

Everything grows over everything. Figs strangle tree ferns, then strangle each other. Climbing aroids with leaves the size of doors grip the trunks. Pale, chlorophyll-poor vines have given up on light entirely and live on the fungus that lives on the dead. Moss grows on the vines. Epiphytic orchids grow on the moss. The layers are so thick that the forest floor is less a floor than an argument about where the floor is. Roots tangle three metres up. Leaf litter lies caught in branch forks and rots there, and seedlings germinate in the rot. There are places where you could climb inside the forest for a hundred metres without once touching rock.

Because most of it grows toward a light that barely arrives, much of the growth is pale and soft: long, blanched stems, leaves that are thin and translucent, reaching. The forest has the quality of a thing grown in a cellar. It is lush in the literal sense, heavy and wet-fleshed and sprawling, and slightly wrong in its pallor.

## The water

It almost never rains in the basin. The haze-lid holds the weather out, or holds it too high.

The water comes from below. Groundwater seeps in from the surrounding country, runs down toward the sea through fractures and old lava tubes, meets the heat, and rises again as vapour. At night the vapour condenses on everything, so the forest drips constantly. It is not rain but a soft, unending percussion of drops falling from leaf to leaf to leaf. The rock never quite dries. It stays sticky and dark with a film of moisture and algae.

Yet this is not a wet place. There are no streams on the surface. The fresh rock is porous and cracked, and any water that pools soon drains away. Water collects only in particular spots: bowls in the flow crust, the cupped bases of the huge aroid leaves, the throats of bromeliad-like epiphytes, and the lava tubes, which hold warm black pools in their low points. Each pool is a city of its own. Frogs breed in thimbles of water in the canopy. There are larvae in every cup.

## The animals

And the life. The life is too much.

The first thing anyone notices about the basin is the sound, but the sound comes from the animals, so the animals come first.

The lava tubes are the heart of it. They run under the whole basin, a braided network of hollow tunnels left when the flows crusted over and drained. They are warm, humid, and dark, and they are full of bats. Not thousands; the number is hard to state. At dusk, which is only a deepening of an already dark day, they pour out of the skylights, the places where the tube roofs have collapsed, in black rivers that take an hour to empty. They eat figs, they eat insects, and they drop seeds and guano across the whole forest, fertilising the rock. The forest exists in the form it does because of them.

There are insects in proportion. Cicadas of four or five species, with emergences that overlap. Crickets. Katydids with pale translucent wings that mimic the blanched leaves. Moths in clouds. Glassy-winged flies that swarm the rotting fig drops. Stick insects longer than a forearm. Beetles that burrow into the soft, damp wood of fallen tree ferns. Termites that climb the dead figs and build earthen tunnels up their bark.

The frogs are unbelievable. The canopy's water cups support tree frogs of dozens of kinds, and on some nights the whole basin seems to be singing. Land crabs migrate through the forest from the higher ground to the sea, crossing the sharp basalt in their thousands, their shells clicking on the glass. Great snails, some as large as a fist, grind along the damp rock, rasping algae. Millipedes as thick as a thumb coil in the litter. Leeches sway on the undersides of leaves.

There are larger animals too, though they are shy and the forest hides them. Something like a small deer that picks its way over the rock on hard little hooves. A civet-like hunter that lives on frogs and crabs. Pythons in the canopy, thick with bats. Monitor lizards hunting crabs in the tubes. Birds that nest in the tube skylights and hunt the swarms.

And there are the monkeys.

## The knappers

The basin is not uninhabited by minds. A troop of macaques, or something close to macaques, has lived here since the first figs fruited. There are many troops, in fact, and they have a thing the rest of the region does not: they use tools.

The fresh rock is full of obsidian. Glassy rinds form wherever the lava cooled fast, and these crack into razor flakes. The monkeys learned, generations ago, that the flakes cut, and that striking one piece of volcanic glass against another produces more. They do not make sophisticated tools. They make edges. They open the giant snails with them. They split fig fruit and dig larvae from rotting trunks. Young monkeys practise knapping, clumsily, and cut themselves. Old monkeys work with a kind of patience.

The effect on the landscape is subtle but everywhere. Around certain warm rocks the ground is littered with flakes: little hearths of debitage, anvils polished by use, shell middens of cracked snails glinting pale in the dark. There are no structures, nothing built, no shelter made. The monkeys sleep in the fig crowns and move constantly. But the ground is marked with the evidence of hands. It is a landscape at the very edge of culture, before architecture, before permanence: the moment of the first tool, repeated endlessly and never elaborated.

Occasionally humans come too. They walk in from the higher country with torches for the obsidian, and they leave the same kind of trace: flake scatters, a charred patch where a fire burned, a strip of cord knotted around a trunk to mark the way out. They do not stay. No one stays. The basin does not allow it.

## The sound

It is deafening.

This is a closed, reflective space. The haze-lid, the glassy rock, the hollow tubes: everything bounces sound back. The noise has nowhere to escape. It builds.

At dusk the bat flight fills the air with a leathery roar, a hiss of wings like a waterfall, shot through with shrieks. Then the frogs begin, a pulsing, clanging chorus that swells and falls, with individual calls like struck glass, like drums, like rattled chains. The cicadas drone underneath it, a shrill, sawing, metallic whine that seems to come from inside the skull. The crickets pulse faster in the heat. The monkeys scream territorial calls from tree to tree, and the calls echo off the rock and come back doubled.

Under it all are the rock sounds: the crack of splitting basalt as fig roots pry it open, the rumble when a tube roof falls somewhere in the dark, the hiss of vents, the drip of condensation, and the constant clicking of crabs on glass.

The lava tubes amplify everything. Standing over a skylight, you feel sound welling up from the ground like heat.

It is never silent. The loudest hours are at night, but the day is only somewhat quieter. People who come here for obsidian leave with ringing ears and describe it afterward as a place where you cannot think.

## The instability

Nothing holds still.

The ground is young and still moving. The fissure rumbles. New vents open, sometimes in the middle of the forest, and the heat kills a patch of trees in a single night, leaving pale trunks standing in a sulphur haze. The lava tubes collapse. A whole section of forest can fall into the ground with a roar, and the next morning there is a new skylight, a raw pit of broken rock full of shattered figs. Then the bats move in. Then the figs grow back.

The growth itself is unstable. The figs grow so fast and so heavy that they tear themselves down. Trunks strangled by other trunks die and rot at the core, and the whole towering mass comes down, crashing through the canopy and taking others with it. Fallen giants lie in the dark, and in weeks they are covered in moss and seedlings. In a year they are gone into soil.

The fauna is volatile too. Swarms explode and crash. A cicada emergence fills the forest one season and is absent the next. The crab migrations ebb and surge. The bat colonies move from tube to tube as roofs fall. The monkey troops fight over the richest snail grounds and the best obsidian, and the balance among them shifts constantly.

The entire basin is a process, not a place: rock being made and broken, forest rising and falling, life boiling over the warm glass. It has the anthill energy of something permanently building itself and taking itself apart at the same time.

## The strangeness

Some things here are not quite right.

The forest glows faintly. Not everywhere, but in patches: fungi in the rotting wood, a pale green light where the fallen figs lie. On the darkest nights you can see the shape of the forest floor picked out in dim, cold smears of it, the outlines of fallen trunks, and the fungal threads spreading through the litter. The monkeys seem to use the glow to navigate. So do the crabs.

The rock rings. The glassy crust, struck in certain places, gives off a clear bell tone, and the whole basin is full of these singing stones. The monkeys strike them. Whether as signal or play, no one knows.

Growth in the warmest zones is faster than it should be. Near the active vents, where the ground is hot and the steam is thick, plants reach absurd sizes in a single season: aroid leaves bigger than a person, vines as thick as a waist, fig roots that seem to move visibly across the rock over a few days. The animals near the vents are larger too, the snails and millipedes especially. It is as if the heat itself were feeding everything.

And there is a smell, everywhere: sulphur, rot, fig sweetness, guano, wet stone, and something sharp and mineral, like struck flint. It clings. Visitors say it stays in their clothes for weeks.

## The whole

Stand at the edge of the basin, on the old higher ground, and look down. You see almost nothing: a low, dark, wide bowl under a lid of haze, its forest a black mass that seems to breathe. You hear it before you see anything. The roar comes up the slope like weather.

Go down into it and the scale is lost. The forest is too dense for sight lines. The dark closes in. You are inside a warm, dripping, shrieking, glowing mass that extends for miles in every direction, over rock that cuts and ground that might open. You pick your way over fresh obsidian by torchlight. Crabs click past your feet. Bats stream overhead in a sound like rain. Somewhere a monkey strikes a stone and a bell tone rings out through the chorus. Somewhere a tree comes down. Somewhere a tube roof falls. Somewhere the ground hisses.

It is a newborn place, still hot from its making, overrun by life before it has even cooled. It is the world at the moment of beginning: rock just formed, forest just rising, the first minds just learning to make an edge. Nothing here is old. Nothing here is still. Nothing here is quiet.
