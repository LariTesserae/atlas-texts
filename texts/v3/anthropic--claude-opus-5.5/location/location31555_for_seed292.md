# The Sill

It is a mountain that has stopped being a mountain. What stands here now is only its root: a tableland of quartzite so old it has forgotten it ever had a peak. Four kilometers above a lowland nobody on top can see, the plateau runs flat for thirty-odd kilometers in every direction. Its edges fall away in cliffs a kilometer and a half deep. Its top has been ground by rain, frost and more rain into something close to a floor. The crew who work the stations call it the Sill, as in a windowsill, a ledge where things collect. What collects is water.

## Inside the Cloud

You do not arrive at the Sill so much as enter it. The last switchback of the funicular track pushes up through the cloudbase, and the world goes white and wet and loud all at once.

The plateau sits exactly at the altitude where the prevailing wind, climbing the escarpment from a warm sea two hundred kilometers away, cools past its dewpoint and gives up everything it carries. It does this constantly. There is no season here that could honestly be called dry. There are only modes of wet:

- **Drift.** Cloud moving across the rock at walking pace, so saturated that it beads on eyelashes and runs off the chin.
- **Drizzle.** A fine, directionless falling.
- **The Wall.** A cloudburst that comes across the table as a visible grey face, a curtain with a leading edge. When it passes over you, you cannot see your own boots. The rain is heavy enough that breathing takes some care.

The air is cool, eight or nine degrees most days, rarely above fifteen, occasionally dropping to a raw two or three when the wind swings. It is the chill of being wet all the time rather than the chill of real cold. Nothing ever quite dries. Paper swells. Wool is worn because wool is warm when soaked.

Light is ordinary, and that surprises visitors. The cloud is thin at the top of its layer, so the plateau lives in bright, even, pearl-grey daylight. It has no shadows to speak of, but plenty of illumination. When the cloud tears open, which it does several times a day and without warning, the sun comes down in hard, sudden columns. Everything wet ignites. The whole table becomes a sheet of glare, steam lifts off the rock in visible sheets, and ten minutes later the Wall comes back and shuts it.

## The Floor

Underfoot, the Sill is a pavement. Billions of years have taken the quartzite down to a pale, pinkish-grey stone, smooth in places as a riverbed. The rock is split into a vast irregular grid by joints, cracks the rain has widened over ages into channels, slots and flutes. From above it would look like a cracked glaze. Between the joint-channels the blocks stand as low islands, a hand's height or a body's height above the grooves. Some have eroded into mushroom shapes, bollards, anvils and tables, fields of them, dense as a crowd.

And all of it is under water, most of the time.

This is the fact of the place. The table is so flat that the rain has nowhere urgent to go. It spreads, sheets and pools. In drift mode the water covers the pavement a finger deep, clear as glass, moving in slow braided currents toward whichever edge the joint-grid happens to favor. In a Wall it rises to the knee, the thigh, and in the low basins to the chest. Brown with tannin, it races through the channels hard enough to knock a person down. The water is always going somewhere, and where it goes changes by the hour. A blocked slot, a fallen mat of moss, a new pothole ground a little deeper by a trapped pebble, and a stream that ran north all morning is running east by afternoon. The crew keep no permanent paths. They read the flow each day the way sailors read wind.

Where the water finally reaches the rim, it leaves. The edges of the Sill are lined with falls, hundreds of them, never the same count two days running. Most are too tall to reach the bottom as water. They break apart halfway down and go to mist, so the cliffs stand in their own perpetual cloud. The biggest, at the heads of the old joint-canyons, drop in solid columns you can hear from anywhere on the plateau. After a Wall, new falls appear along stretches of rim that were dry that morning. Sheets of water pour off the table like a tipped tray and are gone again by evening.

## What Grows

The Sill is lush in the way high cold wet places are lush: dense, low and saturated rather than tall. There are almost no trees on the open pavement. Wind and flood see to that. Everything else is here.

**Moss** is the ground cover and the architecture. It comes in cushions, mats and hummocks, in a hundred greens from black-olive to acid-lime, with reds and bronzes where the light hits hardest. It fills every joint and climbs every block. Where the water runs fast, the moss streams out long and flat like hair. In quiet backwaters it builds into spongy mounds waist-high and centuries old, each one holding its own weight in water and releasing it slowly downstream.

**Sphagnum bogs** fill the basins, floating quaking rafts that swell and settle with each storm. When the current is strong enough, whole islands of it tear loose and drift, bumping against the blocks and sometimes riding all the way to the rim and over.

**Pitcher plants and sundews** crowd the bog margins, because the rain has long since leached every nutrient out of the rock and the only good food here is insects. The pitchers stand in colonies of thousands: bottle-green, wine-red, veined like hands. After a Wall they brim and spill over.

**Rosette plants** stand on the higher blocks: stiff, silver-haired, chest-high giants of the daisy and bromeliad families. Their leaves are furred against the cold, and their central cups hold standing water with its own tiny tadpoles and mosquito larvae. Some flower once in fifty years, sending up a spike taller than a person that is thronged with bees for a week.

**Bromeliads, orchids, lichens and ferns** cover everything that holds still. Beards of grey-green lichen stream off the funicular cables. Ferns grow from the rust on the machinery. Orchids grow on the orchids.

In the sheltered joint-canyons near the rim, where the falls have cut deep slots, there are the only true forests: dwarf trees twisted almost horizontal, their trunks bearded thick with moss. The canopy is so dense and wet that walking under it is like walking inside a lung.

## What Lives

Abundance, and most of it amphibious.

**Frogs** first, because they are the sound of the place. There are dozens of species, most smaller than a thumbnail. Some are black with orange bellies and walk rather than hop. Some live their whole lives in a single bromeliad cup. Some breed in the flood-sheets, laying eggs in the hours after a Wall and relying on speed. Their calls go on day and night, loudest after storms, with an intensity that has a physical pressure to it.

**Swifts** live on the falls. Thousands nest behind the curtains of the big cascades, flying straight through solid water to reach their ledges. At dawn and dusk they pour out in black ribbons, screaming, and work the insect clouds over the plateau.

**Insects** rise off the bogs in columns: midges, mayflies, stoneflies, craneflies with legs as long as fingers, and dragonflies patrolling the channels. When the sun breaks through, the hatches are enormous. The air shimmers with them, and every pitcher plant on the Sill feeds.

**Eels** are the strangest ordinary thing here. They climb. Hatched in the warm sea far below, the young eels come up the escarpment through the mist-soaked moss on the cliff faces, over weeks, a kilometer and a half of vertical wet stone. They arrive on the Sill and live in its channels for decades, fat and dark, sliding over the flooded pavement in the half-light of storms. When they are ready they go back the way they came, down the falls.

**Snails and slugs** are everywhere, some huge and yellow, and a flatworm the length of a forearm hunts them across the wet rock. There are freshwater crabs in the deep potholes. Water birds come and go with the floods: dippers bobbing on the blocks, a small grey heron that stalks the channels, ducks that ride the current to the rim and take off just before the drop. There are rodents in the moss and a small dense-furred cat that almost nobody sees but whose tracks turn up in the mud every morning. Above it all, when the cloud opens, the condors ride the updraft off the escarpment, hanging motionless at eye level just beyond the edge.

## The Works

A century ago someone looked at all this falling water and saw power.

The Sill Hydraulic Company built across the plateau for about twenty years and then, for reasons half financial and half meteorological, mostly stopped. What remains is scattered thinly across the table, a mark here and there in a very large space.

**The funicular** is the main artifact and still the only reliable way up. Its steel cable is draped with lichen, and its track climbs the escarpment in a straight line through the mist, pinned to the cliff by iron brackets thick with rust. The car is wood and iron, heavy and slow, and groans on the cable. A counterweight car passes it halfway, invisible in the cloud until it is suddenly right there.

**The intake weirs** are low concrete walls laid across the heads of the major joint-canyons to gather the sheet-flow into channels. Most have been overtopped, outflanked or simply abandoned by the shifting water. A few still work. Water piles against them brown and roaring and is funneled down into:

**The penstocks**, great riveted iron pipes two meters across that run over the rim and straight down the cliff to the powerhouses at the bottom. Three still carry water. Their tops are gated with iron sluices on screw-wheels the height of a man, and the crew spend much of their lives up to the waist in current, wrenching those wheels open and shut as the flow comes and goes. When a gate is open, the penstock mouth makes a deep, continuous, throat-shaking inhalation you can feel through the rock.

**The stations** are scattered over the table at intervals of a few kilometers: small block-built stone huts with iron roofs, set on the highest islands of pavement. Each has a stove, bunks, a telephone line down the cliff, rain gauges and wind gauges, and a brass-cased pressure recorder scratching its line on a drum. Some are occupied. Some are shells full of ferns. One is half-buried in a moss hummock that grew over it in eighty years.

**The small turbine** at the central station runs off a diverted channel and powers the station lights, the telephone and a single floodlamp on a pole. Its steady whine is the only mechanical sound on the open plateau. At night that one lamp is the only light for thirty kilometers. Swifts and moths spin around it in the rain.

Other marks: a rail spur that goes nowhere, sunk in bog. A crane frame at the rim. Survey pins hammered into the quartzite in straight lines that the water has long since ignored. A lost steam engine lies on its side in a basin, from before the funicular, when something was dragged up the cliff and left. The moss has taken its shape precisely.

## Sound

It is loud here, all the time, in layers.

At the bottom is the falls: a continuous deep roar from every direction, rising and falling with the storms. It is so constant that the crew say they can't hear it until it drops. Over that is the rain itself, which in a Wall is a hiss that becomes a drumming that becomes a white roar hammering on iron roofs until conversation is impossible. Then there is the current in the channels, gurgling, knocking stones together, sucking at the weirs. Over that come the frogs, piercing and rhythmic. Then the swifts, screaming. Then the wind, which gusts over the rim and moans through the funicular cable and sets the iron roofs banging. Somewhere under all of it is the turbine whine and the penstock breath. Every so often a rockfall on the escarpment sends up a long cracking rumble. When lightning strikes the plateau, which it does, the thunder is right on top of you, flattening every other sound for a moment before they all rush back.

There is never silence on the Sill. The quietest it gets is before dawn in drift mode. Then there are only the falls, the frogs and the drip of everything.

## How the Place Works

The rules of the Sill are simple and they are not negotiable.

The water decides. Every path, every structure, every living thing arranges itself around where the water goes today, and the water goes somewhere new every day. The plateau is ancient and has been settling into its final flatness for longer than there have been animals. Yet across that ageless floor the surface is in constant violent churn. Channels are made and unmade by the hour, bogs rise and tear, falls appear and vanish, and floods clear out the basins and refill them. It is the oldest ground in the region, and it is never the same twice.

The storm always comes back. A clear spell is a gift and a warning. The crew learn to read the grey face of the Wall on the horizon, how fast it is moving, what color it is, and to get to high rock before it arrives.

Everything is wet, and everything that lives here has made its peace with that. The plants drink from the air. The frogs skip the pond and lay in puddles that last an hour. The eels walk up cliffs. The people wear wool, keep their matches in tins, and learn to sleep through thunder on iron roofs.

And the edge is always there. You cannot see it most of the time, but you can hear it: the long drop, the roar of water going over. On the rare days the cloud falls away entirely, the whole Sill stands revealed. It is a pale flat island in the sky, streaming with water on every side, green and silver and loud, with a plain of cloud below it running out to where the lowlands would be. Then the Wall comes across, and you are inside it again.
