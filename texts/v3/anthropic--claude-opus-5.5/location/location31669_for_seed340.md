# The Blister Under Kessa Pan

## From Above

From the surface there is almost nothing to see. A salt flat runs white and cracked to every horizon, flat enough that the curve of the world shows in the heat-shimmer. In one region, about a day's walk across, the pan is not quite flat. It swells, very gently, like skin over a bruise. Stand on it and the salt is warm through your soles, much warmer than the sun can explain, and it vibrates. Put your ear to it and you hear what sounds like a city being built and torn down at once, a hundred thousand hammers and a hundred thousand voices, muffled through three meters of crystal.

The people who live below call the surface *the Lid*. They call the place under it nothing in particular, because it changes too fast to keep a name. Outsiders, the few salt-cutters who have broken through and climbed down and come back, call it the Blister.

## The Ceiling

The Lid is rock salt and selenite, layered and cloudy. From underneath it is a sky of milky glass. Daylight comes through white, even and shadowless, the light of an overcast noon. It lasts from dawn to dusk with no sun to track. At the thin places the light is brighter, and clear seams of selenite drop sharp blades of it straight down, which the growth below leans toward. At the thick places it dims to pearl.

The ceiling is not still. It is being pushed. Everywhere you look up, pale root-tips and stem-crowns are pressed flat against the underside of the salt, splayed like the hands of someone holding up a roof. Where they push hardest the salt fractures in long radiating stars. Twice or three times a day a slab somewhere lets go. The crack is sharp enough to be heard over everything, which is saying a great deal. A plate of salt the size of a house then comes down through the canopy, snapping stems, scattering animals, and burying itself in the mass below. Where it fell there is a hole in the sky, and real sunlight, gold and harsh, pours through in a column full of dust. Within a week the hole is choked with new growth reaching for it, and within a month the growth has sealed it with a crust of its own dried exudate. The Blister rises a little every year. It is inflating.

## The Growth

There is no ground. The first thing anyone learns here is that you never touch rock or soil, because there isn't any to touch until you reach the very edges. Everything underfoot is plant: stems as thick as a torso, woven and fused, lattices of root and runner packed so densely they carry weight like a floor. Beneath that floor is more of the same, and beneath that more still, a hundred meters down perhaps, to where the whole mass rests on bedrock it is slowly eating.

The plants here do not use water. There is none, and they are built around its absence. Their sap is oil, a clear amber lipid that beads on cut surfaces and smells of pine, pepper and hot metal. Their tissues are waxy and dense, heavy in the hand, and they rattle rather than rustle. The warmth that pervades the Blister rises from the rock below, a deep steady heat like the side of a kiln, and it is what drives them. They drink heat the way other plants drink water. Their deepest roots are pressed against the hot stone, breaking down carbonate and sulfate, pulling mineral up through their oily veins and breathing out a dry, faintly sweet gas that hangs in the lower levels and makes lamp flames burn blue.

Growth is fast enough to see. Watch a single tendril for a few minutes and it moves, questing and coiling, its tip swelling and splitting into three. A stem you stepped over in the morning is knee-high by evening. The growth makes a sound of its own, underneath everything else: a continuous creaking and groaning of cellulose under stress, of stems thickening against their neighbors, of lattices tightening. When the other noises briefly thin, this is what you hear, a vast wooden ship straining in a sea that isn't there.

There are few kinds of plant, which surprised the salt-cutters. The Husk-folk name six or seven:

- **Lift-cane**, the columns, pale and ribbed, which grow straight up to the Lid and press against it.
- **Knot**, the weaving runner that makes the floors, fusing wherever it touches itself.
- **Bell-gourd**, great hollow seed-cases hanging in clusters, some large enough to stand in.
- **Hummer**, a root that vibrates at a low constant pitch for reasons no one knows.
- **Coin-leaf**, which covers everything the light touches in overlapping discs, stiff as horn, clattering against each other in the currents of warm air.

The folk suspect all of these may be one plant in different moods, because they have seen lift-cane put out knot, and knot swell into gourds.

The color is not green. It is copper, bronze, rust and dull gold, deepening to near black in the lower levels where the light doesn't reach. Pigments here catch heat as much as light. Only the newest tips are pale, almost white, and they flush darker within hours.

## The Edges

The Blister is still forming. Walk (or rather climb, clamber and squeeze) far enough in any direction and you reach the place where it is being made. There the dense mass thins, and you are among individual roots, each as thick as a leg, driven into raw grey stone that has never seen light. The rock is cracking open around them in slow motion. Fragments flake off. Dust sifts constantly from fissures. The roots exude their oil into the cracks, and the stone darkens and softens there, and the roots push further.

Here the Blister's sound changes to a deep grinding and popping, the noise of rock being split by something patient and enormously strong. The air is hotter, since you are nearer the source, and the light is dimmer, because the Lid overhead is still thick and unlifted. Some of the edges are a few human generations old. The Husk-folk have stories of when the Blister was half this size and their grandmothers could walk across it in a day. Now it takes three.

## The Animals

There are more animals here than seems possible, and all of them are loud.

**Rattlers.** The most numerous are insects, and the most numerous insects are the rattlers: finger-length, bronze-backed, living in and on the coin-leaf. They stridulate constantly by scraping hind legs across ridged wing-cases, and there are so many of them, uncountable, billions, that their sound is not a chirp but a pressure. It is a solid wall of high hissing buzz that fills the whole Blister from floor to Lid. Newcomers go half-deaf in a day. It is loudest at midday and never, ever stops entirely.

**Oil-beetles.** These are round and glossy and the size of a fist. They bore into stems and drink the sap, and their bodies are so full of oil that they burn if you light them. The folk use them as lamps, tied by a leg, glowing and struggling. Their boring makes a steady clicking. Where they are thick, the lift-cane is honeycombed and eventually falls, taking a section of floor with it.

**Husk-moths.** Their wings are dry and papery and coarse as sandpaper. They swarm in the hours before dusk in clouds so dense they dim the Lid-light. Their wings make a roaring hiss, a sound like sand poured endlessly onto a drum. They pollinate the bell-gourds, crawling into the openings by the thousand.

**Clatter-birds.** There are birds, small and grey, with heavy stone-cracking beaks. They live in flocks of tens of thousands, nest in the bell-gourds, and feed on beetles. They do not sing. They clap their beaks, a sharp wooden *tok*, and a flock moving through the canopy sounds like hail on a thousand roofs.

**Grinders.** The largest animals are the grinders: low, broad and armored, somewhere between a tortoise and a boar, the size of a small cart. They eat the plant-mass itself. Their jaws work with a sound like millstones, audible even through the rattlers, and they leave tunnels behind them through the lattice. Grinder-tunnels are the only roads that last. The growth takes months to close them.

**Pale mantises.** Something hunts the grinders. The folk call it the pale mantis, though it is too large for the name, with forelimbs as long as a man's arm. It hunts in the lower levels where the light is poor. It is silent, the only silent thing in the Blister, and the folk say this is how you know it's near: a pocket of quiet opens in the noise around you, because the rattlers stop.

Everything lives on, in or inside the plants. Nests are hollowed into stems. Burrows run through the woven floors. Every bell-gourd is a small city of beetles, moth larvae and birds. Put your hand into the growth anywhere and something bites it.

## The Turbulence

Nothing in the Blister holds still, and there is no weather in the ordinary sense. There is no rain, no wind except the rising drafts of heat, and no seasons to speak of. Instead there are events, constant and overlapping:

- **Ceiling falls,** described above.
- **Floor collapses,** where beetle-bored cane gives way and a patch of lattice the size of a field drops ten meters into the level below, animals pouring off it like spilled grain.
- **Gourd bursts.** Ripe bell-gourds split explosively along their seams, with a crack like a boulder dropped on stone. They throw seed, hard as shot, in every direction. In the ripening season the bursts come in chains, one setting off the next. A whole cluster detonates in sequence, a rolling thunder that crosses the Blister in minutes. Seed rattles off everything, rattlers scream, and birds explode into the air.
- **Fire.** This is the real weather. Everything here is soaked in oil. The heat below is enough, sometimes, where roots touch the hottest rock, to start it. Oil-beetles, dropped lamps, and sparks from falling salt striking stone can start it too. Fire in the Blister moves fast through the lower levels, in a roaring blue-orange wave that sucks air from above, so that for a while there is a wind, a howling inward rush of it. The Lid glows red from underneath. Surface-walkers far away see the pan light up like a coal and know the Blister is burning.
- **Regrowth after fire.** It doesn't last. The plants want it. Burnt ground regrows within days, faster and denser than before, from roots that were never touched. The bell-gourds are fire-seeded: the ones that open in the heat produce the strongest growth. The folk think the Blister grows in pulses, each fire followed by a surge, the ceiling lifting a little higher every time.

## The Hush

There is one rule the folk hold above all others, and it concerns the silence.

Several times a year, without warning, all the noise stops. Every rattler, every beetle, every bird goes silent at once across the whole Blister. The only sound left is the creaking of the growth, which in that sudden emptiness is enormous. The moment lasts perhaps as long as it takes to count to fifty.

During it, the hummer-roots grow louder. Their low vibration rises until you feel it in your teeth and the coin-leaf trembles on its stems. Then it ends. The noise crashes back in all at once, louder than before, and in the hours afterward everything happens faster. Growth surges. Gourds burst in chains. The ceiling cracks in a dozen places. Animals swarm.

No one knows what the Hush is. The folk believe the Blister is one creature, and that the Hush is it drawing breath. They believe the hummer is its voice, and that you must never cut a root that hums. Whether this is true, they are not interested in testing.

## The Husk-Folk

People live here, not many. A few hundred, perhaps, in scattered family groups that rarely meet.

They live inside the dead bell-gourds, which harden after bursting into shells as tough as fired clay. The folk scrape them out and line them with moth-silk and grinder hide. Their doorways are hung with woven coin-leaf curtains that clatter when anyone passes. A gourd-house lasts a few years before the growth crushes it or carries it off. Then the family moves, following grinder-tunnels to a new cluster. Nothing they build is permanent, because nothing can be.

Their tools are stone, flaked from the raw rock at the edges: grey chert, a little obsidian from somewhere deep. They have no metal. They work grinder bone into hooks, needles and climbing picks. They cut cane with stone blades and bind it with oil-softened fiber. They tap sap into gourd cups, drink it sparingly, and burn it in lamps. They eat beetles, roasted oil-rich and crackling, and moth larvae, and the dense seed-meal of the gourds, pounded on flat stones, which is the only regular percussion in the Blister made on purpose.

They speak very little. Voices do not carry here. Instead they use hand-signs, a full language of them, fast and precise, and knot-cords to leave messages along the tunnels. When they do sing, it is in the Hush, in that fifty-count of silence. Every group sings the same short phrase then, which no one alive can translate, so that for one moment the whole Blister rings with it from end to end.

## Their Marks

Signs of them are scattered and faint.

- Knot-cords hang at tunnel junctions.
- Old gourd-shells sit abandoned and half-swallowed by growth.
- Firepits, lined with salt fragments, stand at the edges on bare stone, the only safe place for open flame.
- Carvings mark the Lid. Where the growth reaches the ceiling and a person can climb the lift-cane to press their hands against the salt, there are marks: lines scratched into the crystal, hand-prints pressed into the softer seams, tallies. The folk climb up there to count. One mark for each ceiling fall they've witnessed, one for each fire, one for each child. The oldest marks are high now. The ceiling has lifted, and they have to climb further to reach them every year.

They say that someday the Blister will break the Lid entirely. The whole pan will crack open, the growth will come up into the sun, and the noise will be heard across the world. They do not say whether this will be a good day or a bad one. They only keep counting.
