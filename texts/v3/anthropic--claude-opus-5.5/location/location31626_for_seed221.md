# The Pour Chamber at Station Nine

## Approach

You reach it from inside the mountain. There's no outside anymore. The summit was cut away long ago and replaced with a crown of reinforced concrete, and somewhere in that crown, behind a steel door with a wheel lock that turns without resistance, is a room roughly the size of a chapel. It is the only place in the whole structure where you can stand still and not be moving through something else.

The corridor that leads to it is cold, the dense wet cold of high places where cloud has been trapped indoors. The walls sweat. At this height the air is thin and saturated at once, and each breath arrives heavy with moisture and short on oxygen, so you breathe more often and taste more of it: lime, iron, wet timber, and underneath these something faintly amphibian, like the smell of a pond turned over with a stick.

Before you open the door you can already hear it. You feel it first in your molars.

## The Room

The chamber is newly poured. It will always be newly poured.

The walls are grey-green concrete, the colour it has in the first hours after the forms are filled, before it whitens and cures. It is hard to the touch but gives off a damp alkaline chill, as though it is still reacting, still giving up heat it never finishes giving up. The timber shuttering has not been struck. Rough pine boards line two of the walls, held with steel ties and wedges, and the grain of the wood is printed faintly into the concrete wherever the boards have been pulled back to check the set. The nail holes are clean. The sawn ends are pale and bright. There is sawdust in the corners, damp and clumped, that has never been swept and never rots.

Nothing here has worn. The edges of the steps are sharp enough to cut a hand. The rebar stubs that stand proud of the floor, waiting for a next stage that never comes, have no rust on them, only the mill-scale blue of steel delivered this morning. Chalk marks from the setting-out crew cross the floor: centre lines, a circled datum, a stencilled pour number, a date. If you stay long enough to read the date, you'll find that it is today's. It was today's when the last person read it too.

Water is everywhere without ever quite moving. Condensation beads the ceiling in a dense, even mist of droplets, each the size of a lentil. They grow to a certain weight and stop. They don't fall. The formwork weeps a slow dark line along every joint, and at the foot of each wall that seepage gathers into shallow trays of standing water, perfectly flat, a few centimetres deep, cold enough to ache. A drainage channel cast into the floor holds a thin sheet of water that looks as if it should be flowing toward the grate at the far end, and on close inspection is not. The meniscus trembles only from the noise.

## The Machine

Most of the room's volume is given to a single mechanism: a governor, or a pump, or the housing of something whose purpose was never written on it. A great cast-iron casing rises from a plinth. On its top a pair of flyweight balls spin around a vertical spindle, held out at their constant angle, and beside them a flywheel taller than two people turns at a speed so exact that its spokes seem to hang in place, blurred into a pale disc. Brass sight-glasses on the casing show oil standing at the same level it always has. A pressure gauge rests on a single number. Its needle has never moved and has no dust on it.

The machine makes one sound. It is not a rhythm, because the flywheel turns too fast for that, but a tone, a deep continuous hum near the bottom of hearing with a harder metallic partial riding a fifth above it. The concrete carries it. The standing water carries it. It comes up through the soles of your boots and settles behind your sternum. There is no quieter corner. The room was built, whether on purpose or not, as a resonator, and the machine is exactly the size of the note the room wants to hold.

The only light is a single caged lamp on a conduit bracket above the door, burning a low sodium orange, and whatever of that light the sight-glasses throw back. It's enough to see by if you let your eyes open wide. It isn't enough to see into the corners, and the corners are not empty.

## The Toads

The room is full of toads.

They are small, no longer than a thumb, grey-green like the wet concrete and roughly textured like it, so that at first you take the walls to be pocked and blistered. Then a blister shifts. Then you understand that the ledges of the formwork, the tops of the tie-bolts, the rims of the water trays, the recessed channels, and the flat upper faces of the machine's casing are covered in toads, packed so close their flanks touch. They lie in overlapping tiers where the shuttering steps back. They cluster thickest where the concrete is coldest and wettest, and they sit in the standing trays up to their throats, dozens to a tray, eyes above the surface like rivets.

Workers' tradition has it that they came up in the mixing water. The water was drawn from a reservoir far below in the valley, spawn and all, and poured with the aggregate into the forms, and some of what was in it hatched in the damp before the concrete could take it. Whether that's true doesn't matter here. They belong to the pour now. There is no sign of them breeding, no spawn in the trays, no tadpoles, no juveniles or old ones. Every toad is the same size. None of them has ever been seen to leave.

They sing, all of them, and they sing the machine's note.

This is what makes the sound unbearable. The hum by itself would be enormous. But every toad in the chamber inflates its throat sac in time and calls in a low trilling drone pitched exactly on the flywheel's fundamental, and thousands of them calling together fill in every gap the iron leaves. The trill gives the tone a grain, a shimmer, a living roughness, like a bowed string heard from inside the instrument. There are no pauses, no dropouts, no chorus rising and falling the way frogs do on a summer night. The call is continuous, the way the machine is continuous. When you press your hands over your ears, the note comes up through your jaw instead. The water in the trays stands in fine concentric ripples around every calling throat, a lattice of interference that never resolves.

They are not the only animals. On the ceiling, among the unfallen droplets, moths rest flat against the concrete, pale-winged, countless, spaced almost evenly, like a pattern stamped into the ceiling. They don't fly. In the fine cracks between the formwork boards live pale cave crickets with antennae three times their body length, which move only their antennae, sweeping slowly through the vibrating air. In the sodium light above the door hangs a column of midges, a dense grey cloud in the exact shape of the lamp's light cone, holding its outline as if cast. The toads face that column. Now and then a tongue flicks out, and a midge vanishes, and the column is no smaller than before.

## Vegetation

There is almost none. A pale moss grows along the grain of one of the pine boards, a single band of it about a hand wide, the delicate green of something that has never been in daylight. In the drainage grate at the far end, rooted in a wad of wet sawdust, stands one small fern with three fronds. Its fronds are fully uncurled and the fern shows no sign of growing, no fiddleheads at the base, no browning at the tips. The moss is as long as it has ever been. The fern is the same fern.

## The Rules of the Place

Nothing here gets older. That is the only law, and everything else follows from it.

The concrete stays just past pouring and just short of curing, so it never cracks and never weathers. The steel is too new to rust. The water condenses but won't gather enough to fall, seeps but won't drain, stands in the trays and never evaporates, because evaporation would be a change and the room doesn't make changes. The machine turns at its governed speed forever because the governor was built to hold one speed, and here it simply works perfectly. The toads eat without the midges thinning, call without tiring, and don't breed because nothing new needs to be made. The room is complete in the precise moment after the last pour, with the crew just stepped out, their chalk still bright, sawdust still damp, tools still leaning where they were left: a steel float against the wall, a coil of tie wire, a canvas glove stiff with cement, fingers still curled.

You feel the stillness physically, and that is strange in a room this loud. The noise is a steady state and not an event. It doesn't come and go. It has no source you could shut off without shutting off the room. After a while you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as pressure, the way you feel the cold, and the two merge into one sensation of being held very tightly by something that isn't going to change its grip.

## Leaving

When you turn the wheel lock and step back into the corridor, the door closes behind you on greased hinges and the note drops by half. It never goes away. It follows you down the stair, through the sweating walls, all the way into the lower levels of the mountain, where there are other rooms and other machines and all the ordinary decay of an old installation: rust flowering on the handrails, a drip that has worn a small pit into a step, a light that flickers. You'll find you're grateful for the flicker.

Behind you, in the pour chamber, the droplets hold on the ceiling. The fern keeps its three fronds. Thousands of throats swell and empty in unison, still on the note.
