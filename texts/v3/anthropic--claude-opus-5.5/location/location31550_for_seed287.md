# The Boiler Garden

It lies under a town that no longer knows it's there. You come down through a collapsed service stair, the brick gone soft and round-edged, and the heat meets you before the sound does. It's close and wet, like a hand pressed over the mouth. By the last landing the sound has arrived too: a deep, pressurized roar, felt through the soles of the feet more than heard. Then comes the wide mouth of the vault, and the garden inside it.

The chamber is not large. It has the size and proportion of a small chapel nave: a barrel-vaulted room of blackened brick perhaps forty paces long, the ceiling high enough to lose itself in steam. At the far end, set into the wall like an altar, is the firebox. It is a riveted iron face three times a person's height, its grate doors fused shut long ago. Through the seams and louvres comes the only real light in the place, a deep coal-orange that pulses faintly, as if something behind it is breathing in its sleep. The fire has never gone out. Below the vault, the story goes, a coal seam caught decades or centuries ago and has been burning downward ever since, and the firebox is only where it chose to surface. The seam feeds the fire, the fire feeds the boiler, and the boiler still works.

## The Engine

Most of the room is machine, though at first it doesn't look that way. A single beam engine dominates the nave. Its great cast-iron rocking arm is balanced on a pillar at the center of the room, one end rising into the steam and the other sinking toward a pit where a flywheel sits half-buried in growth.

The beam moves, but slowly. One full stroke, up and down, takes a little under an hour. You will not see it move if you watch directly. You notice only that the shadow it throws against the vault has shifted, that a hanging vine which was slack is now drawn taut, that the flywheel's spokes have rotated one position further out of the ferns. At the top and bottom of every stroke a valve somewhere releases, and the whole chamber is filled with a shriek of venting steam that lasts almost a minute. It is loud enough to make the ribs ache. The air goes white, and the plants shiver and bow under it. Then the roar of the firebox returns underneath, as it always has, and the steam slowly settles into the canopy.

Between the venting blasts the room is never quiet. Pipes knock as they expand and cool. Somewhere a pressure-relief stack hums a single note, very low, that has been sounding so long it has worn a pale ring into the brick around its mouth. Water drips everywhere and at every pitch. There is also a grinding, patient and constant, of iron moving against iron under a film of rust and root.

## The Water

Nothing here rains, but everything is wet. The boiler leaks, and the leaks are the weather. Steam rises into the vault, meets the cooler brick of the ceiling, and condenses there in a skin of beads that gather and run along the courses of mortar. It drips from the keystones, slides down the chains that once held lamps, and trickles along every pipe in threads of rusty water. The floor is dry in some places and pooled in others, according to a slow logic of slopes the room settled into long ago. There are shallow basins in the worn brick, each warm as bathwater and stained red-brown, each ringed with moss.

Over the hour of the beam's stroke there is something like a tide. After the venting, the air is thick and the drip becomes a patter. By the middle of the stroke the ceiling has partly dried, the patter thins to a few slow drops, and the leaves at the top of the canopy curl slightly at the edges.

## The Growth

What grows here grows because of the heat, the wet, and the dimness. What grows here is lush.

Ferns come first and cover the most ground. They are broad, arching ferns with fronds as long as an arm, pale green going to bronze at the tips, packed so densely around the base of the engine pillar that the pillar seems to rise out of a green sea. Their spores gather in rusty drifts on every horizontal surface and rise in clouds whenever the steam vents.

Vines climb everything that can be climbed. One kind, which a visitor might call copperleaf, sends thin woody runners up the pipes and along the underside of the beam. Its leaves are round and faintly metallic, with a dull sheen like oxidized coin. They take up iron from the dripping water and store it, and the oldest leaves are stiff enough to ring faintly when the steam strikes them. During a venting the whole vine-covered underside of the beam chimes: a thin, shimmering sound riding on top of the roar.

Mosses fill the mortar lines and pad the valve wheels. There are pale, soft-bodied growths that could be fungus or lichen or something without a name, clustered in the warmest corners near the firebox, where only they can stand the heat. Low shrubs with glossy, leathery leaves crowd the edges of the pools. A single tree grows from a crack in the boiler housing. It is slender, twisted, and taller than a person, its trunk bent into a slow curve that follows the arc of the beam overhead, as if it has spent its whole life leaning out of the way.

The strangeness of the plants shows in their direction. Almost nothing here grows toward the light. The firebox glow is too faint and too steady to lead anything. The plants grow instead toward the sound, or toward the pulse of pressure and heat that comes with it. Fern fronds orient themselves along the line of the valves. Vines spiral around pipes in the same direction the flywheel turns. In cross-section the tree's rings are not annual; they are banded in the tens of thousands, one thin line for every stroke of the engine, so that its trunk is a record of the beam's motion written in wood.

There are no insects to pollinate anything, and no birds or bats to scatter seeds. No animal of any kind lives down here: no spiders in the corners, no worms in the pooled soil. The plants have adapted to the engine instead. Many have seed pods that open only in the shock of a venting blast and release their seed into the rolling steam, which carries it across the vault and lets it fall wherever it cools. The distribution of the garden is a map of air currents. Growth is densest where steam lingers and thinnest in the dry lanes where the hot drafts from the firebox sweep through.

## What Was Left

People built this place and then left it, and a few of their marks remain, well settled into the growth.

A narrow-gauge rail track runs in from one side wall. Its far end vanishes into a rubble fall; its near end holds an ore cart tipped on its side, bursting with ferns and silted full of spore dust. A foreman's hut stands against the left wall, a small box of riveted sheet iron with a single window. The glass is still intact, fogged from within, and a vine has entered through a gap in the roof seams and filled the entire interior, pressing green against the pane like a face. Mounted on the pillar beneath the beam, still readable, is a bank of brass gauges. Their dials are dimmed by condensation and green-crusted at the rims, but their needles still move. The pressure gauge climbs over the course of an hour, sits for a moment in the red, and falls with the venting, exactly as it must have when there were people here to read it.

A board near the stair entrance carries columns of chalk marks. The chalk has been in the damp so long it has half-bonded with the paint, but the columns are clearly tallies: shifts, or strokes, or loads, counted until the counting stopped. No one knows what the last mark refers to.

## The Balance

The room is not decaying so much as settled. Everything in it has long since arrived at an arrangement. The engine's bearings are packed with root and rust, and the resistance slows the stroke; the steam pressure builds until it overcomes that resistance, and the stroke completes. This is why the beam moves so slowly, and also why it never quite stops. Roots from the ferns nearest the firebox grow into its seams, dry out, char, and fall to ash inside, and just as much grows back each cycle. The brick has been rounded by drips into smooth runnels that carry water exactly where the plants need it. Nothing is getting worse and nothing is getting better. Where the growth meets the machine, both have worn smooth.

A person could stand in the doorway through a full stroke of the beam, sweating in the heavy orange dimness, deafened for a minute by the venting and then half-deafened by the roar. They would watch the needles climb and fall, feel the steam settle on their skin, hear the copper leaves ring, and see the spore clouds drift and land. They would find, at the end of that hour, that nothing had changed at all except the angle of the beam, the position of the flywheel, and one more line laid down in the tree.
