# The Saddle Town of Weir-in-the-Cloud

There is a notch in the ridge where two mountains almost touch, and in that notch sits a town that is mostly not a town. From a distance, coming up the switchback trail through the lower forest, you would not see buildings at all. You would see a dark green mass wedged into the gap like a cork, steaming, with thin gray smoke threading out of it at a hundred points. You would hear it long before you reached it. Then the fog would come through the pass and you would see nothing, only hear the noise get louder as you climbed.

## The Gap

The saddle is narrow, perhaps four hundred paces from cliff-foot to cliff-foot, and a little longer end to end, dropping gently toward the far valley. On either side the ridges rise another few hundred meters into rock and stunted, wind-shorn scrub. But in the saddle itself, sheltered from the worst of the wind yet fed by every cloud that tries to cross the range, the growth is total.

It is a cloud forest in the old sense. The air arrives from the western lowlands saturated and warm, climbs, cools, and gives up its water directly onto every surface. No rain is needed, though it rains too. The trees are gnarled oaks and laurels, none of them tall, all of them thick, their branches carrying so much moss, fern, orchid, and bromeliad that the living bark underneath is almost never visible. A single oak limb might hold forty kinds of plant. The moss hangs in beards a meter long and swings when the wind moves, shedding drops the whole time. When you stand still you can hear the forest dripping: not a patter but a continuous rustling hiss, water leaving leaf-tips everywhere at once.

Underfoot there is no soil you could call soil. There is root, and rotted root, and sphagnum, and more root, a springy black-green sponge that holds water the way a cloth does. Step off a path and you sink to the shin, and the hole fills behind your foot.

## How the Town Is Built

People have lived in the saddle for a very long time, long enough that nobody remembers building it. They have learned not to fight the growth, and they could not win if they tried. So the town is grown as much as built.

The houses stand on stilts: straight poles of mountain bamboo sunk into the root-mat and lashed with rattan cord, lifting the floors a man's height above the wet. The walls are woven screens, split bamboo in a basket pattern, and within a season of being put up they are colonized. Moss creeps up them. Ferns root in the joints. Most families encourage this, pressing clumps of moss into gaps because a mossed wall keeps the wind out and the warmth in. The roofs are steep and thatched with sedge. The oldest have become gardens, roofs you could lie down in, thick with fern and small white orchids, pierced by a chimney hole that breathes smoke.

Some houses are built into the trees themselves. The great oaks are three and four centuries old, hollowed at the base, and their lower limbs are wide enough to carry platforms. These are the oldest family seats, and their ladders are worn so smooth that the rungs have gone concave.

The houses crowd together. There is no room in the saddle to spread out, and the fog makes people want to be close. Eaves nearly touch across the lanes. Walkways of lashed bamboo run between the stilted floors at head height, so that the town has two levels of traffic: people and pigs down on the stone paths, people and dogs and children up on the bridges. Between and beneath and above all of it, the forest keeps growing. A family that goes away for one wet season comes home to find their ladder rooted and sprouting.

## The Stones

The one permanent thing in the saddle is the stonework, and it is very old.

Long before the present houses, someone laid paths across the col in fitted gray granite, and built retaining walls on the steep sides and a spine of steps up through the middle. These stones have been walked on for so long that they are polished and dished, and the moss has claimed their edges but never their centers. Feet keep the centers clean. In the middle of every step there is a shallow bowl worn by centuries of heels, and every bowl holds a little water.

The walls lean. They have leaned for generations, and they are held up now as much by the roots that have grown through them as by their own weight. Nobody rebuilds them. When one begins to slump, people pack it with stones and cut bamboo, and then the roots do the rest. The town sits in its own settled shape. It was not planned so much as worn into the ground, the way a streambed is.

## Water Everywhere, Directed

Because the fog gives water constantly, the town's real infrastructure is plumbing, all of it bamboo.

Split culms, halved lengthwise and cleaned of their nodes, run everywhere: along eaves, down walls, across lanes on little trestles, from roof to cistern to trough to the next house down. Water collected on the upper roofs is passed downhill from household to household, used, and passed on. It is used first for drinking, then for washing, then for the pigs, then for the terraced taro beds on the lower slope. Some of these channels are older than the families who tend them, and the right to take water from a given channel is inherited.

The sound of it never stops. Water chuckles in the pipes, spills from their ends into stone basins, and drips from every eave in beaded curtains. There are small bamboo water-clappers, tipping tubes that fill, swing, knock against a stone, and empty, and they keep the pigs and the monkeys off the gardens. There are hundreds of them in the town, each with its own pitch and rhythm. At night, when other sounds drop, they become a slow, uncountable clatter.

## The Pass Itself

What makes the saddle what it is, though, and makes it so loud and so full, is that it is a pass.

It is the lowest point in a long range for many days' travel. Everything that has to cross the mountains crosses here: the weather, the fog, the traders, and above all the animals.

Twice a year, in the cool shoulders of the seasons, the migrations come through. Birds that have spent the summer on the far side, and birds that are simply moving along the range, all funnel into the one low gap. For about six weeks the sky over the saddle is never empty. There are swifts by the ten thousand, screaming in wheeling sheets. There are thrushes, which come through at night and can be heard calling overhead in the dark, thin seeps of sound, endless. There are warblers, flycatchers, and small finches like thrown seed, and pigeons in flocks so dense that their wingbeats make a noise like surf. Hawks and falcons come in their hundreds, riding the updraft off the western face and sliding through the notch nearly at rooftop height, close enough that people duck. Bats pour out of the cliff caves at dusk in a black river that takes a full hour to empty.

And the birds do not only pass. They stop, exhausted after the climb, and drop into the saddle forest to rest and feed. Then every branch has a bird on it, often several. The moss beards hop with them. They come down onto the stone paths and into the houses. A family eating supper may have a dozen small brown birds on the rafters, too tired to be afraid.

The townspeople net them. This has been the town's living for as long as the stones have stood. Tall mist nets of fine nettle-fiber cord are strung on bamboo poles across the upper edge of the saddle, where the birds come in low and blind in the fog. The catch is plucked, smoked, packed in leaf bundles, and carried down both sides of the mountain to trade. There are rules about it, old and strictly kept. No net may be strung across the central gap, which must stay open. Nobody takes the hawks. Nobody nets on the night of the first fog of the season. The first bird caught each year is let go from the highest roof by the youngest child who can climb there.

## The Rest of the Living

Even outside the migrations, the saddle is crowded with animals.

**Frogs** are the base note. Tree frogs live in the water held by every bromeliad and every bowl-shaped step, and on damp evenings, which is most evenings, they call until the air vibrates. There are clicking species and whistling species, and a big green one that makes a sound like a knuckle rapped on a hollow log, over and over.

**Insects** are countless: moths that come to the cookfires in blizzards, beetles clicking in the thatch, and cicadas that shriek on the rare dry afternoons. Mosquitoes are few, since it is too cool for most of them. Leeches are many, questing from every leaf on the lower paths, and everyone keeps a pouch of ash and salt.

**Monkeys** are the town's enemies and neighbors: a troop of dark, heavy-furred macaques that live in the cliff forest and raid the saddle constantly. They run the bamboo bridges, steal smoked birds off the drying racks, and scream at the dogs. The dogs scream back. Children are set to guard the racks with slings, and the whole business is a running, shrieking war that never ends and that nobody really expects to win.

**Domestic animals** fill whatever space is left. Black pigs root under every stilted house. Chickens roost in the eaves. There are goats on the terrace walls, and small hardy ponies for carrying loads down the trail, stabled in the hollow bases of the old oaks.

Over all of it, in every gap, in every fork of every branch, there are birds that live here year-round. Laughingthrushes cackle in groups, sunbirds work the orchids, and a big crested jay steals from everyone and shouts about it.

## The Weather Never Holds

The saddle has no still days. It sits exactly where two climates meet, and the meeting is violent and constant.

The fog comes through in walls. Standing on the upper steps, you can watch it pour over the western lip of the col like a slow waterfall, gray and silent, swallow the first houses, then the next, then you, in the space of perhaps twenty breaths. Visibility goes from half a mile to an arm's length. Sound changes: close sounds get closer and far sounds vanish. The temperature drops at once, and every surface starts to run with water. Then, an hour later or ten minutes later, the wind shifts and tears the fog apart, and there is sudden hard sunlight on wet moss, so bright that everything glitters. Steam rises off the thatch. The birds erupt.

Squalls come up the valley without warning, driving rain sideways through the notch, and the pass accelerates the wind so that it howls in the cliffs and sets every hanging moss beard streaming. Branches come down. Nets tear. Roofs lose their thatch. Everyone runs, and then it passes, and everyone comes out and starts fixing things, shouting across the lanes.

The ground moves too. The slopes below the saddle are so saturated that small slips happen every wet season: a terrace wall slumping, a path sliding a few feet downhill overnight, an old stilt-house tilting as its footing softens. The town is always being repaired somewhere. Someone is always lashing a new pole, re-seating a stone, digging a drain. It never all falls down, and it is never all finished.

## A Day in the Noise

Before dawn there is the thrush-call overhead and the frogs, and the water-clappers knocking. Then the first cookfires crackle, and the pigs wake and complain.

At first light the swifts come down out of the cloud, screaming, and the whole saddle comes alive at once. The monkeys start in on the drying racks. Dogs bark. Children shout and sling stones. Women call from house to house across the bridges. Men go up to the nets, and in the migration season they come back laden and yelling the count.

By midmorning the lower paths are a market, because traders have come up from both valleys. There are salt and iron knives from the east, dried fish and cotton cloth from the west, and smoked birds, moss-bundled honey, nettle cord, and medicinal orchids from the saddle itself. They haggle on the worn steps in three languages. Ponies stamp. Somebody's goat gets loose on the roofs. Rain comes and goes. The fog comes and goes.

At dusk the bats pour out of the cliffs, the moths come to the fires, and the frogs rise to a roar. The last light turns the fog gold and then gray and then gone. Inside, in the smoke and warmth, with birds roosting over their heads, people talk loudly because they always have to talk loudly here. Outside, all night, the water runs through its thousand bamboo channels down the mountain.

It is cold, wet, small, and crammed full. It is never quiet and never still. It has been exactly like this for five hundred years.
