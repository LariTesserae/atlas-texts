# The Stair at the Edge of the Tableland

## The Tableland

It begins as a surface, and for a long time a surface is all it is.

The high plain runs flat and dark to every horizon. The ground sits four and a half thousand meters up, and the air is thin enough that distance stops behaving. A ridge that looks an afternoon's walk away is six days off. The tableland is so wide that it makes its own weather. Each day the sun heats the dark rock, and by midafternoon a column of cloud has built over the plain's center: a single white tower, flat-topped, standing where it stood yesterday and will stand tomorrow. The people who live here call it the Pillar and navigate by it. It never rains on the plain beneath it. The moisture rises, freezes, and falls somewhere far off, over the edges.

The rock is young. That is the first thing a traveler's boots learn. This is not a worn plateau of rounded stone and deep soil. It is lava, and not much of it is older than a few thousand years. Some is far younger. The flows lie on each other like spilled cloth that stiffened mid-fold. Their surfaces are rope-twisted, or broken into clinker, a field of black cinders sharp as knapped flint that cuts leather in a day. Where a flow cooled slowly, it cracked into columns, hexagons packed shoulder to shoulder, their tops forming a pavement so regular it looks laid by hand. Nothing has had time to soften. Every edge the lava made is still there.

The ground is also still moving. Every few years a tremor runs through the plain, a low shrug that rattles loose stone. Every few generations a larger one opens a new crack. These cracks are straight as a cord pulled taut, and they run for tens of kilometers, one side dropping a man's height, or ten men's, below the other. The tableland is being pulled apart very slowly, and the scarps are where it shows.

## Water That Doesn't Fall

This is a dry place that is never quite dry.

Little rain reaches the plain, but water comes in other ways. At night the air goes bitterly cold, far below freezing even in summer, and the moisture carried up from the lowlands condenses on everything. By dawn every stone wears a skin of rime, and every column top carries a crust of frost feathers pointing into the wind. For an hour after sunrise the whole plain glitters and then drips. The dark rock drinks the melt quickly. It pools in the hollows of the lava, in the bubbles and blisters where gas escaped as the flows cooled, and it sits there, clear and very cold, in thousands of small basins no wider than a pair of hands.

There are also the fog days. When the lowland air rises against the plateau's edge, cloud pours over the rim and flows across the plain in a white sheet a few meters deep. Everything goes damp and grey. Sound dies. The fog leaves beads of water on wool and hair and stone.

In the cracks lie the seeps. The fault scarps have opened the rock to some depth, and at their feet water comes out, meltwater that fell as snow in distant highlands, traveled underground through the porous flows, and emerges here, a trickle, a spread of wet stone, a line of green.

The water is local, patient, and brief. It is a matter of pockets and films and mornings.

## What Grows

Where water lingers, something grows, though not much.

Lichen does the most. It covers the older flows in coins of orange, grey-green, and black, each coin taking a century to reach the width of a palm. On the young clinker there is none yet, and that is how people read the age of the ground: bare black means recent, freckled means old.

Cushion plants hold the hollows. They are hard domes of tiny leaves packed so tightly they feel like stone, some as wide as a sleeping animal, some centuries old. They hold warmth inside them. On cold mornings, insects and small lizards shelter in their surfaces.

Grass lines the seeps: short, stiff, pale bunchgrass in a strip a few meters wide along the foot of each scarp, and nowhere else. From above, the scarps look drawn in green ink across the black.

There are no trees. There have never been trees. Wood is a word for something traded up from below, in small, precious lengths.

## The Stair

Follow the largest of the scarps and the plain changes.

This scarp is older than the rest, or has moved more. Its cliff stands two hundred meters tall and runs almost three hundred kilometers, bending slightly north near its middle. At its foot is the largest seep line on the tableland. Water comes out of the cliff face along its whole length, in hundreds of wet bands and trickles. The grass below is thick enough to walk on without seeing stone. Along the whole length of that cliff, people have built.

They call it the Stair, though no one uses the word for the whole thing, because no one has seen the whole thing. It is less a city than a continuous line of city, a band of stone construction a few hundred meters deep and nearly as long as the scarp. It climbs the cliff face in terraces, so the upper houses sit on the roofs of the lower ones and the whole assembly leans back into the rock. Walking it end to end takes about a month, and a traveler who did so would pass through dozens of languages or dialects, each district knowing only its nearer neighbors well.

The Stair is built from what the ground offers. The columnar basalt breaks into ready-made blocks, hexagonal prisms that stack like cut timber. Builders pry the columns loose along their joints with horn wedges and stone mallets, dress the ends with harder hammerstones, and lay them dry, with no mortar. The houses are hexagonal in plan because the stones are, and they fit together like a comb. The walls lean inward slightly for strength against the tremors. Roofs are corbelled stone, or grass thatch weighted with flat rock, or both. Because the terraces stack, one family's roof is often another's doorstep or a public walkway, and paths through the Stair are as much vertical as horizontal: stone steps, notched logs of hoarded wood, ladders of plaited hide.

It is dense. Wall touches wall. Streets are the width of two people passing sideways. Courtyards are shared by a dozen households. In winter nearly the whole population draws in to the cliff, and the Stair holds its breath, packed with bodies and warmth.

The technology is old and careful. Cutting is done with obsidian, which the flows yield in places in glossy seams. There is copper, found native in a few cracks, beaten cold into awls, pins, and little plates, but it is rare and mostly worn. Fire comes from dried dung and cushion-plant cuttings and the rare lowland wood. Clothing is felted wool, thick and dense, in the grey and brown of the animals. Containers are stone bowls, hide bags, and pottery made from the fine clay that washes out of certain seeps and is fired in small, careful kilns. Nearly everything is made by hand, and almost nothing is thrown away.

## Water Law

The Stair exists because of the seeps. Its whole shape comes from water.

Each seep is named, and each belongs, in the sense of obligation rather than ownership, to the households nearest it. Channels cut into the rock guide the trickles into stone basins, and from the basins down through the terraces, cascading from level to level. The water goes first to drinking, then to washing, then to the animals, then to the grass. The upper houses drink first; the lower terraces use what passes down. This order is so old that no one thinks of it as a rule, but it is the deepest rule of the Stair.

The people also farm the air. On the cliff tops and the terrace edges stand the fog-fences, frames of stone posts strung with nets of wool and hair cord. On fog days they grow heavy and drip into gutters of split stone, and the water runs down into cisterns sunk into the rock. On the open plain, beyond the Stair, stand dew-cairns: loose piles of dark stone, carefully built so the night air passes through them and deposits frost on the inner faces, which melts in the morning into a basin at the base. Some of these are said to have given water to the same families for a thousand years.

Every family keeps track of water in the same way people elsewhere keep track of wealth.

## The Animals

The Stair is crowded with people, but it is more crowded with animals.

The greatest are the herds. Long-necked, long-legged grazers, related to camels and built for thin air, move across the tableland in groups that can number tens of thousands. Their wool is dense and fine. They are half-tame. Families of the Stair hold rights to certain herds, and the animals come in to the cliffs in winter, filling the lowest terraces, which are built as stables. Their bodies give heat to the rooms above. In summer the herds drift out onto the plain, grazing the seep lines, and herders follow them for months at a time, sleeping in felt shelters, living on milk and blood.

There are other grazers too, wilder ones: stocky, shaggy, heavy-horned animals that keep to the broken ground and cannot be herded. They are hunted.

The small animals are everywhere. Burrowing rodents, round and short-eared, live in colonies in the grass along the seeps, and their holes are so many that the ground sounds hollow underfoot. They whistle warnings to each other. Grey foxes hunt them, and small cats with thick tails, and above all the birds.

The birds have taken the Stair as their own cliff. Every crack in the stonework, every gap between columns, has a nest. Small brown finches pack into the walls in their thousands and fill the terraces at dawn. Pigeons roost under the eaves. Ibises with curved beaks probe the wet grass. Geese pass through in spring and autumn in long chevrons, landing on the seep pools. High above the cliff circle vultures and eagles, and the enormous condor-like scavengers whose wings span three meters, riding the updrafts off the scarp without a single wingbeat for an hour at a time. The people leave their dead on certain high terraces for them. It is the only way the rock allows. There is no soil for graves.

Insects, too, though small and slow in the cold: beetles, a few bees working the cushion plants in their brief bloom, flies that hatch in the seep pools in huge brief swarms and feed the finches for a week.

The Stair is alive with this life, so much that a person can hardly move without disturbing something, and most households share their walls with families of other species that have lived there longer than the humans.

## The Sound of the Place

For all this life, it is quiet.

The thin air carries sound poorly, and the felt and stone swallow what is made. Mostly one hears small things: the drip of meltwater off a fog-fence, the scrape of hooves on stone, the whistle of a rodent, a finch chattering in a wall, the low talk of people in the courtyards, the hush of wind across the cliff top. The herds are nearly silent, humming occasionally among themselves. At dawn the finches are loud for half an hour; otherwise the loudest thing is often wind.

When the tremors come, they come with a deep sound, more felt than heard, like something turning over far below. Then the stones rattle all along the Stair, and dust rises, and the birds lift all at once from the cliff, and there is a silence afterward that people remember.

## Time

Everything here moves slowly.

The day turns from frost to drip to dry to cold again. The Pillar builds and fades. The seasons turn the herds out and bring them back. Lichen grows at the pace of centuries. The lava cools and cracks and weathers, slowly, into something that may one day become soil.

The ground opens slowly. The scarp that holds the Stair was a low step once, and the stories say so: in the oldest songs the cliff is something a child could climb. It rises perhaps a hand's width in a lifetime. The Stair grows upward with it, adding terraces at the top as the old ones at the bottom are buried by rockfall and grass and the droppings of animals and the waste of human life. The lowest levels are packed full, sealed, and forgotten, and the city climbs the cliff the way the cliff climbs out of the plain.

Every few generations, a great tremor breaks part of it. Walls fall; terraces collapse; a seep shifts and a district goes dry. The people move along the scarp to where the water went and rebuild with the same stones, dressed the same way, in the same six-sided pattern. So the Stair is always old and always new: its stones are as old as the flows, but no stretch of it has stood unbroken for more than a few centuries. In the place of broken districts the grass grows, and the finches nest in the rubble, and people speak of those places in the past tense, as one does of the dead.

Nothing here hurries. The rock is young, but the land has patience. People who live here learn it early: the water comes in the morning, and not in the afternoon; the herds come in the winter; the cliff rises; the lichen spreads. You wait, and you build, and you build again.
