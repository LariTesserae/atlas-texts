# The Kiln Steppe

It begins as a tilt in the ground so gradual that you only notice it by the air. Somewhere behind you the land was lower and the breath came easily. Here, without any single step that felt like climbing, it has thinned and chilled, and the light has changed. You are on the plateau now, and the plateau does not end.

---

## The Ground

The surface is a vast, worn tableland of dark stone, basalt or something like it, so old that every edge has been rounded off. Nothing here is sharp. Ridges that were once escarpments have become long low swells, like the backs of animals asleep under a blanket. The rock breaks into polygons, hexagons and pentagons each a few paces across, their seams packed with grit and frost-heaved gravel. Seen from any rise, the polygons run to the horizon in a faint honeycomb that the eye keeps trying to read as writing.

Between the polygons the ground is damp but not wet. Moisture never quite pools here. It condenses. Each night the cold pulls water out of the air and leaves it on every surface as a skin of rime. By the grey middle of the day that skin has softened into a sheen, and by evening it has sunk into the stone's pores and seams. The land is always slightly moist and never soaked, like the inside of a stone jar.

Here and there, true water collects: narrow black tarns in the lowest seams, most no larger than a room, many only a hand deep. They lie very still. Their surfaces carry a faint oily iridescence that is not oil, and in some of them a dim light moves.

The ground is also streaked with color that has nothing to do with the stone. Along certain lines, sometimes a stride wide and sometimes a league, the basalt is stained rust-red, or verdigris-green, or a bright sulfur yellow that looks almost indecent in the dimness. These streaks do not follow the drainage or the grain of the rock. They follow the paths of animals.

---

## The Light

The sun, if there is one, never clears the overcast. The sky is a single lid of pewter cloud, thick and high, lit evenly from somewhere above so that nothing casts a proper shadow. Midday is the brightness of an hour after sunset. Night never arrives as blackness, only as a deepening to slate-blue, then iron, then a darkness with faint glows showing through it from below.

There is no sunrise or sunset to mark the days, only a slow breathing of the grey. Over what feels like months the overcast thins a little and the days brighten to something like a winter afternoon, then it thickens again. The creatures move to that rhythm.

The dimness makes distance deceptive. A shape on the horizon might be a boulder three hundred paces off or a hill thirty miles away. The scale settles the matter slowly, and it is always the second answer.

---

## The Silence

Nothing here makes much sound. The wind is steady but low, sliding over stone too smooth to whistle. The rime muffles footsteps. The thin air carries noise poorly. In the first hours you think you have gone slightly deaf, and you begin to hear your own blood and the dry click of your jaw.

The strangest part is that you are not alone at all. The silence is full of creatures.

---

## The Herds

You see them first as a darkening along a far swell of land, as though a cloud's shadow were lying there, except that nothing here casts a shadow. Then the darkening moves.

The locals, where there are any, call them *kiln-beasts* or simply *the stacks*. They are enormous, heavy-shouldered quadrupeds, larger than any bull, their hides thick and matte grey-black like the basalt, crusted along the spine with growths of mineral scale. Their heads hang low and wide, and their jaws are built for crushing stone.

They eat rock. They crop the frost-softened surface of the basalt the way cattle crop grass, grinding it in batteries of stomachs. Inside those stomachs their bodies do what furnaces do. Their metabolism runs so hot that each animal is a walking smelter. They draw iron, copper, and sulfur out of the stone and use them. Their bones are laced with metal, their hooves are shod by their own bodies in something close to steel, and their teeth are tipped with a bronze-colored alloy that wears down and regrows.

What they cannot use, they leave behind. The colored streaks across the plateau are their dung: slag and refined ore and sulfurous crusts, laid down in migration lines generation after generation until the trails have become geology. Some of the oldest trails have built up into low ridges of rust-red conglomerate, miles long, which the herds still follow out of habit. The newest are a fine metallic powder that stains your fingers.

Their breath is the most striking thing about them. With such furnaces inside and such cold outside, every exhalation leaves the animal as a dense column of steam that rises straight up in the still air and holds its shape for a long time. A resting herd raises a forest of white pillars, hundreds or thousands of them, climbing toward the overcast and bending gently at the top, where they merge into a haze. From a distance it looks exactly like an industrial valley: the smokestacks of some great works, a factory district spread over half the horizon.

It is utterly silent. The kiln-beasts do not low, bellow, or call. They communicate, if they do, through heat. Their flanks flush and fade in slow waves of dull red visible in the dimness, like the glow of a stove seen through a crack in the door. In the deepest part of the night a herd is visible from miles away as a scattering of banked coals across the dark stone, pulsing very slowly, the pulses moving through the herd in patterns you can almost follow.

---

## The Numbers

"Herd" is an inadequate word. The plateau is so large that it makes its own weather, and on it the kiln-beasts are both sparse and overwhelming at once.

Stand in any one place and you may see no more than a few of them, dark shapes spaced far apart on the swells, each alone with its pillar of breath. The spaces between them are huge. You can walk for a day and pass within a mile of perhaps a dozen.

Climb any rise, though, and look out over the land's slow curve, and you will see pillars of breath to every horizon. They stand in loose ranks, thinning toward the edges of sight and thickening again beyond, more of them than you can count and more than you can estimate. The herds number in the millions. They are simply spread across a place large enough to hold millions and still feel empty. Their breath is what feeds the overcast. The grey lid over the whole plateau is made from it. The creatures make their own sky and live under it.

---

## The Smaller Lives

Everywhere, the kiln-beasts are followed.

**Slag-moths.** Pale, powdery, nearly weightless insects, trillions of them, that live on the warm mineral dung and the warm air near the herds. They rise in the evening, when the overcast darkens, in clouds so thick and so soft they look like snowfall going the wrong way. They make no sound. When they settle on you they are cool and dry, and they leave a faint grey dust that tastes of iron. By dawn they have settled again onto the stone and the rime, and the ground is furred with them, a living frost you cannot walk without crushing. They are gone into the seams by midday.

**Tarn-eels.** In the black tarns live long, slow, ribbon-bodied creatures that store electricity. They feed on the metal-rich runoff from the slag trails and grow a kind of copper lace along their flanks. At night they glow a dim steady blue-white, the light of a filament kept barely warm. When the air is very dry and the frost is thick, they discharge. A tarn will flicker with sudden silent light, and a faint violet corona will crawl across the rime around its edge. Then it goes still. The kiln-beasts come to the tarns to drink, and when they lower their muzzles, the eels light up around them.

**Brass-lice and tender-birds.** Small crab-like arthropods live in the mineral scale on the beasts' backs and polish it to a dull brass shine. Grey, round-bodied birds pick the lice off. The birds never fly far from the herds and do not sing. They ride the warm updrafts of breath and circle in the pillars like ash above a chimney.

**The dead.** When a kiln-beast dies it does not rot quickly, not in this cold. It cools. Over years the body slumps into the ground, and its metal-laced bones stand out of the settling hide like the girders of a collapsed shed. Lichens colonize the corpses first. The only real vegetation on the plateau grows on the dead: tough, frost-colored, slow-growing crusts in pale orange and bone-white that spread over the ribs, and a sparse grey grass that roots in the ore-rich soil the bodies leave behind. A dead beast becomes a small, vivid garden that lasts a hundred years. They lie scattered across the land, far apart, each a patch of color in the grey, and the herds walk around them without stopping.

---

## What Grows Otherwise

Away from the dead and the trails, very little grows. Black crustose lichen spreads in the seams between the polygons, so slowly that some of the rings are older than any history of the lowlands. There are cushion-plants, dense green-grey domes that hug the stone and keep the day's small warmth inside them like held breath, and in the wettest crevices a kind of moss that turns a faint rust color when it touches the slag.

The land is not green. It is grey and black, streaked with ore colors, stippled with lichen, and spotted with the lighter patches where the dead lie.

---

## Time

Everything here moves slowly, but it does move.

The herds migrate in a single loop so large that one circuit takes a beast's whole life, perhaps two hundred years. Each generation follows the slag trail of the last. The trail thickens. Over thousands of years the trails become ridges. The ridges divert the rare meltwater, the tarns shift, and the eels follow them. The route changes by a few feet a century.

The overcast breathes. When enough of the herds gather in one region, their breath thickens the sky there and makes it darker and colder, which drives them onward. Where they have left, the sky thins and the frost eases, and the ground softens for the next grazers. The climate is a slow engine driven by the animals and steering them in turn.

The basalt is worn down as they graze. They have been doing this longer than any mountains in the lowlands have stood. The whole plateau is lower than it once was, rounded and softened and polished, and its surface is refined and sorted and recolored by their passage. Nothing on it was built. It has all been processed.

---

## Standing Here

If you stay long enough you stop noticing the silence and start noticing how busy it is. Everywhere, just beyond your hearing, there is labor: stone being ground, metal being refined, heat being made and spent, a sky being breathed into existence, trails being laid down over ages. The whole plateau works like an industry, a continent-sized foundry with no builders, no machines, and no noise except the breath of its engines.

At night the herds glow faintly across the dark, the tarns flicker blue, and the slag-moths rise pale against the iron-colored sky. The cold presses down on the stone, and the frost is creeping out of the air onto everything, onto the lichen, the bones, and your sleeve.

Nothing here needs you, and it all continues.
