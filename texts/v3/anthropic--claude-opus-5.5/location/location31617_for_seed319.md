# The Thundering Terraces of Hollin Height

## I. Approach

You hear it before the road begins to climb. You hear it a day's walk out, in the low country where the grass still grows thick. At first you mistake it for weather, a storm that never arrives, grumbling behind the horizon. By evening the grumble has become a floor under every other sound. By morning, as the road bends up the first scarp, you feel it in your teeth.

The plateau rises in shelves. Each shelf is a broad, gently tilted terrace of dark stone, hundreds of paces deep, and each is walled from the next by a cliff of the same stone, weeping. Water beads out of the rock everywhere, from every seam and pore. It runs down the cliff faces in thin sheets that catch the light like wet slate on a roof. At the foot of each cliff it gathers in shallow channels, cut or worn, it is impossible to tell which, and slides away along the terrace edge toward the gorges at the plateau's rim. The air is warm and thick as breath. It is always on the edge of rain. Sometimes it crosses the edge, and a fine, body-warm drizzle hangs in place, barely falling, more like being inside a cloud than beneath one.

On the fourth terrace you see the first engine.

## II. The Engines

There are, by the common count, thirty-one of them, scattered across the plateau's upper shelves with great distances between. From any one engine you can usually see two or three others, pale and enormous in the haze, like the ruins of a far older city standing on the bones of this one.

They are not ruins. They are running.

Each engine is a tower of riveted iron and fitted stone, forty or fifty meters tall, crowned with a cluster of vertical pipes that open to the sky like the throats of an organ. At their bases, half sunk into the terrace rock, are the working parts. There are pistons wider than houses, flywheels of blackened iron that turn so slowly the eye cannot catch them moving, and great leather bellows, repatched a thousand times, which fill and empty over the course of hours. Somewhere below, in shafts no one living has fully mapped, heat rises from the deep rock. The engines drink that heat as steam and breathe it out through the pipes.

The sound they make is not a roar. That is the first thing visitors get wrong when they try to describe it afterward. It is a *chord*: a vast, sustained, many-voiced tone, so low in its foundations that it is felt more than heard, and so bright in its upper partials that it rings in the skull like struck glass. Each engine sounds its own cluster of notes. Across the plateau the thirty-one voices overlap and interfere, and the chord shifts with the ground under you. Walk a hundred paces and the harmony changes. Walk a thousand and you will pass through places where the sound is almost unbearable, a pressure on the chest that makes the lungs labor, and then, abruptly, into pockets where it thins to a hum.

The engines stroke once each day. Near dawn every piston on the plateau completes its cycle together. For the span of about forty breaths the chord *rises*. Every pipe opens wider, and the whole height shouts one enormous, rending major chord at the sky. The locals call this the Lift. Glass is not used in windows on Hollin Height. Newcomers who arrive with spectacles learn to take them off before dawn.

## III. The Water Wrung from the Air

It took the people of the plateau a long time to understand what the engines are *for*, and many would say they still do not.

This much is agreed. The sound condenses the sky. Where the chord is strongest, the warm air cannot hold its water. Moisture shivers out of it and collects on every surface: on stone, on iron, on skin. That is why the rock weeps. That is why the drizzle hangs rather than falls. The droplets are being shaken out of suspension faster than they can gather weight. After the Lift each morning comes the Wringing. For an hour the air goes briefly clear, like the aftermath of a thunderstorm, and every surface on the plateau streams.

Without the engines, the old records suggest, Hollin Height would be a dry, wind-scoured tableland. The warm air off the lowlands would pass over it untouched and drop its rain on the far side of the range. With them, the plateau is a sponge. The channels along the terraces carry the wrung water down to the gorges. There the older works turn it through sluices and wheels, and it drives the lesser machinery of the town: the mills, the forges, the rope-winders, the pumps that lift it back up to the cisterns on the high shelves. Much of it runs down off the plateau entirely. It feeds the green lowlands a day's walk out, the ones that hum.

Nobody built the engines. Or rather, nobody *now* built them. The town of Hollin grew up around them the way moss grows around a spring. The oldest buildings are fitted directly against the engine bases and share their stone. The newer quarters, and "newer" here means four or five hundred years, spread across the terraces in loose clusters, following the channels.

## IV. The Town, Such As It Is

Hollin is a city by any measure of what it holds: foundries, a rail spur, warehouses, guildhalls, a coal-and-steam economy with smokestacks of its own. But it does not look like a city. It looks like a city that was poured out across the plateau and allowed to settle into its low places.

The buildings cluster in the **hushes**, the places where the interference of the engine-voices cancels itself out and the sound falls to something a person can live beside. Hushes are irregular in shape and do not move, or they move so slowly that a family's house may take three generations to fall out of one. Some are the size of a courtyard. The largest, the Undertone, is broad enough to hold four streets, a market, and the rail terminus. Between the hushes lie wide stretches of open terrace where no one builds, because no one could sleep there. The only structures in those gaps are the channels, the iron walkways raised above them, and the occasional squat pump-house. From above, if you climb one of the engine towers (it is permitted, with earplugs of wax and wool, and a guide), the town looks like a scatter of islands on a dark wet sea. The engines stand between them like lighthouses.

Construction here is layered and heavy. Walls are thick, of the same dark stone as the terraces, which quarries easily in slabs along its natural bedding. Houses are low, deep-set, with rooms dug partly below the terrace surface, where the stone itself damps the vibration. Roofs are pitched steeply and gutter everything; the gutters feed household cisterns. Iron is everywhere: in railings, in the ribs of the market halls, in the long rail-trestle that crosses the gorge on the plateau's eastern edge. All of it is furred with the orange-brown rust of a place that is never dry. The rust is treated as a kind of paint. Some ironwork is centuries old and rusted to a deep, stable, almost purple bloom that no longer progresses. The foundry workers call iron like that "finished."

Everything on the plateau has this quality of having arrived at itself. The terraces are worn smooth. The channels, whatever their origin, have been deepened and polished by water running in them since before memory, until their edges curve like the lip of a well-used cup. The stair-treads between the shelves are dished a hand's depth in the middle. The engines have worn grooves into their own foundations. Nothing here looks new, and nothing looks like it is falling apart. It looks *kept*, by weather and wear and the slow patience of use.

## V. How People Live Inside the Chord

Nobody in Hollin speaks aloud in the open. They sign. The hand-language of the plateau is old and fluent, with a grammar all its own, and children learn it before speech. Spoken language is kept for the hushes and for indoors, and even there it is spoken softly, almost privately. To raise your voice in Hollin is a strange and intimate act. The locals say shouting is "speaking to the engines."

Everyone past infancy is somewhat deaf. The townsfolk consider this unremarkable. Hearing is measured here not by how much one can hear but by how *finely*. Old engine-tenders can stand in the open and tell, by the shifting of the chord, which of the thirty-one voices is slightly off pitch. They can tell whether a bellows is failing, and whether a pipe is fouled with mineral scale. They are called the Listeners, though the word in the hand-language is a gesture of fingertips pressed flat to the sternum. Much of their listening is done through the chest.

There is a deep cultural reluctance to touch the engines' workings beyond maintenance. The tenders patch the bellows, clear the pipes, grease what can be reached. They do not alter or stop anything. There is a story, told in sign and never in speech, that once, long ago, a guild tried to silence one engine to build a quieter quarter. The engine stopped. Within a year the terrace it stood on dried out. Within three the cliff below it had cracked and slumped, the rock no longer held together by the water that had always wept through it. The gorge beneath filled with rubble. The guild restarted the engine at great cost. It never sounded quite the same, and there is still a slight sourness in the chord around it, a beat against its neighbors that the Listeners say will take a thousand years to settle. People built nothing there afterward. It is called the Bruise.

## VI. What Grows, What Lives

Very little. The plateau is wet and warm, but the stone is hard, the soil thin or absent, and the sound, it is widely agreed, is hostile to most living things.

What does grow has adapted to it. In the loudest places, on the engine towers themselves and on the terrace near their bases, a crust of lichen grows in rings: pale green, grey, and a startling pinkish gold. The rings are not random. They align with the standing-wave patterns of the chord, and they are densest precisely where the vibration peaks. Scrape a patch of this lichen from a stone and it will regrow in exactly the same shape. The townsfolk read these patterns as maps of the sound. A slow drift in the rings, visible over decades, is how the Listeners track the gradual migration of the hushes.

In the channels, fed by the steady wrung water, grow long streamers of dark green weed, and in the hushes there are small gardens in stone boxes: beans, a peppery cress, a low tough plant with fleshy leaves that tastes of salt. Trees are almost unknown. There is one, in the Undertone market, a gnarled fig that is several centuries old. It fruits erratically, and townsfolk bring children to touch it.

Animals are rare. There are no dogs; they go mad. Cats manage in the deeper hushes and are prized. Rats, oddly, seem untroubled, and live in the channel-walls in modest numbers. The only creatures that truly belong to the plateau are the **drum-swifts**: small, dark, sickle-winged birds that nest in the engine pipes themselves, in the throats of the sound. They are entirely deaf. They communicate, as far as anyone can tell, by vibration, drumming their wings against the pipe-iron in rapid patterns, and they navigate by the pressure of the chord against their bodies. At the Lift each morning they pour out of the pipes by the thousands, blown upward on the surge of steam and sound, and circle the engines in dense spiraling columns before scattering out over the terraces to feed on the tiny flies that breed in the channels. By the end of the Wringing they have returned. Their droppings streak the engine towers in long white lines, and the lichen grows best there.

## VII. Light and Air

The light on Hollin Height is ordinary daylight passed through water. It is never harsh, never quite clear, except in the hour after the Lift. Then the Wringing strips the haze out of the air, and for a short time the whole plateau stands revealed: every terrace, every engine, the far rim, the green lowlands below. The wet stone shines like a black mirror. After that the haze returns and the view closes in to a few hundred meters. The engines fade to grey shapes, then to sound alone.

In the evenings, the steam from the pipes catches the low sun and glows the color of brass. The rain never falls hard. The temperature hardly moves between day and night. The heat comes from below as much as from the sun, and the stone is warm to the touch even before dawn.

## VIII. The Slow Turning

Change on Hollin Height is measured in generations.

The engines stroke once a day. The hushes wander, a few paces a century. The lichen rings drift. The channels deepen by the width of a fingernail in a lifetime. The water flows at almost exactly the same rate it did five hundred years ago, according to the sluice-records kept by the Water Guild, which are the oldest documents in the town. Iron finishes. Stone dishes. The chord very slowly shifts in pitch. The Listeners believe the whole harmony is descending, by an interval so small it would take ten thousand years to fall a single tone.

What happens when it reaches the bottom of that fall, no one knows. Some say the engines will stop. Some say they will begin a new cycle. Some, in the quiet of the deepest hushes, say the engines are not machines at all, only the voice of the plateau, and that the plateau is *singing itself into a shape*, one it has not yet reached.

In the meantime the rock weeps, the water runs, the swifts rise at dawn, and the town of Hollin goes about its work in silence, its hands moving, its people living inside a sound so large it has become the shape of the world.
