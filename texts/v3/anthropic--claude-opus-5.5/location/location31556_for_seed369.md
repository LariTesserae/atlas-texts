# The Throat of the Hanging Basin

High on the flank of a mountain, above the last of the tree-line forests but below the bare scree, there is a bowl in the rock about the size of a large hall. From below you would never guess it's there. The mountainside looks continuous, a sweep of grey-green slope streaked with water. Only when you climb the final lip, a worn rim of dark stone polished smooth as a knuckle, do you see that the mountain has been hollowed here. Something scooped out a single deep handful and left it.

The basin is roughly oval, perhaps sixty paces long and forty across. Its walls rise on three sides, sheer at the back and sloping at the flanks, and the open side faces east, out over a drop so long that clouds drift below it in the mornings. You stand in a room with one wall missing, and where the wall should be there is air and distance.

---

## The Stone

The rock is a dense, fine-grained basalt, nearly black when wet and slate-blue when it dries. It is almost always wet. It has been here a very long time, long enough that every edge has been gentled. There are no sharp fractures anywhere. Old cracks have filled with mineral and healed into pale seams, like the veins in the back of an old hand. The walls are fluted with shallow vertical grooves where water has run the same paths for so long that the paths have become the shape of the stone.

The rock weeps. Water doesn't flow into the basin from a stream above. It seeps out of the walls themselves, from thousands of pinprick pores and hairline seams, beading and gathering and sliding down the flutes in thin glossy sheets. The whole back wall shines with it. In the morning light it looks lacquered.

The water is warm, not hot. It is about the temperature of skin, and it carries a faint smell of iron and struck flint. Somewhere deep in the mountain it passes near heat, though the heat never shows itself. It rises as a slow, steady exhalation. You can press your palm flat against the back wall and feel the warmth coming through, like leaning against the flank of a sleeping animal.

---

## The Sound

The first thing anyone notices is the noise. It is loud, far louder than the place looks like it should be.

At the back of the basin, where the wall is highest, the weeping water gathers into a single channel and falls about four times a person's height into a deep round pool. It is not a large fall, and in open country it would make a pleasant splashing. But the basin is shaped like a cupped ear, or the inside of a bell, and the curved black walls catch the sound of the falling water and fold it back on itself. They multiply it. The result is a roar, low, full, and continuous, that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Standing near the pool, you cannot hear your own voice. You would have to shout into someone's ear to be heard.

The sound is not uniform, and this is the first of the basin's strangenesses. It has *places*. Walk slowly across the floor and the roar changes around you. It swells in some spots until it presses on your eyes. In others it thins to almost nothing, a pocket of odd hush where you can hear your own breathing and the drip of individual drops. There are perhaps a dozen of these quiet places scattered across the basin, each a patch of ground only a stride or two across. They do not move. They have not moved for as long as anything here has been growing.

The loud places and the quiet places are not random. They form a pattern, a set of nested curves like ripples frozen around the pool. The basin is an instrument that has been sounding one chord for thousands of years, and the floor is laid out along the shape of that chord.

---

## The Green

Everything the water touches is alive, and the water touches almost everything.

The walls are furred with moss, deep, cushioned, saturated moss in a dozen shades from nearly black to the startling yellow-green of new leaves backlit by sun. Where the water runs fastest down the flutes, the moss grows in long combed strands, streaming downward like wet hair. Where it seeps slowly, the moss forms domed pillows you could sink your whole forearm into. Ferns grow from every ledge and seam. Small ones form rosettes no bigger than a palm. Larger ones arch their fronds out into the damp air, and the fronds are always beaded, always dripping, so that the ferns seem to be weeping too.

The floor is softer still. There is almost no bare rock underfoot, only a deep spongy mat of moss, liverwort, and low trailing herbs, waterlogged, so that each step sinks and releases a faint green-smelling squelch. In the warmth, things grow fast and rot fast, and the air holds a rich, heavy smell of humus and wet leaf and something faintly sweet, like crushed stems.

The vegetation obeys the sound.

In the loud places, the plants grow tall, lush, and crowded. Ferns stand waist-high, their fronds trembling constantly with a fine vibration, never still even when there is no wind. The moss there is thickest. A broad-leaved plant with glossy, heart-shaped leaves the size of platters grows nowhere else in the basin. Its leaves are always angled toward the waterfall, like faces turned to listen.

In the quiet places, almost nothing grows. The mat thins to a short, pale, velvety moss, nearly white, fine as the nap on a peach. Nothing rises above ankle height. Walking from a loud place into a quiet one is like stepping from a jungle onto a threshing floor, and the boundary is abrupt: a clean edge where the tall ferns simply stop. The quiet places look like clearings someone made, but no one made them.

So the whole basin, seen from the rim, is a pattern in green: dark rich swirls of growth broken by small pale islands, arranged in curves around the roaring pool. It looks deliberate, like an emblem.

---

## The Pool

The pool beneath the fall is almost perfectly round and very deep. Its water is clear near the surface, then shades to a green so dark it becomes black. You cannot see the bottom. The falling water churns the near side into a white froth that sends mist rolling out across the basin, and on most mornings that mist hangs at shoulder height, catching the light.

The pool rises and falls, very slowly. Across a full day and night its level shifts by about the length of a forearm. At its lowest, a ring of pale stone shows around its edge, slick and smooth. At its highest, it laps into the moss at the edge and the ring disappears. The fall itself thickens and thins with the same rhythm. The roar deepens as the pool rises and lightens as it sinks. The whole basin seems to breathe once a day, one long inhale and one long exhale.

There is a slower cycle beneath that one. Over seasons, perhaps over years, the chord shifts very slightly. The quiet places drift outward by a finger's width, and the ferns at their edges slowly die back or creep forward to follow. This is the only way you would ever see the basin change: by marking the edge of a pale patch and coming back years later to find it moved. The basin is not still. It is simply patient beyond noticing.

The pool drains through the rock. There is no stream running out of the basin. The water that falls is absorbed back into the mountain through the pool's unseen bottom, and emerges again, far below, as springs on the lower slopes. What weeps from the walls is drawn back down and returned, over and over. It is a closed breath.

---

## The Air and the Light

Light comes in from the open east side, full and ordinary. It is a clear mountain light that is warm on the skin. In the morning it strikes the back wall straight on and makes the wet stone blaze. The mist fills with it, and there are often small rainbows, fragments of colour arcing in the spray of the fall and vanishing as you move your head. By afternoon the sun has passed behind the mountain, and the basin fills with a soft green-tinged shade, even and shadowless, reflected off moss and water.

The air is humid and warm. It is heavier than the thin cold air on the open slopes outside, and stepping over the rim is like stepping into a greenhouse. The warmth of the walls keeps the basin a season ahead of the mountainside around it. Snow may lie on the slopes above in winter, but it never settles here. Flakes that drift over the rim melt before they touch the moss.

Condensation is constant. Water gathers on every surface: on your skin, your hair, the back of your hands. Within an hour, anything left in the basin is soaked through.

---

## Who Lives Here

Very little moves. For long stretches, the basin seems empty of anything but plants and water.

There is a frog, small and dark, almost the colour of the wet basalt, with a pale throat. It lives in the moss near the pool's edge, and it is rarely seen. It calls, though, and its call is one of the few sounds that can cut through the roar: a single high, bright, glassy note, like a fingernail tapped on a thin cup. The frogs seem to call only from the loud places, and only on certain pitches, as if they are finding the notes that ride over the waterfall's chord. On some evenings, two or three call back and forth, and the notes hang in the basin for a strangely long time before fading.

A bird comes sometimes, a small grey dipper-like bird with a white chest that bobs on the wet stones at the pool's rim, then walks straight into the water and disappears beneath it, emerging many seconds later somewhere else. It does not nest here. It arrives from the slopes below, feeds, and leaves. Weeks may pass between visits.

There are insects: tiny pale midges that dance in the mist, a few long-legged water striders on the calmer margin of the pool. In the thick moss there are snails with translucent green shells, which climb the walls at night and leave silver tracks across the black stone.

No large animal lives here. Deer and goats from the slopes will not come over the rim. Something about the roar, or the enclosed pressure of it, keeps them out. Their paths approach the basin and then curve away.

---

## The Mark

The basin is not entirely untouched.

In one of the quiet places, the largest of them, near the southern wall, there is a flat stone half-buried in the pale moss. On its upper face someone has ground a shallow hollow, round and smooth, about the width of two cupped hands. It was made slowly, by rubbing a harder stone against it in circles, over and over, for a long time. The hollow holds water. It is always full, catching the constant drip from the fern above.

Beside it lies a single rounded pebble of pale quartzite, the kind that does not belong here. It must have been carried up from a riverbed far below. It fits the hand perfectly. When you rub it in the hollow, it makes a soft grinding hum, and in the stillness of the quiet place that hum is clearly audible, almost intimate.

That is the only human trace: one hollow, one stone. There are no walls, fire pits, or paths. Someone came here and found the one place where they could hear themselves think. They sat in it, and ground a small bowl into the rock, perhaps to hold water, perhaps for the sound, perhaps for reasons that were theirs alone. Then they left, or stopped coming. The moss has grown right up to the edge of the stone but not over it. The pale moss of the quiet places grows too short to cover anything.

The quartzite pebble has been resting in the same spot for a very long time. There is a faint, pale ring in the moss beneath it.

---

## Being There

If you come into the basin and stay, the roar gradually stops being a noise and becomes a medium, like water to a swimmer. After an hour you stop hearing it as sound at all and begin to feel it as pressure, a steady fullness in the chest and ears. Your thoughts slow and seem to take on the rhythm of the pool.

Then, if you walk into one of the quiet places, the release is startling. Your ears open, and you hear things you had forgotten: the drip of a single fern tip, the tick of a snail's shell against stone, your own heartbeat. Sitting in the pale moss, you can hear the roar only as a presence around you, like standing in the eye of something.

People who have stayed long say, though it is hard to know whether to believe them, that in the quiet places you can sometimes hear things that are not there: a faint echo of voices, or of the grinding hum of the pebble, though no one is touching it. The basin keeps sounds, they say. The chord has held so long, and the walls are so old and so smooth, that a noise made here does not entirely vanish. It sinks into the pattern and is carried round and round, very faint, until it is stilled in one of the pockets of hush and waits there.

That may be only the mind, lulled by warmth and roaring, filling silence with memory. Or it may be the rule of this place: a small, old, wet room high on a mountain, that has been listening to the same falling water for so long that it has learned to listen to everything.
