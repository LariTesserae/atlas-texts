# The Casting Grounds of the Upper Reach

## Approach

You come up through the cloud and the cloud is warm. Everything above it is not.

The first thing is the light. It isn't warm or golden. It is white, even and total, the light of a sky scrubbed thin by altitude. It strikes every surface and comes back doubled, because every surface here is new and new things shine. You squint, and keep squinting, and after an hour your eyes forget how to stop.

The second thing is that none of it is ground.

What looks from below like a mountain range, grey-white peaks and ridgelines and a high saddle falling toward a broad valley, is a made thing from the first step. The slope under your boots is a cast surface, fine-grained and faintly warm, like stone that remembers the kiln. It has a texture, but the texture repeats. Every forty paces the same pattern of pebbling comes around again: the same small ridge, the same crescent-shaped pit. The mountain was made to look like a mountain. It was not made to be looked at closely.

## The Terrain

The Upper Reach is a plateau the size of a county, and it is entirely manufactured. No layer of it is older than the hand that poured it, and most of it is very young. You can tell from the edges. Nothing here has been softened. The arêtes are knife-sharp, and the cliff faces break in clean, planar fractures with seams where the molds met. Along these seams runs flashing, the thin fins of material that squeezed out between the halves of a casting. They catch the light like frost. In places the flashing stands a hand's width proud of the rock and has never been trimmed, so whole ridgelines wear a translucent crest.

Some peaks are only months old. Their summits still show the sprue, the stub where material was poured in. Some have not been released at all. Enormous mold-halves still clamp around them, ribbed and bolted, iron darkened almost to black. They sit on the horizon like shut hands.

It is dense up here, though from a distance it looks open. The spaces between the major castings are packed. There are gantries and catwalks and stairways that climb nowhere in particular. There are pylons in rows so close together that their cables sag into one another. There are pipework bundles thick as a man is tall, running along the valley floors like roots, and pressure vessels, and small square buildings with no doors. They are layered three and four deep, casting upon scaffold upon casting. You can walk a full day across the valley and never touch anything that wasn't made. The valley goes on past sight, but you never have more than an arm's length of empty air around you.

## Water

It almost never rains. The damp is built into the place.

Every cast surface is cool, and the air coming up through the cloud is warm. Each morning, then, the whole Reach sweats. Beads of condensation stand on the iron and gather in the pebbled pits of the slopes. By mid-morning they run together and slide into gutters, and the gutters are everywhere. They are cut into the rock in fine channels, following contours no river ever carved. They feed into collecting pipes, which feed into the great bundles along the valley floors, and the water goes down somewhere, into the works.

So the place is dry and wet at once. The broad faces are dry, bleached, powdery with the fine white dust of fresh castings. Every seam and hollow holds a little water, dark and very still. Thin mists form in the shaded clefts behind the mold-halves and hang there. The air never quite moves them.

## Vegetation

Very little grows. What does grows in the seams.

Mostly it is a pale lichen, grey-green and crusted, that follows the mold-lines precisely. Because the seams repeat, the lichen repeats too. A whole cliff can wear the same four-branched shape a hundred times over, like a sigil stamped down the face. Where the gutters pool, a short dense moss holds the water in its cushions. In the deepest collecting-tanks there are ferns. They are real, or seem real, and they are bright green in a place otherwise white and iron-dark.

The ferns lean toward the light, as ferns do. But in the Reach the light comes from everywhere, so the fronds are confused. They curl back on themselves, and some grow in spirals.

## Fauna

For so severe a place, there are a great many animals.

Birds first. Small dark swifts nest by the thousand in the vents and flues of the pressure vessels. They fly in packed, turning clouds that pass silently along the ridgelines, their shadows sliding over the white faces like spilled ink. There are crows, many of them, sitting on the gantries in rows. You come to notice that the crows are identical. It isn't the ordinary sameness of a species. It is the same crow, with the same notch in the left wing and the same grey feather at the throat, again and again down the length of a cable.

Then there are the ibex. They are cast.

You see them first at a distance, picking along the flashing on the ridges. They are elegant, heavy-horned, and entirely pale, the same fine grain as the mountains. Up close you see the seam down each one, from the crown of the skull along the spine to the tail, where the two halves of the mold met. Some still carry a little flashing along the seam, like a mane of glass. They are alive. They breathe; you can see the damp of their breath. They graze the lichen. They drink from the gutters with the same care as any animal. They startle when you come too close, and their hooves ring faintly on the cast rock.

Nobody knows whether the ibex were made to live here or whether they are a byproduct. Somewhere in the works a mold may exist that no one meant to fill. There may be other such molds. In the doorless buildings you sometimes hear small scratchings from the inside.

Moths come out at dusk, if it is ever dusk. Hares live in the pipe-bundles. Small white lizards bask along the warm edges of the newest castings. They are cast, like the ibex, and seamed. When they shed, they shed their flashing.

## Sound

The Reach is quiet, and the quiet is the strangest thing about it.

A place this industrial ought to roar. It does not. The great presses move so slowly that you only see they have moved by coming back after a week. The pipes carry water without rushing. The pylons carry current without hum. The cast rock is fine and porous and swallows sound. Shout in a valley and your voice goes out perhaps fifty paces, then stops, as if it has struck a wall.

What remains are small sounds, and they seem very loud. Water drips into a gutter. Iron ticks as it cools, and you can hear it from far off. A crow's feet shift on a cable. Ibex hooves chime on a ridge half a mile away. The swifts' wings are loud only at close range, where they make a soft tearing sound like cloth.

Now and then, maybe twice in a season, a mold opens. When that happens, you hear it everywhere at once: a single deep, cracking report that seems to come from inside your chest. Then the quiet comes back and seals over.

## The Slow Cycle

The works have a rhythm, but it runs on a scale you can barely perceive.

A pour takes a season. You can watch it happen if you are patient. A mold-half the size of a hillside is clamped shut, and at its top the sprue fills with a thick, pale slurry. It rises so slowly you would swear it doesn't move. Over weeks it sinks into the mold. Over more weeks the mold sweats, and the iron darkens, and a ridge of mist forms along the clamps. Then comes the report. The mold swings open on hinges thick as towers, and there is a new mountain, sharp and steaming, flashing along its crest.

Nothing erodes here, because nothing is left long enough to erode. Where a cliff has cracked, or a ridge has been worn by generations of ibex hooves, you find a scaffold soon afterward. Then there is a small mold clamped over the damage, and a slow pour, and a fresh surface as sharp as the day it was made. The Reach is always young. It is kept young.

## Where It Breaks

The longer you stay, the less the place agrees with itself.

The castings repeat, and not only in texture. You realise the peak you camped under two nights ago is ahead of you again, with the same sprue and the same notch in its flashing. You had been walking away from it. Either there are many of that peak, or there is one and it has moved. The Reach does not say which.

The shadows lag. In the flat, total light they are faint anyway, but they are wrong. An ibex's shadow will still be grazing after the ibex has lifted its head. A swift-cloud's shadow crosses a cliff a full second after the swifts. Your own shadow, if you stand very still, keeps shifting its weight for a moment after you've stopped.

The valley below you is sometimes also above you. When the mist burns off from a particular high saddle, you can look up and see, far overhead, a pale inverted range. It is this range, gantries and pylons and ridge-crests, hanging where the sky should be, the sprues pointing down. Some take it for a mirage in the thin air. Others think it is where the molds come from. They think each mountain is cast from its own reflection, and the reflection is poured first.

The towns are the worst of it. From the high points you can see settlements on the far side of the valley: small, dense, cast-white, with thin smoke and orderly roofs. If you walk toward one, you arrive at a town. But it is always empty, and always slightly wrong. The doors are solid. The windows are cast in with the walls. The smoke comes from chimneys that have no hearths. Looking back from its edge, you can see the settlement you meant to reach, still across the valley and still smoking. It is the same town at a different hour, perhaps, or the mold it was taken from.

Water sometimes runs uphill in the gutters, briefly, then thinks better of it. The crows sometimes all turn their heads at once toward nothing. Sometimes an ibex walks to the edge of a new casting, lies down on the warm flashing, and holds perfectly still. Over some days its seam closes. After that you cannot tell it from the rock.

## Being There

Living here, if anyone does, means accepting that the ground is a product. It is not a gift. It is fresh, and shaped, and repeated, and in some way it is intended. The Reach is not hostile. The damp gives water. The lichen and moss feed the ibex, and the ibex can feed you. The light is merciless, but it is also constant, clean, and oddly calming. It is cool, never cold. It is quiet enough that you begin, after a while, to hear your own heart as one more small sound among the drip and tick.

But it is not a place that was meant for you in particular. It is a place that is being made, very slowly, in a bright white hush. You are in it while it happens, like a crow on a cable, or a fern in a tank, or one more pale seamed animal walking the fresh flashing of a ridge that was not there last season.
