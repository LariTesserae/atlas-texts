# Fennick, Under the Bulb

## The Approach

There is a low place in a wet country, a shallow basin where the land barely bothers to dip, and at the bottom of it a mound no taller than a barn. It is covered in a single sprawling plant with broad, felted leaves the color of old pewter. Pull back the leaves and there is a skin beneath them, tight and faintly warm, ribbed like the underside of a mushroom cap. Somewhere on the south face the skin has split along a seam, the way a seed coat splits, and the split is tall enough to walk into.

Inside, the mound is much larger than the mound.

This is the first rule, and nothing in Fennick argues with it. The inside is a hollow bulb, a single plant's storage organ, the kind of swollen root an onion or a corm keeps its winter in. It is the size of a small valley, and it is almost entirely empty. The walls curve up and away into a darkness so complete that the ceiling is only an assumption. The air is cool, around the temperature of a cellar in early spring, and damp without being wet. Moisture beads on every surface and never quite gathers enough weight to fall.

On the floor of this hollow, a town is growing.

## The Town

Fennick is small: perhaps forty buildings, three streets, a square, a chapel, a row of terraced houses, a pump house, and, at the far end where the floor slopes up toward the wall, a factory with two chimneys. It is laid out with the careful grid-sense of an industrial village, the kind built all at once by a single company for its workers, and in a way that is exactly what happened. The company is the bulb.

Every part of the town is plant. The walls of the houses are sheets of cellulose stretched over ribs of lignin, pale and translucent as parchment, thin enough that a lamp held behind one would show its veining. There are no lamps, though. The roofs are overlapping bracts like the scales of a pinecone, still tacky with resin at their edges. The cobbles are seeds, hard and ovoid, pressed into a matrix of root-felt. Gutters and downpipes are hollow stems with nodes every forearm's length. The chimneys of the factory are two great fibrous stalks, fluted, their mouths crowned with a fringe of stiff hairs.

Nothing is weathered, because none of it is old. This is the strange tenderness of Fennick: the town is not a ruin being reclaimed by vegetation. It is a town that has not finished arriving. Corners are sharp. Door lintels have not sagged. The paint, where there is paint, is really pigment rising in the tissue, and in some houses it is still rising: a front door blushing from green toward oxblood over the course of a week. The chapel's steeple is only half-grown, a tapering green bud still sheathed in its protective leaf, and the sheath splits a little further every day.

Some buildings are only outlines. At the edges of town you find the ribs of houses without their skins, like the skeletons of leaves after the soft parts have rotted out, except these have never had soft parts yet. You can walk through their walls. Their doorframes stand perfectly upright, waiting.

Across the whole settlement there is almost nothing inside anything. The houses are hollow. There is no furniture, no hearth, no stair. Occasionally a building has grown a single object, a chair or a kettle or a picture frame, rendered in the same pale fiber as the walls. It sits in the exact center of an empty room as though the bulb had been trying to remember what goes in a house and got only that far. The picture frames hold nothing. The kettles have no openings.

## Darkness

There is almost no light in Fennick. What little there is comes from the lichens that grow in the joints between the cobbles, a faint, cold green-white visible only when your eyes have been in the dark for a long time. These lichens are the town's street plan. They trace the edges of the roads and nothing else, so that after an hour of standing still you begin to see Fennick as a map of glowing lines floating in black. You can see the grid, the square, the curve of the lane toward the chapel. The buildings themselves are invisible shapes that interrupt the lines.

The streetlamps are the second rule. They stand at every corner, tall iron-shaped stalks with a swollen pod at the top where a lamp would be, and they are lit. Each pod gives off darkness. It is not shadow, which requires light somewhere to cast it, but a soft emitted absence. Standing beneath one, you cannot see the lichen lines at your feet. Walk away and they come back. On the square, where six lamps stand in a ring, there is a pool of perfect nothing that the townsfolk (see below) gather in, the way moths would gather under a gas lamp in another world.

It follows that Fennick is lit by the factory, and the factory makes night.

## The Factory

The factory is the only building in Fennick that is full.

Inside its long hall, beneath the two chimneys, the bulb has grown machinery: huge, slow, and entirely vegetable. Fluted pistons of green wood rise and fall in cylinders of bark. Belts of braided fiber run over pulleys that are really the swollen joints of some creeping stem. A flywheel as tall as three men turns once every few minutes, and it is a single flat disc of cut root, its growth rings visible, spinning on an axle of heartwood lubricated by a clear sap that drips steadily onto the floor. The sap is sweet and draws insects.

Everything is driven by the bulb's own pressure. In the pump house at the town's center, a network of vessels as thick as a torso draws water up from the damp ground and pushes it, pulse by pulse, through pipes beneath the streets to the factory. This is the heartbeat of Fennick: once every eleven seconds, a deep, soft *thum* that you feel in your shins before you hear it. The cobbles lift very slightly with each pulse. The lichen lines tremble.

What the factory produces is darkness in bales. At the end of the line, stiff papery sacks swell and seal and drop onto a slow conveyor of interlocked leaves. Each sack is a little heavier than it should be and cooler than the air. Workers carry them out to the lamps. At the top of each lamp stalk, the pod opens like a mouth and takes a sack, and the darkness in the square deepens.

The chimneys breathe out a pale, cold vapor, the only thing in Fennick that rises. It drifts up into the unseen dome of the bulb, and sometimes, far overhead, it condenses, and a fine mist settles over the town. This is Fennick's weather: every day or two, a drizzle so light it is felt rather than seen. Then the pistons slow, the pulse deepens, and the whole town seems to lean slightly toward the moisture.

## The Inhabitants

Fennick is teeming, and it is still almost empty. Both are true because the hollow is so enormous and its creatures are so spread out through it.

**The workers** are beetles, a species found nowhere else, each about the size of a hand. They are dull black with a faint green iridescence visible only under the lichen-light. They live in the walls of the terraced houses, burrowing between the cellulose layers, and every morning (there is no morning, but there is a rhythm, a quickening of the pulse that serves) they emerge in single-file lines and walk the lichen streets to the factory. There are thousands of them. On the long straight of the high street you might see one every few yards, each one alone in its own stretch of dark, the line of them extending out of sight in both directions. They carry the bales. They tend the sap-drips. They do not seem to know they are in a town, and they also keep to the pavements.

**The moths** are pale, wide-winged, and silent, and they are drawn to the lamps of darkness exactly as moths elsewhere are drawn to flame. They circle the pods on the square in slow, spiraling hundreds. Occasionally one drifts too close and passes into the nothing, and does not come out. Whether they are destroyed or simply go somewhere else is not known. The number of moths does not seem to decline.

**The bats** hunt the moths. They roost upside down in the empty houses, hanging from the bare ceiling ribs, one or two to a room, and each room holds its bat like a single object the bulb has grown there. Their calls are pitched below hearing. What you notice instead is a faint pressure on the eardrums when one passes close, and the sudden absence of a moth in the lichen-light.

**The snails** are the gentry. They are enormous, the size of a sheep. Their shells spiral in the same proportions as the chapel's half-grown steeple, and they move up and down the three streets so slowly that their progress can be measured in days. They eat the outlines of unfinished houses. Wherever a snail has passed, the skeletal ribs of a not-yet-house are gone, the lichen closes over the gap, and a few days later the bulb begins, patiently, to grow the house again, a few doors down. Fennick never finishes because of the snails, and it never stops growing because of the bulb. The two have reached an arrangement.

**The thing in the chapel** is not seen. The steeple bud is still closed. From inside comes the only sound in Fennick that could be called a voice: a low, slow, hymn-shaped exhalation, rising and falling across a span of hours. The beetles do not go near the chapel. The snails do, and they rest against its walls for weeks at a time with their eyestalks retracted.

**The townsfolk**, the gatherers on the square, are harder to describe. In the pool of darkness beneath the six lamps, where no light at all reaches, there is movement. Brushing against it, you feel wool, and the warmth of a body, and the smell of coal smoke and boiled cabbage. You hear, very faintly, the scrape of a boot and a cough. If you step out of the dark and look back, there is nothing there but the lamps and the ring of lichen. The bulb has not grown people. It seems to have grown the space where people would be, and the darkness is the only medium dense enough to hold them.

## Sound

Fennick is quiet. Its silence has texture, though, and a stranger learns to read it.

There is the pulse, *thum*, every eleven seconds, felt more than heard. There is the drip of sap in the factory and the creak of fibrous belts. There are the tiny scratching feet of the beetles on seed-cobbles, a sound like a fingernail on linen. There is the whisper of moth wings, only audible when hundreds circle close together. There is the hymn from the chapel, below the threshold of the other sounds, so that you only notice it when it stops. Once or twice a day, somewhere in the dome overhead, there is a long, groaning creak, the sound of the bulb itself swelling a fraction larger.

When the drizzle falls, it makes no sound at all. It is too fine. But the lichen lines brighten under it, very briefly, and the whole map of Fennick flickers into clearer existence for a few minutes before fading back.

## The Rules

These are the things a visitor learns, not all at once:

The inside is larger than the outside, and it is growing. The mound in the basin has not changed size in anyone's memory, but the hollow within it is measurably wider every season. The lanes lengthen. New streets bud off the old ones.

Distance is uneven. The high street is three minutes' walk from end to end in one direction and nearly twenty in the other. Going toward the factory, you go downhill both ways.

Objects the bulb grows are copies of copies. The chairs in empty rooms get simpler each time a house is regrown after the snails have eaten it. The newest chairs are just four stalks and a flat leaf. The newest picture frames are empty rectangles of fiber with no corners.

Darkness is a substance. It can be baled, carried, stored, spilled. A split bale on the factory floor leaves a stain of blackness that the beetles walk around carefully for days until it evaporates.

The town is a memory without a source. Fennick is almost certainly the bulb's rendition of a real company village, somewhere in the world above, whose smoke and cabbage and wool and coughing soaked down through the soil into the roots. Whether that village still exists, or ever existed, cannot be learned from here. The signs on the corners are grown in leaf-vein lettering, and they spell *FENNICK* consistently. The street names are all slightly different on each sign, as though the bulb heard them once and never quite caught them.

Nothing wears down. Nothing is old. Everything in Fennick is in the first instant of its own existence, sharp-edged, faintly sticky, still pushing upward. A visitor who stays long enough begins to feel it too: a tightness in the skin, a sense that they are only now being finished.

## Leaving

The seam in the south face is still open. Walking back toward it, the lichen lines thin out, the pulse fades from shin to heel to nothing, and the air grows a degree or two warmer. Then the felted pewter leaves push aside and there is the low wet basin, a grey sky, ordinary rain.

Behind you, the mound is the size of a barn.

If you put your palm flat against the skin beneath the leaves and wait eleven seconds, you will feel it.
