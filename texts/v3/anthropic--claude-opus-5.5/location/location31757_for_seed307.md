# The Blister

Near the top of a mountain too young to have a name, just under a summit that is still growing, there is a hollow in the rock about the size of a bread oven. You would never find it by looking. The mountain is black and glassy and nothing about the outside suggests a room within. The only clue is a thread of steam from a crack no wider than a fingernail, and at that altitude the wind tears it away before it becomes anything.

Inside, the hollow is wet, hot, and packed with life.

---

**The shell**

The walls are volcanic glass that cooled only recently. They have no weathering, no dust, no softening. Every edge is still sharp enough to split a hair. The surface bubbled as it hardened, so the interior looks like a wet bread crust made of black mirror. There are hundreds of small domes and pits, each one reflecting all the others. Where the glass is thin, a dull orange glow shows through from somewhere deeper, where the rock is still molten.

The floor is not flat. It is a tilted basin of broken plates stacked like cards, left behind when the roof cracked and partly fell in during some earlier year. Each plate has a blade-sharp lip. Between the plates are channels no wider than a finger, and every channel holds water.

**The water**

The water comes up from below as steam, meets the thin, bitterly cold air seeping through the upper cracks, and condenses on every surface. The walls weep constantly. Beads form, merge, slide, pool, reheat, and rise again, so the whole chamber runs through one small circuit of water many times an hour. Rain, spring, and sweat are the same event here.

The pools are warm enough to hurt. They carry a faint sulfur-green tint and a skin of minerals that crystallizes at the edges into white fringes, fine as frost, which dissolve and re-form as the water level rises and falls. In some places the minerals have grown into hollow straws that hang from the ceiling like tiny icicles. They are pure salt and silica, and they break at a breath.

The air is saturated. Nothing ever dries. A dead thing here does not dry out; it softens and dissolves back into the pool.

**The light**

The light is ordinary daylight, which is strange given how enclosed the place feels. It enters through three or four hairline cracks in the roof. The glass above is thin enough to pass sunlight, so the whole ceiling glows faintly, like a smoked lamp. Between that and the dozens of pools and polished surfaces, there are no deep shadows. The light is even, a little grey, a little green. You can see everything clearly, and there is very much to see.

---

**What lives here**

Almost nothing grows. There are no mosses, ferns, or lichens. The only thing resembling vegetation is a film, a living varnish of heat-loving microbes that coats the wettest glass in sheets of rust, violet, and a sulfurous yellow. It is slick and slightly gelatinous, and in places it has thickened into small wrinkled cushions the size of a thumbnail. That film is the entire base of the food web, and it feeds a population that should not fit in a room this size.

Look closely at any square inch of wall and it is moving.

**The grazers** are the first layer. They are translucent crustaceans, each about the size of a grain of rice, with a faint pink tinge from whatever pigment they take from the film. They crowd so densely over the colored patches that the patches seem to breathe. They scrape and shuffle, climb the wet walls in unbroken ribbons, and stream down the channels in columns ten animals wide. There are tens of thousands of them. When a drop runs down the glass it carries a dozen with it, and they climb out at the bottom and start back up.

**The skaters** live on the pool surfaces: long-legged, dark, flea-quick things that ride the surface tension and eat anything that falls in. At any moment there are more skaters on a single pool than there are stars you can name.

**The veil-flies** hang in the steam. These are pale, gauzy insects with wings too big for their bodies. They drift rather than fly, lifted by rising vapor and dropping where it cools. The steam is so thick with them that it has visible texture, like fog with a grain. They lay eggs in the mineral fringes. The eggs look like the fringes. Neither you nor they can be sure which is which.

**The threadhunters** are what the others fear. Each is a centipede-like animal as thin as a sewing thread and as long as a hand. They are almost impossible to see against the wet glass, because their bodies are transparent and filled with the same pale fluid as the pools. You notice them only when a column of grazers suddenly parts, or when a skater vanishes downward without a ripple. There may be hundreds of them. There may be only one, very long, wound through every crack in the chamber.

**The lens-beetles** are the strangest, and the only ones that make anything.

They are small, domed, and black like the glass. Each carries a single drop of water held between its forelegs, clamped in a basket of stiff hairs. They carry these drops everywhere. When the sun comes through a roof crack, a lens-beetle will climb into the beam, raise its drop, and tilt it until a bright point of focused light falls on a patch of film, a cluster of veil-fly eggs, or a sleeping grazer. What is under the point cooks. The beetle eats it. The drop is its tool, and it is a well-kept one. The beetles tend their drops constantly, topping them up from the walls and rejecting any that turn cloudy.

Some lens-beetles also carry a second tool, a sliver of glass chipped from the chamber's sharp edges. They have been seen using it to pry grazers off the walls, and, once, to cut a threadhunter in half. The beetles build nothing. They leave no marks except where a patch of film has been burned in a neat, tiny circle. They are toolmakers who have never made a thing that lasts.

---

**The quiet**

For all this crowding, the chamber is almost silent.

The steam should hiss, and it does, but only at the crack where it exits, and faintly, as if from another room. The water should drip, and it does, but a drop landing in a pool this warm and mineral-heavy makes no sound, just a small dent and a ring. None of the animals has a voice. The grazers are too small to click, and the skaters weigh too little to splash.

The loudest thing in the chamber is the glass. Every few minutes, as a patch of wall cools or warms, it makes a single high tick, like a fingernail against a wineglass. Then another somewhere else. Then nothing for a long time. If you were small enough to be inside, you would hear these ticks, your own blood, and nothing else.

---

**The rules that bend**

This is where the place stops making ordinary sense.

**The counts do not hold.** Watch a single pool and count its skaters, and you will get forty. Look away and back, and you will get forty-one, then thirty-eight, then forty again. Nothing entered or left. The numbers shift like a quantity that has not fully decided what it is. The same is true of the grazers, the veil-flies, and even the pools. There are eleven pools, or twelve, depending on when you count and from where.

**The dead return.** A lens-beetle burns a grazer to a pink smear. Moments later, on the far wall, there is a grazer with the same crooked left antenna, feeding as if nothing happened. The threadhunters kill constantly, and the population never thins. Whether these are the same animals, or copies, or something the chamber repeats as a bird repeats a call, no one here can say. The animals behave as if death were real. They flee, hide, and freeze. The room does not seem to agree.

**The water falls both ways.** Most drops slide down, as expected. Some slide up. There is no pattern to which ones. Two drops can form side by side, the size and shape of twins, and one will run down the wall while the other climbs to the ceiling, where it hangs, swells, and falls upward into a crack as if falling into sky. The grazers ride the rising drops as calmly as the falling ones.

**The reflections lie.** In the polished glass, each wall shows the chamber in reflection, but not quite this chamber. In the mirrored version the pools are in slightly different places, the lens-beetles hold their drops in the wrong forelegs, and sometimes there are animals in the reflection that are not in the room: a larger beetle, a thing with too many wings, a threadhunter as thick as a wrist. The animals ignore the reflections, all except the lens-beetles, which occasionally stop at a mirror patch and hold up their drops toward it, as if showing it something, or checking.

**The room is always at the top.** This is the deepest strangeness, and it only becomes clear over a long time.

The mountain is growing. New lava is pushed up from below, adding height and pressure. By any normal process, a pocket like this should end up buried deeper as the summit rises above it. Instead the chamber rises with the summit. It is always just below the peak, always a hand's depth beneath the new crust. No one has seen it move. It is not a room that travels through the rock so much as a room the rock keeps rearranging itself around, reforming its walls a little higher each season. Below it, deep in the mountain, lies a column of sealed empty bubbles, each one a former chamber. Some still hold dried mineral fringes, and a few still hold the shapes of tiny animals, perfectly preserved in glass. The chamber leaves itself behind as it climbs.

The fauna seem to know this. Every so often, on no fixed schedule, the grazers all begin climbing at once, the whole population streaming upward in a single living sheet. The skaters leave their pools. The veil-flies rise. For an hour the ceiling is so thick with animals that the light turns pink. Then the ceiling glass cracks with one tick, louder than usual. When the animals settle again, the room is a finger's width higher than it was, and the lowest part of the floor has sealed over into solid black glass.

---

**Its day**

Morning comes when the first crack catches the sun. The ceiling glows. The lens-beetles climb into the beams and begin burning. The film brightens in the warmth, and the grazers swarm it.

Midday is hottest. The pools nearly boil. The steam thickens until the veil-flies are carried out through the exit crack in a pale thread that the wind shreds instantly. Some of them may reach other places. If they do, none have come back to say so.

Afternoon cools slightly as the sun moves off the cracks. Condensation increases. The walls run with water, and drops go up and down in roughly equal numbers. The threadhunters begin to feed in earnest.

At night the only light is the orange glow of the molten rock below, showing through the thinnest glass. The chamber turns to embers. The lens-beetles fold their drops against their bellies and sleep. The grazers cluster in piles so dense they look like pink moss. The glass ticks as it cools. The counts shift in the dark, where no one is watching, and by morning there are more animals than there were, or fewer, or the same.

---

If you could somehow stand on the summit above it, in the thin cold wind with the whole range falling away below and the sky nearly black overhead, you would see only a black peak, steaming slightly. You would have no idea that a hand's depth below your boots is a room full of weeping mirror and silent animals, burning their food with drops of water, dying and not staying dead, and riding a mountain up into the sky one bubble at a time.
