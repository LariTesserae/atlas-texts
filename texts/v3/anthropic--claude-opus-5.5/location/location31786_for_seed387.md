# The Sluice Quarter

## Beneath

Under a river, under a city, under the foundations of a customs house whose clerks have never heard of it, there is a vault about two hundred paces long. It is full of water. It is full of noise. And it still holds a street.

The street was built on purpose. Long ago someone decided the river's weir needed a keeper, and the keeper needed a house, and the house needed neighbours: an engine-fitter, a lamp-man, a chandler, a family whose job no record survives to explain. They dug a pocket into the riverbed clay, lined it with fired brick, arched it over, and set eleven narrow houses along one cobbled lane that ran from the foot of a spiral stair to the doors of the engine hall. Then they built the engine, and for a while the Quarter was a small working town, sealed and humming and lit, with the river running overhead like weather.

At some point the ceiling let the river in. Nobody left a note. The engine did not stop.

## The Water

The whole vault is submerged, from cobbles to keystones. There is no air pocket, no silver skin of surface overhead, only the brick arch lost in dark and the water pressing everywhere at once.

The water is warm, close to blood-warm. The engineers sank the engine's footings into a seam of hot spring beneath the clay, and it rises through cracks in the floor in slow shimmering columns, like heat above a summer road. Where it meets the cooler river water pouring through the ceiling breaches, the water turns glassy and warped. Lamplight wobbles through these mixing layers. A hand held out into one feels two temperatures braided together, tepid and then briefly hot.

The water never rests. That is the first law of the place.

## The Noise

The sound arrives before sight. It is a physical force, not something heard at a distance.

The engine hall at the east end houses a turbine twice the height of the houses, an iron drum in a cage of brass. River water crashes down on it through four intake throats in the ceiling, and it spins. It has been spinning, as far as anyone could tell, for longer than the city above has had its current name. Water carries sound far better than air, so the roar does not fade with distance. It saturates the vault. The houses throb with it, and the cobbles hum. A diver's ribs would resonate like the body of a cello, and their teeth would buzz in their sockets.

Inside the roar there are other sounds, once you learn to pick them out:

- **The bearing-scream.** A high, rising metallic whine every forty seconds or so, as some worn part of the turbine passes a tight spot.
- **The sluice-knell.** A tremendous iron clang when one of the gates in the west wall cycles open or shut. It hits like a blow to the sternum.
- **The chatter.** Loose things rattle continuously: shutters on their last hinges, a chain against a lamppost, pebbles skittering in the gutters.
- **The groan.** A low, geological note beneath everything, felt more than heard. The brick arch flexes under the weight of the river and the city stacked on top of it.

There is no silence anywhere in the Quarter. In the lee of a heavy door, in a cellar, inside a cupboard, the roar merely changes pitch.

## The Light

It is dim, but not dark. The engine was built to power its own street, and it still does.

Iron lampposts stand along the lane, one between every pair of houses. Their glass globes were sealed against damp and have held against flood. Inside each, a carbon filament glows the colour of weak tea, fed by cables running back along the gutters to the turbine hall. The current is irregular. The lamps brighten and sag with the engine's rhythm, dimming slightly with each bearing-scream, flaring when a sluice opens and more water hits the blades. The whole street appears to breathe.

Between the lamps the dark is deep. The vault is mostly empty space, and the light reaches only a few body-lengths before silt and turbulence swallow it. Each lamp holds a small room of amber visibility, full of drifting motes. Between those rooms lie long stretches of brown-black water.

The engine hall glows faintly, with a greenish phosphorescence from somewhere in the machinery that nobody has named. Nobody has gone close enough to name it.

## The Current and Its Rules

The Quarter is turbulent, but its turbulence has structure. It is a storm that repeats.

Water enters from above through the ceiling breaches and the turbine intakes, and leaves through the sluice gates in the west wall, which open onto a culvert running back to the river downstream. The gates open and close on a schedule. They are driven by a cam on the turbine shaft, so the timing drifts as the engine wears. When the gates are open, the whole vault pulls westward in a single strong draught. When they slam shut, the pressure has nowhere to go, and the water rebounds east in a surge that runs the length of the lane and breaks against the engine hall.

Between these two states the water churns. Vortices spin off the lampposts and wander down the lane, pulling silt up in columns. Eddies form behind every doorframe. The hot upwellings from the floor rise, get caught by the draught, and are dragged sideways into long shimmering streamers. Things are always moving in the Quarter: grit, flakes of rust, dislodged brick. Nothing moves in a straight line.

Over the long years, this produced the **Circuit**. Anything that falls into the vault and is too heavy to escape through the sluices joins a slow loop. It is dragged west, thrown back east, tumbled along the gutters, lifted by an upwelling, and dropped again. The Circuit has a fixed path. It runs down the north gutter, through the open door of the fourth house, out its broken back window, across the lane at the chapel steps, and along the south gutter to the engine hall.

Objects on the Circuit have been polished by it. There are bottles frosted white by abrasion. There is a brass doorknob worn to a smooth bead. There is a pocket watch, its case sanded thin as paper, its face long gone. There are countless round stones that were once angular chunks of fallen brick. The Circuit is a slow tumbler, rounding everything it carries. Given long enough, it would reduce the whole Quarter to smooth pebbles.

## Erosion and Age

The Quarter is old and settled into its condition. It is not decaying quickly, and it is not collapsing. It found a balance long ago and keeps it.

The brick walls facing the current have been scoured into shallow ripples, like a beach at low tide. The corners of buildings are rounded. The cobbles in the centre of the lane are worn into a gentle trough where the surge runs hardest. The house doors that faced west were torn off decades ago. Those that faced east still hang, their paint gone and their wood silvered and hard.

The iron is the most striking part. Every exposed iron surface wears a coat of rust thick and textured as fur. Where warm spring water rises past the ironwork, the rust grows in long orange tassels that stream out in the current: beards on the lampposts, fringes on the railings, trailing pennants on the turbine cage. These are not plants. They are colonies of iron-eating bacteria, the only thing in the Quarter that could loosely be called growing. There is nothing green here and nothing that photosynthesises. The lamplight is too poor and too strange. There is mineral, metal, biofilm, and water.

## The Houses

The eleven houses are small, two storeys each, with narrow frontages. They are empty, and emptier than their size would suggest. The current long ago stripped them of anything that could float or tumble. What remains is fixed or too heavy to move:

- a cast-iron stove, its door open, its belly full of silt;
- a bathtub with clawed feet, rust-bearded, bolted to a floor that is slowly giving way beneath it;
- a stone sink;
- a set of brass hooks along a hallway wall, one still holding a coat's worth of rust-stain in the shape of a coat;
- stairs leading up into completely black upper rooms, where the current is gentler and silt lies in perfect undisturbed drifts.

The upper rooms are the stillest places in the Quarter. The roar reaches them, but the water barely moves. Silt settles in them like snow and records every rare disturbance. Some of those drifts show faint tracks.

The seventh building is a chapel, or something like one, taller than the houses, with a round window above the door. The glass is gone, and the current pours through the window's empty spokes, making the chapel whistle. It adds a thin, wavering note to the roar, rising and falling with the sluices, which in the Quarter is the closest thing to music.

## Living Things

There is life here, but very little. The vault is mostly an empty space of moving water, and living things are scattered widely through it.

**The pillar-fish.** A few pale catfish, the colour of wet parchment, live in the dead-water pockets behind the lampposts, where each post's wake creates a small calm. They hold position there all day, noses into the current, barbels trembling. They seem to eat what the Circuit delivers: flakes of rust-bacteria, organic scraps, fallen insects from the city above. They have eyes, but the eyes are clouded. They navigate by the lamps' pulsing and by the vibration of the engine, which they probably experience as the shape of the world itself.

**The riders.** Once in a long while, a thin dark eel comes through, entering with the river water, riding the westward pull, then turning and riding the rebound east. The riders seem to travel the Quarter deliberately, using the surge the way birds use thermals. None have been seen to stay.

**Shrimp in the chapel.** In the chapel, where the whistling current keeps the water oxygenated, tiny translucent shrimp cluster in the stone carvings around the altar. They catch lamplight from the doorway and glint in it.

That is the full census. The Quarter supports roughly as much life as it can hold without being noticed.

## The Strangeness

Almost everything here has an ordinary explanation. The hot springs explain the warmth, the turbine explains the light, the cam explains the sluices, and the bacteria explain the rust beards.

What does not quite add up is the engine itself. It has run, as far as anyone can tell, for a century or more, without oil, maintenance, or a hand on its levers. Its bearings should have seized long ago, and its blades should be scoured to nothing by silt. Instead it keeps turning, screaming and roaring and wearing, but never wearing out. The lamps keep glowing. The sluices keep cycling. The whole system appears to have tipped into a condition where the water that wears it down is also, somehow, what keeps it going.

The greenish glow in the engine hall may be related. So may a few other oddities. The silt drifts in the upper rooms sometimes show tracks nothing living in the Quarter could have made: long, even marks, as though something walked there upright, slowly. Two of the eastern doors are sometimes found closed after a surge that should have opened them. The lamps nearest the engine hall, in rare moments, dim all at once and out of rhythm with the turbine, as though the engine has turned its attention elsewhere.

These are small things, easy to miss inside the roar. The Quarter is not haunted in any theatrical way. It is simply a little more coherent than it should be, a little too well kept for a drowned place with no keeper.

## Above

The customs house above sits on a heavy stone foundation, and its clerks sometimes feel a faint thrumming in the floor. They blame the river, the trams, the pipes. On very still nights, when the city's traffic dies down, some of them swear they hear something regular under it all: a long whine, then a deep iron clang, repeating at intervals slightly longer every year.

Beneath them, in the warm dark, the street keeps its hours. The lamps breathe, the water pulls west and slams east, the pillar-fish hold their small calms, the Circuit turns its polished cargo, and the engine goes on roaring.
