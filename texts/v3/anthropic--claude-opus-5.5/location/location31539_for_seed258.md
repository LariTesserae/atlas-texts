# The Held Terrace

## Approach

You come up onto it the way you'd climb into a loft: the last of the slope is steep and soft, and then it levels, and you are standing on a floor.

It is not ground. There is no soil here and no stone. What holds you up is a mat of root, the roots flattened and interlaced so tightly they have become a surface. It has a grain like planed timber, pale buff shading to green where the light reaches down into it. The weave repeats. You can follow a single root for thirty paces as it passes over four and under four, over four and under four. Then it turns at a right angle, precisely, and goes back the way it came one course over. Someone meant this. Or something did.

The plateau runs out ahead of you for what might be several miles. It is hard to judge, because the air is thick with water that hangs rather than falls. It gathers into a fine grey fleece in the middle distance and thins again at the edges. Everything you can see was grown, and everything you can see was built. After a while you stop feeling any difference between the two words.

## The Sound

Before anything else there is the sound, and it does not stop.

It is a chord, low and enormous, felt in the sternum more than heard. Under it runs a second, higher tone, almost a whistle, holding steady a little above your head. They never shift. There is no rhythm in them, no swell, no breath. It is like standing inside an organ with every key held down by a patient weight.

The source becomes clear once you look for it. Across the plateau stand the stems: hollow, jointed, ribbed like bamboo but far larger. The widest could admit a person walking upright. Each rises from the root-floor and ends in an open mouth, some flared, some narrowed to a slot. Wet air is always moving up out of the valleys below, warmer than the air on top, and it passes over those mouths and through them. The water condensing on their inner walls runs down in threads and falls into chambers at their bases, and the falling adds its own grain to the note. Every stem has been tuned by its length and bore and the shape of its lip. Every stem sounds all the time.

It is loud enough that you must raise your voice to be heard by someone beside you. After an hour you stop noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Then some small sound cuts through, a drip nearer than the rest or the tick of an insect, and you understand how vast the silence underneath would be.

## The Structures

They stand far apart. That is the second thing you notice. This is not a city or a forest. It is closer to an unfinished floor plan laid across a wide space, with its rooms and passages sketched out and given room to breathe.

Here is a colonnade: eleven trunks in a perfect row, each the thickness of a barrel. They are smooth-barked, and at head height each one branches into four limbs that reach out and fuse with the limbs of its neighbours. The result is a vaulted arcade roofed in overlapping leaves the size of doors. The leaves are thick and waxy, and they shed the condensation into gutters. The gutters are leaves too, curled lengthwise and grafted end to end, and they carry the water to the ends of the colonnade, where it drops through a slot in the floor into somewhere unseen.

Past the colonnade, maybe three hundred paces off across bare woven floor, there is a stair. It rises from nothing and goes to nothing. It is a flight of broad treads made from shelf fungus, or something like it: flat, creamy, dense, with fine pleated gills underneath. Every tread is the same depth, and every riser the same height, to within the width of a fingernail. It climbs to about twice a person's height and stops in the open air. Its top step is crisp-edged and finished, waiting for a landing that was never grown.

There are walls with windows in them. They are hedges, if a hedge could be grown flat as a sheet of paper and three storeys high, the leaves so small and dense they make a surface like felt. The windows are clean rectangles where the growth simply is not, with sills of thickened stem. Through them you see more floor and more fog, and a far-off tower of twisted vine.

There are rooms that have three walls, and rooms that have walls on every side but no door, so you can only look in from above, from the stair that goes nowhere. There are basins at waist height, cupped from single immense leaves and full to the brim with clear water that does not overflow. There are benches. No one sits on them.

## The Rawness

Nothing here is weathered. Nothing is worn. You begin to see how strange that is.

A place this wet should be soft at its edges. You would expect streaks of algae, roots lifting the floor, the rot and slump and sag of living things settling under their own weight. There is none of it. Every edge is as sharp as the day it formed. The seams where one plant fuses into another are clean and exact, like joinery. No leaf has a torn margin. No bark has a scar. Nowhere does water pool where it was not meant to pool.

It does not feel old. It feels new, newer than anything you have ever stood in. It feels as though the whole plateau had grown up to this point a moment ago and then stopped.

Many things are unfinished. You find an arch whose two sides reach toward each other and do not meet; a hand's width of air hangs between their tips, and they are not straining. You find a floor that thins at one edge into an open lattice, then into single parallel roots laid out like the warp on a loom with no weft. Beyond them the plateau drops away into the white. You find a tower that is only a helix of vine climbing an empty column of air, a scaffold for something that was never poured into it.

Nothing is broken. It all reads as *not yet*. The place is under construction and has been for as long as it has existed. Nothing on it is being built.

## The Stillness

It does not change.

That becomes the governing fact once you have stayed long enough. The drone does not waver. The fog stays at the same distance whichever way you walk, and thickens and thins in the same places. The light is an even, sourceless daylight filtering through the moisture. It does not seem to move across the sky. Shadows fall straight down and stay short.

The plants do not grow. You can mark a tendril against a leaf-edge and come back later, however much later, and it has not advanced by a hair. The water in the basins does not rise or fall, though the gutters fill them and the slots drain them. Somewhere the rates are matched perfectly. Leaves do not drop. Buds do not open. Buds exist here, fat and green at the tips of some stems, and they stay closed.

If you cut a leaf, the cut seals within a breath. A clear bead rises along the wound and hardens, and the leaf looks exactly as it did, minus the piece you took. The piece in your hand does not wilt. Carry it off the plateau and it stays green and cool in your pocket for weeks, as though it has not noticed it was removed.

The warmth is constant too. It is the warmth of breath close to skin, of a greenhouse at dusk. Your clothes are damp within minutes and stay exactly that damp. The air tastes faintly of cut stem and of something sweeter under it, like resin or honey.

## The Animals

There are few, and they are easy to miss.

In the chambers at the bases of the singing stems, where the condensation falls, live small pale frogs. They are translucent enough that their bones show as fine grey lines. They do not call, or if they do, the drone swallows it. They sit at the lip of the water with their throats pulsing in time with nothing. Watch one long enough and you realize the pulse matches the higher tone of the chord exactly. Every frog in every chamber pulses together.

In the colonnade there is sometimes a moth. It is large, dun, and slow. It lands on a leaf and folds its wings, and the pattern on them repeats the four-over-four-under weave of the floor. Then it stays. Hours later it is still there. It is possible that it was always there and that you only now saw it.

Once, far off, you may see something walking. It is tall and narrow and moves with long, deliberate strides between two of the distant structures. The fog takes it before you can make out whether it has two legs or four or six. It does not come back. Nothing crosses its path afterward. The floor where it walked shows no mark.

There are no birds. There are no flies. The stillness seems to discourage anything that lives by quick decisions.

## What the Place Is

Walk long enough and a sense of plan settles on you. It is not a thought so much as a pressure, the way you can feel the shape of a room in the dark.

The structures are sparse because they are placed. The spaces between them are not gaps but reserved ground, the way a sketch leaves white paper where the courtyards will be. Sightlines run clean from the stair to the colonnade to the helix tower. Face a certain way and the singing stems stand in ranks, graded from low to high like pipes in a case. The unfinished arches are not failures. They are held exactly at the stage before closing, like the last breath before a word is spoken.

There is no builder here. There are no tools, and no mark of a hand or blade anywhere. The joinery is the plants' own, the fusion of living tissue into living tissue. The floor wove itself. The stair shelved itself out of the air, tread by tread, to exactly the right height, and stopped.

It becomes difficult not to think of the whole plateau as a single organism: one plant, or one thing that used to be many plants and is now one. It set out to build itself into a shape, and got partway, and then drew in its breath and held it. The drone is the held breath, the sound of the air it is not yet letting go. The water is the sweat on it, beading and running in the channels it cut for that purpose. The warmth is its warmth.

Why it paused, you can't know. Perhaps it is waiting for something. Perhaps the pause is the shape, and this interval of *almost* is the finished form: a building made entirely of anticipation, its arches permanently about to meet. Perhaps it has not paused at all, and simply builds on a scale of time in which a thousand of your years is less than a heartbeat. Then the hand's width between the arch-tips is closing at a speed you would need to be a mountain to see.

## Leaving

At the edge of the plateau the woven floor thins to that bare warp of roots. Between them you can see down into the valley: ordinary valley, with rock and soil and rivers that change course, trees that fall and rot, weather that comes and goes. It looks frantic from up here, and lovely, and very loud in a different way. It is full of rush and failure and replacement.

You go down. For a long time afterward, in ordinary quiet, you catch yourself listening for the chord. Now and then in a still room you hear it, or think you do: one sustained tone under everything, patient, waiting for the breath to be let out.
