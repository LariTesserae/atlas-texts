# The Undercroft at the Working Face

It begins as a sound before it becomes a place.

You hear it in the descent: a low, continuous grinding, too even to be geological, too deep to be anything you would call a machine. It comes up through the soles of the feet and settles behind the teeth. By the time the stair turns its last corner and opens out, the sound has become the medium you stand in, the way water is the medium a swimmer stands in. Then the stair ends, and the city is there.

It is not large. Forty paces would carry you across its plaza. The street that runs out from it could be walked end to end in the time it takes to finish a thought. Above, the vault is low enough that a thrown stone would strike it, perhaps six storeys up, curving down on every side to meet the tops of the façades. The whole of it could sit inside a cathedral with room to spare. And yet it is unmistakably a city: three tiers of frontages stepping back from the plaza, balconies, arcades, a covered market with its stalls laid out in rows, stairways climbing between levels, a drainage system, a fountain basin, a bridge across a narrow cut. It has the grammar of a place where thousands live.

No one lives here. Nothing lives here that moves.

---

## The Stone

Everything is cut from a single dark mass, a basalt or something that has agreed to behave like basalt, and all of it is new. You can tell by the edges. Nothing has been softened by use. The stair treads have no dip worn into their centers. The corners of the buildings are sharp enough to open skin if you drag a palm along them carelessly. Window reveals meet their sills at angles so exact that the shadows inside them look drawn with a rule.

There are no tool marks. The surfaces are smooth but not polished. Tilt your head close to a wall and you see a faint diffraction in the dim light, a shimmer of color. It comes from ridging too fine to feel, laid in repeating bands like the growth lines of a shell. The stone was not carved. It was *arranged*. Somewhere in the rock, and still working in it, is a lattice that tells matter where to go. The city is what the lattice has concluded so far.

Scratch a wall with a key and the mark stays white and raw for an hour. Come back the next cycle and it is gone, the surface closed over like skin. Chip a corner and the corner returns, though not always exactly as it was. Sometimes a fraction of a degree sharper. Sometimes with one more of those fine diffracting bands.

The stone is warm. Not hot, but warmer than rock this far down has any right to be, the warmth of a body or a loaf an hour out of the oven. The warmth comes up out of it steadily. It is the heat of the arranging, the energy spent moving matter into place, bleeding off into the air. Lay a hand flat on a pillar and you can feel, beneath the warmth, a vibration that is not quite the grinding you have been hearing. It is finer and quicker, a trembling like a held breath.

---

## The Water

Warm stone and cool deep rock and a sealed vault produce weather.

The air is saturated. Every surface carries a skin of condensation that gathers into beads, and the beads gather into threads that run down the façades in slow, branching paths. The walls weep. Along certain seams, where one planned block meets another, the weeping is constant, and the water leaves faint pale traces of mineral that the stone reabsorbs within days.

The city was built for this. Every street has a runnel cut down its center, a channel the width of a hand and exactly as deep, and every runnel carries water. Balcony edges drip into catch-basins, and the catch-basins overflow into downspouts carved inside the columns. You can hear the water moving inside the walls, a hollow gurgle that rises and falls. The runnels converge on the plaza fountain, which does not spray. It simply fills, dark and perfectly still at the center while its edges tremble with arrivals, and it drains away through an opening in its floor you cannot see into. Where that water goes, no map says.

Several times a cycle the air changes. The grinding does not falter, but a second sound rises beneath it: a long, rising exhalation somewhere behind the walls, the ventilation drawing breath. Pressure shifts. You feel it in your ears. The humidity, pushed past what the air can hold, condenses all at once near the vault, and a fine rain falls. It is not a drizzle but a true fall of droplets so small they drift. For a few minutes the whole city is veiled, the far façades gone grey and soft. The rain lands on stone warm enough that it steams faintly where it strikes, so the streets smoke. Then the exhalation ends, the rain thins, and the water returns to its channels, loud for a while, rushing, before settling back to its ordinary murmur.

---

## The Green

The plants were here before the stone.

That is the strangeness you notice only slowly. The mosses on the north-facing walls are not growing *on* the stone. They are growing out of it, rooted in pores the lattice left for them, their rhizoids threaded through the rock as though it had set around them. The ferns in the gutters stand in slots precisely their width. The vines on the arcade climb along grooves that run exactly where a vine would want to climb. Someone, or something, seeded the green into the plan. The city was drawn around a garden.

The garden is lush where it has been placed, and absent everywhere else. Ferns crowd the edges of the runnels, their fronds bowed under beads of water, a pale luminous green that seems too bright for the dim air until you realize it is not reflecting the light but brightening the dark near it, very faintly. The walls of the covered market are sheeted in moss so thick and even it looks upholstered. Along the upper balconies, trailing plants hang in long curtains that sway in the ventilation's breath and drip continuously onto the street below.

At the center of the plaza, rising from a planter cut into the paving, stands the only thing here that might be called a tree. Its trunk is smooth and pale, almost white, and it branches low and wide, reaching toward the vault. Its leaves are broad, dark, and waxy, shedding water in constant small cascades. Its roots do not stay in the planter. They run out across the plaza just beneath the surface of the paving stones. You can see them through the stone, where it thins. Where the paving is youngest, near the far edge, the rock is faintly translucent, like smoked glass or old horn, and the pale roots show through it, and so do other things: fine dark filaments, branching like the roots but straighter, finer, more deliberate. The lattice, and the tree, growing together.

There are no flowers. There is nothing to pollinate them.

---

## The Silence Inside the Noise

This is a loud place, and yet the first thing it teaches is an absence.

The grinding never stops. The water never stops. The ventilation breathes. The stone, cooling where it has recently finished, ticks and pings, small metallic sounds that arrive from random directions like an irregular clock. Every sound is amplified by the hard new surfaces, thrown back and forth across the plaza until it arrives from everywhere at once. Speak aloud and your voice returns doubled and tripled from the balconies, bright and hard-edged.

But there is no buzzing. No scurrying. No wingbeat, no chirp, no scratch of a claw in a gutter. The moss is dense enough to hide a thousand small creatures and it hides none. There are no webs in the corners of the arcade. There is no dust. No animal has ever found its way down here, or none has been permitted, and the ear, which expects life in a warm wet green place, keeps listening for it and keeps finding nothing. The noise is all process. Nothing in it is appetite.

The rooms are the purest form of this emptiness. Step through any doorway, and there are doorways everywhere, and you enter a chamber finished with total care: floor, walls, ceiling, a window looking onto the street, a niche in one wall exactly the right height for a lamp, a second doorway leading deeper. There is nothing in any of them. No furniture, no fixtures, no hinge for a door that was never hung. The rooms go back and back into the rock, a honeycomb of prepared space, each one warm and damp and dim, each one echoing your footsteps back at you with a slight delay as if it is considering them.

The doorways are subtly wrong. Most are a little too tall and a little too narrow, proportioned for someone longer and slimmer than you. A few are so low you would have to crouch. One, in the upper tier, is round and waist-high. The stairs have risers of three different heights, alternating in a pattern you cannot quite hold in your head. The city has been planned for inhabitants, but it has not made up its mind who they will be.

---

## Light

What light there is comes from the stone itself.

Along certain seams, where the lattice is densest or the arranging most recent, the rock glows. It is a deep dim amber, the color of embers seen through smoke, never bright enough to read by, but enough to find your feet. The glow is strongest near the floor and at the bases of walls, so the city is lit from below. Faces become masks. The façades are underlined in faint fire and fade upward into darkness, and the vault is invisible except when the rain falls and each droplet catches a little of the amber on its way down.

Faint green comes off the ferns. There is the diffraction shimmer when you move your head. The fountain gives nothing back; it is a black eye in the center of the plaza.

The glow is not constant. It pulses, very slowly, over a period of minutes, brightening and dimming together across the whole city like a sleeper's breath. When the ventilation exhales and the rain comes, the glow rises. When the rain ends, it sinks. You begin to suspect that the heat, the water, the light, and the growing are all one process seen from different sides.

---

## The Working Face

At the end of the one street, where it should turn a corner or open onto a wider avenue, there is no end. There is the face.

The buildings simply stop being finished. A façade that is sharp and complete at one end becomes, ten paces on, a façade still being decided. Its window is present but its sill is soft-edged, the stone there dull and grainy rather than smooth. Beyond that the rock is translucent, warm enough to be uncomfortable to touch, and shapes are visible inside it. There are the ghosts of a doorway, the rising line of a stair, a runnel waiting to be opened. Beyond that is raw rock, rough, unarranged, sweating heavily.

This is where the sound comes from. Up close, the grinding resolves into a thousand smaller sounds, crackling, hissing, a deep creak like ice on a lake, all issuing from inside the stone as the lattice reaches into it and begins to insist. The heat pours off it. Water streams down the unfinished surfaces and steams. Seedlings of fern stand already rooted in the translucent zone, pale and folded, waiting for the stone around them to finish becoming a gutter.

If you mark the face and return after a few cycles, it has moved. It has advanced perhaps an arm's length, perhaps a room's depth. Behind it, the street is longer by the length of one more doorway. The city is growing at the pace of a plant. It grows steadily, toward something, without hurry and without pause.

---

## What It Is

It is a city in the stage before cities have people. It is a nursery, in the way a seedbed is a nursery, only here it is the houses that are germinating. Warmth, water, and green have been arranged to make the place ready, and the stone keeps arranging itself into the shapes of a settlement it has perhaps learned from somewhere and is reciting from memory, slightly misremembered, in dark new rock.

Stand in the plaza under the pale tree while the rain falls and the amber rises and the ventilation breathes, and the city feels less empty than *expectant*. Every room is prepared. Every lamp niche is waiting for a lamp. Every runnel carries water to a fountain no one drinks from. The noise goes on and on, the sound of a place being made for someone who has not yet arrived.

Then the rain thins. The glow sinks. The water rushes and then murmurs. At the end of the street the working face creaks, and a little more of the rock becomes a door.
