# The Steam Works at Kettle Point

The lava here is not old enough to have a proper name. The maps show it as a gray tongue pushed out into the shallow bay, a fan of black rock perhaps six hundred meters across. It is edged with sea on three sides and joined to the mainland by a causeway of crushed basalt and rail. The rock still has the shape it cooled into. It is ropy and slabbed, split into plates that tilt against one another like broken paving, and the edges are sharp enough to open a boot sole. Nothing has rounded it yet. Rain has not worn it, and the sea has barely begun to chew the margins. Where the flow met the water it shattered into black sand and glassy gravel. That is the only soft ground on the whole point, and it is already built on.

The heat never left. Forty meters down the rock is still hot enough to boil water, and that is why anyone came.

## The shape of the place

The Steam Works occupies nearly the entire point, but it is not large. You could walk its perimeter fence in twenty minutes, and you would never be out of earshot of the turbine hall. It is a settlement in miniature, a company town compressed onto a raft of stone.

The **turbine hall** stands at its center. It is a long brick building with a sawtooth roof, its clerestory windows so fogged and mineral-streaked they admit only a brown, submarine light. Around it the other buildings keep their distance, as if giving it room to breathe:

- a **separator yard**, where the wellheads rise from the rock like iron hydrants the size of chapels, each capped with gauges and bleed valves
- two **cooling ponds**, rectangular and walled in concrete, their surfaces permanently smoking
- a **machine shop** with a traveling crane
- a **pump house** on the seaward edge, drawing bay water for the condensers
- a row of **dormitories** for the shift crews, two stories of whitewashed block with iron balconies
- a **canteen**, a **dispensary**, a **stores shed**, and the small **office building** where the log books are kept

Between them run the pipes, and the pipes are the real streets of Kettle Point. They are lagged in aluminum cladding or bare and rust-bloomed, and they run on concrete saddles at waist height, at head height, and sometimes three stacked together on steel gantries. Footbridges step over them. Doors open onto them. You learn the town by learning where the pipes will let you cross.

There is a great deal of open ground between the buildings: black rock, gravel pads, a few painted parking lines where nothing is parked. The Works was laid out with room to expand, and it never did. The spaces are wide and empty, crossed by the occasional worker in a stained boiler suit and full of drifting vapor.

## The air

Kettle Point sits under its own weather. The ponds, the bleed valves, the cooling tower vents, and the fissures in the rock where natural steam still escapes all put moisture into the air faster than the bay wind can strip it away. The result is a low, permanent cloud, a dome of vapor roughly the footprint of the point. From the mainland it looks like a white cap on the water. From inside it is a ceiling perhaps sixty meters up, pearl-gray, glowing faintly where the sun sits behind it and never parting.

The light underneath is dim and even. There are no hard shadows at noon, only a soft gray pressure from above. At the edges of the dome, where the vapor thins over the water, the light turns yellow. Sodium lamps burn on the gantries day and night, because nobody ever found a time of day when they weren't needed. Each lamp wears a halo in the wet air.

The heat is enormous and wet. It does not come from the sky but rises from the ground and seeps out of the lagging, and the air carries it everywhere. Fifty degrees near the separators. Thirty-eight on the dormitory balconies at midnight. Everything sweats. The rock weeps condensate along its cracks. The brick walls are streaked with white salts and orange iron where water has run down them for decades along the same paths. Metal rails are always beaded. A handkerchief is soaked in an hour. The smell is sulfur, hot iron, wet concrete, and, when the wind comes off the bay, the green reek of tidal mud.

Rain falls, but you can hardly tell it apart from the air. It drips from the ceiling of vapor as warm as bathwater, and the gutters run with it continuously.

## The noise

The Works is never quiet. The turbines run at one speed, a deep, even drone felt in the sternum more than heard. Laid over it is the hiss of steam from a hundred bleed points, the rattle of the condenser pumps, the clank of a valve wheel somewhere, and the metronomic thud of the reciprocating pump in the seaward house, which has beaten at the same pace for as long as anyone on shift can remember. Every so often, never on schedule, a relief valve on one of the wellheads lifts. It shrieks, a sound like a locomotive in agony, for three or four seconds, then reseats. The workers do not look up.

Conversation happens at a shout, or by hand. The crews have a small, private sign vocabulary, and new hires learn it in their first week: *valve, gauge, hot, come here, stop, tea.*

The animals are loud too, because they have to be.

## The living things

Plants have barely found the point. The rock is too young and too hot, and there is little soil. What grows grows in the cracks:

- mats of acid-green moss where condensate trickles steadily
- a few tough ferns in the lee of the pond walls, their fronds tipped brown from the heat
- rust-colored algae in the drainage channels
- one fig tree, planted by some long-gone shift foreman beside the canteen door, stunted, dripping, and somehow fruiting

Everything else is bare black rock and concrete.

Animals, though, have come in numbers. The point is warm, wet, sheltered, and full of hiding places, and the bay around it is rich.

**Swifts** nest by the hundreds under the turbine hall eaves and in the gaps of the pipe lagging. They wheel in and out of the vapor ceiling in screaming flocks. Their calls are the only sound shrill enough to cut through the machines.

**Rock pigeons** crowd the warm roof of the machine shop. **Egrets** stalk the pump house outfall, where the discharge water pours back into the bay hot and full of stunned fish.

**Crabs**, small and dark red, live in every crack of the lava margin. At dusk they swarm up onto the gravel pads, so many that the ground seems to crawl. The workers walk carefully, and the crunch underfoot is part of the evening.

The drainage channels hold **frogs**, a single species of small brown frog that bred up in the warm water and never stopped. At night their calling is a steady mechanical trill, eerily in tune with the pumps.

There are **geckos** on every lamp post, fat on moths, and **cockroaches** in the dormitory walls. **Bats** live in the disused cable tunnel. Mosquitoes, oddly, are rare; something in the sulfur keeps them down. The ponds themselves are too hot for life at their centers, but at their cooler corners the concrete is furred with orange and green bacterial mats. **Shore flies** hover over them in low, humming clouds.

The animals have arranged themselves along the gradients of heat and water, each at the temperature it can stand, and they stay there.

## The rule of the place

What defines Kettle Point, more than the heat or the noise, is that it does not change.

The reservoir beneath it is vast relative to what the Works draws. The flow from the wells was set, long ago, to a figure that the rock can supply indefinitely. The turbines run at that figure. The ponds hold at that temperature. The vapor ceiling sits at that height. The wind off the bay is steady in direction because the heat of the point generates its own gentle convection, drawing air in low from the water and lifting it up the column. The system has found its balance, and it holds.

The log books in the office show this in their neat columns: pressure, temperature, output, recorded every hour for decades, and nearly identical line after line. The shifts rotate. The crews change. But the swifts nest in the same eaves, the salt streaks run down the same walls, and the relief valve on Well Four lifts about as often as it did when the office clerk's father kept the books. Even the fresh lava stays fresh. The new rock is too hot for lichen and too sheltered from surf for erosion, so the sharp edges stay sharp.

People who work here long enough stop noticing time. Days are marked only by the shift whistle, which sounds three times in each twenty-four hours, and by the crabs coming up at dusk. Beyond the causeway, on the mainland, seasons happen. Under the dome it is always this: warm rain, gray light, the drone of the turbines, the screaming of swifts, and steam rising from black stone that has not yet finished cooling and perhaps never will.
