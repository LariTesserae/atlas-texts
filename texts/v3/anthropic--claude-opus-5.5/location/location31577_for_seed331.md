# The Stilling Pan

It is a shallow bowl, perhaps four hundred paces across, and you do not notice you have entered it until the sound goes out of your footsteps.

Outside the rim there is ordinary country: warm lowland scrub, a slow river somewhere, cicadas in the heat. Then the ground tilts gently downward and the cicadas thin, as if heard through a closing door. By the time you reach the first pool the air is so quiet that your own breathing seems to happen at a distance. What replaces the sound is light. The whole basin glitters, not with the hard flash of sun on water but with a fine, crawling shimmer on every surface, as though the place were covered in frost that kept catching and losing the sun.

The shimmer is the sound. It has only been turned into something else.

---

## The ground

The Pan was once something taller. You can read it in the stone: pale, creamy, layered rock like old travertine, worn so long and so evenly that nothing sharp is left anywhere. It lies in low terraces, each lip rounded like the edge of a bar of soap that has been used for years. The terraces step down toward the center in shelves only a hand or two high, and every shelf holds water: warm, shin-deep, clear to the bottom, spilling over the lips in thin sheets that never quite become streams.

The rock weeps. Water comes up through it rather than across it, rising from some deep, patient source and seeping out along bedding planes, so the terraces are always slick and always full. Where the seep is strongest, the stone has grown pale crusts of new mineral, frilled and delicate. Where it has stopped, the older rock lies bare and polished, the color of a knuckle. The whole bowl is a single slow process of dissolving and re-depositing that has gone on for so long it has almost reached an equilibrium. It is nearly flat, and nearly finished.

Between the terraces there is a great deal of open space: plain water, plain stone, and air. The Pan does not feel crowded, at least not at first glance.

## What grows

The vegetation gathers where the water lingers and leaves the bare lips alone. There are cushion mosses in wet greens so saturated they look lit from within, thick enough to hold a footprint for an hour. There are stands of a reed with translucent, amber-colored stems, jointed like bamboo, each joint holding a bead of trapped water that acts as a lens. Floating mats of something like duckweed spread and contract across the pools according to a schedule no one has worked out.

There are only a few trees, perhaps nine in the whole basin, each standing well apart from the others. They are figs, or something descended from figs, and each one has grown around one of the old pillars. Their aerial roots come down in curtains, fuse into the stone at the pillar's base, and grip it like a hand around a glass. The trees are enormous for such small ground, broad and low-crowned. Their leaves are waxy and turn their faces constantly, following not the sun but the shimmer on the pillars.

## The pillars

There are eleven of them, which is the sum of the built world here, apart from a few other traces.

They are slender, about twice a person's height, and they are not stone, though they have taken on the color of the stone around them. They are made of a material that is neither glass nor metal nor ceramic. It is faintly warm, faintly yielding under a fingernail, and it heals scratches within a minute. Each has a subtle twist along its length, like a wrung cloth.

They are unimaginably old. You can tell because the ground has worn down around them. Each pillar stands on a foot of exposed foundation, a flared root of the same material that was once buried. The foundations carry faint waterlines, dozens of them, recording the slow lowering of the whole basin over what must be tens of thousands of years. The builders set these into ground that is no longer there.

What the pillars do is **drink sound and pay it out as light.**

Any vibration in the air of the Pan is pulled toward the nearest pillar, the way heat is pulled into cold. Somewhere in that twisted material it is converted, and it re-emerges as a faint luminescence scattered across nearby surfaces: water, wet stone, leaves, wings. A single spoken word produces a brief, pale ripple that runs across the pools around you and fades. A shout makes the terrace glow like a struck bell made visible. The conversion is not perfect. Low frequencies leak through as a sensation in the chest more than a sound, and the rim, beyond the pillars' reach, keeps its drips and rustles. But in the heart of the Pan the silence is nearly total, and the brightness is nearly constant.

The brightness is constant because the Pan is full of life.

## The fauna

It takes a few minutes before you understand how much of what you are looking at is moving.

The water in the pools is not merely clear. It is thick with glass shrimp, millions of them, each a fingernail long and fully transparent except for a pinhead of red at the heart. Where they school, the water bends light strangely, and you realize the "shimmer" in the deeper pools is partly their bodies. Among them move small silver fish with enormous eyes, and on the terrace lips there are snails, uncountable snails. Their shells are ridged in the same cream as the rock, packed so densely along the wet edges that the terraces appear to have a textured rind. When a shadow crosses them they all retract at once, and the lip of the terrace shifts color in a single wave.

The air is worse, or better. Midges rise from the mosses in columns that lean and twist like smoke, dense enough to cast shade. Tiny moths with mirrored wings, the scale of the metallic paint on a beetle's back, fill the space under the fig canopies. Every wingbeat is a sound, and the sound is taken by the pillars and returned as light, so each cloud of insects glows faintly with its own flight. A swarm moving across the basin is a traveling patch of soft radiance, visible even at noon. Damselflies, dozens of species, hover and dart in the sunnier air. Some have wings clear as nothing, some the color of weak tea, some patterned with dots that pulse.

Then there are the frogs. They are everywhere, one to every handspan of moss in the damp season: small, glossy, gold-green frogs with throats that inflate into near-transparent bubbles. They call constantly, and you will never hear them. What you see is the call converted, a pulse of light that flares on the surfaces nearest the calling frog, like a match struck under water. Over the generations the frogs have learned to use this. The males angle themselves toward the pillars and time their calls so the flash lands on the stone before a female. They are courting in light they can only make with sound.

The predators have learned as well. Long-legged wading birds, gray and white and silent as herons, stalk the terraces. One species has lost almost all its song, since there is no point in singing here. Another has done something cleverer: it clacks its bill in a rapid, rhythmic pattern near a fig's roots, and the resulting glow on the leaves draws moths down out of the canopy toward the bird's waiting beak. Bats hunt the Pan at dusk, and because their echolocation is also drunk by the pillars, they hunt blind. They fly instead by the light their own calls make, each chirp painting a brief, pale map of the moths ahead. In the half-dark the Pan is scattered with these little interrupted flares, like someone searching for something with a failing torch.

The first overwhelming thing about the fauna is their number. The second is that all of it is happening silently, within a few steps of you, while the open stretches of stone between the terraces stay bare and still.

## Traces

Beyond the pillars there is almost nothing built. What there is:

- **A channel** cut straight across three terraces, perfectly straight and perfectly smooth, now half-filled with mineral crust. Water still runs along it, though no water should run on ground this flat. It flows slowly and steadily toward the center.
- **A single low structure** near the east rim, shaped something like a house: a rounded box of the same pillar-material, the size of a garden shed, with no door, no windows, and no seam. Rain does not bead on it. The snails do not climb it. When you put your palm against it, it warms slightly and you feel, through your hand rather than your ears, a slow pulse like a very large, very calm heartbeat.
- **Small objects in the silt**, occasionally: a smooth ring, a disc the size of a coin that is colder than the water around it, a short rod that lengthens when held and shrinks when set down. They are rare and scattered, and the herons sometimes pick them up and drop them, the way they would test a pebble.

No inscriptions. No clue to purpose beyond the fact of what the pillars do. Whoever built this either wanted silence and light, wanted a place where something could listen without being disturbed, or wanted something else entirely and the silence is only a side effect of an engine still running after its owners have long gone.

## The day

**Dawn** is the brightest hour, which is backwards from how it should be. Before the sun has cleared the rim, the frogs, birds, and insects all wake at once. In any other marsh this would be a roar. Here it is a flood of light that rises up from the mosses and water and runs up the pillars until they stand like lit candles in the blue half-dark, and the whole basin blazes silently for twenty minutes before the real sun arrives to join it.

**Midday** is hot, humid, and dazzling. The shrimp sink to the cool bottom of the pools. The frogs pull in under the moss. The midge columns thin and stand still. The light now is mostly sunlight, broken into thousands of pieces by the water sheets over the terrace lips and by the lensing beads in the reeds, so the air is full of small moving caustics. This is the quietest the Pan becomes in any sense.

**Afternoon** brings the rain nearly every day in the warm season. The clouds build over the lowland outside, and when they break over the Pan, you see the rain rather than hear it. Every drop striking water or stone is a tiny sound, and every tiny sound is converted, so a downpour here is a sheet of sparks. The basin glitters from end to end, and the reeds and fig leaves run with light. The animals respond as if it were a second dawn. The frogs call into it and the birds hunt through it. When there is thunder, and it is rare but it happens, the pillars take the thunder too, and for one long second the whole Pan goes white. It is soundless, like being inside a camera flash. The birds hunch. The snails retract. Then it is over, and the rain goes on sparkling.

**Evening** belongs to the bats, and to the fireflies. The fireflies have given up generating their own light, since sound does it for them more cheaply. They produce a faint, high-pitched whine instead, too thin for anything but the pillars to catch, and each insect wears a small halo made of its own voice.

**Night** is not dark. It is dim and full of drifting glows, like a sky seen from underneath, and it is full of motion: the shrimp rising, the fish feeding, the snails grazing the terraces in their millions, a sound like nothing because it is being turned into a slow, pale luminescence along every wet lip of stone. It looks as though the terraces are gently breathing.

## Being there

Standing in the Pan is intimate and disorienting in equal measure. The world is loud with life, but none of the loudness reaches you through your ears. Instead your eyes fill with it. You learn to watch for the flare that means a frog under your foot, and the patch of brightness that means a midge swarm is about to engulf you. Speaking feels indecent, because your voice lights up the pool in front of you for everyone to see.

People who spend long here, and a few do, return to the ordinary world with a strange habit: whenever someone speaks to them, they look not at the speaker but at the space just beside them, as though waiting for the words to appear.

The Pan itself goes on, nearly flat, nearly finished, still weeping water up through its ancient stone, still drinking every sound made within it and paying each one back as light. The engine hums in its doorless house. The trees tighten their roots around the pillars by a fraction of an inch a year. Somewhere under the terraces, the source of the water rises, patient as ever. The frogs keep calling silently, in bright light, to anyone who can see.
