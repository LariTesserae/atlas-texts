# The Hollow of Unceasing Voices

There is no sky here, though for a long time you might not notice. The dark is too big for a ceiling. It goes up and out in every direction with the openness of a night plain. Only the rain gives it away: it falls straight down, cold and irregular, in heavy single drops that arrive from no cloud. Somewhere far overhead, beyond any reach of sight, is a roof of stone, sweating.

This is a basin under the earth, a sunken lowland roofed by rock and lying only a little above the water table. It is wide enough to have regions: shorelines, uplands no taller than a house, marshes of mineral slurry, long gentle slopes of rubble worn smooth. A person could walk for days and never touch a wall. Nobody has, because nobody has ever been here, and nothing here was made by anything that makes things.

What fills it is noise.

## The Sound

The sound comes before everything else, and it never stops. It is not one sound but a pressure made of millions: clicks, chirrs, trills, stridulations, thin ascending whistles too high to hear that you feel instead as an ache behind the teeth, wet croaks from the pools, and under all of it a slow grinding hum. The hum comes from the stone itself.

It is deafening in the literal sense. Within an hour an ear would stop resolving it into parts and hear only a roar, like standing inside a waterfall made of insects. But the roar has structure. It swells and thins in regional currents. Over the eastern flats it is dominated by a dry, fast ticking. In the fluted galleries it rises into a shimmering whistle-chorus, and along the warm springs it drops into a bubbling bass. You could map the basin by ear, and nearly everything that lives here does.

## The Ground

Beneath the noise, the land is old and has settled into itself.

The bedrock is a pale, soft limestone, banded with darker seams of something like flint. Ten thousand years of dripping have not cut it so much as softened it. Every edge is rounded. Every rise wears a skin of flowstone, cream and ochre and rust, laid down in thin glossy sheets that ripple like poured wax. The slopes are terraced by rimstone dams, low scalloped walls of calcite a hand's width high, each holding a shallow pan of still water. There are thousands of them, stepped one below another down every incline, like rice paddies built by patience alone.

The pillars are what give the place its shape. Stalactite and stalagmite met here so long ago that the join has vanished, and what remains are columns. Some are as thin as a wrist, some as thick as a hill, and they rise out of the dark and continue into it. In the central reaches they stand close enough to form forests, groves of stone trunks fluted and knuckled and hung with draperies of translucent calcite. The draperies are the source of the hum. The basin's slow breathing moves air through the fissures, and the thin stone curtains vibrate. Each resonates at its own pitch. Taken together they sound like a vast, sustained, never-resolving chord, played on glass underwater.

Between the columns the floor is layered with something that is not rock. It is guano, deep and ancient, compacted in the lower strata into a dark crumbly soil and fresh on top. It is soft and warm and it moves: it seethes with life. In places it lies meters deep. It is the basin's real ground, the stratum everything else depends on, and it smells of ammonia, mushroom, and wet iron, a smell so thick it has texture.

## The Water

Water here is intermittent and local. It is never absent and never a flood.

It comes in three forms. First, the ceiling rain: condensation gathering overhead and falling in scattered cold drops, heavier in some regions than others, so that there are "wet districts" and "dry districts" with nothing visible to divide them. Second, the rimstone pools: countless shallow pans of water so mineral-heavy that it feels slick between the fingers, each one skinned with a floating film of calcite so fine it breaks like frost.

Third, and most important, the springs.

In a few dozen places across the basin, water rises from below. It is warm, not hot, and it reeks of sulfur. Where it seeps out, the ground is coated in bacterial mats: white, lemon-yellow, sometimes a startling pale pink, in slick gelatinous sheets that ripple in the current like wet silk. These are the nearest thing the Hollow has to vegetation. They are not plants. They eat the chemistry of the deep earth, and everything in the basin ultimately eats them.

## The Heaving

The springs do not flow steadily. They pulse.

Every few hours, on a rhythm that drifts and has never quite repeated, something far below shifts and the springs surge. Warm water floods out of the vents and spills across the mat fields. A breath of humid, sulfurous air rolls outward across the basin, and the whole Hollow responds. This is the Heaving, the basin's only equivalent of a day.

As the warm air rises and meets the cold stone overhead, the rain thickens. For an hour or so the whole basin is in drizzle, and a low fog forms in the hollows. The stone draperies change pitch as the air moves differently through them, and the great chord slides upward, a slow glissando everyone hears. It is the signal. The noise, already unbearable, doubles.

The mats bloom in the fresh flow. In the pools, larvae hatch in their billions: pale, soft-bodied, finger-length things with feathery gills that wriggle up out of the slurry and molt, all at once, into winged adults. They rise from the mat fields in columns that look, if you could see them, like smoke. They are nearly blind, weak fliers, driven upward by the warm air, and they fill the lower dark with a whining so dense it has weight.

Then the hunters come.

## The Fauna

Life here is overwhelming. It is not diverse in the way a forest is diverse; there are perhaps a few hundred kinds of animal. But their numbers defy sense. They are packed into every volume of air and every crevice of stone, layered, touching, stacked. A column's surface is not stone but a pelt of roosting bodies. A patch of guano holds more animals per handful than a meadow holds per acre.

**The grazers** live closest to the springs: pale snails with translucent shells that glide across the mats in slow herds, amphipods like grains of rice, sightless shrimp in the warm runs. The mat fields crackle constantly with their feeding, a sound like frying.

**The crawlers** inherit the guano. Cave crickets as long as a hand, with antennae three times their body length, carpet whole slopes. Their stridulation, a fast dry ticking, is the dominant voice of the eastern flats. Beetles, millipedes, and pale spiders hunt among them, and these are hunted in turn by long, flat, many-legged predators that flow over the rimstone like spilled oil.

**The flyers** are the basin's true populace. They are small furred creatures, bat-like in the way anything that flies in the dark by sound ends up bat-like, though their wings are narrower and they have no eyes at all, only a blunt face crowded with ridges and folds for shaping sound. They roost on the columns in their millions. At the Heaving they drop from the stone in waves, and the air becomes a lattice of calling bodies passing within finger-widths of one another, never colliding, feeding on the rising larvae in great looping sweeps.

They sing to see. Each species, and within species each colony, uses its own frequency band, and here the basin reveals its strangest habit. Each colony roosts on a column that resonates in its register. The stone gives their voices back to them, amplified and clarified, and a colony on its home pillar can "see" farther than a colony anywhere else. Over uncounted generations, flyers and stone have sorted themselves into agreement. Walk through the column forest and each great trunk sings with its own voice, a thicket of pitches, every pillar a tower of a single note. Young flyers leave to find their own stone. Where no pillar matches them, colonies do something stranger: they roost for generations on imperfect columns, and their droppings, calcified by the dripping water, slowly thicken the stone and shift its resonance until it fits. Some pillars in the Hollow are more guano than rock. They have been tuned.

**The deep things** are few, and seldom sensed. In the largest springs there are long pale swimmers with mouths like bags that sift the warm water for shrimp. In the high dark there is something that hunts the flyers and makes no sound at all.

## The Rule of Silence

In a world made of noise, silence is the only warning.

The silent hunter, whatever it is (large, slow-winged, known only by its absence), moves through the high dark without calling. It navigates, perhaps, by listening to everyone else's echoes. Where it passes, the flyers nearest it stop singing, and those beyond them notice the gap and stop too.

So the Hollow has a phenomenon found nowhere else: silence moves through it like weather. A hole in the roar opens somewhere in the dark and spreads outward in a ring, a travelling front of stillness hundreds of meters across. Inside it the only sound is the stone's hum and the patter of ceiling rain, suddenly audible. Everything lies flat against the pillars, not breathing. Then the ring passes and the noise floods back in behind it, and the hole drifts on somewhere else.

On the busiest Heavings several of these silences drift through the basin at once, meeting, merging, splitting. Heard from a distance, the roar of the Hollow is threaded with them, pockets of hush that wander like shadows. In a place without light, they are the shadows.

## The Light

There is almost no light, but there is some.

The bacterial mats glow faintly in patches, a cold blue-green barely distinguishable from the afterimages the eye invents in total dark. Some of the larvae carry a spark of luminescence as they rise, so for a few minutes after a Heaving, the lower air over the springs is freckled with tiny drifting points, ten thousand faint embers climbing into nothing and going out one by one as they are eaten. Nothing here has eyes to see it. The light is an accident, a byproduct of chemistry. It is beautiful and wasted.

Away from the springs, there is only the dark, absolute, and full of sound.

## Between Heavings

Then the pulse subsides. The springs slacken to a trickle, the rain thins to its old scattered drops, and the fog settles into the rimstone pools and vanishes. The great chord slides back down to its resting pitch. Sated, the flyers return to their pillars, and the roar eases to what passes here for quiet: only overwhelming, not unbearable.

In the slack hours the slower processes of the basin continue. Water drips, and each drop leaves a layer of stone a molecule thick. Rimstone walls rise by a hair. The guano settles and compacts, and the crickets work it over. Flowstone creeps. The pillars thicken. Somewhere in the silent high dark, the hunter digests.

The Hollow has been doing this for longer than it would take to tell. Its shapes are not young. They have settled into an old, rounded balance, the landform and the life folded into each other so thoroughly that neither could be described alone. The stone is shaped by the animals, and the animals are shaped by the stone's voice. Everything feeds on the breath of the deep earth, and the whole place sings, endlessly, to see itself.
