# The Kettle

You would not find it by looking. The lava field around it is only a few decades old, a black, jagged plain that runs almost level to the sea, still sharp enough to cut boots. From above there is nothing to see but a low blister in the rock, a dome no taller than a barn roof, cracked across its crown. Steam leaks from the crack in a thin, steady plume that the wind usually flattens before it rises.

You hear it before anything else. From half a mile off there is a sound under the wind, a deep continuous exhalation, like a furnace door left open, or surf that never pulls back. Closer, the sound separates into layers. By the time you reach the blister's edge it is loud enough that you feel it in your breastbone.

There is an entrance on the seaward side, where part of the dome's wall slumped inward while it was still soft. The rock there folded like cloth and set that way, forming a ramp of glassy black ropes you can climb down.

---

## The Room

Inside is a single hollow, roughly egg-shaped, perhaps forty paces long and fifteen high at the center. It is a gas bubble that formed in the lava as it cooled. Its walls have the texture of something recently molten: slick and glazed in places, bristling in others with little spines and drips where the ceiling sagged and hardened mid-fall. Nothing here has been worn down. Every edge is still the edge it cooled with. Run a hand along the wall and it comes back scored.

The air is hot the way a laundry is hot, wet and total. It is close to body temperature near the entrance and well above it deeper in. Your clothes are soaked within a minute, and you can't tell sweat from condensation. The whole room is sweating. Water beads on every surface of the ceiling, gathers on the tips of the lava drips, and falls, so there is a constant scattered rain inside the chamber, warm drops on your head and shoulders. The walls weep. Thin sheets of water slide down the glassy faces, catching the light in long wavering bands.

The light comes from the crack in the crown, a jagged seam of ordinary daylight. It enters as a solid-looking blade, visible from end to end because the air is so full of vapor. Where it strikes the floor the steam glows white. Everywhere else it is dim, grey, and diffuse, with every surface faintly reflective.

## The Vent

At the far end of the chamber the floor drops into a fissure about as wide as a doorway. This is the source of the sound and the heat. Steam comes up out of it steadily, under pressure, in a column that hits the ceiling and spreads in rolling sheets across it before sinking again along the walls. The whole room circulates like the inside of a lung.

The noise at the fissure is enormous: a roar with texture, full of crackle and hiss where water meets hot rock somewhere below. Close by you cannot hear yourself shout. Farther back it becomes almost bearable, a wall of sound you learn to stand inside.

The steam carries minerals. It smells faintly of struck matches and wet stone. Wherever it condenses it leaves a trace of silica behind, too little to see in a day or a year. Over decades the traces add up.

## The Machine

Someone found this place when the lava was barely cool and decided to use it.

Bolted over the fissure, on footings drilled straight into the rock, is a steam engine. It is not large, about the size of a small cart: a riveted iron housing with a hood that funnels part of the vent's output into a single intake pipe. From there the steam drives a piston that turns a flywheel as tall as a person. The flywheel drives a belt, and the belt drives a dynamo in a squat cast-iron casing set a few paces back on a leveled shelf of rock. Beside the dynamo stands a small corrugated shed, three walls and a roof, which once held tools, a stool, and a logbook swollen shut with damp.

The machine is still running.

Nobody tends it. The shed has been empty for a long time. But the vent never stops, so the machine never stops. The flywheel turns at a steady, unhurried speed. Its governor, two iron balls on hinged arms, spins above the housing at exactly the height it has always spun, holding the pace constant. The piston knocks on every stroke, a deep iron *thunk* that comes through the roar of the vent like a heartbeat through a chest. The belt slaps. The dynamo whines, a thin high note that rides over everything else.

A cable runs from the dynamo up the chamber wall, clipped to iron staples hammered into the glassy rock. It ends in a single lamp hanging from a hook near the entrance: a bulb inside a wire cage, glowing a dull steady orange. It lights nothing important. It may once have lit the path down the slumped ramp. Now it burns in a room full of daylight and steam, as it has for years, because nothing has told it to stop.

There was probably meant to be more. A second set of footings sits empty near the wall with its bolts rusted to nubs. A length of heavy pipe leads into a borehole in the rock and stops. Someone planned to carry power or heat out of this pocket to somewhere else, and either the plan was abandoned or the place it was meant for no longer exists.

## The Slow Stone

Once the machine is familiar, this is the strangest thing about the Kettle: it is turning to stone.

The silica in the steam deposits on everything, and it deposits most heavily where steam touches cooler metal, which means the machine. Every exposed surface of the iron wears a skin of pale mineral crust. On the housing it is thick enough to have rounded the rivets into soft bumps and blurred the edges of the cast lettering, so the maker's name reads only as a row of shallow dents. The empty footings are lumps of pale sinter. The shed's corrugations have filled in on the side facing the vent, so that wall looks less like metal than like a rippled shelf of rock.

The moving parts stay clean. The flywheel's rim, the piston rod, the spinning governor, and the belt are kept polished by their own motion. The crust builds right up to the edge of whatever moves and stops there. The machine has become a dark iron core that works inside a pale stone body that is slowly closing around it. Over the years the clearances shrink. The crust on the flywheel housing now comes within a finger's width of the rim, and in places the rim has begun grinding a groove into it. The groove squeals faintly on each turn, a new note that did not used to be part of the sound.

At some point, decades away, the stone will win. A clearance will close, the flywheel will bind, the governor will seize with its arms out, and the machine will stop. Then the steam will keep coming, the crust will keep building, and eventually the engine will be a pale formation on the floor of a bubble in the rock, shaped suspiciously like something built.

Until then it runs. Nothing in the room changes fast enough to see except the turning of the wheel.

## What Grows

Very little. The heat and the sulfur keep most life out. What does survive has taken the narrowest margins.

Near the entrance, where the air is only warm and the daylight reaches, a few ferns grow from cracks in the slumped ramp. They are small, tough, and dark green, their fronds permanently beaded with water. They grow almost horizontally toward the opening, straining away from the heat.

Along the runoff channels, where condensation gathers and trickles toward a low point in the floor, there are mats of something between moss and slime. They run in bands of color that follow the temperature. Near the entrance they are rust-orange. Farther in they turn olive, then a bright sulfur-yellow, and closest to the vent they are a pale, almost luminous green. Past that line nothing grows. The bands shift by inches over the seasons as the vent breathes a little stronger or weaker. You would only notice if you came back years apart.

There are no animals. No insects hover in the steam and no birds nest in the crack. The Kettle is too hot, too sour, and too loud. The only movement is water, steam, and iron.

## The Sound, Again

After a while you stop hearing the sound as noise and start hearing it as a structure.

The vent's roar is the floor of it, broad and constant. The piston's knock is the pulse, slow and regular. The dynamo's whine is the thread across the top, and the new squeal of the flywheel against its crust is a small rough note that comes once per turn. Under all of it is the dripping: thousands of warm drops falling from the ceiling onto rock, iron, and the shallow pool at the low point of the floor, a scattered patter that never quite falls into rhythm.

There is also a resonance. The chamber is a hollow egg of hard glassy rock, and at certain pressures the vent's roar hits the room's own note. When that happens, perhaps a few times a day, the whole Kettle hums. The pitch is low enough to rattle the shed's metal walls and make the lamp swing on its hook. It builds over a minute or so, holds, and fades as the vent's output drifts off pitch again. The machine seems to settle into the hum. The knock of the piston falls into phase with it for a while before slipping out.

Nobody designed that. It emerged from the size of the room, the pressure of the steam, and the speed the governor holds, and it will last only as long as all three stay as they are.

## Rules of the Place

- **Everything wet stays wet.** Nothing dries in the Kettle. Paper swells, wood softens, cloth grows heavy. Only stone and iron endure, and iron endures only by becoming stone.
- **Motion keeps things clean.** Whatever moves stays free of crust; whatever holds still is buried by it. The machine's working parts are the only surfaces in the room the mineral cannot claim, and only for as long as they keep moving.
- **Time runs slowly and in one direction.** The vent pulses on a scale of hours, the algae bands shift over seasons, and the crust thickens over years. The one fast process in the room is the flywheel, which goes around and around and changes nothing.
- **The place is sealed off by its noise.** Inside the Kettle you cannot hear the wind or the sea. Outside, once you have climbed back up the ramp and walked a few hundred yards across the black rock, you cannot hear the Kettle at all, only a faint exhalation under the wind, which you might mistake for surf.

Behind you, in a pocket in the rock that almost no one knows about, the lamp is still burning orange in the steam and the stone is still closing in.
