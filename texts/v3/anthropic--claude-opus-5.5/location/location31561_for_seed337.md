# The Wet Edge

## Approaching

You come on it without warning. The plateau is thin grass and gravel under a wide, pale sky. The ground stays level, and then it stops. There is no slope, no scree, no crumbling lip. The land ends at a line so clean it looks drawn, and below that line is a city.

It lies in the ground, not on it. It is a great irregular pit, perhaps a mile and a half across at its widest and wandering off in lobes and side-cuts where the stone was good. Its walls drop in terraces and steps, and every face is honeycombed with doorways, windows, stairs, niches, channels and ledges. There are no roofs to speak of. The city was not built. It was subtracted. Every room is a hollow left behind where the stone was taken away.

The first thing to understand is that none of it is old.

## The Stone

The rock is a soft, cream-to-honey limestone, fine-grained and so full of water when first opened that it cuts almost like hard cheese. Quarriers elsewhere would call that water *quarry sap*. Here it is simply *the wet*, and the whole life of the city turns on it.

Fresh stone gives to an antler pick and a wooden mallet. A flint blade, drawn with patience, will score a line a finger deep. Once exposed to air the stone begins to dry, and as it dries it hardens. The pore water carries dissolved lime to the surface and leaves it there as a skin. Within a season a cut face goes from yielding to stubborn. Within a few years it rings under a knuckle like fired clay. The upper terraces, the oldest parts of the city, are hard as pottery now. Nobody could carve them further if they tried.

So the city cannot stay where it is. It lives at the wet edge, the band of freshly exposed, still-soft rock at the bottom and back of the pit. That is where the work happens and where new rooms are opened. Every generation the city sinks a little further into the earth, following its own softness downward.

Nothing here has weathered. There has been no time. Every surface carries its making. Chisel furrows run in neat diagonal courses across the walls. Pick-scars cluster like fish-scales. On some faces you can still read where a cutter paused, shifted her weight, and began a new stroke. Edges are sharp enough to cut a careless palm. Corners meet at true angles. There are no rounded thresholds or hollowed steps, no gentle wear of feet and water. Even the stairs worn by traffic have only begun to soften, a faint polished dip at the center of each tread on the busiest routes. It is the only sign that time has touched the place at all.

The color deepens with age. New-cut stone is almost white, wet and slightly translucent at the edges like soap. A year's exposure turns it cream. Old faces near the rim have gone a warm biscuit color with a faint grey bloom. Look up from the bottom of the pit and you see the city's history laid out as a gradient of color. You stand in white. The sky is framed in tan.

## The Air

The pit holds cold. On still nights the plateau's chill slides over the rim and pours downward, and the lowest terraces fill with it the way a bowl fills with water. Mornings are the city's damp hour. Condensation beads on every face, darkens the stone, and gathers in drops that run down the chisel grooves as if the grooves were cut for them. On the deepest levels a thin mist often lies at knee height until mid-morning, then lifts in slow rags and burns off as sunlight reaches down the walls.

The damp is not an accident. It is a crop. Cutters want their faces wet, and the dew helps. Along the working edge they hang wetted hides over surfaces they plan to carve, and they lay stone basins at the tops of walls so water seeps down through the rock. There are channels no wider than a thumb, carved on purpose to lead dew and rain across faces that would otherwise dry too soon. The whole city is laced with them: fine grooves wandering down the walls, branching and rejoining, guiding moisture like a vine guides sap.

It rains perhaps one day in five in the cool season, rarely in the warm. When it rains, the city drinks. Every roofless room becomes a catch-basin. The channels run audibly, the only time the city could be called loud. All the water finds its way down to the sump, a dark pool at the lowest point of the current cut. By the dry weeks the sump has shrunk to a green-black eye, and the lines of people carrying water are longer and slower.

It is cool rather than cold. At noon the sun-facing terraces grow almost warm, and the stone gives off a faint mineral breath, like a cellar or a quarry after rain. In the shaded clefts it is always the temperature of a well.

## Density

There is no empty room in this city, and almost no empty wall.

Because every dwelling is a hollow, neighbors are not beside you. They are around you, above, below, on the far side of a partition left a hand's breadth thick. Rooms are cut as close as the stone will allow and sometimes closer. Here and there a wall has been breached by accident, and the two households have simply shared the opening ever since, or filled it with a wicker screen.

The terraces are packed. Doorways stand shoulder to shoulder, stairways cut across stairways, and narrow ledges serve as streets, porches, drying yards and workshops all at once. Laundry hangs from pegs driven into the soft newer stone. Baskets of cut blocks, bundles of reeds carried in from the plateau streams, clay jars, hides stretched on frames, sleeping children, grinding stones and coils of rope all compete for every flat surface. Where the pit widens into open floor, a market happens most mornings, so crowded that a person can hardly turn around.

Even in the air there is little room. Rope-and-pulley lines stretch across the side-cuts, carrying baskets from terrace to terrace. Hide awnings on poles give shade. Smoke from small hearths rises in thin columns and gathers under the rim on still days, a faint blue haze that makes the sky look farther away.

## Sound

For all its crowding, the city is quiet.

Part of that is the stone. Soft, damp limestone does not ring. A pick into fresh rock makes a thud, not a crack. A flint drawn across wet stone makes a whisper. The main sound of the working edge is a low, constant rasp: dozens of cutters sawing with sand and water and strips of wood, scraping, smoothing. It is less like a quarry than like someone brushing a horse in the next room.

Part of it is custom. The walls between households are thin, and the pit carries voices. A word spoken at the bottom can be caught three terraces up. People speak low from long habit. Children are hushed. Arguments are held in whispers or taken up to the plateau. There is a quiet, practical courtesy to it. Nobody wants to be overheard, and everyone always could be.

So the soundscape is a layered murmur. Water drips in the channels. Feet whisper on stairs, since most go barefoot or in soft hide. Querns grind somewhere. A mallet thumps on a wooden wedge. Now and then comes the knock of a block settling into a basket, a baby's cry quickly soothed, or wind passing over the rim with a long, soft note like breath across a jar-mouth. At dusk the swallows come, and their thin calls are the brightest sound of the day.

## Tools and Work

The people of the pit work stone with antler, flint, bone, fire-hardened wood, and a very little copper. There are perhaps forty copper chisels in the whole city. Each is named, handed down, and kept greased in a leather sleeve. Most cutting is done with antler picks, flint gravers, wooden wedges soaked to swell, and saws that are nothing more than bands of wood or rawhide drawn back and forth with wet sand.

Blocks cut from the wet edge are not wasted. Some are hauled up the ramps and pulley-lines and carried off across the plateau to trade. Some are set out to cure on the upper terraces, where they harden into building stone. Grinders, basins, hearthstones, lamp-dishes and weights are all shaped soft and then left to set. The city's wealth is the softness of its floor. Its craft is timing: working each piece while it yields and finishing it before it hardens.

Everyone knows the phrase "before it sets." It means *now, while there's still time*. It is said to children, to lovers, to the old.

## Life in the Stone

Plants are few, and they grow where water is.

Mosses come first. On shaded faces that stay damp, a green velvet follows the dew channels in thin lines, so that some walls look drawn in green ink along their grooves. Fine maidenhair ferns cling where seeps emerge from bedding planes. Pale liverworts spread in the corners of cisterns. On the older, drier terraces there is almost nothing, only a grey-orange crust of lichen beginning at the rim.

Here and there a real plant has taken hold. A wild fig roots in a crack near the eastern rim, its pale roots flattened against the stone and feeling downward. Tufts of grass line the upper ramps. Households keep small gardens in hauled soil: beans, onions and a few herbs in stone troughs placed where the sun reaches longest. Reeds and rushes gather around the sump in the wet season and are cut back for thatch and basketry before they can spread.

Animals are rarer still. Swallows and swifts nest in the cleanest, highest niches and hunt the midges that rise from the sump at dusk. Rock doves occupy some of the abandoned upper rooms. A small grey lizard basks on the warm terraces at noon and vanishes into chisel cracks at a footstep. Children prize them and never manage to catch one. A few dogs belong to households. Rats, surprisingly, are scarce. There is little soil to burrow in and little that stays hidden in so open and crowded a place. Once in a long while an owl roosts on the rim and watches the pit through the night, and people take it as a mark of good or bad fortune depending on which terrace it faces.

## The Rhythm of the Place

The city runs on several cycles at once, and all of them are visible.

**Daily:** The cold pools overnight. Dew gathers at dawn, and the cutters rush to use the morning's softness. Sunlight descends the walls through the morning, one terrace at a time. People follow the warmth outward onto the ledges at midday and retreat into the shade and damp of the rooms in the afternoon. Hearths are lit at dusk. The swallows hunt.

**Seasonal:** Rains fill the sump and drain through the channels. In the dry season the city shrinks toward its water. Work moves from face to face as some dry out and others are wetted. Reeds grow and are harvested. Traders come over the plateau when the ways are firm.

**Generational:** The slowest and most important. The floor lowers. Households move downward, following the wet edge, and leave their old rooms to harden. The upper terraces become storehouses, workshops, curing yards, dove-cotes, and finally tombs. The dead are laid in sealed niches in the hardest, oldest stone, near the rim, as close to the sky as the city reaches. The living go deeper. A grandmother can point up the wall to the room where she was born, now pale and hard and holding someone else's grain.

Nobody knows how deep the soft stone goes. Some say the wet edge will carry on forever. Others say that one day the picks will strike hard rock, and the city will have to stop and learn to live in its own hardening, or else climb out and begin again somewhere new. The oldest cutters keep a record in scratched marks at the bottom of the pit, a lineage of depth. The newest marks are always the whitest.

## Standing at the Bottom

Stand at the wet edge at midmorning and look up. The walls rise around you in steps of darkening color, white through cream to tan, crowded with doorways and ledges and moving people. Water whispers down its thousand small grooves. Smoke threads upward. A basket sways overhead on its rope. The mist is lifting off the sump. A swallow cuts across the square of sky. Someone near you draws a flint through soft stone with a sound like a long exhale, and the face under the blade is wet and bright and new, as if the world under it were only now beginning.
