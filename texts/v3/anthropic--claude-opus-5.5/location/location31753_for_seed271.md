# The Drip Hollow

The ground gives no warning. You cross flat, low country, grey scrub on thin soil over pale rock, the land barely above the level of a sea you can't see. Then the earth simply stops, and there is a hole.

It is not large. Forty paces across, perhaps, and roughly round, the way a thing becomes round after a very long time of being worn at from every side equally. The rim is soft-edged limestone, the color of old bone, its lip rolled over and smoothed until it looks less broken than poured. Long ago, measured in something larger than centuries, the roof of a cavern gave way here. What remains is half a room: open sky over the eastern side, and over the western side a great undercut shelf of rock that still holds, curving overhead like the brim of a hat pulled low.

## Going Down

There is a way in. On the south side someone cut steps into a slump of fallen rubble. There are eleven of them, shallow, each hollowed in the middle into a smooth dish by feet that stopped coming a long time ago. The edges have gone round. Moss holds the back corner of every step, and only the back corner, where the drip-water gathers and lingers. The pattern repeats down the stair exactly: a green crescent tucked into each tread, eleven small moons.

As you descend, the air changes. Up top it was dry, warm, and moving slightly. At the fourth step it stops moving. By the eighth it is heavier and closer, the way a room feels when many people have just left it. At the bottom it is warm the way skin is warm, and it holds a dampness you feel on your forearms before you see it anywhere.

## The Light

The light is dim, and it never becomes anything else.

Sun enters only on the open eastern side, and even there it comes at a slant, through the height of the rim, already tired. It falls on the floor as a pale, diffuse wash, not a beam. Nothing here casts a sharp shadow. Under the overhang the light thins to a grey-gold half-dark in which your eyes keep adjusting and never quite finish. The back wall is visible but not legible. You can see that there is something carved there, and you have to go close and put your hand on it to learn what.

Noon and late afternoon look nearly the same down here. The rim takes the edges off the day. Dawn and dusk are the only real events, and they are quiet ones: a slow brightening, a slow withdrawal.

## Stone and Water

Everything is limestone, and the limestone is wet without being soaked. It sweats. The overhang carries a fine, even beading of condensation across its whole underside, thousands of droplets held in place like a skin of glass seeds. Some of them, very slowly, grow heavy enough to fall.

That falling is the hollow's one clock. Beneath the deepest part of the overhang, a single point on the ceiling has become the place where water prefers to leave. It lets go one drop at a time at a steady interval: about the length of a slow breath out, then a pause, then the next. The drop falls perhaps three times a man's height into a basin.

The basin is partly natural and partly not. The drip wore a hollow into the floor on its own, and at some point hands took that hollow and finished it. They squared one side, cut a lip, and made a shallow channel leading away toward the open part of the floor. The channel is dry. The water in the basin never rises high enough to reach it. It sits a finger's width below the lip, clear enough that the bottom shows pale, and whatever falls in seems to leave again through the stone itself, at exactly the rate it arrives. The level does not change. It did not change yesterday. The mineral line on the basin wall is a single thin band, crisp and unrepeated. It has never had cause to draw a second.

Elsewhere the water is only a suggestion: a darker stain spreading down the back wall like a shadow of something that isn't there, a place where the floor feels cool underfoot. The eastern half, under open sky, gets the occasional rain. When it comes it soaks straight into the porous floor and is gone within the hour, and it leaves no puddles. The hollow does not collect. It only holds.

## What Grows

Very little, and only in exact places.

Maidenhair fern, a dozen fronds, clings in a curtain along the seep line on the back wall, where the stain is darkest. Each frond is small, and the whole colony is no bigger than a spread cloak. They grow outward into the dim and no farther. Where the wet ends, they end.

A fig lives on the rim. You don't see the tree itself from below, only its roots, which have come down the northern face in a long, pale, braided rope, fused to the rock in places and hanging free in others. The root reaches the floor, spreads into three toes, and grips. It has been doing this so long that it has flattened itself against the stone like wax. It is the largest living thing in the hollow, and you could almost miss it, because it has taken the rock's color.

Beyond these: the moss moons on the stair, a grey-green crust of lichen on the sunlit rim stones, and a few tufts of wiry grass on the eastern floor that seem to have arrived by accident and stayed out of stubbornness. Most of the floor is bare. It is packed pale grit, a little rubble, and smooth faces of bedrock showing through like the backs of submerged animals.

## What Isn't Here

No insects move around the water. That is the first absence you notice, because warm, still, damp air should hum. It does not. No mosquito hovers over the basin, no ant runs a line across the floor, no spider has strung the corner between the root and the wall. Nothing nests in the overhang, and nothing has left droppings on the ledges. No bird calls from the rim.

It isn't eerie, exactly. The hollow simply seems to have been set aside, or to have never been found by anything that crawls or flies, or to have found them unnecessary. The ferns get by without pollinators. The fig drops its fruit onto the rim above and none of it falls in. The place is complete without company.

## Sound

Mostly there is quiet, a warm, padded quiet. The rock drinks sound the way it drinks rain.

The drip is the exception, and it is not loud. It is a small, clean *tock* as the drop meets the basin, followed by a faint ring that the overhang catches and returns once, softly, before it fades. Then comes the long gap of silence, and then the drop again. After a few minutes you stop hearing it as a noise and start hearing it as the room's pulse.

If there is wind up on the flats, you may hear it pass over the rim as a soft, hollow breath. It never comes down. The air at the bottom stays exactly where it is.

## What People Left

People were here, a few of them, lightly, a long time ago, and they left only the things that stone allows.

There are the eleven steps. There is the finished basin and its dry channel. A low wall of stacked, unmortared stones, knee-high, curves across part of the overhang's mouth. It isn't long enough to close anything off. It seems meant to mark the covered part as a different kind of place from the open part. Several stones have slumped, but the wall still reads as a line.

On the back wall, at chest height, someone carved a niche about the size of two cupped hands. Its floor is blackened, faintly, by smoke that was once there, and its ceiling holds a darker smudge where a small flame licked upward again and again. Nothing sits in it now. Its edges have softened like everything else. The dampness has been gently undoing its corners for longer than anyone can say.

Near the basin, pressed into the grit, are a few shards of fired clay: an unglazed reddish rim piece and a curved fragment of a vessel's shoulder. They have settled half into the floor and wear the same pale film of mineral as the stones around them. The hollow has begun, slowly, to make them its own.

There is nothing else: no metal, no glass, no tool marks sharper than a chisel's. The people who used this place came with water jars and fire and patience, and they left with all of it but the shards.

## The Rule of the Place

The hollow's one law is balance. What arrives, leaves at the same rate. The drop falls, the basin receives it, the stone takes it back. The rain soaks in. The ferns grow only as far as the wet. The warmth never builds, because the rock absorbs it, and it never drains, because the rock gives it back at night. Each day repeats the one before so closely that a person sitting here would lose count of them.

The erosion that made this place is real and continuing. The drop is still, very slowly, deepening the basin, and the condensation is still, very slowly, rounding the niche. But it runs at a pace so far below anything a life can feel that the hollow seems not to be changing at all. It has reached its shape, and now it only keeps it.

You can sit on the low wall in the grey-gold dim with the warm damp on your skin and listen to the drop fall: *tock*, the faint ring, the silence, *tock*. Nothing else will happen. That seems to be the point.
