# The Plating Tarn

## From the Rim

The crater is barely a century old. You can tell from the edges, which haven't softened yet. The basalt breaks off in plates and shards, black and glassy where it cooled fast, rust-brown where the first rains got into it. Nothing grows on the rim. There is no lichen, no crust of moss, not even the green film that usually settles on wet rock within a few years. The stone is as clean as the day it hardened.

The rim encloses a tarn perhaps a hundred and twenty paces across, high enough on the mountain that the air is thin and cold and the sky overhead is a hard, dark-edged blue. The water fills the crater almost to the lip, as if the mountain had cupped its hands and was trying not to spill.

The water is the first strange thing. It is so clear that for a moment you don't register it as water. You see straight down past the rim, down the funnel walls, into a crowded, glittering space full of movement. The sun reaches the bottom. Light falls through the whole column undimmed, as it would through a clean window, and every surface below is plainly lit.

The second strange thing is the noise. You hear it before you understand it. The rocks under your feet carry a deep, regular thud. Through your boots, through your shins, into your teeth. *Thoom.* A pause of about eleven heartbeats. *Thoom.* Between the thuds comes a continuous texture of sound: clicking, ringing, a high electric whine like a transformer in wet weather, and the rattle of countless small hard things striking other small hard things.

It sounds like a factory, but there is no factory. There is nothing made here at all.

## The Walls

Go under. The water is cold, not quite freezing. It bites the scalp and wrists but lets you stay awhile. It is also heavy. It carries more dissolved mineral than ordinary lake water, and you sink slightly faster than you expect. It tastes of iron and pennies and something sharper underneath, like the air after a lightning strike.

The crater walls funnel steeply downward, and they are not bare basalt. They are **plated**. From about two meters below the surface all the way down, the rock wears a skin of metal. There is copper, mostly, bright where it's fresh and blooming green-black where it's older. There are veins of something paler, zinc or nickel, and in places a dull grey film that may be lead. The metal isn't smooth. It has grown in dendrites and frost-patterns, in fern shapes that are not ferns, in branching crystalline trees a hand's-width tall. It looks like vegetation. It is not. It is what the current did.

The whole tarn is electrified.

You feel it as a faint crawling on your skin, a tingle strongest near the walls. Your fillings, if you have them, will hum. Put a hand close to a copper frond and a tiny blue thread of bubbles begins to stream from the metal toward your palm. The water is electrolyte and the walls are electrode, and something in the tarn is running a current through it all, continuously, day and night.

## The Makers

It is the animals.

They are everywhere, and the density is the next thing that overwhelms you. There is almost no open water. Every cubic arm's-length holds dozens of bodies, and the walls are furred with them.

**The coil-eels** are the source. They are long, flat, ribbon-bodied things, grey as slate, with a row of pale plates down each flank. Each one is a living battery, in the manner of the electric eels of warmer places, but they do not discharge in bursts to stun prey. They hold a steady, low potential, and they *organize*. They lie against the walls in ranks, head-to-tail, hundreds of them in lines that run from rim to floor like cables on a switchboard. Their tails anchor into cracks in the basalt. Their heads face upward. The whole crater is wired with them, eel after eel in series, and the voltage between the top of the tarn and the bottom is enough to plate metal out of the water onto every surface in between.

They whine. That's the high sound, the transformer noise. Each eel resonates faintly as it holds its charge, and ten thousand of them together make a chord that never stops.

**The platers** do the fine work. They are small, beetle-sized, with shells of their own deposited copper, shining like new coins. They crawl over the walls in their thousands, and they have learned, or evolved, or been shaped by whatever shapes this place, to *steer* the current. A plater settles on a patch of wall and alters its own charge, and metal blooms beneath it. When it moves, the metal follows. They are building the dendrite forests, and the forests are not random. Look long enough and you see repetition in them, the same branching figure recurring at intervals down the walls, like a motif in lace or a stamp pressed over and over.

They click. When two platers meet, they tap shells, *tik-tik*, and part. When thousands meet, it sounds like rain on a tin roof.

**The grinders** live on the floor. They are fist-sized and armored, and they do the crushing. The crater floor is a rubble of fresh basalt, still sharp, still breaking down from its eruption, and the grinders work it. They take stones into their gizzard-chambers and roll them against internal plates until the stone is sand and the sand is silt and the metals locked inside it are free to dissolve into the water. The eels then draw those metals out onto the walls. It is a supply line: ore to solution to finished metal.

The grinders are loud. A thousand of them crushing stone at once is a sound like gravel in a cement mixer, a constant grating rumble that comes up from below.

**The glassmouths** are harder to see. They are nearly transparent, long-jawed, with flickers of bioluminescence along their gums that hardly register in daylight. They are hunters. They eat platers and young eels and sometimes each other, but they seem to take only from the edges, the stragglers, the ones out of rank. They cull. The rows stay straight.

There are others. There are tiny drifting motes in the water that might be larvae of any of these. There are hairy grey mats in the deepest cracks, slimy and simple, the nearest thing here to a plant, though they are probably bacterial and certainly not green. And there are pale, jointed things like shrimp that live inside the metal forests and seem to do nothing but polish them.

## The Engine

At the bottom of the crater, in the exact center, is the vent.

It is a throat of glassy rock perhaps three meters across, and it is the heartbeat. Every eleven heartbeats or so, a pulse of hot water surges up from it. It is not violent, not a geyser, more like a slow lungful. It is warm enough to shimmer the light and rich enough in dissolved mineral that the water above it goes briefly milky. With each surge comes the *thoom*, a pressure-wave that shakes every body in the tarn, rattles the platers on their walls, and sets the coil-eels ringing like struck wires.

The creatures are synchronized to it. With each pulse the eels' whine drops in pitch and swells in volume as they draw down their charge. The platers all tap at once. The grinders pause, then resume. The rhythm is the vent's, and everything else keeps time to it.

This is the engine of the place. The vent supplies heat and mineral. The grinders supply ore. The eels supply current. The platers shape it. The glassmouths keep the lines in order. Nobody built it, and yet it has shifts, and supply chains, and quality control.

## At the Surface

Electrolysis splits water, and the gas has to go somewhere.

Along the walls, from every point where metal is being deposited, threads of fine bubbles rise. They stream up the copper forests in silver curtains, and by the time they reach the top the whole margin of the tarn is fizzing gently. The gas is hydrogen, mostly, with oxygen mixed in from the other electrode, and it collects in the lee of the crater rim on still days in a thin invisible layer.

Sometimes, not often, perhaps once or twice a season, a static spark jumps from a coil-eel's head to the surface, or a fleck of fresh-exposed metal oxidizes too fast, and the layer ignites.

It does not explode. It *pops*. It is a single sharp crack, loud as a rifle shot, echoing around the rim, and a sheet of pale blue flame lasts less than a second. The water beneath flinches. Then everything resumes: the whine, the clicks, the grinding, the *thoom*.

On cold mornings, when the air is densest and the gas pools thickest, the pops come more often. Mountain birds, if any ever came here, would learn to avoid it. None do.

## The Long Cycle

Everything here happens slowly, which is the last thing you begin to notice.

The platers move perhaps a finger's width in an hour. The dendrite forests grow a hair's thickness in a season. The wall-plating extends downward at the rate of a few centimeters a year, and it has reached the bottom in only the deepest, oldest corner. Where it has reached the bottom, it is beginning, just beginning, to plate over the grinders' rubble, to seal the floor.

The tarn is enclosing itself in metal. Given a few more centuries, the whole crater will be a copper bowl, lined seamlessly from rim to vent, a vessel grown from within. What happens then is unclear. The grinders will have no more fresh rock. The supply chain will break. Perhaps the animals will die, or move, or change. Perhaps there is another crater higher on the mountain, younger still, where the first coil-eels have just begun to lay themselves in lines. Perhaps this is how the mountain reproduces its lakes.

Or perhaps the metal bowl is the point. The copper forests, with their repeating motif, may be a pattern the animals are *printing*, a message or a map or a circuit, and when the bowl is closed the current will finally run the whole way round and something will switch on.

Nobody knows. Nobody has been here long enough to know. The rock is too young and the processes too patient.

## What It Is Like to Be There

You float in the clear, cold, bright water, surrounded on every side by bodies. They touch you, brush past you, click against your mask. Your skin tingles with the current. Your teeth hum. The *thoom* comes up from below and presses on your chest, and the eels flanking you on the wall draw breath with it and ring. Metal forests glitter green and gold in sunlight that comes all the way down. Silver bubble-curtains stream past you to the surface. Somewhere above, a spark cracks the air.

There is no plant in all of it. Nothing grows here. Things are *made* here, by bodies, out of rock and heat and current, in a crowded, deafening, electrified, luminous little crater at the top of a mountain that is still, geologically speaking, cooling.

It is loud as a machine shop and slow as a glacier and as crowded as a reef, and there is not one made object anywhere in it. The machine is the animals, and the animals are the machine, and the mountain goes on breathing heat into the bottom of them, once every eleven heartbeats, forever.
