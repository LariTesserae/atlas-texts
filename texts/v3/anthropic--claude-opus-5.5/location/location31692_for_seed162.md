# The Rolling Heights

**I.**

At first it looks like a mountain range, the way anything this big and jagged looks like a mountain range from far off: a wall of dark stone going up into a low bronze sky, ridge behind ridge, each one sharper than the last. Get closer, climb a while, and the resemblance breaks down.

The ridges are too regular. They run in parallel ranks, hundreds of kilometers long, spaced so evenly that from any high point they look like the teeth of a comb or the grooves of a file. Each ridge is a single slab of stone standing on edge, tilted a few degrees off vertical, all tilted the same way. The slabs are thin for their height. Some are only forty meters thick and three thousand tall, with edges fresh enough to cut a hand. Nothing up here has had time to weather. There is no talus, no rounded shoulder, no soil worth the name. The stone is dark gray-green, faintly laminated, with a sheen like the scale left on iron when it cools.

And the stone is warm. Lay a palm against any exposed face and it feels like the flank of a large animal, or a boiler wall an hour after the fire has been banked. The warmth comes from below. The whole plateau, which is roughly the size of a continent and sits at an altitude where the air ought to be thin and freezing, is being pushed up out of something hot.

**II.**

It is still being pushed up. That is the first real thing to understand about the place.

The slabs emerge. Along the base of each rank runs a seam, a long straight lip of darker rock that glows a deep, sullen red at night. Out of that seam, very slowly and without stopping, the next slab rises. It comes up pressed flat and hot, like a sheet from a rolling mill. The older slabs ahead of it are shoved forward and upward, tilting a little more with each year until they topple and shatter into the valley beyond. There the fragments are taken back down into another seam.

So the land runs like a conveyor. It is extruded, lifted, advanced, collapsed, and swallowed, and then it comes up again somewhere else. The whole continent is one enormous process, and the ridges are its output, stacked in rows like finished plate in a warehouse no one owns.

No one built any of it. There are no structures here, no roads, no tools lying in the dust, nothing a hand ever touched. But it is impossible to spend a day here without reaching for the language of industry, because the place behaves industrially. It runs on schedules. It has tolerances. It produces.

**III.**

The weather belongs to the plateau alone. The plateau is too large and too hot to share a sky with the lowlands, whatever lowlands there may be. Heat rising off a continent of warm stone builds a permanent lid of cloud a few hundred meters above the highest ridges. The cloud is thick and brown-gold and slow-churning, and it lets through a light like late afternoon seen through smoked glass. That dimness is constant. There is no true sunrise or noon, only a slow brightening and dimming of the lid, and the red glow of the seams coming up as the ambient light fades.

Water is scarce but not absent. It arrives mostly as condensation. When the lid sags low and its cooler underside touches the warm upper edges of the slabs, fog forms and beads on the stone. It runs down the faces in thin sheets, gathering in the lamination lines, dripping from overhangs. By the time it reaches the valley floors it has mostly evaporated again, rising back into the lid. What survives collects in pockets: hairline cracks, the cupped hollows where two slabs lean together, shallow warm pools in the rubble fields. Every few weeks the lid lets go and it rains. The rain is hot, heavy, and brief. For an hour every face on the plateau runs with water, the pools overflow, and the air fills with steam. Then it is over.

Almost everything here happens in strict silence, and the rain is no exception. It falls without any sound of falling.

**IV.**

The quiet is the second thing to understand.

This is a turbulent place. Slabs topple every hour somewhere within sight. Landslides pour into the valleys in slow gray rivers. The lid boils constantly, and lightning moves through it, and occasionally through the stone itself: a white thread running along a lamination line, gone in an instant. Vents in the valley floors cough steam in exact rhythm, like pistons. The whole plateau shudders and grinds and heaves.

And almost none of it makes a sound.

Sound does not travel properly here. It seems to be absorbed, or delayed, or redirected. You can watch a three-kilometer slab crack at its base and fall. You can see the dust bloom and feel the ground jump under your feet, and hear nothing. Then, hours later and often somewhere else entirely, the sound arrives. A traveler resting in some quiet side valley may be startled by the roar of a collapse that happened the day before, two hundred kilometers away. The noises of the plateau seem to be shipped around it like freight, released in places that have nothing to do with their origin.

What you can hear in the present moment is small and close. There is the tick of stone cooling, the drip of condensation, the soft whirr of insects, your own breath and pulse. The loudest constant sound is a low hum felt as much as heard. It comes from the stone, and it rises in pitch when lightning is about to run.

**V.**

The plateau is alive, densely so, in the places where anything can hold on.

The exposed slab faces are mostly bare, but every crack, ledge, and lamination line is crowded with pioneer growth. Mosses come first: thick, rust-colored, spongy mats that drink the condensation. Behind them come ferns, tall and tight-curled, with fronds that unroll so quickly you can watch it happen over the course of a morning. In the sheltered clefts where two slabs lean together there are small dim forests: cycad-like trunks, broad dark leaves angled to catch whatever light filters down, curtains of hanging root.

Everything grows fast because nothing gets to stay long. A slab that rose green-bare ten years ago may be densely wooded along its seams now, and it will fall within the next ten years. The plants seem to know it. They flower constantly and seed constantly, and their seed is carried on the thermals up into the lid and rained back down onto the newest stone. A rising slab may be sprouting moss before it is fully clear of its seam.

The most important plant is wirevine. It is black, glossy, and thin as fishing line, and it runs in immense quantities across the stone, crossing whole faces and bridging between slabs. Wirevine conducts current. When lightning runs in the stone, the vines carry it, and at night you can see the network flickering faintly blue-white across the ridges like wiring diagrams of something enormous. Other plants root along the wirevine and seem to feed on it, on the charge itself. Where the vine is densest, the growth is densest too. These are the lushest places on the plateau, close and humid and faintly buzzing, with a smell like ozone and wet iron.

**VI.**

The animals are abundant, and they behave like parts of the process.

In the valleys there are herds of tall, narrow grazers with long jointed legs and heads held low to crop the moss from the rubble. They move in exact formation, with the same spacing as the ridges. They migrate along the valleys at a steady pace that matches the advance of the slabs, so that each herd stays always the same distance from the seam behind it and the collapse front ahead. If you watch one long enough, you realize its entire life is spent riding the conveyor, never getting ahead and never falling behind.

In the air there are birds, or things like birds, that ride the thermals rising off the warm stone. They circle in rings stacked one above another like the rotating arms of a governor, spinning faster as the heat rises and slower as it falls. If you watched them long enough and learned to read them, you could probably use them to tell the temperature of the ground below.

In the wirevine thickets live beetles by the million, small and copper-backed, and they rotate. They spin in place on the vines, all in the same direction. The spinning seems to have something to do with the current, and the air above a dense colony is faintly charged. Your hair lifts.

Everything eats, everything breeds, everything is crowded together. Nothing here has room to spread out and wait. The whole ecology feels pressed and urgent, running on shifts.

**VII.**

The deeper you go into the plateau, the less it holds together.

Distances stop being reliable. A ridge that is plainly a day's walk away is reached in an hour, and the next one takes a week. The regular spacing of the ranks, so obvious from high points, cannot be confirmed on foot. It is regular when viewed and irregular when crossed.

Things repeat. You will see the same ridge twice: the same notch, the same fern-choked cleft, the same fallen slab lying at the same angle, fifty kilometers apart. The herds contain animals that are identical down to the scars on their flanks. And sometimes a slab rising from its seam carries fossils in its laminations: ferns, beetles, a grazer's jawbone. Those fossils are of the plants and animals living on the surface right now. Sometimes they are of the particular individuals. You can find the fossil of a beetle and then find the beetle itself, alive, spinning on the vine above it.

Time does not run in one direction everywhere. In some valleys the process runs backward. Fallen slabs reassemble and rise, sliding back toward their seams, and the growth on them shrinks and unrolls into bud. The herds in those valleys walk backward, placidly, at exactly the right pace.

Shadows keep their own schedule. In the dim light they are faint to begin with, but if you look closely they do not always point away from the brightest part of the lid. They seem to follow the seams instead, bending toward the glowing lips, as if the light they answer to comes from underneath.

And there are places, rare and usually deep in the interior, where the plateau seems to be producing things it has no reason to produce. A slab emerges with its surface impressed in regular patterns: grids, spirals, repeated shapes like the outlines of rooms or the faces of dials. They are stamped into the stone as if the rolling mill had been engraved. None of these are artifacts. Nobody made them, and they fall and shatter and are swallowed like everything else. But they look like the plateau is practicing, trying out forms, working toward something it has not arrived at yet.

**VIII.**

There is no center to the plateau that anyone has found, and no edge that stays put. The seams shift. New ranks open up. Old ones close. The continent flexes over years and centuries. It is growing in some directions and consuming itself in others, and the overall balance is impossible to know.

What it amounts to is a continent-sized machine that nobody built, running in silence under a dim bronze sky. It is warm and damp in its cracks, green wherever it can be, crowded with life keeping time to it. It is always making itself, always destroying itself, sharp-edged and new, every surface freshly made. It does not seem to know it is not a factory. Or perhaps, deep in the interior, among the stamped slabs and the backward valleys and the repeating ridges, it is learning to become one.
