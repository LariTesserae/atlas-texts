# The Foil Heights

The first thing you notice is that nothing has worn down.

The tableland rises in broad, stepped terraces, each lip as sharp as the moment it cooled. The rock is dark and new, a basalt so young it still carries the gloss of its skin. Where it has cracked, the fractures are clean and conchoidal, curved like the inside of a shell, edged finely enough to part a thread. No grit gathers in the seams. There is no dust anywhere, because nothing here has ever been ground against anything else. No rain has fallen, no frost has pried, no wind has carried sand. The land looks less like terrain than like a casting: freshly poured, freshly set, not yet handled.

From the edge of any terrace you can see a very long way. The heights run outward in tiers for what may be forty or sixty miles. They form shallow basins, low domes where the flow once swelled and stopped, and long ridged tongues where it crept and froze mid-motion. The air is thin and very clear, and distance does not blur. A dome twenty miles off shows its surface texture as plainly as the rock at your feet. What distance does is space things out. Everything here stands far apart.

## The Growth

What lives here is plant-like, and in its own way it is lush. But it is lush the way a single cathedral is lush: dense and ornate within itself, and set down in enormous emptiness.

There is no water, and the vegetation has never needed any. Its tissues run on a warm, slow, amber fluid, a salt-melt thick as honey. It carries charge rather than moisture. It never evaporates, never freezes, and never flows faster than a finger's width per day.

The most common growth is **foil-moss**. It spreads across the basalt in mats a few paces wide, each one isolated in an expanse of bare stone. Up close it is a tangle of fine metallic filaments, verdigris-green shading to bronze, each strand drawn impossibly thin. The moss pulls copper and iron directly from the rock through contact alone, dissolving the mineral grain by grain into its sap and extruding it again as wire. Lift the edge of a mat and the stone beneath is etched into a faint relief map of the filaments, as if the rock had been lightly acid-printed.

Rising from certain moss beds, perhaps one every half-mile, are the **kiln-trees**. Their trunks are ceramic: pale, glazed, slightly translucent, with a warm glow inside, like fired porcelain held against a lamp. They grow in slow spiraled courses, each layer fused to the one beneath. Their crowns are made of leaves that are nothing but foil, broad sheets thinner than paper. They are copper on the upper face and dark oxide beneath, and they are angled with a precision that feels engineered. The leaves do not rustle. They are too stiff and too still for that. They hold the sunlight and turn it into current, and the current runs down through the trunk into the moss and out into the rock.

Between the kiln-trees, the ground is threaded with **root-cable**. These are thick braided cords of metal and ceramic that surface and dive, surface and dive, sometimes for miles, linking one tree to the next. In a few places the cable has bundled so densely that it forms low ridges, like the ribbing on a hull. If you trace a cable long enough, you learn that every kiln-tree on the heights is joined to every other. The whole tableland is a single circuit.

Scattered on the high domes are **retort-pods**: squat, bulbous growths of dark glass, each sealed and smooth. Inside, visible through the walls, the amber sap is doing something. It separates into layers, settles into bands of different colors, and precipitates tiny bright crystals that collect in the bottom of the bulb like sediment in a flask. They are refineries. Nobody built them, and nobody takes their product. The crystals simply accumulate, pod by pod, in perfect patience.

## The Industry Without Industrialists

This is the strangeness at the heart of the place. Everything here is working, and none of it was made.

The heights perform the labor of a mine, a smelter, a foundry, and a power station, all at once and at regional scale. Ore is extracted, metal is drawn, glass is fired, current is generated and distributed, and compounds are separated and purified. Yet there is no structure anywhere, no tool, no discarded part, no track or scar. The processes are carried out entirely by growth, and they leave no waste. The land is not being mined. It is being *digested*, so slowly and so cleanly that the evidence is only a subtle etching under the moss.

There is no purpose visible either. The current flows through the root-cable and returns to the trees. The metal is drawn and becomes more moss. The crystals collect and are never used. It is industry in closed loop, an economy with no consumers, producing for the sake of balance alone.

---

## Stillness

Nothing changes here. That is not a figure of speech.

The sun stands high and a little to the west, and it does not appear to move. If it moves at all, it does so on a scale longer than any visit. The light is steady and ordinary, a clear, unremarkable daylight, and the shadows of the kiln-trees lie across the basalt at an angle that has not shifted in any memory the land might hold. Temperature holds at a mild, even warmth. It comes partly from the sun and partly from the rock itself, which still keeps some faint heat from its making and gives it up so gradually that the loss cannot be measured. There is no wind, because nothing is warmer or colder than anything else by enough to push the air around.

The plants do not grow in any way you could see. The foil-moss mat you stand beside may have been this size for a thousand years, or ten thousand. The retort-pods fill at the rate of a crystal every season, if seasons existed. The whole system sits at an equilibrium so exact that it looks like a held breath. It is not frozen, because everything is quietly functioning. It is simply balanced, every input matched to every output.

This is also why the land is still raw. Erosion needs imbalance: something hotter, wetter, faster, or harder moving against something that yields. Here nothing moves against anything. The basalt will stay razor-edged for as long as this equilibrium holds.

---

## Sound

There is almost none.

No animal has ever lived on the heights. There are no insects, no birdsong, no scuttle in the moss. No wind moves the leaves. No water drips. The rock does not tick with cooling. Most of the time, the silence is so complete that your own pulse becomes the loudest thing in the landscape.

But if you kneel and press your ear to a length of surfaced root-cable, you can hear the current. It is a low, even hum at the very bottom of hearing. It does not waver, pulse, or rise. It is the same note on every cable, across the entire tableland, joined into a single tone that has perhaps been sounding since the first kiln-tree fired its first course of ceramic.

Very rarely, perhaps once in an afternoon that never ends, a retort-pod will drop a crystal onto the pile inside it. The sound is a single faint *tink*, glass on glass, small and bright. Across a mile of empty basalt, in air this clear and still, you can hear it perfectly.

---

## The Edge

At the outermost terraces, the heights end in cliffs. This is where the flow reached its limit and stopped. The faces drop sheer and unweathered into a haze far below, too distant to resolve. Nothing grows on the cliff faces. The last foil-moss mats stop a few paces back from the drop, as if they knew where the circuit had to close.

Stand there with your back to the edge and look inward. You will see terrace after terrace of dark new stone, dotted at great intervals with bronze mats. Porcelain trees glow faintly in the steady sun, their foil crowns motionless. Dark glass bulbs sit on the domes, full of sediment. The whole of it is humming too low to hear, working without pause, going nowhere, and changing nothing.

It feels less like a landscape than like a machine that has not yet been switched off, or one that was never switched on. It just is, running quietly at the top of an unworn world.
