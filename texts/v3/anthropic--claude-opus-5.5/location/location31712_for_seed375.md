# The Gypsum Quarter

## The basin

It lies in a shallow dip in a dry plain. The dip is barely deep enough to count as a basin: stand at the rim and the floor is only a few storeys below, a pale bowl perhaps a quarter-hour's walk across. The plain around it is anhydrite, grey-white and gritted, split into plates that curl at the edges like old paper. Nothing grows out there. Nothing moves except the haze.

In the basin there is a town.

At first it looks like a ruin, because nothing so pale and so quiet seems like it could be anything else. But nothing here is old. The edges are wrong for a ruin. Every wall ends in a crest as thin as a fingernail, and the crest is sharp enough to open skin. The corners have not been softened by a single season of wind. The stairs have never been climbed. Whatever this is, it was finished recently. Some of it was finished this morning.

## Streets and rooms

The streets are narrow, about as wide as two people walking side by side, and they almost make a grid. They run in long, near-parallel lanes that bend a few degrees off true, cross each other at slightly wrong angles, and open without warning into small plazas. The plazas are roughly round, with a low basin-shaped depression at the centre. The depressions are always dry. Their bottoms hold a crust of fine white needles.

The buildings are one, two, occasionally three storeys, stacked in cells. They are made of two materials:

- **Gypsum foam** forms the bulk of the walls. It is chalk-white, porous, and light enough that a slab the size of a door could be lifted with one hand. Pressed with a thumb, it gives slightly and then holds the print.
- **Selenite** is set into the foam as panes, in long clear blades and sheets that serve as windows, skylights, and sometimes whole roofs. It is soft enough to scratch with a fingernail and clear as still water.

Inside, there are rooms, hundreds of them. Every one is empty.

They are not empty the way a looted house is. They are empty the way a freshly blown bottle is, as if nothing has ever been inside. The floors are smooth. There are alcoves in the walls at roughly shoulder height. There are ledges at roughly sitting height. There are doorways, always facing gently downslope toward the centre of the basin. There are stairs, with risers a little too tall and treads a little too narrow, climbing to upper rooms that are also empty.

The spaces between rooms are huge compared to what occupies them. A visitor could walk through thirty rooms and see nothing but light on a wall. That emptiness is the defining quality of the place: a town built entirely of vacancy.

## Who builds it

The builder is a plant, if it is a plant.

Along the base of every wall, where wall meets floor, runs a pale grey thread about as thick as a finger. It is dry to the touch and faintly warm. The locals, if there were locals, might call it mason-root. It is sparse: a single strand per wall, sometimes none. It is also continuous. Follow one far enough and it turns a corner, crosses a threshold, climbs a stair, and joins another. The whole town may be one plant.

Its roots go straight down through the basin floor, far down, to wherever the last trace of brine sits in the deep rock. This is the only liquid in the place, and there is almost none of it. The mason-root draws it up in minute quantities. It uses the water and exhales the minerals, laying down gypsum foam grain by grain as it creeps. Where it passes, a wall rises behind it like the wake of a boat.

The root has leaves, a few. They are small, flat, and colourless, arranged in pairs, and they turn toward warmth rather than light. They also flower. The flowers are tiny glassy cups that open at dusk. Each holds a single bead of concentrated brine, the only wet thing for miles.

## The day

The basin has a rhythm, and the rhythm never stops. The town is always in the middle of being made.

**Morning.** The sky is a dim bronze haze, lit from a sun too low and diffuse to cast a firm shadow. Overnight, a frost of salt needles has formed on every exposed surface. It is not ice, because there is no water for ice, but mineral crystals forced out of the air by the cold. The streets glitter faintly, and nothing makes a sound.

**Midday.** The foam warms. The mason-root works fastest now. You can watch a wall crest thicken by a hair's width if you are patient. You cannot see the growing, only notice that the edge has moved against a scratch you made an hour ago. New rooms begin as curved lips of foam on the ground, which rise, lean toward each other, and close. A plaza might be a room by evening.

**Late afternoon.** Dust arrives. It comes off the plain in low, slow sheets, pouring over the basin rim and filling the streets like a tide made of flour. It moves at walking speed and flows around corners, into doorways, and up stairs. For an hour the lower storeys are submerged in pale drift. Then it drains away through gaps in the town that were not there the day before, as if the streets had been laid out as channels for it. Perhaps they were.

**Dusk.** The cold wind comes down from the rim. The flowers open. The walls begin to contract as they cool, and they crack. Hairline fractures run through the foam in branching patterns, and sometimes a whole upper room shears away and settles into the street below. None of it can be heard.

## The silence

The gypsum foam eats sound.

Its pores are so fine and so numerous that noise sinks into them and does not come back. A voice carries about an arm's length before it thins into nothing. Footsteps are inaudible. A falling slab of wall lands in front of you with the heft and violence of a real collapse and makes no sound at all, only a puff of dust and a small shiver in the floor.

In the open, away from the walls, you can hear the faint hiss of dust moving. Inside the town, you hear your own heartbeat, your own blood, the click of your own jaw. After an hour the silence stops being restful and becomes a pressure, as though the walls are listening very carefully and taking everything.

## The light at night

The selenite remembers the day.

The panes absorb the dim bronze daylight and release it slowly through the night as a cold, pale glow, the colour of watered milk. The town is never fully dark. Every window, skylight, and translucent roof gives off a soft light, so that at night the basin looks like a lantern with its flame turned almost to nothing.

The light is strongest in the upper rooms, where the most selenite is. From the rim at midnight you see the town as a scatter of faint luminous cells, some bright and new, some already fading. The ones that are dimming have been cracked and abandoned. The mason-root has moved on, and without it the selenite slowly clouds over.

## Fauna

Animals are rare, and a visitor might see only a few in a day.

- **The cup-moths** are small and glass-winged, nearly invisible except when they catch the light. They come out at dusk to drink from the mason-root flowers, a bead of brine each. They spend their days in the alcoves, folded flat against the wall, indistinguishable from the foam.
- **The salt-spiders** hunt them. They are the size of a fingernail, pale as the walls, and they live in the fine white needle-crust in the plaza basins. They build no webs. They wait. When a moth descends to a low flower, the spider moves once, very fast, and then it is still again.
- **The tall one** has been seen, perhaps, a few times. It is thin and long-legged, taller than a doorway, with a small head and a slow, deliberate way of walking the streets. It stops at the crests of new walls and seems to taste them. It may feed on the youngest foam. It may prune the town. It may be the reason the streets run almost straight. Nobody knows. It moves as silently as everything else, and it does not seem to notice people. The first sign of it is a long shadow thrown across a lit window at night, then gone.

## The rules

A few principles hold everywhere:

- **Doorways always face downhill**, toward the centre.
- **No room is older than a season.** The town is constantly consuming its old walls. They crack, collapse, and turn to the dust that fills the streets, and that dust is eventually drawn back up into new foam. There are no ruins here, only construction and demolition happening at the same time, forever.
- **The town does not grow larger.** It stays within the basin, never climbing the rim, never spilling onto the plain. It turns over within its boundary like a tide pool.
- **The spaces between things matter more than the things.** The rooms seem to be shaped around the dust flows, the cold wind, the paths of the tall one, the pooling of night air. The empty volume is what is being built. The walls are only its outline.

## What it seems to be for

The strangest thing about the town is its proportions. The alcoves are shoulder height. The ledges are sitting height. The stairs are almost, but not quite, comfortable to climb. The doorways are the right width for a person, and the streets are the right width for two people walking together.

It is shaped like a place meant to be lived in. It feels as though it has been practising for a long time, taking the measure of something it has never met, getting the rooms very slightly wrong and then tearing them down and trying again, every night, in total silence, in the cold, by the pale light of its own windows.

Nobody has ever lived here. The town does not seem to mind. It keeps building.
