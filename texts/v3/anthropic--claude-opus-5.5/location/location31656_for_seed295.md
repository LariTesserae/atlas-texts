# The Galleries of the Screaming Mountain

It helps to understand first that there is no ground here.

There is a mountain underneath somewhere, probably. But for as far up and down its flank as anyone could climb, every surface underfoot and overhead was placed by hands. It is dressed stone, fitted without mortar, block against block so tightly that roots have had to wait centuries for a seam to open. The builders are long gone. They left no tools, no hearths, no metal, no inscriptions anyone has found. What remains is the stone and the shape they gave it: a city that did not sit on the mountain but replaced its skin. It climbs in stacked galleries, and every roof is the floor of the level above. Streets are stairs. Plazas are landings. Walls are the backs of other rooms.

You cannot see most of this, because it is dark.

## The Light, or What Stands In for It

The galleries face east and overlap like the scales of a pinecone, each level cantilevered a little past the one below. Light that arrives from the sky has to pass through three things before it reaches anything. First comes the cloud that sits on these heights most of the year. Then comes the forest that has grown on every open terrace. Last are the deep eaves the builders cut, as though they had wanted shade more than anything.

So the outer terraces live in green twilight, a murk the color of pond water, bright enough to see a hand but not its lines. One room back from the edge it is dusk. Two rooms back it is night. Beyond that the city runs inward into the mountain: vaults, corridors, and chambers stacked on chambers, some high as a grain silo. In those rooms there has never been light. There has only ever been the idea of it, carried in on the bodies of animals who come in from the evening and leave again before dawn.

The eye adjusts, and then it stops adjusting, because there is nothing more to take in. After that you learn the place with your other senses.

## The Noise

You hear it long before you arrive. From the valley it is a kind of weather, a grinding hiss that rides the updraft and seems to come out of the rock itself. By the lowest terraces it has turned into something you feel in your sternum. Inside the first vault, speech stops being possible. You can shout directly into someone's ear and they will see your mouth move.

The noise is mostly birds, oilbirds, hundreds of thousands of them. They are big, reddish-brown, owl-faced nightjar cousins that live their whole lives in the dark rooms of the city and navigate by clicking. Their clicks alone would be a din, a constant dry rattle like gravel poured onto slate. But oilbirds also scream. They do it when disturbed, and when a neighbor lands too close, and apparently for no reason at all. It is a torn, rasping shriek, somewhere between a hinge and a strangled cat. In a stone vault with a hundred thousand bodies in it, every scream sets off others. The result never quite resolves into a roar. It stays a texture, the sound of a chamber being scoured from the inside.

Under the oilbirds are the bats: fruit bats, nectar bats, and insect-eating bats in colonies of many millions. Their calls sit mostly above human hearing, so what reaches you is the wingbeat. It is a papery, continuous rushing, the sound of the city breathing. Under the bats are the swiftlets, clicking in their own frequency, a finer and quicker rattle. On the outer terraces the frogs join in from the flooded cisterns, along with cicadas as loud as saws, and the cloud forest's own birds shouting over all of it to be heard.

Somewhere inside all of this, water drips. You will never hear it, but you will feel it on the back of your neck.

## Water

This is not a wet place, exactly. It is a damp one. There is a difference, and the whole ecology lives inside that difference.

Real rain comes only in a season of a few months, and even then it arrives as long gray afternoons of drizzle more than as storms. The rest of the year the moisture comes from the cloud. Fog pushes up the valley every night, and the forest on the terraces combs it out of the air. Beads gather on moss and fern, fall onto stone, and run along channels the builders cut into every step and sill.

Those channels are the one real gift the old city still gives. Every terrace tilts a few degrees inward. Every stair has a gutter along its riser. The water runs off the outer forests and into the mountain, down through slots in the floors and through drains under the vaults, to cisterns cut deep in the lowest levels. Some of these still hold water. Some have silted up into black mud swamps. Some are dry, and the channels that feed them now just carry the drip down onto whatever lies below. That is often a colony, and under the colony a guano heap, and inside the heap a slow chemistry.

Deep inside, the air stays close to saturated and very warm, because the bodies of millions of animals are heating it. Condensation stands on the vault ceilings like sweat. The walls are slick. Nothing ever dries. But you could walk for hours without meeting standing water, only surfaces that are always a little wet.

## Warmth

The height should make the place cold, and at night on the highest open terraces it nearly is. But the inner city is a furnace of animal life. A single roosting cluster of fruit bats radiates heat you can feel from an arm's length. A chamber full of them feels like a greenhouse with the vents shut. The oilbirds nest in ledges packed so tight that the rock around them is warm to the touch. Their chicks are notoriously fat, and so heavy with oil that people once rendered them for lamp fuel, though no one does that here.

The warmth rises through the city the way heat rises through a stack of stones, and it spills out onto the terraces in a steady damp exhalation. It is part of why the terrace forests grow so heavily at an altitude where the surrounding peaks are bare and yellow grass.

## The Terraces

The outermost galleries are forest now: epiphytes and tree ferns, figs, and dense understories of arum and begonia. The builders must have meant these as fields. They are flat, walled, and irrigated, with a lip on the outer edge to hold soil. The soil stayed, and over many centuries it was thickened and fed until it became something richer than the builders could have made. Most of that feeding came from above, from the animals.

Every tree is layered with other plants. Mosses sheathe the trunks. Bromeliads sit in the crotches holding their own pools of water, each with its own tadpoles, mosquito larvae, and tiny crabs. Orchids, peperomia, and filmy ferns grow in the thin mats on the branches. Strangler figs have wrapped themselves around the old stone pillars that once held up the eaves. In many places it is now impossible to say whether the fig is holding up the stone or the stone is holding up the fig.

Roots have gone into every seam. They are the slowest force here and the only one that truly undoes the builders' work. A root finds a crack in a century. It widens the crack over the next few. Eventually, maybe once in a lifetime, a block tips out of a wall and falls three terraces with a crack nobody hears over the birds. The city loses a stone. The forest gains a gap, and fills it within a decade.

## The Floors of the Dark Rooms

Inside, the stone is buried.

Every chamber with a colony has a floor of guano. In the oldest halls, near the ceiling roosts, it lies deeper than a person is tall, a compacted, fibrous, warm, living mass. The top few centimeters are fresh and wet and seething. Below that the guano grows drier and darker, and deeper still it grows mineral, crusting into something almost like soil and almost like rock. People have dug into it in places. The layers go down and down: seeds, wings, beetle shells, the fine bones of animals that died and were not eaten. It is a record of the whole city's time without its builders. Nobody knows how deep it goes in the deepest halls.

Oilbirds eat fruit. They fly out at dusk, sometimes a long way into the valleys, gorge on palm fruits and laurel berries and wild avocados, and return to regurgitate the seeds in the dark. So the guano floors are sown, constantly and thickly, with forest seeds. And the seeds sprout.

This is the strangest sight in the city, and it is only strange because it happens in the dark. Walk into an old oilbird hall with a light and you will find the floor covered in a pale, ghostly lawn of seedlings. They are palm and laurel sprouts, thin and white as bean sprouts, up to knee height, leaning toward nothing. They have enough stored energy to grow a little way in search of light that isn't there, and then they wilt and fall and become more floor. Every year a whole forest germinates in these rooms and dies unseen. It is the slow engine of the floors. The seeds bring the forest's nutrients in, and the city keeps them.

Around the seedlings and inside the guano are the scavengers: cockroaches in numbers that make the floor glitter and shift when light touches it, tenebrionid beetles, mites, millipedes, whip spiders, and pale crickets with antennae three times their body length. Cave-adapted crabs live in the deepest cisterns. Centipedes hunt bats. Snakes, long slim rat snakes, hang from the vault edges at dusk and pick bats out of the air as they stream past.

## The Hour of Crossing

The city has one rhythm strong enough to override the noise, and that is dusk.

Toward evening, a change passes up through the galleries. The screaming grows more urgent. The rushing of wings thickens. Then, from every opening on the mountain's face, the animals begin to leave. First come the swiftlets, fast and low. Then the bats come out in rivers, pouring from the eaves in tight twisting columns that uncoil into the valley air like smoke. From the valley floor, the whole mountainside seems to be exhaling something dark.

Last come the oilbirds, clicking and screaming as they go out to feed. For an hour after sundown the air above the terraces churns with wings. The outer forests take on a new population of insects drawn to the warmth, and new hunters behind them. Owls and bat hawks, which have waited all day, take their share.

Then comes the quietest time the city ever has, which is not very quiet. The inner rooms are left with the chicks, the scavengers, the drip, and the hum of the insects. Many hours later, before dawn, everything comes back, carrying the valleys home in its gut.

The season turns more slowly. In the rains, fruit is abundant and the oilbirds breed. The halls fill with chicks, the screaming doubles, and the guano rises faster. In the dry months the colonies thin a little, and the terraces go slightly yellow at the edges. The cisterns drop, and frogs gather in the last of the water. It all comes back. The rhythm is centuries deep, and it has barely shifted since whoever built this left.

## What the Place Is

It is a city that never stopped being inhabited, only changed tenants. The builders made a vast warm cave with good drainage, a thousand ledges and eaves, fertile flat ground at every level, and water channeled into the dark. Then they left, and everything that could use such a structure moved in. The birds added their nests of spit and mud and down, and the bats added their heat. The forest took the terraces, the roots took the seams, and the floors took everything that fell.

Nothing here is untouched, and nothing here is maintained. The city is being slowly rebuilt by its occupants, and slowly taken apart by its gardens. For the moment the two are in rough balance. It is a stone mountain full of warm dark and noise, and every surface was made by someone.
