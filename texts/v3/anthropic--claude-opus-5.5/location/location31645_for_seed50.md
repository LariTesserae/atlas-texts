# The Throat

From a distance, from the ridges on the far side of the valley, it looks like smoke. A pale, trembling pillar stands on the shoulder of the mountain, just below where the snow gives up and the rock goes bare. People who have climbed up to look at it say it isn't smoke. They say it is water, rising.

---

## The Column

High on the mountain there is a fissure in young, dark stone, a crack so fresh its edges are still sharp enough to cut rope. Water comes out of it, and it doesn't trickle or pool. It is driven up from somewhere deep and hot, then cooled on the way until it arrives cold as a knife. It rises in a hollow cylinder perhaps four body-lengths across and three times that high. At the top it spreads out, loses its strength, and falls back down the outside of itself in a heavy curtain.

The result is a tube: a wall of water rushing upward on the inside and downward on the outside. Between the two moving sheets there is a thin seam of shuddering air, and inside the tube there is a room.

Nobody built it. Nobody could have. It is simply the place the water doesn't go.

## Getting In

You don't walk into the Throat. You go through.

On the downhill side, where the fissure is narrowest, the rising wall thins for a moment every few breaths, as the pressure below stutters and recovers. The people who come here, and few do, learned to count those stutters. They wait with their backs to the mountain, soaked through in seconds, and when the wall goes glassy and thin they throw themselves forward. It hits like a falling tree. Then they are inside, gasping, on their knees on wet stone, in a sudden bright hush that is not quiet at all.

## The Sound

The sound isn't loud the way thunder is loud. Thunder comes and goes. This never stops, and it comes from every direction at once, because every direction is water. Up close it stops sounding like a roar and becomes weight. You feel it on your teeth and your breastbone. Your ribs hum with it. Within a minute you can't hear your own voice at all, and within an hour you've forgotten you have one.

The sound has layers, if you stay long enough to find them:
- **The low floor.** A tone below hearing, felt more than heard, which is the mountain itself resonating like a struck bell.
- **The shear.** A hissing tearing where the up-rushing inner sheet grinds against the air.
- **The collapse.** At the top, where the water turns over and begins to fall, comes a constant booming crash, like surf that has forgotten how to pause.
- **The stutter.** Every few breaths the whole thing hiccups, and there is a split second where the tones slip against each other and something almost like a word passes through the chamber.

People who spend too long here say the words are real. They say the Throat is speaking and has been since the day the rock split. They say the stutters are its breathing.

## The Light

It is bright inside, surprisingly so, brighter than the grey cloud-scraped slopes outside. The top of the column is open to the sky, and daylight pours down the shaft. Every surface of the moving water catches it, bends it, and throws it back.

The walls are never still, so the light isn't either. Rippled bands of brightness crawl endlessly up the inner walls, following the water, so the whole room seems to be ascending. Rainbows form and don't hold. They slide upward, break at the lip, and reform at the base. At noon, with the sun directly overhead, a disc of white light burns on the floor, and the spray hangs in it like a slow crowd of stars.

At night there is nothing. The water swallows the stars. The roar goes on in total black.

## The Stone That Is Being Born

This is the part that makes the Throat stranger than a fountain.

The water is loaded with dissolved stone. It was pressed out of rock too deep and hot to name, and when it meets the thin cold mountain air it gives the stone back. Every wet surface grows a pale, glassy crust, faintly green where the light goes through it. It builds on the floor in ridges and terraces. It builds up the base of the walls in delicate frilled shelves, like fungus on a tree or like frozen splashes. Wherever the spray settles, stone settles with it.

It grows fast, visibly fast. Leave a handprint pressed into the soft white skin of the floor and come back after a season, and your hand will be sealed in stone as thick as a fingernail. Lay a stick down and it is a stone stick by spring.

That means the room is closing. The shelves at the base reach inward year by year. The floor rises. The fissure below narrows under its own deposits, so the jet grows stronger and louder and thinner, and the tube tightens. Old visitors say that in their grandparents' time the room was twice as wide. In some future time it will close entirely, into a solid spire of green-white glass-stone with a single roaring needle of water at its heart. Then the pressure will find some other crack, and the mountain will open again somewhere nearby, and a new Throat will begin.

So everything here is new, and fragile, and half-finished. The ground is sometimes still soft underfoot, like wet sugar. There are no weathered edges, no dust, no soil. Nothing has had time to wear. It is a place still in the act of being made.

## What Lives Here

Very little, and all of it strange.

**The green.** On the shelves where the spray is finest and the light is strongest there grows a slick, bright-green film, a living skin that feeds on light and minerals. Nearer the floor grow tiny stiff cushions of moss, each one a perfect dome. The moss grows just fast enough to stay ahead of the stone, reaching up out of its own burial like a swimmer treading water. Where it loses the race you can see it inside the stone, green shadows sealed in glass, layer under layer, the ghosts of a hundred earlier summers.

**The fall-swifts.** These are dark little birds, sooty and narrow-winged, that nest on the underside of the shelves. They go in and out through the water. They don't wait for the stutter. They just fold their wings and pierce the curtain like thrown darts. Nobody knows how they survive it. Their nests are cups of mud and feather that stone over within weeks, so each bird builds on top of last year's petrified nest. The oldest nesting sites are tall lumpy towers of stone cups, one on top of another, like tiny chimneys. There are never more than a handful of birds. Each spring some don't come back.

**The pale thing.** In the fissure, down where the water comes up out of the dark, something lives. People who have lain on their bellies and looked down into the throat of the spring have seen it: a long, colorless, ribbon-like body, holding itself against the current. It has no eyes anyone has ever seen. It seems to feed on whatever the water brings up from below. Some say there's only ever been one. Some say it's the same one that was there when the rock split, and that it came up with the water from wherever the water comes from.

## The Marks of People

The people who come to the Throat come alone, or in twos, and they don't stay long. They leave very little, and what they leave, the stone takes.

- **Stacked stones.** Small cairns of dark mountain rock are carried in from outside and piled on the floor. Each gets glazed over by the pale mineral skin until it looks like a figure wrapped in a shroud. The oldest ones are melted into the floor, just humps, with only their silhouettes to show they were made.
- **The ladder.** Against the northern wall leans a ladder of lashed poles, very old, which someone must once have dragged through the curtain in pieces. It's been stone for a long time. It reaches up to the first big shelf, where there's a shallow hollow big enough for one person to curl in. It's too heavy to move now, and too fragile to climb. It's become part of the wall.
- **The knot-cords.** Speech is impossible here, so visitors talk with their hands, and with cords. Tied to the ladder and knotted around the cairns are lengths of braided plant fiber and hair, with knots along them in patterns. Some are messages: names, counts of days, prayers. Most have been stoned over, rigid as bones. A few are recent and still soft. Nobody who ties a cord expects anyone to read it. They tie them the way you'd say something aloud in an empty room.
- **Hands.** All over the floor, in layers, are handprints. People pressed them into the soft new stone, and then more stone grew over them, and then more hands were pressed on top. If the light is right you can see them going down, fainter and fainter, like hands pressed against the far side of a frozen lake.
- **The bowl.** In the middle of the floor there is a basin pecked out with a stone hammer. It's filled with clean still water, the only still water in the whole place, which collects spray and holds it. It is always full. Anyone who comes drinks from it. It tastes of cold metal and of nothing at all.

## The Rules of the Place

Over time certain truths have settled about the Throat, the way stone settles about a stick:

1. **Nothing you bring stays yours.** Anything left behind becomes part of the room within a season.
2. **Nothing speaks but the water.** Voices don't work here, and there's a feeling, nothing formal, that it would be rude to try.
3. **You leave on the stutter.** Some people stay too long and lose the count, and then they're trapped until someone else comes, or until they choose to try the wall without the gap. Some of those don't come out on the other side. Nobody knows where they go.
4. **The room is always smaller than you remember.** Everyone who returns says so.
5. **Something is being said.** Stay long enough, a full day and a night, and the stutters in the roar begin to line up, to make a rhythm, to repeat. Nobody has ever written it down, because it can't be written down. People who have heard it come out quieter than they went in, and they tend to come back.

## The Feeling

It is cold inside the Throat. It's not a killing cold, but a clean mountain cold that works slowly into your hands. The air is so full of fine spray it's barely air. You breathe through a cloth or you choke. You are soaked within moments of arriving and stay soaked the whole time you're there. The light is dazzling and never still. The noise is so total that it becomes a kind of silence, a solid thing you sit inside, like being held.

And it is empty. It holds a few birds, a little moss, some shrouded cairns, a stone ladder, and a basin of still water. Mostly it holds space: a tall bright hollow of rushing, roaring nothing, standing on a mountainside in the middle of an up-pouring river that shouldn't exist, making new stone out of itself, speaking one long word it hasn't finished saying.

Someday it will close. The pale ribbon in the fissure will be sealed in glass. The ladder and the hands and the knot-cords will be locked inside a green-white spire, and the water will break out somewhere else on the mountain and start again.

But for now there is still a room, and you can still get into it if you learn the count.
