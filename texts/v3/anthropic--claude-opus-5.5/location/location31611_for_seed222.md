# The Hushlands of the Upper Pans

## The Ground

The plateau sits about four thousand meters up, near enough to the equator that the height steals none of the warmth. Here the sky is not blue so much as a deep, saturated cobalt that darkens toward violet at the zenith. Sunlight arrives almost undiluted by air. Noon comes down like a weight, and every surface throws light back.

The rock is welded tuff, pale ochre going to bone-white, laid down in some eruption so old that the volcano no longer exists. Wind, frost and the slow chemistry of dew have worked the stone so long that it has stopped looking eroded and started looking finished. Nothing is sharp. The land swells into low rounded domes, some the size of a house and some the size of a hill, their flanks smooth as the backs of sleeping animals. Between the domes lie the pans: shallow bowls worn into flat rock, from a hand's width to a hundred meters across, their floors lined with fine white silt. Some are dry, and some hold water in the mornings.

Where the domes stand tallest, the stone is riddled with tafoni, honeycomb hollows eaten into the rock by salt crystallizing in its pores. The larger hollows are deep enough to crawl into. The smaller ones pock the stone like the inside of a bone. Almost every one of them is occupied.

From any rise you can see that the plateau forms a basin maybe twenty kilometers across, ringed by larger domes. To the west the ring breaks at the escarpment, where the land falls away in one long step toward a floor of cloud. On most days that cloud-sea sits well below the rim, white and still, and the plateau floats above it like an island in the sky.

## The Combs

Scattered across the basin, a kilometer or two apart, stand the combs.

They are hexagonal lattices of a smoke-grey material somewhere between glass and ceramic, with a faint oily depth when light strikes it at an angle. The smallest are the height of a person. The largest rise as high as a five-story building, open honeycombs whose cells are each about the size of a cupped pair of hands. Each stands alone. None casts quite the shadow it should: light passing through the lattice bends slightly, so that each comb throws a pattern of wavering caustics on the stone around it, bright lines that slide and knot as the sun moves. At noon these patterns are so intense that the rock under a large comb is marked with faint burn-lines, lace-work scorched into the tuff over centuries.

Touch a comb at midday and it is cold, cold enough to ache. Beads of water stand on it even under that sun.

This is what the combs do. They take in vibration, the pressure-waves of sound, the tremor of wind against stone, the hum of insect wings, and turn it into cold. No one living knows what principle they use. The transduction is too efficient and too selective for any engine made now. The effect is simple enough to observe. Near a comb, the world goes quiet. Air moving over the plateau gives up its noise at the lattice, and the cold that the combs bleed from that noise pulls water out of the thin air. They are silence-engines and dew-engines at once, and whether they were built for the silence or for the water, or for something else entirely, is lost.

Walking toward one, you notice the change before you understand it. First the wind softens. Then the crunch of grit under your boots thins to a whisper. Within twenty paces of a large comb, you can clap your hands and hear only a faint dry pat, as if from another room. Speak and your voice seems to leave your mouth and stop. It is not unpleasant. It is intimate, like being inside a held breath.

## The Water

The plateau is dry country. Rain is rare, and the air is thin and would be parched if left alone. The combs do not leave it alone.

In the last hours before dawn, when the stone has shed its heat into the clear sky and the air is at its coldest, the combs sweat heavily. Water gathers in their cells, fills them, and spills. It runs down the lattice in threads, drips from the lower edges, and finds its way across the rock along channels that have been worn by thousands of years of this same dripping. Every comb stands at the head of a small radiating network of grooves, each ending in a pan.

So the mornings are wet. Just after sunrise the pans nearest the combs hold clear, cold water a few fingers deep, perfectly still, reflecting the cobalt sky so well that the ground seems pierced with holes into the sky beneath. Mist lifts off them as the first light hits. Farther from the combs the pans are only damp, the silt dark and soft. Farther still, they stay dry and white all day.

By noon most of this water has gone, drunk or evaporated. The pans shrink to wet crescents, then to cracked white floors. Only the deepest, in the shade of the largest combs, hold water through the heat. The land runs on a daily tide of moisture, and everything alive keeps time to it.

Sometimes, in the warm season, convective cloud builds over the escarpment in the afternoon, great white towers lifting out of the cloud-sea, and a storm crosses the basin. When it passes near a comb, the rain falls in silence. You can watch the drops strike the rock and splash and hear nothing. Lightning walks between the domes without thunder. The only sound is the storm's roar out in the open ground, rising and falling as the cells drift between the combs, as if someone were turning the world's volume up and down.

## What Grows

Little grows, and what does grows slowly.

Lichens are the most successful: orange, sulfur-yellow, a grey-green that is nearly black. They do not grow evenly. They follow the dew. Each comb's drip-channels are traced in lichen, so that from a height the plateau is marked with branching colored veins, each system radiating from a grey lattice like a map of a nervous system.

Around the wetter pans grow cushion plants, dense hard mounds of tiny leaves packed so tight that you can stand on them. A few are wider than a table and must be centuries old. Silver rosettes grow in the lee of domes, their leaves covered in fine hairs that catch and hold the morning mist. And in the tafoni, where shade and seepage meet, there are ferns, small and wiry, the only soft green in the whole landscape.

Once every few years one of the rosettes sends up a flower stalk twice the height of a person, crowded with pale blossoms. There are never more than a handful at once across the entire basin, and they can be seen from kilometers away, standing like candles among the domes.

## What Lives

For all its sparseness, the plateau is full of animals. They are small, many and quiet.

The pans at dawn are crowded. Swarms of midges hang over the water in columns that twist and lean in the still air, soundless because the combs drink their whine. Beetles with iridescent green-black wing cases come to the wet margins to drink. Small lizards, several kinds, emerge from the tafoni as soon as the light reaches them and bask on the warming rock, then go down to hunt the beetles.

In the silence, the animals have learned to speak with light.

The lizards have throat fans patterned in ultraviolet and electric blue, and they flash them at each other from rock to rock, arguments in color. Small birds with mirror-bright patches on their wings dart among the domes, and when they turn in the sun they send out flashes that can be seen a kilometer off, a kind of heliograph. Flocks moving across the basin glitter as they turn. The whole plateau twinkles with signaling on bright mornings, and it is all silent.

Near the dead combs the animals use sound. Further out, away from any lattice, you hear crickets, the occasional bird call, the squeak of the rock rabbits. But near the combs, they simply never evolved to be loud. A call made there would vanish. So the communities near combs and those far from them are almost different worlds, sharing the same rock.

The rock rabbits are the largest common animal, round and grey with long whiskers, closer to chinchillas than hares. They live in colonies in the tafoni and come out at dawn to graze on the cushion plants and drink from the pans. Their predators are a pale falcon that hunts along the dome crests, and a long, slender snake the color of tuff that waits at the edges of pans with only its head above the silt.

The falcon's hunting is one of the strangest things on the plateau to watch. It uses the combs. It stoops out of the bright sky and cuts through a comb's zone of silence, and the rabbits, which listen for the rush of wings, cannot hear it coming. Some of the rabbit colonies have learned to set watchers who scan the sky and flash warnings with a flick of their white tails. The falcons, in turn, have learned to come in from the direction of the sun.

Larger animals pass through. A kind of small wild camelid, sleek and fawn-colored, crosses the basin in family groups, stopping at the pans to drink. It moves carefully and silently even in the open. In the heat of the afternoon, they lie on the domes in the shade of the largest combs, and you can walk among them without their noticing you, since you make no sound.

## The Days and Seasons

The whole plateau breathes in a daily rhythm.

Pre-dawn is cold, wet and dark, and the combs drip. At dawn, the light floods in all at once, the mist lifts, and every animal on the plateau converges on the water. The mornings are busy, glittering and silent. By midday the heat is intense, the light almost unbearable, the pans shrinking. Most animals retreat into the tafoni and shade. Only the lizards stay out, and the combs throw their slow caustic patterns across the empty rock. In the afternoon, clouds may build over the escarpment, and the wind picks up, and sometimes storms come. At dusk the animals emerge again for a second, briefer round of feeding, and then the night comes, clear, cold and full of stars. Out on the open rock there are faint night sounds, the calls of insects, the scrape of something moving. Near the combs there is nothing at all.

## The Walking Stones

The combs are not fixed.

This is not obvious in a lifetime, but it can be read in the rock. Behind each large comb, running across the tuff, is a faint trail: a band of stone polished to a soft gloss, scored with the old burn-lines of caustic light. And along these trails are the abandoned drip-channels, dry grooves leading to dry pans, lichen long dead, marking places where a comb used to stand.

As far as anyone can tell, the combs move a few centimeters a year. They seem to move toward sound: toward the edge of the escarpment where the wind is loudest, toward the passes where storms come through. Some trails are kilometers long. Following one is like following the track of some enormously slow animal. Across the basin, the old trails crisscross, and the lichen-veins of dead dew systems lie over one another like palimpsests.

Some combs have failed. There are three or four dead ones on the plateau. They are cracked and slumped, their lattices fallen open, the grey material gone chalky and white. They no longer draw water or silence, and standing near one is a shock. Suddenly there is wind, the calls of birds, the scrape of your own footsteps. It seems very loud. The pans near a dead comb are dry. Around them there are ruins of their lichen systems, and in their broken cells the birds that still call out have built their nests.

## Other Marks

The combs are the main traces of whoever came before, but there are others.

Running straight across the basin is a line of fused pavement, a band of glassy black stone a few meters wide, perfectly level. It does not follow the land. It crosses domes by cutting through them, the rock sheared away on either side with faces as smooth as polished glass. It goes over pans as a bridge, suspended on nothing that anyone can see. It runs from one side of the basin to the other and stops at the escarpment, ending in the air above the cloud-sea. Birds perch on the end of it.

Scattered here and there are small stone huts, low and round, built of stacked tuff without mortar. These are much newer. Herders, perhaps, or travelers, used them. Some have fallen in. There are cairns on the higher domes. In one of the huts someone has painted, on the inside wall, a picture of a comb and, beneath it, a hand with the fingers raised, as though for silence.

The people who come here now are few, and they communicate in gestures when near the combs. It is considered bad form to shout on the plateau, even in the open. There is a sense that the quiet belongs to the place.

## At Night, By a Pan

Lie at the edge of a pan near a large comb on a clear night. The stone is still warm at your back from the day. The air above you is cold and very clear. The stars are more numerous than you have ever seen, bright and steady at this altitude. The comb stands dark against them, a lattice of black cutting the starfield into hexagons, and you can feel its cold on your face from ten meters away.

There is no sound. Not wind, not insects, not your own breath. You can hear, if you listen, only the faint pulse of blood in your ears, and even that seems muffled.

Toward the end of the night a drop of water forms on the lowest edge of the comb, grows, falls. It strikes the pan and sends out rings across the black water, rings that carry the stars. It makes no sound. Then another falls, and another, and the water begins to gather, and far off in the east the sky starts to go pale.
