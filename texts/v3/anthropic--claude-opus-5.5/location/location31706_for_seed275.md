# The Rootward Reach

## Entering

You come down into it through a shaft cut only a few years ago. The stone at its lip is still sharp enough to split a glove. Nothing here has been worn. The walls of the descent are raw granite and blue slate, fractured in clean planes, and every seam is threaded with pale roots as thick as a wrist. The roots did the fracturing. They are still doing it. Stand still against the wall and you can feel a pressure in the rock, very faint, like a held breath that never lets out.

Then the shaft opens, and the ground falls away beneath the ceiling, and you understand that the ceiling is the sky.

The Reach is a cavity roughly the size of a river valley. It is perhaps twenty kilometres long and six across, and its floor slopes gently toward a far end nobody has reached on foot. It is not a cave in the usual sense. No water carved it. It was grown. The cavity exists because the plants opened it, root by root, prying the rock apart and eating the stone. The Reach is getting larger. The surveyors' maps are out of date the season after they are drawn.

## The Canopy That Is the Sky

Overhead, three hundred metres up, the roof is a single continuous organism. The miners call it the Lid. It is a mat of broad, overlapping, waxy leaves, each one as large as a sailcloth. They are pressed flat against the rock and hang downward like the underside of a tremendous mushroom. The leaves give off light.

It is not a glow or a phosphorescence. It is plain, working daylight, slightly cool, slightly green at the edges. It is bright enough to read by at the floor and bright enough to cast clean shadows. The Lid wakes over roughly an hour. A dimness at the far end brightens, and the brightening moves up the valley like a tide coming in. It dims the same way about fourteen hours later. There is no sun to pace it. Nobody knows what the Lid is keeping time with. The prevailing theory is that it tracks the real sun on the surface through some chemistry in the rock above. The prevailing joke is that the real sun is keeping time with the Lid.

Between the leaves hang long pendulous tassels, some a hundred metres in length. They sway in a draught you cannot feel at ground level. These are the Lid's flowers, and they are the origin of the weather.

## Dryness

There is no water here. The Reach is not a desert, because a desert implies water that has been lost. Here it was never part of the arrangement.

The plants do not drink. They take what they need from stone. Their roots secrete a slow chemistry that splits feldspar and mica and pulls something vital from the lattice. What returns up their stems is not watery sap but a clear, faintly amber oil. It is thin as lamp spirit when warm and thick as honey in the cool of the lower valley. Cut any stem and it beads oil. Crush a leaf and it smells of pine resin, struck flint, and cold metal.

The air is extraordinarily dry. Lips crack within an hour for newcomers. Static builds on clothing and discharges in small blue ticks when you touch a railing. Nothing rots. Dead leaves fall and lie where they land, curling slowly into brittle scrolls. Over years they compact into a grey-brown felt, ankle-deep in some places and waist-deep in others. That felt is the floor of most of the Reach, soft and springy. It swallows sound.

It is cool, around ten degrees on most days. Sometimes a warmth comes up from below, from wherever the deepest roots are working, and the valley floor sweats oil for a week. Workers call these weeks *the sweat*. The whole Reach smells like a varnish shop, and everything becomes slightly tacky.

## The Growing Floor

Across the whole valley floor, from wall to wall and from the Lid to the felt, is vegetation. Every surface you can see is alive, or was.

**Pillar-trunks** rise from the floor to the Lid, some straight and some leaning. The largest are thirty metres in diameter. They are not trees in any surface sense. They are more like tendons. They hold the roof up, or more precisely they hold the roof *apart*, pushing it upward as they thicken. Their bark is grey and ridged like poured concrete. There are several thousand of them, spaced irregularly, often a kilometre from their nearest neighbour. In between is open air and lower growth.

**Crust-scrub** covers the ground in rolling mounds: dense, knee-high, wiry, and blue-green, with small hard leaves like fingernail clippings. It grows directly on bare rock in the newest parts of the Reach. It is always the first to arrive where fresh stone is exposed.

**Lantern-fern** grows in stands along the slopes. Its tall fronds curl into hollow cones that trap the Lid-light and seem to hold it a little longer at dusk. In the brief twilight the stands stay lit for twenty minutes after everything else has gone grey.

**Strangler-lattice** wraps around the pillar-trunks and sprawls out between them. It is a fine net of stems that hardens as it ages until it is rigid as wrought iron. Old lattice can carry a rail line. Much of it does.

In the newest reaches, at the growth front, you find **the pale**. These are root masses that have broken through the cavity wall but have not yet produced leaves. They look like frozen white waterfalls poured out of the stone. They are soft for a few weeks. Fauna swarm on them.

## The City

The town is called Stave. Its people rarely call it that. They call it *down*, as in "going down tomorrow" or "born down."

Stave does not look like a city from any single viewpoint, because it is too spread out. Its buildings are scattered across the valley in clusters. A pumphouse here, a row of dormitories there, and then a full kilometre of crust-scrub before the next thing. Yet it is unmistakably urban in its organisation. It has street numbers, zoning, a municipal authority, and schedules. It is a city stretched thin over a valley, like a town map printed on a rubber sheet and pulled.

It is built mostly of plant. The pillar-trunks are hollowed and inhabited. Whole neighbourhoods spiral up their interiors: stairwells cut into the living wood, windows bored through the bark, balconies bracketed with lattice. The trunks do not seem to mind. They heal around the openings slowly and must be re-cut every few years, like trimming a hedge. Ground-level buildings are made of pressed felt block, dense and warm, stacked and bonded with hardened oil. Metal is used sparingly. It is expensive to bring down and tends to become coated in a slow film of plant resin.

Everything is connected by rails. A lattice-borne narrow-gauge network runs along the hardened strangler stems, strung between pillars at heights of twenty, fifty, and a hundred metres. The cars are small, open, and quiet, and they move at the pace of a brisk walk. Looking up during the day, you see them crossing the air in all directions like beads on invisible threads.

## Industry

Stave exists for the oil.

Sap-oil is tapped from the pillar-trunks through steel spigots driven deep into the wood. It runs down collection channels into settling vats, then into the refineries. There are three refineries. Each is a long, low hall of felt-block and iron, sited near the largest trunk clusters. There the oil is heated, separated, and fractioned. The light fraction burns cleanly and runs the engines. The middle fraction is lubricant, varnish, and the base of a dozen chemicals the surface buys at high prices. The heavy fraction, black and nearly solid, is the insulation for Stave's electrical system.

The electricity is the strange part. The roots carry a potential. Push a copper stake into a large root and another into the rock a hundred metres off, and a current flows. It is weak but steady and inexhaustible. Stave's power grid is a vast array of these stakes, called *taps*. They are wired together in series along the root-mats, delivering a constant low hum of current to the whole town. The lights never flicker. The motors never surge. Nobody fully understands the source. The plants do not seem to be harmed by it. The current may in fact be something the plants *want* to give off. Where the taps are densest, growth is fastest.

The engines of Stave are therefore slow and constant rather than loud and forceful. Belts turn at walking pace. Presses descend over minutes. Winches creak up the pillar-trunks at the speed of a climbing insect. A factory floor here sounds like a clock shop: many small ticks, a low drone, no roar.

## The Quiet

The Reach is very quiet. The felt absorbs sound. The dry air carries it poorly. There is no running water, no wind at ground level, and no rain. The engines are slow. People speak softly out of long habit, and visitors from the surface who shout are stared at.

What you hear, standing alone in the scrub between neighbourhoods, is mostly texture: the crackle of a dead leaf settling, the faint dry rasp of insect legs, and the tick of static. Occasionally there is a single deep *crack* from far off, like a rifle shot, echoing for a long time. That is a root splitting rock somewhere. The Reach getting larger. The townspeople stop mid-sentence when they hear one, count the echoes, and then go on talking.

## Weather

Despite having no water, the Reach has weather. It is made of pollen and spore.

The Lid's hanging tassels release pollen in pulses, several times a month, in response to something nobody has identified. The pollen is fine, pale gold, and very light. It descends slowly as a luminous haze, catching the Lid-light, and settles over everything. For a day the whole valley is golden-dim, soft-edged, and hazy. Visibility falls to a few hundred metres. The rail cars run on bells. People wear gauze.

After a pollen fall, the ground plants answer. The crust-scrub releases spore clouds that billow out low along the floor in rolling grey banks. They look like fog, but they are dry, faintly warm, and smell like crushed pepper. Spore banks drift along the valley's gentle slope toward the lower end over two or three days, pooling in hollows and draining slowly away.

These cycles overlap. There is almost always something moving in the air somewhere in the Reach: a gold haze near the Lid, a grey bank rolling along the floor, a clear stretch between them. It is weather in every respect but moisture.

## Fauna

The Reach teems with animals. Almost all of them live on oil.

**Resin-moths** are the most visible. They are pale and broad-winged, from palm-sized to the width of a door. They drift in huge slow flocks under the Lid, feeding at the tassels. In daylight they look like scraps of paper blowing. At twilight they descend to the lantern-fern stands, and the stands fill with shifting pale shapes.

**Tap-beetles** are the pests of Stave's industry. They are hard, black, and fist-sized, and they bore into trunks alongside the spigots and drink the oil. They move in herds of hundreds across the felt, making a sound like distant rain. It is the closest thing to rain the Reach has.

**Felt-hares** are grey, silent, and long-eared, and they burrow in the deep litter. They eat crust-scrub and come out in the evenings in their thousands. The quiet of the Reach at twilight is punctuated by their soft, rhythmic thumping as they signal danger to one another.

**Lattice-cats** hunt the hares. They are lean, pale, and oil-slick. They live in the strangler nets high above the floor and drop on their prey from twenty metres up. They have learned the rail schedule and ride the cars, and nobody can stop them.

**Stonewhales** are the strangest. They are huge, slow, blind creatures, the size of a rail car, with bodies like overlapping plates of fresh slate. They live at the growth front, among the pale. They eat root, and in eating it they grind the rock behind it into fine gravel, which accelerates the splitting. They may be the root system's partners, or its gardeners, or a part of it. People who have spent time at the front say the stonewhales and the pale move together, the whales always a few metres behind the newest roots, like cattle behind a plough.

## The Rules of the Place

There are things everyone down knows, and newcomers learn quickly.

The Reach is younger than the town. Stave was founded eleven years ago, in a cavity then barely two kilometres long. Every year the walls recede. The town follows. The oldest neighbourhoods, in the first trunks, are now a full day's rail ride from the frontier.

Nothing here erodes. There is no water to wear the stone, so every break stays sharp. The cavity walls are all fresh fracture: bright, crystalline, and unweathered. The whole place looks as though it were made last week, because in a sense it was. Only the living things soften it.

The plants respond to the town. Places where people gather grow faster. Taps accelerate growth. Rail vibration seems to guide the lattice, which grows along the lines over the years and thickens under the tracks, as though it had decided to support them. Some residents believe the vegetation is, slowly and without intent, building the city with them. A few believe it is building the city *for* them, and that the question worth asking is why.

No one has reached the far end of the Reach. It lies beyond the last pillar-trunks, beyond the pale, beyond the working ground of the stonewhales. It is in the dimness where the Lid is thinnest and the light grows faint. Expeditions report that the Lid there is not yet fully formed. It is still a mat of pale tassel-roots without leaves, and the light is not yet on. Past that, they say, is only the dark edge of the cavity, still being opened.

The deep cracks come from there. You can hear them from anywhere in Stave, if you are quiet, and everyone down is quiet. The roots keep splitting the stone, the stonewhales keep grinding, and the Reach keeps growing, sharp-edged and dry and full of light.
