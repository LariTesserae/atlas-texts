# The Plateau of Unattended Building

## The Approach

The ground rises for days before it stops, and when it stops it stops all at once: a clean escarpment, sharp-edged, new, as though the land were lifted out of the lowlands last season and hasn't settled yet. Above the lip the air thins but does not cool. It is hot up here, a wet, close heat, the heat of a greenhouse left shut in summer. The clouds that should sit above a place this high sit *below* it, a white floor lapping at the cliffs, and they breathe their moisture upward so that everything on the plateau is always slightly beaded, always slightly running.

There is no stone at the top. Where you would expect rock there is a floor, and the floor is made.

## The Floor

It is woven. Not metaphorically: the entire tableland is paved with a lattice of roots, flattened and interlaced in a basket-weave so regular it looks like the work of a patient craftsman. Each strand is as thick as a forearm, pale green going to white at the joins, and the weave is tight enough to walk on without seeing gaps. Press a heel into it and water wells up around the edges of your foot, clear and warm, because the lattice holds the rain the way a sponge does and gives it back under pressure.

Under the first weave lies a second, laid at a diagonal to the first, and under that a third. Nobody has found the bottom. Somewhere down there the living layers become dead layers, then a peat of old architecture compressed into black, but that depth is a guess. What's certain is that the floor is young on top. The upper weave is replaced constantly. Strands creep out from the seams, find their neighbors, lock, and thicken. On a still day, if you lie with your ear to it, you can hear it working: a faint continuous creak, like rope taking strain.

Nothing on the plateau is "natural ground." Everything is laid, fitted, joined. And none of it was done by hands.

## The Buildings

Across the open miles of the tableland stand structures, and there are not many of them. That surprises people. You would expect a place like this to be a jungle, a crush, but the builds are spaced far apart, sometimes a mile or more between one and the next. Between them the woven floor runs flat and empty and steaming. Standing in that open space you can see a dozen buildings at once on the horizon, each alone, each distinct, like the scattered follies of an estate whose owner died long ago.

They are *buildings*. That's the strangeness, and it doesn't wear off. A tower of fused trunks rises two hundred feet, square in section, its corners true, with regular openings at every level that can only be windows. A long colonnade of paired stems runs arrow-straight for half a mile toward nothing, each pair of columns joined overhead by a lintel of grafted wood. A dome stands on eight ribs, its skin a shingling of enormous leaves overlapping like tiles, with an oculus at its crown that lets the rain fall in a single column onto a round basin grown from the floor to catch it. There are stairs that climb the outside of towers, their treads level, their risers even, leading to doors.

The doors open. They are panels of thick, rubbery tissue hinged on a living joint, and they swing on turgor pressure: as the day heats and the sap rises they close, and as evening cools they sag open. Behind them are rooms. The rooms are empty, and they are always empty. There are no furnishings, because nothing here needs furniture. They are rooms in the shape of rooms, made for no one, grown from an architectural grammar the plants seem to hold without having learned it from anybody.

No animal has ever walked these floors. No bird has nested in the towers, no insect has bored the beams. It is architecture in a world that never had architects, and it builds anyway, the way a crystal grows facets.

## The Rule of the Right Angle

The deepest law of the plateau is that growth here seeks geometry. A vine that would coil anywhere else runs straight. When it meets another it meets it at ninety degrees and fuses. Branches level themselves. Trunks plumb themselves. Arches find the catenary. It is as if the plants were responding to some pressure that isn't gravity or light but *form itself*, a tendency toward the joist, the lintel, the plane.

The geometry is not perfect, and the imperfections are where the weirdness lives. Some builds are clearly unfinished, or confused. A staircase spirals up a column and continues past the top into open air, the treads getting thinner, until the last dozen steps are no wider than a finger and sway in the wind, still reaching for a landing that will never come. A wall grows windows on both faces but the windows don't line up, so light enters one side and meets solid wood. One structure in the north repeats the same small room hundreds of times in a honeycomb, each room identical down to the curve of its doorsill. Another seems to be trying to build something that has no name: angles that almost close, a volume that folds back into itself, a doorway that opens onto a doorway that opens onto the first doorway again. People who have spent long here say there are forms on the plateau that look like buildings from other places, a minaret, a pagoda eave, a Gothic buttress. They argue about whether that means anything. Probably the shapes that work, work everywhere, whether stone or sap, whether anyone intends them or not.

## Water Works

The heat and the wet are what drive it all. Rain falls here most afternoons, heavy and warm, straight down in cords. Between rains the cloud-floor below exhales, and mist boils up over the escarpment edge and across the plateau in slow, hot rolls.

The builds handle water like engineers. Gutters of split stem run along every roofline. Downpipes of hollow trunk carry the runoff to the floor, where it vanishes into the weave. The most spectacular structures are aqueducts: channels carried on arches across the open floor, sometimes for miles, moving water from one build to another with no apparent reason except that channels, too, are a form the plateau likes. They leak. Every joint weeps. The arches are hung with long beards of water, and the ground beneath each aqueduct is a line of pools, shallow and blood-warm, rimmed with a green skin of new growth.

Everything drips. Every surface sweats. Wood here never dries enough to crack from dryness; it cracks from swelling instead, and when it goes it goes loud.

## The Turbulence

Nothing on the plateau stands long. This is the second law, and it is why the place never becomes a forest of ancient towers. The buildings are always young, their wood white and green and running with sap, edges crisp, nothing weathered. They don't have time to weather.

A build can rise in days. A shoot as thick as a man appears at a seam in the floor at dawn and by evening is a column. Within a week it has neighbors, lintels, a roof. Growth at that speed is violent. You can watch it if you stand close: the bark splitting and resealing, fibers visibly twisting as they tighten, sap welling from the wounds and hardening into glossy amber knots. The heat drives it. On the hottest days growth runs so fast that the structures outpace their own strength.

Then they fail. Towers grow too tall for their bases and lean, groaning, for hours, and then come down, a two-hundred-foot fall that sends a shock through the woven floor you feel a mile off. Domes over-swell and burst along their ribs. Aqueducts sag, and when a span collapses the water it carried floods out across the floor in a hot brown sheet. Whole wings of a building will simply rot in an afternoon, going from firm to mush as some internal signal flips, until the rooms fold in on themselves like wet paper.

And the fallen material doesn't lie still. Within hours the floor-weave has reached up into the wreckage and started pulling it down, digesting it, feeding it back into the layers. Within days, new shoots rise from the site, and something else begins: rarely the same shape, often a rhyme of it, as if the plateau were drafting and redrafting, never satisfied.

At any given moment, looking out across the open miles, you'll see builds in every phase: one rising, pale and raw; one complete and briefly perfect; one leaning; one a heap already sprouting. The place is a construction site with no crew, a demolition yard with no wreckers, running endlessly under the hot rain.

## The Sound

For a place with no animals it is astonishingly loud.

The baseline is water: rain drumming on leaf-tile roofs, the roar of downpipes, the continuous sizzle of drips on the floor-weave, the slap of aqueduct leaks into their pools. Over that sits the building noise, a vast, distributed creaking, the sound of an old ship multiplied by a whole landscape, wood under tension on every side. Growth has its own sounds: snaps and pops as bark splits, a wet tearing as grafts fuse, a deep, almost tonal groan as a column thickens.

Then there are the events. A collapse sounds like a thunderclap with a long, splintering tail. Some builds sprout seed-pods along their eaves, woody capsules the size of a head, and in the heat these dry just enough to detonate, flinging seed a quarter-mile with a crack like a rifle shot. On hot afternoons the pods go off in volleys across the plateau, and the whole place sounds like a skirmish. The swelling doors, closing in the heat, bang shut one after another in the empty rooms, a slow ripple of slammed doors spreading across miles as the temperature peaks.

What's missing is the thing your ears keep searching for: a voice, a buzz, a call, a footstep. No birdsong answers the dawn. No insect whines in the wet dark. The noise is all mechanical, all structural, all *made*, and the absence of anything alive in it gives it a hollow quality, like listening to a city from which everyone has just left.

## Light and Day

The light is ordinary daylight filtered through a lot of moisture: bright but soft, a little diffuse, shadows present but without hard edges. Mornings are clearest, when the air has rained itself out overnight and the tableland stands sharp against the sky, every tower visible to the horizon, the cloud-floor below the cliffs white and blinding. By midday the mist climbs up and everything goes pearl-gray and close; builds a half-mile off become silhouettes, then ghosts. The afternoon rain comes down in walls you can watch approach across the open floor, a gray curtain with the hiss of it running ahead.

Evening is when the doors open. As the air cools a little, turgor drops, and all across the plateau the panels sag inward on their living hinges with a long, soft sigh of wood. The rooms stand open to the night. Water drips in the dark. Growth slows but never stops; you hear it creaking all night, and at intervals, somewhere far out, the dull boom of something coming down.

## The Emptiness Between

The most unsettling thing about the plateau may be its spacing. The builds keep their distance. When a new shoot rises too near an existing structure it withers, or grows sideways, away, or joins the older build as an annex. Something regulates density here, some chemistry through the floor, so the place never fills. There is always open woven ground between presences, always long sightlines, always a sense of too much room.

Walking those spaces, a mile of wet lattice between one empty tower and the next, you feel the scale of what's being made, and for whom: for no one, for nothing, for the working-out of form in a hot, wet, high place where plants learned the grammar of shelter without ever having anything to shelter. The towers go up. The rooms stand ready. The doors open every evening onto nothing, and every morning, by heat, they close.
