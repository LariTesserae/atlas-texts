# Cantle, the Town That Keeps Rehearsing

**The Basin**

You come down into it rather than up. The land has been sinking toward this place for so long that the approach is barely a slope, a few meters of fall spread over a morning's walk. The last true stone you pass is a boulder worn so round and soft that moss has filled its every seam, and it looks less like a rock than a sleeping animal. After that there is no more stone. Everything underfoot is alive.

The basin is small, perhaps nine hundred paces across, bounded by a low ring-levee that is not earth. It is root: one continuous coil of it, grey-green and barked like an old olive, thick as a collapsed wall and warm to the touch. It breathes. If you lay your palm on it long enough you feel a slow pressure rise and fall under the bark, and then a faster flutter beneath that, which the few people who have studied Cantle call *the clock*.

Inside the ring the ground is a single organism, which those same people call the Sward. It looks like water-meadow from a distance: low, bright, flat. Up close it is a woven mat of fibers, pads, bladders, and stolons, layered so densely that it bears your weight the way a hammock does. Between the pads lies standing water, tea-colored and body-warm, and when you step the mat dips and water wells up clear around your ankles, then drains away through pores that open and close with small wet clicks.

Nothing grows *on* the Sward. The Sward grows. The ferns, the reeds, the flowering stalks, the vines that loop and hang in the humid air are all the same body, all extruded from it, all connected underneath. It is total. There is no soil here, no bedrock, no leaf litter, only the living mass and the water moving through it.

**The Buildings**

And yet it is a town.

Scattered across the open Sward, far apart, stand the buildings. There are perhaps forty at any moment, never quite the same forty. Each rises out of the mat on a thickened trunk-footing and spreads into rooms, galleries, stairs, and roofs, all grown of glossy lignin the color of wet cedar, veined with pale conduits that pulse faintly when you touch them. There is a tower with an open belfry. There is a long low hall with a colonnade. There is a bridge that crosses nothing but a wide shallow pool, and a street, an actual paved street, cobbled in hard seed-husks and gutter-lined, that runs for sixty paces from nowhere to nowhere, with lamps along it that are closed flower-heads on curved stalks.

The spaces between them are vast by comparison, open lawn and mirror-water and hanging mist. Standing in the middle of Cantle you can see the whole of it at once, every building isolated, like pieces set out on a board before a game.

This is the first strangeness, and the one that takes longest to see: every building is *worn*.

Not weathered in the usual sense, since the lignin is fresh, often only hours old. It is worn in its *design*. The tower is not a particular tower. It is the idea of a tower, smoothed of every ornament and quirk, reduced to shaft, stair, and opening. The hall's columns have no capitals, only a gentle swelling where capitals once were. Doors are rounded rectangles. Windows are rounded rectangles. Over the lintels of several buildings there are raised marks that were once, clearly, lettering, but they have softened into single rounded glyphs, like words spoken so many times they have become a sound.

The explanation, as far as anyone has one: the Sward was engineered, an unthinkably long time ago, as a *city-seed*. It was a living construction system designed to grow a town for a population and keep it in repair. The population is gone. They may never have arrived. The Sward has kept building anyway, tearing down and regrowing its town on a cycle of hours, and it has done this so many millions of times that its plans have eroded the way a river erodes a stone. Every rebuild copies the last, slightly imperfectly, and the imperfections have averaged out into archetype. Cantle is ancient in the only way a thing made of fresh growth can be ancient: it has been remembering itself for so long that it has forgotten everything but the essentials.

It is a settled final form that never stops moving.

**The Churn**

Because it does not stop.

Stand still for a quarter of an hour and you will see a building begin to go. It starts at the roofline. The lignin darkens, goes soft and spongy, and then slumps. Walls fold inward with long groaning creaks. Whole rooms subside into the Sward like a cake collapsing, and the mat around the footing heaves and bubbles as it takes the material back, digestive, hot. The smell is sharp and sweet, fermenting apple and cut green stem, and the water around a dissolving house steams.

Elsewhere, something is rising. The Sward humps up into a blister the size of a barn, splits along seams, and a frame unfolds out of it with hydraulic speed: ribs pushing up, arching, locking together with cracks like pistol shots. Membranes stretch between the ribs, thicken, and harden into wall within minutes. Stairs spiral up inside like the uncurling of a fern. The whole thing shudders and hisses as the conduits fill. In under an hour, a hall stands where there was lawn.

At any moment, somewhere in the basin, two or three buildings are falling and two or three are rising. Sometimes a dozen go at once, in what the observers call a *surge*, and the whole Sward rolls like a swell at sea. Walkers are thrown off their feet, pools slosh into new pools, and the mist boils up off the warm water so thickly the town vanishes and reappears in pieces. Streets reroute. The bridge that crossed nothing is suddenly crossing a newly drowned channel. Nothing is where you left it.

This is the rule of Cantle, and it is the first thing you learn: **keep moving.** The Sward builds around stillness. Sleep in a doorway and you may wake inside a wall, gently enclosed, unharmed, cradled in a pocket of warm lignin that will open again, eventually, when that building's cycle ends. The mat does not mean harm. It simply assumes anything that stays is meant to be housed.

**The Sound**

Cantle is loud.

Beneath everything runs the Sward's own noise: a constant wet percussion of pores clicking open and shut, water gurgling through its veins, the deep bassoon groans of load-bearing tissue taking weight. Under the mat, too low to hear properly but felt in the teeth, there is a steady hum. It is the clock, the computing pulse of the organism, running whatever ancient instructions it still runs.

Above that are the building-sounds: the cracks and pops of rising frames, the long sighing collapses, the hiss of pressurizing conduits. The flower-lamps along the orphan street sometimes open all at once with a sound like a crowd drawing breath.

Over all of it, filling every gap, are the animals.

**The Citizens**

Because the town is not empty. It has simply been inherited.

The frogs came first, or seem to have: a hundred kinds, from thumbnail-sized emerald things clinging to window-glass (the windows are sheets of taut, clear cuticle) to blunt-headed bulls as heavy as a loaf of bread who sit in the gutters of the street and boom. Their chorus in the evening warmth is enormous, rising in overlapping waves. Nobody who has spent a night in Cantle forgets it.

Birds: swifts that nest in the belfries and ride the rising towers upward, launching from the tips as they crest, as if the growth itself were a thermal. Herons stalk the pools between buildings. Bright small finches pick at the seed-husk cobbles, and the cobbles, it seems, are regrown with a little extra seed in them each time.

In the steaming water around digesting houses swim long bronze eels, feeding on whatever softens and sloughs. There are crabs, broad pale ones with oversized claws, that scuttle into dissolving buildings and carry off door-frames and stair-treads before the Sward can take them back. They build their own low shanties out of these stolen pieces along the inner curve of the root-levee, a second, smaller, scavenged town at the edge of the first. Dragonflies are everywhere, in clouds, metallic, clattering. At night the air fills with moths drawn to the flower-lamps, which glow a soft butter-yellow from within, fed by the same conduits that fill the walls.

And this is where Cantle becomes most itself, and strangest:

**the town has begun to build for them.**

Look closely at the newest halls. The doorways are growing taller and narrower, heron-shaped. Some of the windows have ledges, and the ledges have gutters that keep a finger's depth of water standing, perfect for frogs. Belfries in recent towers have scalloped, cup-shaped niches just the right size for a swift's nest. Down in the lowest rooms of certain buildings the floors are no longer floors but shallow pools with smooth ramps leading in, eel-rooms.

Nobody designed this. The Sward was made to shape its town around its inhabitants, to learn what they used and grow more of it. Over the ages of its rehearsing, with no people to learn from, it has been learning from whoever lived in its rooms. The archetypes are drifting again, very slowly, away from the human and toward the marsh. In a few million more rebuilds, Cantle may no longer look like a town at all. It may look like something no one has a word for: a settlement, orderly and deliberate, with streets and lamps and public halls, made entirely for frogs and birds and eels.

**Weather**

The air is warm and saturated, always. The Sward sweats it. Its broad pads transpire so heavily that mist lies on the basin every morning, and clouds sometimes form *inside* the ring, low and local, and rain on the town from a height of forty meters while the sky beyond the levee is clear. This rain is part of the machine. The Sward seeds it, drawing water up from the deep table and dropping it again where the next buildings will need it. Some hours are all drizzle. Some are sun sharp on wet surfaces, every leaf and wall and pool gleaming, the light ordinary and bright and full of flying things.

When a surge comes the weather goes with it: sudden squalls, warm gusts pushed out from collapsing halls, fog that rolls across the lawns like a tide and pulls back again.

**What Remains Constant**

Very little. The ring of root. The hum of the clock. The water, always near the surface, always warm.

And one thing more, which observers have noted and no one understands. At the exact center of the basin, in a place where no building ever rises, there is a single small bench. It is grown, like everything, but never taken back. It is worn to its essence: a plank of lignin on two legs, facing east. Its seat is polished smooth and slightly dipped, as if by a great deal of sitting.

Frogs sit on it now. In the afternoons a heron sometimes stands there.

The Sward keeps it exactly as it is. It may be the last thing it remembers being asked for.
