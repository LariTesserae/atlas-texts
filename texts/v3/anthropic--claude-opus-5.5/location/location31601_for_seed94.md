# The Condensing Highlands

## First Impression

Nothing here was built, and yet it looks manufactured.

The plateau begins at the top of a scarp so old its edges have gone soft. The cliffs run for hundreds of kilometers, black basalt worn into rounded, shouldered forms, and every surface of them weeps. Water beads from the rock in sheets so thin they read as a varnish, and the stone shines as if wet paint had just been laid on it. Above the scarp is the tableland, and the tableland is green in a way that feels deliberate: level, dense, ranked, running to a horizon that curves slightly with the planet. From the edge it looks like a pelt thrown over the world.

The air is cool, around eight or nine degrees at midday, and it is always damp enough that breath does not quite fog but hangs. The light is ordinary daylight, filtered and even, the kind that makes no shadows worth mentioning. The first thing a visitor notices is not the view. It is the quiet.

## The Silence

The Highlands are carpeted, without exception, in cushion moss several meters deep. It is a living felt made of stems, dead stems, water, and air, and it absorbs sound the way snow does, only more completely and permanently. A shout travels perhaps thirty paces before the moss swallows it. Footsteps make no noise at all; the foot sinks, the moss exhales a little cold water, and that is all.

Underneath the silence, if you kneel and press your ear to a trunk or to the moss itself, there is sound: a slow ticking, very regular, like a clock in another room. Then a sigh. Then ticking again. The whole plateau is doing this, everywhere, all the time, too quietly to hear standing up.

This is the industry of the place.

## The Pump Stands

Rising out of the moss are the trees locals would call *lifters*, if there were locals. They are tall and narrow, perhaps forty meters, with smooth grey bark ringed every half meter by a raised collar, so that each trunk looks segmented like a piston rod or a bamboo culm. They have no proper branches. Near the crown the trunk splits into a fan of hollow, trumpet-mouthed stems, each one open to the sky.

The lifters grow in stands, and the stands are ranked. They are not planted, since there is no one to plant them, but the lifters are clonal, spreading by root, and each new trunk emerges at exactly the distance where the chemistry of its neighbors' root exudates allows it. The result is a grid. For kilometers the trunks stand in rows so straight that walking between them feels like walking the floor of a vast shuttered mill, a hall of columns with a ceiling of drifting cloud.

What they do is lift water, and not a little of it. The roots of a single stand go down through the moss, through the peat beneath it, through hundreds of meters of fractured basalt, into an aquifer that lies far below the level of the surrounding lowlands. The collared segments of the trunk contract in sequence, bottom to top. That is the ticking: each collar clamps shut, then the one above it, a slow peristalsis that drives water upward in pulses. At the crown the water is atomized through the trumpet stems and released as mist.

A single stand exhales a column of fog visible from a day's walk away. There are tens of thousands of stands.

## The Weather Engine

The plateau is large enough to make its own weather, and it does, on purpose, in the sense that a factory does things on purpose.

**Night: intake.** As the air cools after dark, the mist the lifters released during the day condenses back onto the moss. The moss drinks. By dawn the entire surface of the Highlands is saturated, heavy, glistening, so full of water it quivers underfoot like a mattress filled with liquid.

**Morning: drain.** The saturated moss begins to release its excess downward and outward. It does not go toward the lowlands directly. It goes into the fissures of the plateau and out through the cliff faces, which is why the scarp weeps without cease. The weeping is the plateau's exhaust. The lowlands below are rich because of it; rivers begin fully formed at the cliff base, emerging from the rock in wide, calm sheets.

**Midday: pressure.** The lifters work hardest at noon. Pulses come faster. The fog columns thicken and rise until they merge overhead into a single ceiling of cloud, flat-bottomed, extending across the whole tableland. Under that ceiling it is bright but shadowless. Over the course of a year this ceiling is almost permanent; the Highlands are a plateau that has roofed itself.

**Afternoon: discharge.** This is when the lightning comes, though "lightning" is too loud a word for it.

## The Charge

Water moving through narrow channels builds static charge. Water moving through billions of narrow channels, pulsed, day after day, builds a great deal of it. The moss holds this charge in a way that ordinary plants do not. Threaded through the cushion, a few centimeters below the surface, are pale mats of fungal hyphae, flat and ribbon-like, running in long, parallel strips. They are conductive. They carry charge along the plateau the way wires would, gathering it from the moss and concentrating it toward the ridgelines.

On the ridges the fungal ribbons surface and form bristling crowns, grey-white fruiting bodies like upturned brushes. In the late afternoon, as the cloud ceiling thickens, these brushes begin to discharge upward into the fog. The discharge is almost silent: a crisp ticking, a faint fizz, a smell of ozone so sharp it is almost minty. The light is thin and blue, visible only in the grey hours. Walking a ridge at dusk means walking through a slow cold sparkle, hair lifting from the scalp, the skin of the forearms prickling.

The charge does work. Charged fog droplets cling to the moss more readily at night; the whole intake cycle depends on the afternoon's discharge. Without it, the mist would blow off the plateau and be lost. The Highlands are, in effect, an electrostatic condenser the size of a small continent, and it has been running for millions of years.

## Density

There is no bare ground. There is nowhere to put your hand that is not already occupied.

The moss is covered in lichens, the lichens are covered in liverworts, and the liverworts support a scurf of tiny white orchids that bloom and die within a week. The lifter trunks are furred to twenty meters with epiphytes: pendant mosses, filmy ferns so thin they are translucent, bromeliad-like cups that hold water and within that water whole ecosystems of larvae, algae, and small swimming things. Between the collars of the trunks, in the slight ledge each one forms, grow rings of tiny succulents, one ring per segment, so that each tree is banded green and grey and pink from base to crown.

Even the fog is inhabited. Spores drift in it thickly enough to give the air a faint grain, like old film. Tiny winged insects, pale as the mist, hang in it by the billion, feeding on the spores, hardly moving, carried upward in the lifter columns and settling back down at night with the dew.

Walking here is less like entering a forest than entering a crowd.

## Fauna

The animals are many and they are quiet, because the place has selected for quiet.

**Moss-rollers.** The dominant grazers are round, low, heavy animals the size of a large dog, covered in dense water-shedding fur the same green-grey as the moss. They move by pressing themselves forward in slow undulations, eating the top centimeter of the cushion as they go. Their paths leave long, shallow grooves that the moss fills back in within a month. From above, a herd's trail looks like brushwork. They do not vocalize. They communicate, if at all, by thumping the moss, which carries low vibration far better than it carries sound, and a rolling herd can be felt through the soles of the feet before it is seen.

**Collar-birds.** Small, long-toed birds nest in the segment rings of the lifters, each pair claiming a single collar. When the collar contracts beneath them they ride it, bobbing, and many of them seem to sleep better during the pulses than between them. They feed on the mist insects, snapping them from the air in short silent sallies. A lifter stand at midday is full of these birds rising and falling, dozens per trunk, and the effect is of a machine in motion, pistons flickering.

**Couriers.** Along the ridges, following the fungal ribbons, run columns of beetles, black, glossy, finger-length, moving in continuous streams several abreast. They carry spores. Each beetle has a shallow bowl on its back into which the fungal brushes shed, and the beetles carry the spores downhill, into the valleys, where they deposit them in newly bare patches, torn moss, the edges of landslips. The columns never stop. They run day and night, in both directions, one lane loaded, one lane empty, and have done so for so long that the ribbons they follow are worn into shallow channels. The only real sound on a ridge, apart from the discharge, is the faint dry rustle of millions of beetle legs.

**The stalker in the fog.** There is a predator. Nobody would see it clearly. It is long, low, and pale, patterned like fog on moss, and it moves only in the thickest part of the afternoon, when visibility drops to a few meters and the air is full of charge. It seems to navigate by the electrical field itself: it follows the gradients the fungal ribbons create, and it can sense the faint disturbance a warm animal makes in charged air. Moss-rollers lying still in the fog are almost invisible to it. Moss-rollers that move are not. When it kills, the only sound is a single muffled thump. The rollers have learned to freeze at the first scent of ozone. So the Highlands go stiller still, every afternoon, as the charge builds.

## The Shape of the Land

The plateau is ancient, and it has reached a kind of truce with itself. There are no sharp forms left. Ridges are long, smooth whalebacks. Valleys are wide, shallow bowls. The drainage runs inward, toward the plateau's center, because the aquifer has been drawn down there more than anywhere, and the ground has subsided over millennia into an immense, gentle basin.

At the center of that basin is a lake, if you can call it that: an expanse of standing water perhaps sixty kilometers across, barely a meter deep, completely covered by floating moss so that it looks like the rest of the land except that it moves. Wind rolls slow swells across it, and the moss-carpet rises and falls, and the lifters that grow in it stand in water up to their lowest collar. The lake is where the plateau's rivers go. It is also where the lifters draw most strongly; the water here descends through the lake bed and is pumped back up. It is a closed loop, a great cycling reservoir, and the fog over the lake is so dense that it rarely clears.

This is the one place in the Highlands where the silence is truly total. Even the ticking is dampened by water. Even the beetles do not come here. The collar-birds do, in their millions, and they ride the pulsing trunks in the grey, rising and falling, a mill with no workers and no noise.

## Rules of the Place

- **Water moves uphill here as a matter of course.** Streams on the plateau do not descend to the lowlands; they are drawn toward the lifter stands and up.
- **Sound does not travel.** Anything that relies on hearing to hunt or to find a mate has long since vanished. What remains communicates by vibration, by scent, by charge.
- **Everything is paid for in charge.** The afternoon's electricity is the plateau's working capital. On rare clear days when the cloud ceiling fails to form, the discharge goes nowhere, the night's condensation is poor, and the moss begins to dry by noon of the next day. Two clear days in a row are an emergency. The whole ecosystem seems to know it: the lifters pulse harder, the beetles run faster, the rollers stop feeding and lie flat.
- **Nothing is idle.** There is no fallow ground, no resting season. The cycle turns every day, all year. The Highlands have no winter, only the slight annual thinning of the cloud ceiling, when the cold comes through a little sharper and the discharge crackles a little louder.

## Standing Here

You would stand at the edge of a lifter stand at noon and look down the rows, the columns receding into grey, their crowns lost in the self-made cloud, each one pulsing slowly, ticking just below hearing, the birds riding them. The moss would be cold and wet around your ankles, and you would feel it drinking. The air would taste of iron and mint. A herd of rollers would pass somewhere behind you, felt more than heard.

You would think: this is a factory. And you would look for the chimneys, the owner, the signs of anyone who made it, and there would be nothing. Only moss, water, and the slow accumulated logic of a place that learned, very long ago, to run itself.
