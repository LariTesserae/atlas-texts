# The Breathing Quarter

It starts with a crack in a salt flat. Nothing marks it except that the air above it shivers more than the air elsewhere. The crack widens into a slot, the slot drops into a throat of pale stone, and the throat opens without warning into a room the size of a small town.

Down there is a city. No one lives in it, and nothing has lived in it for a very long time, though it is not quiet and it is not still.

---

## The Hollow

The cavity is shallow in the way a held breath is shallow. It spreads wide and only a few storeys high, a lens of emptiness pressed into the bedrock just below the desert floor. The ceiling is close enough to see: a lid of banded sandstone, rust and cream and bone. It sags in places, and in those places the builders propped it with columns. The columns do not look like supports. They look like they grew there.

The floor lies lower than the flats above, lower than anything for a long way around. Heat pools here. It rises from below through fissures in the floor, where some deep mineral fire has been burning since before the city and will burn long after it. The air is thick and dry, and it smells of hot stone, pitch, and something faintly sweet, like scorched sugar. There is no moisture at all. Skin tightens within minutes. Lips split. Nothing in the hollow has ever been wet.

The light is dim and red. Most of it comes from below: the fissures glow a dull ember-orange where the rock is hottest, and that glow washes up the walls of the buildings from underneath, so every doorway and lintel casts its shadow upward. A little white daylight enters at the far end, where the ceiling has cracked. It falls in three thin blades that swing slowly across the city over the course of a day, like the hands of a clock no one reads.

## The City

The builders had no mortar, because mortar needs water. Everything here is dry-stacked, carved, or fitted. The streets are cut directly into the floor of the hollow as sunken channels, knee-deep to waist-deep. The houses rise on either side in courses of shaped sandstone blocks, set so precisely that a blade could not slip between them. Where stone would not stack, it was carved away instead. Whole terraces are hollowed out of single outcrops, and their rooms open into one another like the chambers of a shell.

Their tools were simple: stone, bone, perhaps soft copper. You can read them in the walls, in the regular dimpling of pecked surfaces and the long parallel scoring where blocks were ground flat against each other. There are no nails, no hinges, no glass. Doorways are covered, where they are covered at all, by panels of woven fibre gone stiff as bark.

The city is dense. Houses lean shoulder to shoulder. Stairs climb to roof-terraces, and bridges of single stone slabs cross from roof to roof. At the centre, beneath the highest point of the ceiling, stands a stepped platform surrounded by open plazas. By the layout, it is clearly the heart of things.

And it is empty. Room after room holds nothing at all. There are no pots, no bones, no ash in the hearths, because there are no hearths. The builders did not need fire in a place like this. It is the emptiness of a place that was cleared out deliberately and left. Walk the channels and the space opens around you: long vistas down straight streets, plazas where the dust lies undisturbed, silence between the buildings so wide it feels like distance.

## Weathering

Nothing here has been touched by rain, and nothing has rotted. Time has still worked on the city, slowly and without let-up, the way it works on anything left alone long enough.

The agent is moving air. Hot air has flowed through these streets every day for centuries, carrying a fine grit of salt and sand, and it has softened everything. Corners are rounded like river stones. Carved reliefs on the lintels, figures that might be people or might be plants, have been polished into suggestion: a curve that was a shoulder, a hollow that was an eye. The stair treads are dished, though no feet have worn them for ages. The wind did it, pooling in each step and scouring it into a shallow bowl.

There is equilibrium in it. The city is no longer decaying so much as settling into the final shape the air will allow. It is smooth, low, and rounded, and it is becoming geology.

## The Gardens

Something grows here. It grows everywhere.

There is no water, so these plants do not drink. They feed on the hot gases seeping from the fissures, a thin breath of sulphur and hydrocarbons that they take in through leathery leaves and fix into resin. Their sap is not sap. It is amber oil, thick and slow, and it beads at every cut and wound and hardens into glossy knobs. When the red light from below catches them, whole walls seem studded with dark jewels.

They are the colour of old copper and dried blood, with deep maroon leaves, near-black stems, and flowers like curled tongues of brown wax. They cling to the building faces and spill from the roof-terraces. They fill the sunken streets knee-high in some quarters, so that walking the city means wading through a slow, warm, rustling tide.

The builders planted them deliberately. The roof-terraces are gardens, edged with low stone lips to hold the drifting grit that serves as soil. The plazas are set with ranks of carved basins, each positioned exactly over a fissure. The plants in those basins grow tallest, lush shrubs taller than a person, heavy with resin. It becomes clear the city was built around the vents, laid out like a farm to harvest heat. The builders tended these oil-plants and bled them, the way other peoples in other places tended olives or rubber.

The gardens have gone on without the gardeners. They are wild now, in the patient, crowding way of anything left alone to grow, and they are moving.

## The Breathing

The hollow breathes.

Twice a day, as the desert above heats and cools, the pressure between surface and cavity shifts. The warm dense air trapped in the hollow is drawn upward through the crack in the ceiling. Fresh hot gas from below rushes through the fissures to replace it. For about an hour the whole city is in flow: a slow, strong exhalation up and out, pulling from every street and doorway at once.

The builders knew this, and they turned the city into an instrument.

The walls are pierced with holes. They are small, round, and carefully graded, cut in rows through the stone, and they are everywhere once you learn to see them: in lintels, parapets, the risers of stairs, the sides of the great basins. When the breathing begins, air forces through them, and the city starts to sound.

It builds gradually. A thin whistle rises from one quarter, then another answers from across the plaza. Low tones swell from the deep street-channels, drones so heavy they are felt in the ribs more than heard. The hollowed terraces resonate like the bodies of drums. Within minutes the noise is enormous: a vast chord of hundreds of pitches, shifting as gusts move through the streets. It rises and falls, overwhelming in the enclosed space, loud enough that you cannot hear yourself shout. The sandstone ceiling throws it back down. The whole cavity rings.

No one knows what it was for. Perhaps it was worship, or a clock, or a way of calling the harvest. Perhaps the builders simply liked it. It plays on without them, twice a day, every day.

## What Moves

For a place without animals, a surprising amount happens here.

The plants grow fast, visibly fast over hours, if you watch one tendril against a mark on a wall. Warmth and gas are unlimited here, and the only limits are space and light, so they compete fiercely for both. Vines race toward the three blades of daylight and track them as they swing, stems twisting slowly through the day. Shrubs in the basins crowd and shade each other. The losers yellow, dry, and crack apart in the heat, and the winners' roots take over their soil.

The plants reproduce by force. Their seed pods are hard resin cases that swell with trapped gas as they ripen. When the pressure passes some threshold, they burst with a sharp crack like a knuckle or a snapped branch, flinging seeds across the street. During the warm hours they go off continuously, a scattered popping from every direction, sometimes in chains as one burst jars another loose.

The stone speaks too. As the ceiling heats and cools with the desert above, it creaks and ticks, and occasionally lets go with a boom as a stress-fracture spreads. Flakes of sandstone drift down from the ceiling like slow snow and settle on the gardens. The grit collects in the terraces and becomes new soil. The fissures pulse and flare, some brightening, some fading, as the fire below shifts.

The wind carries all of it together: pollen, grit, seeds, resin-dust, the sweet scorched smell. Up through the streets and out of the crack in the ceiling it goes, into the brighter desert above. There, a few seeds catch on the salt and sit waiting for a warmth that almost never comes.

## The Strangeness

What lingers, after time spent here, is how *finished* it feels. The builders made a complete thing and then left it, and it has kept running exactly as they designed it, perhaps better. Gardens tend themselves, the instrument plays itself, and the heat-farm harvests itself into shining beads of oil that no one collects. The resin accumulates, layer on layer, on walls and in basins, slowly burying the city in amber.

In a few thousand more years, perhaps less, it will all be sealed: the streets full, the holes stopped, the gardens preserved inside their own secretions. The breathing will go on, but the music will fall silent.

For now it plays. The hollow is hot, red, dim, and roaring twice a day, full of plants and empty of everything else, and it lies hidden under a crack in the salt where the air shivers.
