# The Low Gallery at Kessit Deep

Kessit Deep is a chamber under a city that sits on rock that is always warm. The chamber is not large. Someone standing at its center could throw a stone against any wall, and the stone would strike raw basalt still scored with drill-steel grooves, each groove sweating a thin bead of water.

The chamber is new. It was blasted eleven years ago to house a pump. The pump was meant to draw geothermal brine up to the district heating mains above, and it still does that, slowly and with great noise. Nobody finished the room around it. The walls were never lined and the floor was never leveled. The rock hasn't had time to become a cave. It is still a wound, and the edges of every fracture are sharp enough to cut a thumb.

---

## The Rock

The stone is black basalt, vesicular in bands, with seams of pale zeolite running through it like fat through meat. Where the blasting charges went off, the rock is shattered into plates that hang in the ceiling at angles, held by nothing but their own interlocking. Engineers painted orange rings around the worst of them years ago. The paint has since gone brown under a skin of mineral scale. No plate has fallen yet, though some have visibly shifted.

Water comes from everywhere and nowhere. It does not flow. It weeps. The rock is hot, about as hot as a bath you'd have to lower yourself into slowly. The water that emerges from its pores is hotter. It beads, runs a finger's length, and stops. There it evaporates, leaving a white rind of silica and calcite. These rinds are the only erosion Kessit Deep has, and they work in reverse: they build rather than remove. Along the lower walls they have grown into soft ridges, like wax down the side of a candle. Given a few thousand years, they might become flowstone. Right now they are the thickness of a fingernail.

The floor holds three shallow pools where the blasted rubble was never cleared. They are the color of weak tea and steam faintly. One is warm enough to scald. The workers call it the Kettle and keep their tools out of it.

---

## The Air

The air is so wet it has weight. It sits on the skin, and clothing becomes a second, clinging skin within minutes. Condensation forms on anything cooler than the rock, which means almost nothing. Only the steel of the pump housing is cooler, chilled by the brine moving through it, so the pump drips constantly from every flange and rivet, like something that has just been pulled from a river.

The smell is sulfur first, faint and eggy, from the brine. Under it is hot iron. Under that, and eventually over everything, is ammonia. This smell belongs to the animals.

---

## The Animals

The first thing anyone notices on descending the ladder shaft is that the walls are moving.

The chamber holds cave crickets. They are long-legged, pale, and humpbacked, and there are so many of them that the basalt reads as a shifting grey-brown texture rather than stone. They prefer the upper walls, where it is warmest. They move in slow, general drifts, like a crowd changing position rather than individuals choosing to go somewhere. When a lamp swings toward them, a section of wall the size of a door ripples and resettles.

Below the crickets, in the damp margins near the pools, live cockroaches. They are a heat-loving kind with glossy mahogany shells. They come out of the rubble in their thousands whenever the pump cycles down and the floor vibration eases. They eat the crickets' dead, and they eat anything else.

The ceiling belongs to the swiftlets.

Nobody planned the swiftlets. They found their way down the ventilation bore in the second year and discovered what they had always wanted: darkness, heat, humidity near saturation, and a rough ceiling with endless ledges. They build their nests from their own saliva, small translucent cups glued into the drill scars, and now there are tens of thousands of nests. The birds navigate the dark by clicking, a dry, rapid ticking sound, and when the colony is active the chamber fills with it. It sounds like rain on a tin roof, or like a vast clock with every gear slipping at once.

Twice a day, at dusk and before dawn on the surface, the swiftlets pour up and down the ventilation bore. They go out to hunt insects over the city and come back full. The exodus takes almost forty minutes. During that time the bore roars with wingbeats, and the air in the chamber actually moves. It is the only wind Kessit Deep ever has.

Their droppings fall constantly. The floor beneath the densest roosts is covered in guano a hand-span deep, dark and crumbling. That guano is the base of everything else. The cockroaches eat it. Mites eat the cockroaches' leavings. Pseudoscorpions no bigger than a grain of rice hunt the mites. The crickets graze the fungal mats that grow on the guano's surface. A single square of guano turned over with a trowel reveals more living things than a square of forest soil.

There are also bats, though fewer. A small colony of leaf-nosed bats hangs in the one recess the swiftlets don't favor, an overbreak above the old explosives store. They are quiet by comparison. On still nights, between pump cycles, one can hear them chittering.

---

## What Grows

Almost nothing grows here, because there is almost no light.

The exception is around the work lamps. Kessit Deep has seven of them, sodium lamps in wire cages bolted to the rock at intervals. They burn a thick orange that makes everything the color of rust. Around each lamp, in a rough halo about an arm's length across, the wet rock has turned green. This is lampenflora: algae and cyanobacteria that drifted in on air or boots and found just enough light to photosynthesize. Closer to the bulb, where the light is strongest, a few mosses have taken hold, tiny and bright, and one small fern. The fern grows directly under Lamp Four, and the workers regard it with a protectiveness they would not admit to.

The halos grow a little every year. They are the chamber's slowest clock.

Elsewhere, on the guano, there are fungi: white threads, grey fuzz, and a tall-stalked mold with black heads like pins. They are not plants, and they do not need the lamps.

---

## The Built Things

The pump dominates. It is a vertical line-shaft turbine pump in a housing of riveted steel, three stories tall, rising through the ceiling into a bored shaft. Its motor sits at the top, out of sight. What sits in the chamber is its lower body and the snarl of pipe around it. There are the brine intake, the reinjection line, and the pressure relief manifold. Each is lagged in insulation that was once white and is now streaked brown and grey with droppings. The lagging has split in places, and cockroaches live in the gaps.

The pump runs on a slow cycle, dictated by the heating demand of the city above. In winter it runs almost constantly. In summer it rests for hours. When it runs, it produces a sound more felt than heard: a deep, grinding hum that comes up through the rock and into the soles of the feet, overlaid with the sharper rattle of a bearing that has been "due for replacement" for six years. When it starts after a rest, it makes a single long groan, and every swiftlet in the ceiling goes silent for the space of a held breath before resuming.

Around the pump, the chamber was meant to become a small works. It got as far as a scatter of structures and no further.

- **A control hut**, prefabricated steel, with a single window gone opaque with scale. Inside there is a bank of analog gauges and a telephone that connects to the surface. It still rings about twice a week.
- **A crew shelter**, the same design, with four bunks. The workers who maintain the pump stay here on long shifts. The hut is sealed as tightly as it can be. Even so, the crickets get in, and the workers have stopped minding.
- **A catwalk** of galvanized grating that runs around the pump at the height of its main flange. It is the only surface in the chamber that stays relatively clean, because the grating lets guano fall through.
- **A transformer cage** in one corner, humming at its own higher pitch. It is fenced with chain link, and swiftlets nest along the top of the fence.
- **The old explosives store**, a short blind tunnel with a steel door, now empty and used for tools.
- **The concrete footing** of something never built. It is a rectangular slab, finished and cured, with bolts for a machine that was never delivered. It is now the only truly flat surface on the floor, and it is thick with guano. From above, a narrow dry margin traces its edges. Nobody quite knows what it was meant to hold.

Between these things there is a lot of empty floor: broken rock, pools, dung, and dark. The structures stand apart from each other like the first buildings of a town whose settlers stopped arriving.

---

## The People

There are usually three of them. Sometimes there is one, and never more than five. They come down the ladder shaft in waders and hooded oilskins, carrying hand lamps and respirators they wear only when the ammonia gets bad. They check the pump. They check the gauges. They write in a logbook with a pencil, because ink runs.

Over the years, the crews have developed customs.

You don't sweep. The guano is the floor, and the floor is alive. Scraping it only exposes the rock to scale and ruins the footing.

You don't shine lamps into the roost. You point your lamp downward and to the side. A lamp straight up sends a section of the colony into panic, and panic means droppings.

You don't disturb the fern.

You tap the pump housing before you touch it. The cockroaches take the vibration as a warning and leave the flange you mean to open.

You listen to the swiftlets. Their clicking thins when the air goes bad, when carbon dioxide from the brine builds up in the low spots. The birds notice before the gauges do. When the ceiling goes quiet outside the pump's groan, the crew goes up the ladder and waits.

Twice a year, licensed collectors come down with ladders and thin knives to harvest the swiftlet nests from the lower ledges. The nests are worth a great deal in the markets above. The collectors take only nests the chicks have fledged from, in theory. The crews watch them carefully.

---

## Sound, Across a Day

At rest, with the pump off, the chamber is loud with life. There is the swiftlets' ticking, the dry scrabble of cockroaches over rubble, the drip of condensate, and the transformer's hum.

When the pump starts, it adds a grinding bass that the rock carries, the rattle of the bad bearing, and the hiss from a relief valve that never fully seats.

At the swiftlets' changeover, a roar of wings pours up the bore, and for forty minutes the chamber sounds like a storm.

After the colony leaves at dusk, the chamber is emptier than at any other time. There is only the pump, if it's running, and the crickets, and the bats in their recess. Workers who have been down here for years say this is when they sleep best.

---

## Slow Things

Nothing here changes quickly, except the birds.

The scale rinds grow at the rate of a fingernail a decade. The lampenflora halos spread by a hand-width a year. The guano deepens, and in places it has begun to compact into something that will eventually be rock. The ceiling plates shift by fractions of a millimeter. The engineers measure the gaps once a year and sigh.

The rock is cooling, very slowly. The pump draws heat from the deep aquifer faster than the earth replaces it, and the brine comes up a quarter of a degree cooler each year than the year before. Someday, perhaps in a century, it will no longer be worth pumping. Then the pump will be shut down. The lamps will go out. The halos will fade, and the fern will die.

The swiftlets will stay, and so will the crickets, the cockroaches, and the guano, in a room that will finally have time to become a cave.
