# The Weeping Heights

It begins as a pale line above the clouds, and for a long while that is all it is: a band of bright stone floating over a sea of white. The cloud sea is the first thing you have to understand here, because it is the source of most of the light. The sun stands high and hard in a thin, violet-blue sky, and the clouds below take that light and throw it back up. Everything is lit from two directions at once. At midday nothing has a shadow. Things look pasted onto the air, their edges too sharp, their undersides as bright as their tops. The eye keeps searching for depth and doesn't find it.

The heights are a vast tableland of columnar stone. The columns are packed so tightly that from a distance they read as a single surface, like the top of a honeycomb or the end of a bundle of reeds. They are five-, six- and seven-sided, grey shading to green, some as thin as a wrist and some as broad as a threshing floor. They stand at slightly different heights, so the whole plateau is terraced and stepped in a million small increments, a staircase in every direction at once. Between the columns run seams, some a finger wide and some deep enough to swallow a person. Water moves in all of them.

## The Stone That Weeps

Nothing here is dry. The air at this altitude is thin and cold, a clean, steady cold like the inside of a spring. Moisture condenses on every surface. Each column sweats. Beads form on the polished faces, gather, run, and join into films so thin they are only visible as a shimmer, a slight wrongness in how the stone reflects the double light. The rock is not wet the way a riverbed is wet. It is wet the way skin is wet: from within, continually, as if the stone were breathing out.

Ages of this have shaped the columns. Their tops are cupped into shallow basins, each holding a disc of perfectly still water. Their flanks are fluted with vertical grooves, worn by the running films into something like the folds of a heavy curtain. Where two columns lean together, the water has cut arches between them. The erosion here is old and patient. Every edge is rounded, every surface smoothed to a dull satin. The place does not look damaged or decaying. It looks finished, as if the water and the stone negotiated for a very long time and reached an agreement.

Part of that agreement is strange.

## Which Way Down

On most of the plateau, water behaves as expected. It runs downhill, fills the seams, gathers in the deeper channels, and moves toward the edges of the tableland in thin, fast, braided streams. At the edges, where the columns end and the drop to the cloud sea begins, it pours over the lip in long ribbons.

Some of those ribbons fall. Others rise.

There is no dividing line, no visible rule. At one stretch of cliff the water tips over the edge and drops a thousand meters into the clouds, as it should. Fifty paces along, identical water runs to an identical edge, pauses in a trembling bead at the lip, and then lifts. It climbs into the sky in threads as fine as spider silk, twisting slowly around each other, catching the light, until they are too thin to see. Above the heights, on the brightest days, the sky is full of these threads. They look like scratches on glass.

Water that goes up comes back down, eventually, as rain. But rain here does not always come from clouds. It condenses out of clear sky, out of nothing, in sudden hard showers that drum the column tops for a minute and then stop. Sometimes the rain falls sideways. Sometimes a single column is rained on while its neighbors stay untouched, a narrow gray pillar of falling water hanging over one patch of stone like a thumbprint on the world.

The still pools on the column tops are the strangest thing. Look into one and it does not reflect the sky above it. It reflects some other sky: overcast when the real sky is clear, or at night when it is noon. Some show stars. Some show the underside of the cloud sea, lit gold, as though they looked out from below. A few, the old travelers' marks suggest, reflect the heights themselves from a vantage that doesn't exist, somewhere out in the empty air past the cliff edge, looking back. In those pools you can sometimes see columns that aren't there and channels running in directions they don't run.

## The Sound

The heights are loud, and not with any single noise. They are loud the way a workshop is loud, full of overlapping sounds from many sources.

There is the water, first. Thousands of small streams in thousands of stone seams make a steady hiss that never drops away. Over it comes the drumming of the sudden rains on the basins, a hard, hollow patter, because the columns are resonant. Struck by falling water, they ring. Each has its own pitch, set by its height and girth and the depth of its basin. A shower passing over the plateau plays across the columns like fingers across a hundred instruments at once: a scattered, clanging, bell-like tumble of tones that swells as the rain arrives and thins as it moves on.

Then there is the wind. It comes up from the cloud sea in long surges, pushed by the heat of the lit clouds below, and blows across the fluted column faces and down the narrow seams. The seams sing. Some drone low, a note felt in the teeth more than heard. Others whistle. When a strong gust crosses the whole plateau, the combined sound is enormous: a chord hundreds of voices deep, slightly out of tune with itself, rising and falling on the wind's rhythm. It is less like music than like the plateau clearing its throat.

And some sounds arrive out of order. A column's bark of cracking stone, the heavy crash of a slab breaking free and hitting the seam below, is sometimes heard before it happens. You hear the crack, turn, and see nothing. Then, a breath or a minute or an hour later, the slab falls, silently, its sound already spent. Other sounds come late. On calm evenings the heights sometimes replay the storms of the afternoon, faintly, in the right order, drumming rain and ringing stone on a dry, still plateau. The echo has taken the long way around.

## Nothing Living

No birds come here. No insects hum in the seams. Nothing grazes, nothing hunts, nothing burrows. The absence of life shows most in the silence underneath the noise: all of this sound, and none of it is a voice.

What grows here is mineral. On the wettest faces, where films of water run constantly, pale crusts bloom overnight. They are salts and silica drawn out of the stone and left behind by evaporation, and they grow in patterns eerily like plants: fern fronds, branching coral, the fine radial frills of a mushroom's gills. They are white, faintly green, sometimes rose where the rock holds iron. Each morning the plateau is furred with them. By afternoon the sun and rain have dissolved most of them back into the running water. They regrow the next night in slightly different shapes. Over years, the old marks suggest, certain forms recur: the same spray of crystalline fronds on the same column, the same starburst in the same basin. It isn't life, but the stone seems to remember what it has made.

In the deepest, shadiest seams there is something else, a grey slickness on the stone that might be the simplest kind of film, something bacterial or less than that. It darkens in rain and pales in sun. It is the closest thing here to a living presence, and it is not much.

## The Marks

Someone came here once. Many someones, perhaps, across a long span of time. Their marks are scattered thinly across the heights, a few to a day's walk, never many together.

The marks are simple. There are cairns of flat slabs, stacked by hand on the tops of columns and carefully balanced. There are channels cut into the stone with chisels, and you can see the tool marks, short parallel strokes, patient and a little uneven. There are stone basins shaped and set into the seams, some with lips carved into spouts. There are a few low shelters of stacked slabs, roofless, with hearthstones inside that show no sign of fire. In places, pins of soft reddish metal, beaten copper maybe, are driven into cracks in the stone. They're greened with age, some with frayed stubs of cord still knotted around them. There are tally marks scratched into the column faces in rows of five.

Look long enough and a purpose shows through. The channels mostly run toward the cliff edges where the water falls down. The basins are placed to catch water before it can reach the edges where it rises. The spouts point downward, again and again, sometimes with small weights of stone set on their lips. The cords on the copper pins once ran out over the cliff edge. They were plumb lines, you realize: weighted strings let down into the air to show which way was down.

The builders were trying to teach the water. Or remind it. Or, perhaps, just trying to hold on to some certainty about direction in a place that offered none.

Their marks do not behave either. The same cairn appears in three different places, identical down to a chipped corner on the fourth slab. A channel is cut with the same chisel slip, the same place where the tool skated and gouged, on columns miles apart. The tally marks, when counted, don't agree with themselves; the same row reads differently on different days. One roofless shelter, visited twice from opposite directions, has its doorway facing you both times. It is as if the heights took the things people made and copied them, carefully and a little imperfectly, the same way they copy the mineral fronds. There is no telling which cairn was the first one, or whether any of them are.

## Distance and Return

The tableland is large, a whole region of stepped stone, and from any high column you can see a long way over the terraced surface: the silver threads of streams, the flash of basins, the pale scattered cairns, the ringed edges where the plateau ends in bright air. The far edge curves slightly up at the horizon, as though the heights were the inside of a very shallow bowl.

Walking across it is not straightforward. The columns are packed so densely that every route is a matter of stepping, climbing, and crossing seams, a slow three-dimensional puzzle. Routes loop. A path heading steadily away from a cairn arrives, after an hour, at that cairn, or at one of its copies, there's no telling which. Features seen in the distance are not always where they should be when you reach the place you saw them. Distance here seems to be more a matter of how many columns you cross than how far you go, and the count is not fixed.

## A Day on the Heights

Before dawn the plateau is at its coldest and quietest. Frost rimes the column tops and the mineral fronds stand at their fullest, white and delicate. The streams slow and thicken. The pools show their other skies most clearly in the dark, glowing faintly with borrowed daylight or borrowed stars.

At sunrise the cloud sea below catches light first, flushing gold and then white. The double lighting begins. Warmth rises from the lit clouds, and with it the wind: first gusts, then surges, and the plateau begins to sing. The frost melts, the films run faster, and the upward threads at the cliff edges begin to climb.

By midday the light is almost unbearable, shadowless and total. The mineral fronds dissolve. Sudden showers condense out of clear air and drum across the columns in ringing, clattering waves. The sky above fills with rising threads. The heights are at their loudest now, wind chords and rain bells and the ceaseless hiss of water, all of it overlapping, all of it bouncing between the columns until the direction of any sound is impossible to trace.

In the afternoon the rising water returns. The threads above break into drops, and the drops fall as a fine, sparkling rain that makes rainbows below and above and sometimes curled into full circles around the columns. Slabs crack free of weathered columns and fall into the seams, sometimes heard in advance, sometimes after.

As the sun lowers, the cloud sea dims and the double light fades. Shadows return, long and blue, falling in only one direction again. The wind drops. In the evening stillness the replayed storms sometimes come: soft phantom rain, the ghost-ringing of columns, the sound of the afternoon arriving late. Then the cold returns, the frost forms, the mineral ferns begin to grow, and in the pools the other skies wake up.

## What the Place Is

The heights are not hostile. They are cold but not freezing, wet but not drowning, loud but not violent. The stone is smooth underfoot, the water is clean and very cold, the light is bright and clear. It is, in its way, a peaceful place. Everything in it has been settled for a long time.

But it is settled around a question, and the question has no answer. Down is a local custom here, observed in some places and not others. Sound keeps its own time. Reflections show elsewhere. Things that are made get made again. The plateau is not falling apart; the breakdown of rules is itself old, worn smooth like everything else, part of the landscape's long equilibrium. The builders' channels and plumb lines are the only things here that argue with it, and the heights have quietly absorbed them, copying them among the cairns and the crystal ferns as one more pattern the stone returns to.

Stand at one of the edges at midday, with water rising past you into the sky on one side and falling past you into the clouds on the other, with the columns ringing behind you and the light coming from everywhere at once, and the place makes a kind of sense. It isn't sense you can hold on to. It is closer to the calm of a long argument that neither side needs to win.
