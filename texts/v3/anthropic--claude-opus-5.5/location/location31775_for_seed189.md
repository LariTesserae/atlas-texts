# The Hollow of the Unripe Root

## Finding It

You would never find it from above. The ground there is ordinary: packed clay, a scatter of dry grass, the kind of low hill nobody names. About a body's length beneath it, a root has swollen into something like a tuber, a gourd, or an unopened seed. From the outside it is the size of a sleeping ox, its rind ridged and green-bronze and warm to the touch like a flank. If you press your ear to it, you hear roaring.

The only way in is a split. The rind has cracked along one seam, recently. The edges are still bright and wet, pale as the inside of an apple, with fibers sticking out stiff as bristles. The opening is barely wide enough to turn your shoulders through. The place is young. It has been open to the air for days, maybe weeks. Nothing in it has had time to soften.

## The Interior

Inside there is almost nothing.

That is the first thing, and it takes a long moment to understand. You expect pulp, seeds, the wet crowded dark of a fruit. What you find is a hollow, and the hollow is too large. The walls curve away from you in every direction, and they are farther off than the rind you just squeezed through could possibly allow. The ox-sized gourd holds a space you could lose a village in. There are no pillars and no floor debris. There is only a curved plant-wall enclosing a volume of warm, faintly gold, nearly empty air.

The emptiness is what makes it feel full. The plant is everything: the floor, the sky, the horizon. There is no stone, no soil, no water that isn't held in tissue or beading off it. You are inside a single organism, and it has made a room of itself. It didn't build around the space. It grew the space the way a lung grows its own interior.

## The Walls

Up close, the inner surface looks like fresh-cut melon crossed with a cathedral wall. It is a membrane of translucent cells, each the size of a thumbnail, packed in hexagons and faintly luminous. Below that layer you can see the next, and below that the next, receding into green-gold depth like looking into a clear river. Ribs of darker vascular tissue run through it in branching courses, some as thin as thread and some as wide as a trunk. They converge toward a region of the ceiling far overhead.

Because the hollow is new, the walls are sharp. Where the tissue has pulled apart during growth, there are ridges and knife-edged flanges of translucent flesh, and sheets of membrane stretched thin as drumskin across small side-pockets. Starch has crystallized along some seams into white glittering crusts, crisp as frost, and it cuts your fingers if you're careless. Nothing has eroded, because there is no weather yet to erode it.

There is weather coming.

## The Light

The light is ordinary daylight, and that is the wrong thing about it.

It is clear, neutral, mid-morning light, the kind that makes colors true. It doesn't flicker and doesn't come from any point. It seeps out of the walls. The plant took in sunlight at the surface through leaves you never saw, and somewhere in its chemistry it stored that light rather than spending it. Now it releases it here, below the ground, into a room that was never meant to be seen.

Stay long enough and you notice the light carries weather. Sometimes it dims as if a cloud has crossed a sun that isn't there. Sometimes it goes long and amber like late afternoon, even when you came in at dawn. Once, briefly, it turns the cold blue-white of noon in a season the land above hasn't had in a very long time, if ever. The plant is replaying days: light from before it was a plant, from before the hill was a hill. There is no pattern to which day comes next.

## The Sound

It is loud in here. Not a painful loudness, but constant, the kind that lives in your ribs.

The roar you heard through the rind is the plant drinking. Water moves through the great vascular ribs in surges, pulled upward from roots that go down somewhere you can't follow. Each surge comes as a deep rushing through the walls, like standing behind a waterfall you can't see. Under that is a slower sound, a creak, then a groan, then a sharp report like green wood splitting. That is growth. Whole panels of the wall shift by a finger's width at a time. The hollow is still expanding, and each expansion announces itself as a crack that rolls around the curved space and comes back to you many times.

The echoes are wrong too. If you speak, the hollow answers, but not always with your words. It returns your voice shaped into something else: a word you were going to say, a word you almost said yesterday. Sometimes it sounds like someone else speaking your words, a little older. Most people who come here learn to stay quiet, not out of fear but because the answers are hard to stop listening to.

And sometimes a sound arrives before its cause. You hear a sharp splitting crack from a wall, and a breath or two later the wall actually splits exactly there. The hollow is loud with its own near future.

## The Water

This is a damp place, not a wet one. Water is present but scarce, and it moves in cycles.

The warmth, like a body at rest or a summer afternoon, draws moisture out through the walls. It condenses on the membranes in beads, then in slow sheets that gather along the lower curves. Some of it pools in shallow basins where the floor dips: small clear puddles, green-tinted, warm as bathwater, faintly sweet with sugar. They don't last. The walls drink them back within hours.

Every so often, and more often lately, it rains inside. Condensed water gathers high up where the veins converge and falls in a thin, slow drizzle. The drops fall too slowly. They drift like seeds in still air, taking the time a feather would, and they fall straight regardless of where you stand, as if "down" in this hollow means toward the center of the plant rather than toward the center of the earth. When they land on the sharp new starch crusts, they soften them. This is the first erosion, the beginning of the hollow's aging. In another hundred years the knife-edges will be rounded, and the white frost will be wet paste.

## What Else Lives Here

Almost nothing. It is a pure-plant world, and the plant permits very little else.

There is one resident: a creature the pale brown of dry paper, about the size of a hand, shaped something like a moth and something like a seed husk. It has no mouth that anyone has found. It clings to the walls near the great veins, and when the sap surges it shivers in time with the roar, its wings trembling as if it feeds on the sound itself. There may be a dozen of them in the whole hollow. They are hard to see and harder to count, because when you look away and back, one is sometimes in two places. Or two of them are the same one, seen at different moments.

Very rarely something larger comes. You hear it before you see it: slow, heavy footfalls across the membrane, too far away to have made a sound you could hear, and yet you hear them clearly. Whatever makes them keeps to the far curve of the hollow, where the distance is greatest and least reliable. Nobody has gotten close. The moth-things go still when it passes. The plant's roar drops to a murmur, like a room of people noticing someone at the door.

## The Traces

Someone has been here, or will be.

Near the entrance, pressed into the wall, is a flint blade: knapped, pale grey, a good working edge, the kind of tool meant for cutting fiber. The wall has grown around it. Fresh tissue has closed over the hilt so that only the edge shows, like a stone in a riverbank. That shouldn't be possible. The hollow is only weeks old, and the tissue is layered as if the blade has been there for years.

Farther in, a cord of twisted plant fiber is knotted around a thin rib of vascular tissue. It is well made, three-ply, tight. The knot isn't one you know. The cord is new. The ends are still bright and unfrayed. It was tied recently, or it hasn't been tied yet and is only waiting.

Beside the largest of the shallow pools, three stones are stacked on the membrane floor. There is no stone anywhere else in this hollow, and no way for stone to have come here except in a hand. The stack is small, knee-high, carefully balanced, with the flattest on top. On the top stone is a smear of dried pigment, red-brown. It could be a handprint. It could be the size of your hand.

These are the only made things in the place. They don't feel like intrusions. They feel like part of the plant's answer, the way the echoes are.

## The Cycle

The hollow is active, and it is moving toward something.

Every day it is a little larger inside, though the rind outside doesn't grow. The walls split, creak, and extend. The veins thicken. The rain comes more often. The light replays more days, faster, sometimes several in an hour, so that shadows swing around the walls like the hands of an agitated clock. The sap-roar builds toward something like a pulse. By day it is irregular. By what counts as night it slows into a rhythm, deep and even, like a very large heart falling asleep.

Those who have spent a long time here, if anyone has, might say the hollow isn't empty so much as reserved. This is where the plant will one day put the thing it is growing toward. It isn't a seed. It is some fuller, stranger future self. The space is the shape of that future, grown in advance and held open, a negative of what's to come. The light and the sounds leak backward from that future. The blade and the cord and the stones may have come from it too. They were left by someone who arrived after the hollow filled, reached back into the time when it was still empty, and set these things down for whoever came first.

## Leaving

You leave the way you came, through the bright split seam. Your shoulders scrape against bristling fiber. You come up into the ordinary clay and the dry grass and a real sky, which by comparison seems very small.

Behind you the ground hums. If you lay your ear to the hill, you can still hear it: the roar, the crack of growth, and faintly the echo of your own voice saying something you haven't said yet.
