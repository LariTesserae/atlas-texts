# The Hollow at the Top of the Stroke

## Approach

You come up out of the cloud and find a hollow, cupped high on the shoulder of a mountain that has not had time to become a mountain. The rock is new. It has the look of something broken open this morning: black basalt split into facets so clean they still hold a faint glassy sheen, edges sharp enough to open a palm. No talus has gathered at the base of the walls. Nothing has weathered, slumped, or softened. The fracture lines are crisp as a diagram.

The hollow is small, perhaps three hundred paces across, a bowl you could shout across if shouting worked here. It is bounded on three sides by those fresh walls and on the fourth by the drop you climbed, where the cloud sits level and white like the surface of a liquid that has never been disturbed.

The cloud does not move. You will notice this first, and later you will understand that it is the key to everything else.

## The Sound

Before your eyes adjust, you hear the place.

It is loud: a deep, ceaseless mechanical sound, the voice of a working engine house. Under everything is a long compressive *thrum*, like a piston stroke that has been pulled out to the length of a held breath. Over it runs a quick ratcheting tick, a sound like belts slapping over pulleys, the whine of something spinning at speed, and now and then a hiss like steam venting from a relief valve.

There is no engine. There is no pulley, no belt, no valve. You can walk every part of the hollow and find nothing made, nothing fitted, nothing anyone has laid a hand to. The noise comes out of the place itself: out of the rock, the water, the moss. Press your ear to the basalt and the thrum is inside it, deep and even. Put your hand in a pool and you feel the tick through your fingers.

The sound never rises or falls. It never pauses and never builds. After an hour you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as ground.

## Light

The light is dim, a blue-grey dusk that seems to have no source. There is no sun behind the cloud and no brighter patch of sky. The light has no gradient either. The shadows under the overhangs are exactly as deep as they were when you arrived, and they point nowhere, because nothing is casting them from any particular direction. They pool beneath things the way water pools.

In the deeper clefts there is another light: a faint violet crawling along the seams of the rock, the color of an electrical arc seen through smoked glass. It gives off a thin ozone smell, sharp and clean. Where it runs, the moss grows thickest.

## Water

Everything is wet, though it is not raining and has never rained. The walls weep. Water beads out of the basalt itself, from pores too fine to see, and gathers in lines down the faces of the stone, bright and cold. It collects in shallow basins cut into the floor of the hollow, pools with perfectly sharp rims, each one full precisely to the brim and not a drop over.

On the northern wall there is a waterfall, and it is the strangest thing here. It is roaring, loud as a mill-race, and it is not falling. The water hangs in a white sheet from lip to base, frozen mid-motion, every droplet in the spray suspended where it was. It has not iced over. It is liquid, cold and wet to the touch; your fingers come away soaked. It is simply not going anywhere. The roar comes out of it anyway, full and continuous, the sound of a thousand tons of water per minute that are not moving at all.

The air is saturated almost to fog. Condensation forms on your sleeves, your eyelashes, the hair on your arms. It never drips. It forms and holds.

## Vegetation

For all the cold (and it is cold, a raw mountain chill that sits in the joints), the hollow is lush.

Moss covers every surface that is not sheer, in deep cushions the green of old copper. Look closely and you can see fine metallic filaments running through it, brassy threads woven into the living tissue like wire in insulation. The moss is warm to the touch, faintly, the way a working machine is warm. When the violet light crawls near, the filaments brighten.

Ferns grow in the clefts, huge ones, fronds taller than a person, unrolling from fiddleheads coiled as tight and regular as springs. Each frond is ribbed along its length with thin hard ridges like the teeth of a gear rack. When you brush one, the ridges click against your hand in perfect sequence.

There are a few trees, perhaps seven in the whole hollow, spaced well apart: dwarf conifers with bark in overlapping plates like riveted iron, rooted directly into the new rock. Their needles are dark and wet. Their cones are small, dense, and heavy, machined-looking, each scale identical. If you pick one up and turn it in your hand you can hear something spinning inside it, a high fine whine, a flywheel the size of a seed.

Between these presences there is space: bare black stone, open pools, the wide still floor of the hollow. Nothing crowds. The plants hold their distances the way parts in a well-designed mechanism hold their tolerances.

## Fauna

Here, finally, is movement, and there is a great deal of it.

The hollow teems. Beetles the size of a thumb, bodies lacquered green-black, travel the moss in endless lines. Their wing-cases open on hinges and underneath the wings spin rather than beat: little rotors, humming. They fly short fixed routes from fern to stone to fern and back.

Small birds, dark-feathered and neat, work the walls in pairs. Their calls are the ratcheting tick you heard coming up: a sound like a pawl dropping over a toothed wheel, *tk-tk-tk-tk*, precise and metallic. They pick at the weeping water on the stone and fly on and pick again.

In the pools there are eels, pale and thin as cable, coiled in slow loops. When they pass close to one another the water between them flickers violet, and you can feel a faint prickle in the air, a current jumping. The pools are full of them. Every pool.

There are larger animals too, sparse among the rest. There are goats with horns that spiral in exact, even pitches, like screw threads, standing on ledges no wider than their hooves. There are marmots whose whistles are pure steam-hiss. There is something low and long that moves through the ferns and that you only ever see as a ripple in the fronds, accompanied by the long thrum, as if the deep sound under everything were briefly walking.

Watch any one of these creatures for long enough and you begin to see it. The beetle crossing the moss in front of you takes a route, and the route closes on itself. It comes back to the point it started from, at the same angle, at the same speed, and goes around again. The bird picks at the same bead of water, flies the same arc, lands in the same place. The goat shifts its weight, lifts its head, lowers it, and shifts its weight, lifts its head, lowers it.

Every animal in the hollow is running a loop. The loops are different lengths. Some take seconds and some take what feels like an hour. But none of them opens. None of them goes anywhere new. The hollow is crowded with motion, and none of the motion adds up to change.

## The Rules

This is what the place is: an engine that was never built, caught at the top of its stroke and held there.

Nothing here wears. The basalt keeps its fresh-broken edges because no time passes on them. The waterfall is loud because it is always in the instant of falling, and it is still because that instant never gives way to the next. The pools are full to the brim and never overflow, because whatever enters them in one moment leaves in the same one. The fog on your sleeve does not drip because the droplet is always forming.

The animals are the only exception, and not truly one. They are the working parts. Their loops are the cycles of the machine, the rotations and reciprocations that keep the stroke held. They turn so that nothing else has to. If you stood very still, you might believe the whole mechanism depends on the beetle's circuit closing, the bird's arc returning, the eels passing and passing each other in the pools, making their small violet sparks.

The place produces something, you are fairly sure. The sound is the sound of production, of scaled and mechanized making. But nothing accumulates. There is no output, no pile, no product, no waste. Whatever is made is made in the same instant it is used. The hollow is a closed system at perfect equilibrium: all effort, no result, no gradient anywhere to let energy leak out into the world.

Reality is thin here in exactly the way a held breath is thin. Sound exists without motion. Wetness exists without flow. Warmth runs through moss that has never grown, because it has always already been grown. A cone in your palm contains a spinning flywheel that has never started spinning and will never stop.

## Leaving

You can leave. The cloud at the lip of the hollow will take your foot like any cloud, and the slope below is ordinary slope. But going down, you will notice that the drops of water on your sleeves have not fallen. They will stay there, beaded and bright, halfway through forming, for the rest of the day.

And for a long while afterward, in quiet rooms, you will hear it faintly: the long thrum, the ratchet, the roar of water that does not fall. It is the sound of a machine that nobody made, running without any change at the top of a mountain.
