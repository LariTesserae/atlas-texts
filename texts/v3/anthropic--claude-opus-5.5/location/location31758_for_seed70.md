# The Held Sea of the Uplands

## The Approach

From the dry lowlands the range looks as if it is wearing its sky too low. The foothills are ordinary: tawny scree, the dust of recent rockfall, low thorny scrub growing from cracks that have not yet had time to soften. But about a third of the way up, where the slopes steepen into black ridgelines, the air ends. A horizontal plane of water hangs there, flat as a tabletop, and it runs the length of the range and past the horizon in both directions.

The mountains rise into it. So does everything above that line: the peaks, the high valleys, the saddles and cirques. They are all submerged in a warm, clear sea that sits on top of the land instead of beneath it.

From below, the underside of the water is the strangest thing to look at. It is a ceiling that isn't quite still, a skin of silver-green that breathes. Light comes through it softened and moving, so the foothills are dappled like a riverbed. In places long threads of water hang down from the surface, each one a few fingers thick, falling so slowly that they look like glass rods. They never quite reach the ground. At some point in their descent they bead, separate, and drift back upward like released bubbles.

## The Line

Climbing to the waterline is like reaching the edge of a pond from underneath. The rock here is basalt so young it still has the glassy skin it cooled with. It has been faulted into shelves and knife-edges that cut boots and palms. Nothing here has been rounded yet. The fractures are clean and the angles are raw, and slabs lie where they fell, their broken faces bright.

At the boundary the rock is wet on its upper side and dry below. A band of pale mineral crust, a few centimeters thick, marks the line. It is laid down over what must be decades as the water's edge creeps a little up or down. If you press your hand upward through the surface, it passes into warmth. The water is the temperature of skin, maybe slightly more. It resists a little, heavier than it should be, as if it has more reason than water usually does to stay where it is.

This is the first rule of the place: **the water is held.** Nobody knows by what. It does not pour down the mountainsides. It fills the high country the way air fills a room, and it keeps its lower surface level across valleys and passes, ignoring the gravity that pulls at everything else. Rocks that break loose in the drowned uplands tumble slowly down through it. When they reach the underside they drop out into the air and fall the rest of the way at ordinary speed, trailing a brief rain.

## Inside

Above the line, the uplands are a forest.

The first thing you notice is how full it is. There is no open ground. Every ledge and crack in the new basalt has been colonized. Ribbon-plants anchor in fissures and stream upward in dense sheaves, dark green shading to bronze at the tips, each one several times the height of a person. Between them grow broad paddle-leaves on flexible stems. They turn their faces slowly toward the brightness, and they overlap so completely that the slopes look shingled. Wherever a surface is left, it wears a velvet of short fronds, moss-soft and rust-colored, that closes over any footprint within a day.

The forest grows in layers. Close to the rock is the velvet, and above it the paddles. Above those the ribbons rise and sway, and above the ribbons, in the high open water over the valleys, float the canopies.

These canopies are the landscape's great feature. They are colonies of something between plant and float, masses of green bladders the size of fists or of houses, bound together by fine root-threads. They drift at fixed heights above the ground like clouds, but closer and more deliberate. Each is tethered to the slopes below by hundreds of hair-thin lines that glint when the light catches them, so that every valley is strung with a faint vertical rigging. The canopies filter the sunlight into green. Directly under a big one it is dusk. Between them, shafts of ordinary daylight come down clean and gold, and the forest floor is striped with them. The stripes shift by only an arm's width across an hour.

The vegetation is lush in a way that feels almost pressurized, as if it has too much of everything it needs: warmth, light, and minerals leaching fresh from rock that has not yet given them up. The plants exhale oxygen in tiny silver beads that cling to leaf-undersides by the thousands and occasionally let go all at once. Then a whole slope shimmers, and a slow upward fizz rises past you toward the surface far above.

## The Upper Surface

Nobody sees the top of the sea without swimming up through several hundred meters of it. It lies well above the highest peaks, and there the water meets actual sky. The surface is calm, stirred only by the gentlest swell, because no wind can find much purchase on a sea hanging in the air like this.

From there the whole range lies below, dark ridges netted in green, the canopies floating like lily pads in the mid-depth. Beyond the edges of the held water, the lower world is visible, dry and brown and very far down. The view makes plain what the place is: a mountain range that shouldered up out of an ancient warm sea and brought a piece of the sea with it, and the sea never let go.

## Sound

It is quiet. The water mutes everything, and there is little here to make noise.

What sound there is arrives through the body more than the ears. There is a low pressure-hum that seems to come from the rock itself, fluctuating over hours. It may be the basalt still settling, still cooling somewhere deep, ticking as it contracts. Now and then comes a single sharp crack, very far off, as a new fracture opens or a slab gives way. It is followed long afterward by a soft, drawn-out rumble as the loosened stone drifts down through the water and finally breaks out of the underside into air.

Closer by, when a colony releases its oxygen, there is a faint crackling fizz like carbonated water poured into a glass. When a big canopy shifts against its tethers, the lines give a thin, almost musical creak, one long note held and fading. That is the loudest living sound in the uplands, and it may come once or twice a day.

## Fauna

Animals are rare, and the few that live here seem almost incidental to the forest, as if the place has not fully decided to allow them.

Glass-snails graze the velvet on the rock faces. They are transparent, the size of a thumbnail, and slow enough that their trails are visible as cleaned lines only after several days. Among the ribbons live small pale fish-like things with very large eyes. They hang motionless between the fronds and turn their whole bodies to watch anything that moves, but they never approach.

There are stories of something larger. It is broad and flat, gray-green, and wider than a canopy colony. It passes through the open water above the valleys at a pace so leisurely that it takes most of an afternoon to cross from ridge to ridge. It is seen very rarely, and never close. Its shadow moves over the forest like a cloud's, and the paddle-leaves below it slowly close and then, after it has gone, slowly open again.

That is nearly all. Whole days pass in the high valleys with nothing moving but the plants and the light.

## Time

Everything here happens slowly.

The lower surface of the sea shifts over years, not days. The mineral crust at the waterline records a slow rise and fall, a tide with a period no one has measured, perhaps a decade or more. When the water drops, a narrow band of forest is left in the air. It does not die immediately. It hangs limp and dripping, turning brown at the edges, and the upward-falling droplets from the underside keep it barely alive until the sea returns.

The canopies grow by a bladder or two a season. The glass-snails live, it is said, longer than people. The rock is young, but it is young on the scale of mountains, which means it will stay raw and sharp for a very long time before the patient work of the water and the roots begins to round it off.

There is a single seasonal event, if it can be called that. Once a year, in the warmest weeks, the canopies all release their seed together. It rises in fine drifting clouds, amber-colored, and for several days the entire held sea is hazed with gold, as though filled with pollen-light. The shafts of sun between the canopies become solid-looking beams. Then the seed settles and vanishes into the velvet, and the water clears again.

## The Rule Underneath

The deeper you go into the uplands, the more it seems that the water is not really being held against its nature. It is the landscape's own element, and the forest, the rock, and the rare slow animals all assume it. The strangeness belongs to the dry world below, where the water runs out and falls and cannot keep its shape.

Up here the sea stays where it was when the mountains lifted it: warm, green, full of light, and very still.
