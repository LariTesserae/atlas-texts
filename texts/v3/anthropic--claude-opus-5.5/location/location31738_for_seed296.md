# The Grus Crown and the Town of Kell

## The Mountain That Was

It was once the highest thing in the world. Nobody on it doubts this, though no one can prove it. The evidence is in the shape. The summit of the massif is not a peak but a dome, a vast rounded shoulder of stone several kilometres across, lifted so far above the lowlands that the air thins and the sky, on the rare clear hours, goes the dark blue of deep water. Mountains this high are supposed to be young and sharp. This one is neither. It has been worn so long that it has forgotten how to have edges.

The stone is granite, or was. Over a span of time the town's geologists speak of only in exhausted round numbers, water has worked into every grain boundary and rotted the rock from the inside. They call it **grus**. It looks solid, grey and pink, speckled with black mica. Press a thumb into an outcrop and it gives, crumbling to coarse gritty sand that runs through the fingers like dry sugar. The whole dome is made of this, hundreds of metres deep: a mountain of stone that has already become sand but has not yet been told.

Scattered across the surface stand the **corestones**, the last unrotted hearts of the granite. They are enormous rounded boulders, some the size of houses, some the size of churches, left behind as the rotten stone around them washed away. They sit on the dome like loaves set out to cool. Some balance on points no wider than a table. Some are stacked three high, and the stacks lean. In places they are so dense that the ground between them is a maze of damp corridors a shoulder's width wide, roofed with moss where the boulders nearly kiss overhead.

The town is built among them.

## The Cloud Tide

The weather on the Crown has a rhythm so regular that the people of Kell tell time by it more readily than by clocks.

Dawn is the clear hour. Cloud that sat on the dome through the night drains downhill as the lowland air cools and sinks, pouring off the edges of the plateau in slow white falls that can be watched from the rim, cataracts of vapour spilling into the valleys below without a sound. For an hour, sometimes two, the Crown stands above a sea of cloud in raking, thin, cold sunlight. Every wet surface flashes. Frost from the night sits in the moss, melting from the tips inward. This is the only time the town has shadows.

By mid-morning the lowlands warm, and the cloud begins to climb back. It comes up the flanks as a rising grey wall, and when it crests the rim, the light goes. From then until the next dawn, Kell lives inside the cloud: a dim, pearl-coloured, directionless light, as if the whole world were lit from inside a sheet of paper. Distances collapse to thirty metres, then twenty. Corestones loom and dissolve. The air is not raining. It is simply full of water that has not decided to fall, and everything it touches grows beads.

On some afternoons the cloud arrives charged. These are the heavy days, when the lowland storms have been building beneath, and the cloud that climbs the Crown carries their electricity with it. Hair lifts from the back of the neck. The tips of iron railings fizz faintly. At dusk on the heaviest days, a pale blue fire crawls along every wire in the town, and the whole of Kell hums.

## The Harp

The town depends on that cloud for its water and its power, and it has built an instrument to take both.

Strung across the dome in long parallel ranks are the **nets**: kilometres of fine iron and copper wire stretched between tall lattice pylons, in sheets like the strings of some enormous harp laid on its side. The locals call the whole array exactly that. The cloud drifts through the Harp constantly, and the wires comb water from it. Droplets gather, merge, and run down the strings into gutters at the base, then into channels cut in the grus, and down into cisterns lined with glazed tile. On a thick night the Harp drips like a forest after rain, a steady soft patter along every rank.

The wires also bleed the cloud of its charge. Each rank is grounded through banks of tall glass-and-foil accumulators housed in squat brick sheds, and from these the town draws its current. It is not a steady supply. It swells in the afternoon, peaks at dusk, and sags toward dawn. On heavy days the accumulators overfill and must be vented through spark gaps. They crack like rifle shots, far off in the fog, and each crack briefly lights the cloud violet. On thin days the town rations. Lamps in Kell are dim at the best of times, amber filaments glowing inside frosted glass. On the thinnest nights they pulse faintly with the breathing of the weather.

Every pylon is fixed to its wires by white ceramic insulators, thousands of them, ribbed like bells or seashells. They are the most important objects on the mountain. They are also what the town makes.

## The Works

Rotten granite leaves behind two things: the quartz sand of the grus, and in the deeper pockets, where the feldspar has gone all the way down, **kaolin**, soft white clay that smells of rain on stone.

Kell was built to dig it. The town's industry is porcelain: insulators first, then everything else. Pits are cut into the dome where the white clay lies thickest, shallow terraced bowls whose walls slump and must be shored with timber. The clay is washed in long settling troughs fed by the Harp's water, pressed, aged in dark cellars, thrown and moulded, and fired in electric kilns that draw on the dusk surge. The kilns are the warmest places on the mountain. Workers sleep near them in winter. At night, the glowing inspection ports of the kiln rows are the brightest things for kilometres, small orange eyes in the fog.

The finished porcelain leaves the Crown by funicular: a single steep track that descends the eastern flank in a straight line and vanishes into the cloud below. Its cable is wound by an electric winding-house at the top. The cars go down loaded with crates of white insulators, cups, tile, and laboratory ware. They come back with coal, iron, grain, and glass. The ride takes most of an hour. For most of it, the car is inside grey nothing.

Here is the thing everyone in Kell knows and no one quite says aloud: every crate that goes down the funicular is a piece of the mountain leaving. The Crown has been eroding for longer than there has been life to watch it. Now the town is helping. The pits deepen year by year. The dome lowers by a hair. Somewhere below, in cities they will never visit, the Crown is being drunk from, and wired into, and set on shelves.

## The Moss

If the town is slowly eating the mountain, the moss is eating the town, and much faster.

The Crown is lush in the only way a place this high and cold can be. Nothing is tall. The richness runs sideways and downward, in cushions and mats and hanging curtains. The ground between corestones is a deep, spongy, saturated carpet of moss, a dozen species in bands of colour: rust-red, lime, bottle-green, a grey so pale it looks frosted even in summer. Step off a path and the foot sinks to the ankle, and water wells up cold around it. Cushion plants form hard, dense domes the size of sheep, so tightly packed a person can stand on them. Low shrubs with small leathery leaves, bronze on their undersides, grow in the lee of boulders. In the stagnant pools that fill every hollow of the grus, carnivorous plants lift sticky red rosettes to the fog. The soil here is only rotten stone. It has nothing to offer, so they eat what drifts into them.

Everything the cloud touches, lichen claims. It hangs from the Harp in long grey-green beards that sway in the drifting fog. It crusts the pylons in orange and chartreuse. It grows on windowpanes and lampposts, on the iron wheels of ore carts left standing a week, on the shoes of anyone careless enough to leave them outside.

The moss grows at a pace that seems wrong, and it is the town's first strangeness. In the constant wet and the constant charge, a bare stone step will carry a green film in a fortnight and a cushion thick enough to lose a coin in by midsummer. The people of Kell scrape. Every household owns long-handled iron scrapers. Every street has a crew. The scrapings are carted to the rim and tipped over the edge in soft green avalanches that tumble into the cloud.

The scraping never quite wins. So Kell grows the other way: up.

## The Layered Town

Kell is dense, and it is deep. Buildings of brick and hauled timber crowd between the corestones, leaning on them, notched into them, bridging the gaps between them, so close that neighbours can pass bread from window to window across a lane. Where the boulders make natural walls the houses use them: a room might have one side of rough granite, beaded with condensation and furred with moss, and three sides of plastered brick.

Moss, washed grus, and the scrapers' spill slowly raise the ground. Over generations, what were ground floors become cellars, then crawlspaces, then sealed rooms entirely. The front doors of the old quarter open onto what used to be second-storey balconies. Stairwells lead down into dim, dripping, abandoned floors where the air smells of wet stone and old iron. Beneath them lie older floors still, filled solid with compacted moss, and the town's children dare each other to follow them. Some streets are canyons of four storeys in which only the top two are lived in. Beneath the newest quarter, the earliest Kell is thought to lie entirely buried: a whole town of rooms packed with moss and silt. Every so often a pit crew cutting for clay breaks into one. They find a hearth, a clock, a porcelain cup of the old pattern, preserved perfectly in the cold and the dark.

Across the upper levels runs a second town of walkways, iron catwalks and roofed wooden bridges joining building to building and corestone to corestone. Along them run the wires: current from the accumulator sheds, drawn in looping, sagging bundles, bearded with lichen, dripping. In the cloud, the town looks less like a place than a web: pale lamps strung on dark threads, hanging in grey.

## Sound

Kell is quiet. The moss is the reason. It absorbs everything. A shout dies ten metres out. Footsteps on cushioned lanes make no sound at all. Metal dropped on a mossy step lands with a soft *thup* and is gone. People speak low without meaning to, because a raised voice feels like an intrusion here, and because it carries nowhere anyway.

What remains is small and constant. The Harp drips along every rank, a patter so steady it becomes silence. Condensation runs down pipes and ticks into cisterns. The kilns creak and ping as they cool. The funicular cable murmurs over its sheaves, a low rolling note that rises when a car is climbing. When the wind comes, and it does come, the wires of the Harp sing: a long, thin, unresolved chord that shifts with the gusts and can be heard across the whole dome, the only sound that travels here.

Then the discharges. The crack of the spark gaps on heavy days. On the heaviest, the hum: a deep, felt vibration that seems to come from the ground as much as the wires, as if the whole Crown had a note and the charged cloud was drawing a bow across it.

## The Rare Animals

Few animals live on the Crown, and the town notices each one.

There is a bird, slate-grey, the size of a thrush, that nests inside the hollow bells of the insulators, and nowhere else anyone has found. No one knows where it nested before the Harp. It forages alone in the fog along the wires, picking at the beetles in the lichen beards, and it calls rarely, a single bright two-note whistle that is startling in the muffled air. The pylon crews will not disturb a nest. An insulator with a bird in it is left in place even if it cracks.

There are pale beetles in the moss, and translucent slugs as long as a hand that graze the lichen from windowpanes at night and leave silver trails the lamplight catches. There are small dark spiders whose webs, strung between corestones, fill with dew each night until they sag like fishing nets, a minor, living echo of the Harp.

And there is something larger that is seen perhaps twice a year: a shaggy, slow, long-legged grazer, moss-coloured, which comes up from the flanks on the thickest nights and moves through the outer ranks of the Harp browsing on the lower cushions. It never enters the streets. People who have seen one close speak of the smell, wet wool and green, and of how it stood perfectly still when a lamp was raised, until it could not be told from a boulder. Then it was simply gone.

## The Moving Crown

For all its age and softness, the Crown is never still. It is only slow in some places and fast in others.

The cloud comes and goes twice a day. The charge rises and falls. The moss advances and the scrapers push it back. Frost heaves the grus on winter nights, lifting paving stones, tilting fences, loosening corestones by a fraction. In spring the meltwater finds new channels, and the rotten flanks of the dome let go in slow flows of wet sand that pour off the rim in fans. Now and then a whole balanced boulder lets go too, rolls with a long soft grinding the moss cannot fully swallow, and goes over the edge into the cloud. Storms from the lowlands rise beneath the town and throw lightning upward into the Harp. The pits deepen. The ground rises under the town even as the mountain lowers beneath it.

The people of Kell hold two ideas about their place at the same time, and are at peace with both. The first is that the Crown is the oldest, most finished thing in the world, worn to its last shape and waiting only to become sand. The second is that it is alive: growing, breathing its cloud, humming on heavy nights, slowly swallowing their houses into itself and keeping them.

On clear mornings, in the hour before the tide comes back, you can stand at the rim and see the whole of it at once: the white dome, the black ranks of the Harp glittering with water, the tangle of roofs and catwalks between the grey loaves of the corestones, lamps still burning pale in the daylight, all of it resting on a mountain made of dissolving stone above a sea of cloud. Then the grey wall rises over the edge, and the light goes soft, and the dripping begins again.
