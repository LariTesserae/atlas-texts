# The Precipitate

## The Ground

Start with the ground, because nothing here is older than it, and it is not old.

Kneel anywhere in the city and press your palm flat. The stone is warm. Not hot, but warmer than the air, like the flank of a sleeping animal. It is a pale, porous gray-green, a kind of tuff that never erupted from anything. Break a crumb between your fingers and it is still faintly damp inside, gritty and soft as stale bread. In a week it will be hard enough to ring when struck. In a month it will hold a house. Tomorrow, the patch of ground you are kneeling on may not be here at all.

The land is low, barely above the grey tidal water to the east, where the old coast used to be. It is so low that on the flattest days the horizon and the ground seem to meet in a single seam of fog. There are no hills, only swellings: long soft ridges where the stone was laid thick, and shallow troughs where it was laid thin and has already begun to sag back toward the water table. Nothing has had time to erode. There are no gullies, no river-cut valleys, no weathered faces. Every edge is either crisp and new or slumped, like a cake taken too early from the oven. Rain does not carve channels here. It is absorbed, drunk down into the pores, and returned hours later as a warm mist rising from the pavement.

The whole region, perhaps forty miles of it running up the old estuary, is only about sixty years old. Before that it was sea.

## The Condensers

They made it from the air.

Stand at almost any intersection and look up, and within a few streets you will see one: a Condenser. They are the tallest things in the flatland, black lattice towers wrapped in miles of fine copper mesh, rising into the permanent low overcast so that their tops are always lost. The cold sea-fog moves inland in slow, heavy rivers, pushes through the meshes, and something happens there that the engineers explain in several contradictory ways. The fog comes out the other side thinner and colder. The mesh weeps a grey slurry, and the slurry runs down the tower legs into settling basins, cures, and swells.

Air becomes mud. Mud becomes stone. Stone becomes city.

The process gives off heat, and that is the secret of everything else here. The air is cold, a raw, bone-damp cold just above freezing for most of the year. On clear winter nights, when those come, frost rimes the older rooftops. But the new ground radiates warmth for months after it is laid. The city floats on a thin, spreading skin of that borrowed heat, and everything alive has arranged itself around it.

The Condensers are always being moved. They run on wide-gauge rails, and a tower that stood at the end of your street in spring may be three districts away by autumn, dragged by teams of silent electric tractors to wherever the Survey has decided the land must grow next. Where they leave, the ground stops rising and begins, slowly, to settle.

## The Quiet

The first thing a visitor notices, after the warmth underfoot, is the silence.

This is an industrial city of perhaps two million people. It is crammed wall to wall with foundries, rail yards, spinning sheds, and tram lines, and it makes almost no noise.

The fresh stone is the reason. Until it cures, the precipitate is acoustically fragile. A sharp sound at the right pitch, such as a shout, a hammer blow on iron, or the shriek of an unoiled wheel, can set up a resonance in the green stone that shatters it along invisible planes. Whole streets have been lost this way. A dropped crate of bottles once calved a four-acre district into the tidal flats, and the older residents still name it when they want to warn a child. So the city has learned to hush itself.

The machines here are wrapped. Engines turn in housings lined with felt and wool. Tram wheels are rimmed in rubber, and the rails are bedded in moss. Factory hammers fall onto leather anvils. The great looms of the spinning districts run in long halls padded like asylums, and the only sound that escapes them is a soft, continuous hiss, like breath through teeth. Bells are forbidden. Time is announced by lamps: amber for the hour, green for the shift change, a slow red pulse for a tide surge coming in.

People speak low. They have developed a whispering public voice, clear and articulate and never raised, and a rich language of hand signals for crowded streets. Markets, and there are many, run on gestures and murmurs. Walk through one and you hear a dense, rustling susurrus, thousands of soft voices overlapping like wind in dry grass, with the occasional muffled thud of a sack set down carefully. Fog swallows the rest.

The quiet is not calm. It is the quiet of a crowd holding its breath.

## The Crowding

There is no space here. Everything is packed.

New ground is valuable the instant it exists and is occupied almost before it has cured. The Survey stakes lots on wet slurry. Builders move in on the third day, when the stone will bear a man's weight on boards. By the second week there are frames. By the end of the month there are families, workshops, a noodle stall, a chapel, and a pigeon loft, all stacked five and six storeys high and leaning together for support, because the ground beneath each one is settling at a slightly different rate.

The architecture is light by necessity: timber, woven reed panels, sheet tin, oiled canvas, and the precipitate itself cut into blocks from the older, harder ground. Buildings are made to be moved. Many stand on screw-jacks, and every household owns a set of levelling wrenches. As the land sags or swells, the city adjusts itself continuously. Floors are re-trued, doors are rehung, whole houses are winched a few inches west. At dawn you can see men on every rooftop sighting along spirit levels and signaling adjustments to one another across the fog.

Streets are not fixed. They are renegotiated. Where a trough deepens and floods, a street becomes a canal for a season, and flat-bottomed barges replace the handcarts. Where a Condenser has been moved in, a new swell rises through what was a courtyard, and the buildings around it tilt outward like petals opening. People go to sleep facing one neighbor and wake up facing another. Addresses are given relative to things that move more slowly: "three houses north of the Kettle Condenser, as of spring." Maps are printed weekly and are out of date by the time the ink dries.

Nothing is ever finished. Scaffolding is the city's natural state, bamboo and ash poles lashed in endless tiers, draped with sheeting, and threaded with ropes and pulley-lines along which buckets and baskets and small children travel.

## The Green

For all the industry, the city is overwhelmingly green.

The warm, wet, mineral-rich new stone is the most fertile ground anyone has ever seen. Spores ride in on the fog, and within days of curing, fresh precipitate is furred with moss: thick, brilliant, cushion-deep sphagnum and feather moss in a dozen shades from lime to bottle-dark. Ferns follow within weeks, first bracken and then shield ferns, lady ferns, and immense cold-loving tree ferns that should not survive at this latitude but do, their trunks rooted in the warmth. Within a year there are alders and birches and willows, forcing their way up between buildings and through the cracks of sagging streets. Their roots are, many engineers believe, a good part of what holds the city together.

Every surface is colonized. Moss upholsters rooftops and wraps chimneys. Liverworts sheet down walls like green wax. Lichens crust the Condenser legs in orange and grey rosettes. Window boxes are unnecessary; windows sprout on their own. The mushrooms come in autumn: pale ranks of inkcaps along the tram lines, shelves of bracket fungus on the timber frames, and in the settling districts great fairy rings that open in a single night across a flooded square.

The green behaves strangely here, because it grows too fast. You can sit on a doorstep in the afternoon and watch a fern uncoil. Vines climb scaffolding faster than it is built. A neglected tram car will be a mound of moss in a month. The city's gardeners are really its demolition crews, working with long-handled hooks and machetes and wicker carts, keeping the growth at bay just enough to let people pass. Cleared greenery is not wasted. It is packed into dense bales, dried, and burned in the felted furnaces of the foundries, so the green feeds the industry that feeds the stone that feeds the green.

The smell of the city is the smell of this cycle: wet moss, peat smoke, warm mineral dust, coal-tar on the rails, and under it all the flat, cold salt of the fog.

## The Animals

Animals thrive in the warmth and the cover, and they are everywhere.

Gulls, grey and enormous, own the rooftops and the Condenser tops, and they have learned the silence too. They do not cry here as they do on other coasts. They communicate with bill-clacks and wing gestures, and a flock lifting off a roof makes only a sound like a sheet being shaken. Pigeons are kept everywhere, in lofts on nearly every building. The city's messages still largely travel by bird, because radio interferes with the Condensers and telephone lines snap as the ground shifts.

In the moss live multitudes. Pale ground beetles the size of a thumbnail swarm across fresh stone, tasting it, and their movement over a newly laid district looks like a shimmer of light. Slugs grow long and orange and ornate. Moles, which were brought in deliberately decades ago to aerate the curing stone, have become a citywide population, and their molehills rise overnight in any open patch. The Survey both employs and hunts them.

In the flooded troughs there are eels, huge silver-dark eels that move up from the tidal flats and live in the warm canals, and are fished at night by lamplight. Herons stand in the shallows, perfectly still, among the pilings. Small deer, long-legged and shaggy and pale-coated, drift through the older settling districts eating lichen from the walls. They are half-tame and wholly silent, and it is considered bad luck to shoo one from your doorway.

Stranger things live in the Condensers. In the lattice, high up where the fog is thickest, there are colonies of a small, soft, grey bat that no naturalist has been able to match to any known species. They are said to have come with the fog. They cluster on the copper mesh and seem to feed on it, or on whatever the mesh draws out of the air, and in the evenings they pour down the tower legs in long smoky ribbons and disperse into the city without a sound.

## The Turbulence

This place never holds still.

On any given day, somewhere in the flatland, a district is being born. The slurry basins overflow, and grey mud creeps out across the tidal flats in lobes, steaming in the cold. Crowds of surveyors, builders, speculators, and squatters wade in behind it, marking claims with colored flags on the wet surface. Somewhere else a district is dying: the old ground, cut off from its Condenser, is slowly cooling and slumping, the buildings sliding a few degrees further off true each week, the canals widening, the moss giving way to reeds, until one tide the whole quarter goes soft and the water comes in. People evacuate on barges, taking their doors and windows with them.

Sometimes the change is sudden. A crack runs silently across a plaza, and in an hour a street has dropped three feet, and the buildings on either side lean in and touch across it. A Condenser overperforms in heavy fog, and a lobe of hot slurry pushes through the ground floors of a neighborhood like a slow grey tide. The workers there have to move the machines, the stock, and the sleeping infants up the stairs, and then, a week later, cut new doors into the second floor because the street is now up there. Storms off the sea drive fog so dense it seems solid, and every Condenser in the region runs at once, and the ground swells visibly, rising and heaving and cracking, while people ride it out on their jacks and their rooftops.

And through all of it, the crowds keep moving. Markets migrate with the ground, following new land like herds following grass. Factories are dismantled and rebuilt on rails. The whole city drifts, district by district, generation by generation, eastward into the sea, making land ahead of itself and abandoning land behind. Somewhere far to the west, the old first districts from sixty years ago have already sunk back to marsh, with only the tips of a few chimneys showing above the reeds, mossed over, full of birds.

## The Light

The light is ordinary daylight, flat and pale and even, the light of a permanent overcast bright enough to read by without casting shadows. It comes from everywhere at once, filtered through fog, and gives everything a soft, grey-green clarity, as if the whole city were underwater in very clean water. At noon on the rare clear day the sun comes through, and the wet moss on every roof blazes emerald, the mist rising off the warm stone turns gold, and the Condensers stand out suddenly, black and immense and far more numerous than anyone ever remembers.

Then the fog comes back in off the sea, the meshes begin to weep, and under your feet, very faintly, the ground grows.
