# The Kettle

It starts as a crack in a street floor, and the street keeps going down.

You could miss the entrance from above. It's a split in basalt no wider than a doorway, ringed with a low iron rail somebody bolted there in a hurry. The air rising out of it smells of wet stone and hot metal. Look over the rail and you see a staircase, then another below it at a slight angle, then a gantry. Below that, a smear of orange light and green so dark it is nearly black. The heat reaches your face like the breath of someone standing too close.

The people who live down there call it the Kettle. It's a small place. You could walk from one end to the other in ten minutes if the alleys let you walk straight, and they never do.

---

## The Rock

The fissure is new. That's the first thing to understand about it, and everything else follows from it.

It opened within the lifetime of the oldest residents, a single night of the ground groaning. By morning a seam had parted in the earth, perhaps two hundred paces long and sixty deep, narrow at the top and belling out below like a split bottle. Its walls have never been weathered. They are black and glassy in places, grey and granular in others. Their edges are still sharp enough to open a palm if you steady yourself carelessly, and people who have lived here a while keep their hands to themselves. Shards the size of roof tiles lie wedged in corners and have not yet rounded. Where the rock has flaked, the fresh faces glint.

It is still settling. Every few weeks a faint tick runs through the walls, like a cooling kettle, and a thin rain of grit falls onto the rooftops. Nothing large has fallen in years. Everyone still sleeps with a hand's breadth of space between their bed and the wall.

The bottom is hot, and not with sun-heat. The warmth comes up through the floor, and your feet feel it before your skin does. Somewhere below the lowest level, the rock that split open is still close to whatever split it. The deeper you go, the more the brick sweats and the more the air thickens. By the bottom it hangs like damp wool.

---

## The Town

The Kettle was built quickly and has been built over ever since. Nothing was ever torn down to make room.

The first structures were iron: gantries and ladders pinned into the walls with long bolts, and platforms laid across the gap where it narrowed. Then came brick, carried down in baskets. Then more brick on top of the first, and wooden rooms hung off the brick, and tin sheds wedged beneath the wooden rooms. The result is a town packed like a drawer of spoons. Houses lean on houses. A single staircase might serve six doors, pass through someone's kitchen, and end at a window you have to climb through to reach the next staircase. Alleys are one shoulder wide, then half a shoulder. In places the roofs of the lower terraces are the streets of the upper ones, and you walk over chimney pots.

There is no open square. The nearest thing is the Landing, a riveted iron platform halfway down where three staircases meet. It is about the size of a large room, and it is always crowded with crates, potted plants, a bench, a rusted weighing scale, and coils of hose. Even the empty spaces are occupied by something waiting to be used.

The rivets are bright and the paint is barely chipped. The mortar has hardly discolored. Nothing here has had time to become old. It's a young town in a young crack, and it has the rawness of a fresh scar.

---

## The Engine

At the very bottom, where the fissure pinches shut into a seam you could not slip a hand into, sits the Engine.

It's a beam engine, as tall as four houses, painted a deep bottle-green that has held up surprisingly well in the damp. It draws its heat from the rock itself. Pipes run down into the seam, deeper than anyone has followed, and come back up full of steam. The great beam rocks on its pivot, and the flywheel turns.

It is slow, and that is the second thing to understand. The beam completes one stroke roughly every forty seconds. It rises with a long hiss that you feel more than hear, pauses at the top as if considering, then descends. Everything in the Kettle runs from it. The pumps, the lifts and the looms in the weaving sheds are all driven by belts and shafts that climb the walls like vines. So is the dynamo that feeds the lamps.

Nobody can say for certain who built the Engine or when. Some residents say it was lowered in from above in pieces. Others say it was here when they arrived. A few say, not entirely joking, that it was here before the crack opened, and the crack opened to let it breathe. The Engine keeps its own counsel. It has never stopped.

---

## Light

The Kettle is dim, and the light pulses.

The lamps are sodium-orange bulbs in wire cages, strung along the alleys and fixed to walls at head height. They are fed by the dynamo, and the dynamo rises and falls with the Engine's stroke. So every forty seconds the whole town brightens, slowly, to the color of low firelight. Then it sinks back to a deep amber, barely enough to see the edges of steps.

Residents stop noticing this within a week. Visitors find it hypnotic, then unsettling, then comforting. Conversations fall into its rhythm. People pause at the dim, and speak on the bright. Children learn to time their dashes across the gantries to the swell of light. Old people tell time by it: so many breaths to boil water, so many to walk to the Landing.

From above, a thin blade of daylight falls through the crack at midday. It reaches about a third of the way down, then gives up. Below that there is only the orange pulse, the faint red glow of the pipes nearest the Engine, and the dull shine of wet brick.

---

## Water

The Kettle is not wet, but it is always on the edge of it.

The rock is dry. The air is thick with vapor that has nowhere to go. It beads on every cold surface: iron railings, the underside of tin roofs, the lenses of the lamps. Most of the time it just clings, and everything glistens faintly without ever running.

Once a day, when the Engine's relief valves open, the town has weather. Nobody sets the hour. It comes when the pressure in the deep pipes decides. A great hush of steam vents from the stacks around the Engine and rolls upward through the alleys. It meets the cooler air near the top of the fissure and falls back as rain.

The residents call this the Breath. It lasts perhaps half an hour. It is warm, fine, and almost soundless, more a heavy mist than a downpour. Every gutter and pipe in the Kettle is angled to catch it. Water runs into barrels, into cisterns under the stairs, into long troughs along the terraces where the gardens are. Then the Breath stops, the air clears a little, and the town drips for an hour. After that it goes back to sweating quietly until the next one.

In the lowest level there is one permanent pool, where a pipe cracked years ago and was never fully fixed. It is the size of a bathtub and too hot to touch. The water is perfectly clear, and on its bottom grows a thin rust-red film that nobody has identified.

---

## Green

For a place with so little light, the Kettle is astonishingly overgrown.

The plants don't grow toward the sun. There isn't enough of it. They grow toward heat. Their leaves are dark, a deep violet-green that looks black in the dim part of the pulse and faintly plum in the bright. They are broad and thin, and they turn, slowly over days, to face the nearest hot pipe. The steam lines are the brightest things in their world, and the plants treat them that way.

Ferns spill from every crack in the brickwork, fronds curled tight at the ends. Mosses carpet the undersides of gantries in thick, springy mats that hold the Breath's water for days. Figs have rooted in the mortar of the older walls. Their pale roots run down the brick like wax from a candle, feeling toward the pipes, and in a few places they have wrapped a steam line completely. Nobody cuts them away. A wrapped pipe loses heat to the fig, and the fig grows, and the residents say this is a fair trade.

People garden everywhere. Every windowsill, every stair-edge, every bit of flat roof holds pots and troughs of something growing. There are tomatoes that stay small and bitter-sweet, and beans trained up the railings. There are dark leafy greens, peppers, and something like a squash whose vines run along the gutters for whole terraces. Mushrooms grow in the damp corners, some cultivated, some not. The cultivated ones are pale and tender. The others are ivory, very tall, and slender as pencils, and people leave them alone.

Near the Engine the growth is densest. The heat there is nearly unbearable, but the plants love it. A kind of creeper with leaves like black velvet coats the stone around the stacks. It has never been seen to flower. Occasionally it smells, very faintly, of cloves.

---

## Sound

For a town built around a machine, the Kettle is quiet.

The Engine's stroke is a presence more than a noise: the long hiss, the soft deep thump at the bottom of the stroke. It comes up through the soles of your feet. The belts and shafts hum at the edge of hearing. Water drips. Steam whispers from joints. A door closes somewhere above. Someone's kettle, an ordinary one, starts to sing and is taken off the heat.

The walls swallow sound. The rock is glassy but porous in places, and the plants soak up everything else. Voices carry only a few steps. You can hear your own heartbeat on a still night, and, as residents like to point out, it goes at about the Engine's pace or a little faster. People speak softly by habit, and the Kettle's own words are short. A shouted word here feels violent, and almost nobody shouts.

---

## Animals

There is very little animal life.

Moths are the most common: small, grey, soft, drawn not to the lamps but to the pipes, where they sit in rows along the insulation and sometimes, on the hottest lines, simply stop moving. A few spiders live in the corners and catch the moths. There are beetles in the moss, dark and shiny, the size of a fingernail.

There is one animal that people talk about. It is slender, long-bodied, and furless, with pale grey skin, the size of a small cat. It moves along the pipes at night, very slowly. It has no visible eyes. Residents call it the pipe-hound, though it is nothing like a dog. It has never been seen eating. It has never been seen in daylight, such as daylight is here. Most people see one perhaps twice a year, and the sighting is an event. There is a superstition that it is warm to the touch, warmer than the pipes. No one has tested this.

No birds come down. There are no rats, which surprises visitors most of all.

---

## People

The Kettle is packed with houses, but not with people. This is the strangeness that settles on you over time.

Every building looks lived in. There are plants on the sills, pots on the stoves, washing on lines. Names are chalked on the doors, fresh names in careful handwriting, renewed when the Breath smudges them. But you can walk the whole town and meet perhaps a dozen people. Doors stay shut. Lamps in windows brighten and dim with the pulse, and you rarely see a shadow cross behind them.

The residents you do meet are unhurried and courteous, and they are always doing something slowly. One man spends a morning adjusting a single valve by quarter-turns. A woman waters her troughs one cupful at a time, timed to the pulse. They are generous with food and with small conversations. If you ask them where everyone else is, they say "in," or "about," or "sleeping." They say it pleasantly, without concern, and they change the subject.

Sometimes, late, you will hear a door open and close on a terrace you had passed and thought empty. In the morning a trough there will have been watered.

---

## Time

Everything here is slow.

The Engine sets the pace, and the town seems to have agreed to follow it. Plants grow at their own speed, which is steady and patient. Weather comes once a day. The walls settle over years. A fig root takes a season to wrap a pipe. People take their time, and it seems to cost them nothing.

Visitors report that they stay what feels like a few days, and come up to find a week has passed. Or they stay what feels like a week, and come up to find it is still the same afternoon. The residents shrug at this. Time down here is measured in strokes, they say, not hours, and strokes are not quite the same length as hours. They don't say which one is longer.

---

## Leaving

The only way out is up the stairs you came down.

You climb through the gardens and past the chalked doors. You cross the Landing, past the crates and the scale. You go through somebody's kitchen, where there is a warm loaf on the table and no one in sight, and out a window onto the next staircase. The heat thins as you climb. The orange pulse fades, and the thin blade of daylight appears, cool and white and shockingly bright.

At the rail at the top you look back down. The Kettle is a smear of amber and black-green, pulsing gently, every forty seconds. Your heartbeat, you notice, has slowed to match.

You'll find yourself, for days afterward, waiting between one breath and the next.
