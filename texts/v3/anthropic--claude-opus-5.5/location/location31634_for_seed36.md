# The Underleaf

## Descent

You come down into it the way you'd come down into water. There's a lip, a crumbling edge of dry white ground where the upper world ends, and below the lip the green begins. It isn't a forest seen from above. It's the top of a mass, a living roof that seals a hollow in the earth the way a scab seals a wound. You push through it and it closes behind you, and you are inside.

The first thing is the light. You expect dimness, the gloom of a cave or the brown shade under old trees. There is none. The roof you came through is made of leaves layered so thin and so many that the sun passes through them the way it passes through the webbing of a held-up hand. By the time it reaches the floor, a hundred and fifty meters down, it has been filtered a thousand times and has come out stronger, somehow. It glows green-gold, then gold-white, and it arrives from every direction at once. Nothing here casts a shadow. Your hand held in front of your face is lit on the palm and the back equally. The light doesn't fall. It soaks.

The second thing is the heat. It's the heat of a closed hand, of a glasshouse at noon with the vents shut. It is heavy and damp, and it sits against the skin without moving, because nothing here moves the air.

## The Mass

The Underleaf is a single thing, or seems to be. What you'd call trees are pale columns of stem tissue, some thick as a house, some thin as a wrist. They rise from the floor and branch and rejoin and branch again until they vanish upward into the luminous ceiling. They are not bark-covered. Their skins are smooth and faintly translucent, the color of celery hearts or new bamboo. Pressed close, you can see fluid inside them, a slow clear shimmer, like looking into a glass rod.

Between the columns, nothing is empty. Lateral stems cross the spaces at every height, forming galleries, catwalks, lattices, braided ropes of green thick enough to walk on. From these hang curtains of fine filament, root-hair or tendril, so dense that you part them with both arms like a bead curtain. Broad leaves grow from the undersides of everything, and even these, far below the roof, are turned not upward but outward, toward the ambient glow, which comes from all sides.

The floor is not soil. It's a felt of roots, springy and slightly warm, compressed by nothing, because nothing has ever walked here enough to compress it. Dig your fingers in and there is only more root, finer and finer, down past where you can reach. Somewhere below, perhaps, is rock. No one has seen it.

The whole hollow is roughly the size of a valley. Standing anywhere, you can see a long way, because the light doesn't fade with distance. It stays even. You see the galleries receding in layers, column behind column, each a little paler with humidity, until the far walls of the depression become a haze of green-white. The space is crowded and vast at once, like looking into a very large, very full aquarium.

## Newness

It takes a while to notice what is missing, and what's missing is age.

Nothing here is worn. Every stem is smooth. Every leaf edge is whole and unfrayed. No branch is broken. There is no litter on the floor: no fallen leaves, no deadwood, no humus, no rot. The tips of the tendrils are soft and pale, the way new growth is soft and pale, and yet they are not growing. Mark one and come back after a day or a season. It will be exactly as it was.

This is the strangest law of the place. It is finished, and it is new, and it is new permanently. It's as if the Underleaf was grown in a single long breath and then stopped at the moment just before anything could begin to go wrong. The surfaces carry no scars. The junctions between stems are seamless, never calloused. The first morning of a garden has been held still.

Scratch a stem and it does not heal. It doesn't need to, because the scratch doesn't stay. Look away and look back, and the skin is unbroken, not repaired but somehow never having been cut. Some who have come here say the place doesn't resist damage so much as forget it.

## Water

The Underleaf is damp but not wet. There is no rain here, no streams, no dripping.

Condensation beads on the undersides of the broad leaves, and the beads stay. They hang in thousands, each one a perfect small lens full of the omnidirectional glow, and they don't fall. Touch one and it wets your finger. The one beside it doesn't move.

Here and there are cup-plants: forks in the lower stems where the tissue has formed a bowl, and each bowl holds a still pool of water, clear, blood-warm, a handspan or an armspan across. The water in them doesn't evaporate or rise. It's drinkable and faintly sweet, like the water at the base of a cut flower's stem.

In some of the deeper galleries, pockets of mist hang between the columns. They have edges. You can put your arm into a fog bank and pull it out again, beaded, and the fog stays exactly where it was, a soft gray room in the gold.

## Sound

It's quiet, but not silent.

Nearest to silence is the base sound of the place: a very faint, very high ticking, almost below hearing, like a clock in another room. Put your ear against a column and it becomes clearer. It's something inside the stem, fluid moving through narrow places, or cells doing something they never finish doing.

Your own sounds seem too loud. Footfalls are absorbed by the root-felt, but breath carries. Speaking feels like shouting in a library. Voices don't echo; they soak into the tendrils and are gone.

Once in a long while, something else. A single deep, soft note, like a finger drawn across the rim of a glass, from somewhere across the hollow. It happens rarely. No one knows what makes it. It doesn't repeat on any schedule anyone has found.

## What Lives Here, Besides the Plant

Very little moves.

There are moths, or something like moths: pale, broad-winged, almost translucent, sitting motionless on the undersides of leaves. You can walk past a hundred and never see them, because they take on the light like everything else. Disturb one and it flies a short, slow, looping distance and settles again. They don't seem to eat. They don't seem to die, either; there are no wings on the floor.

In the cup-pools, sometimes, there is a thing like a small clear shrimp, hanging in the water column. It turns when you lean over its pool, keeping its face to you. It's the only thing here that seems to watch.

And once, maybe, something larger. Travelers who stayed long enough describe a shape moving along the high galleries, slow, many-limbed, the size of a dog or a deer, pale as the stems. It walks the way the moths fly, without hurry. It has been seen perhaps a dozen times in all the stories. It has never been seen to eat, or rest, or leave.

## Traces

People have been here. Not many, not for long, and they left little, because the Underleaf doesn't let much stay.

What remains is what the plant tolerated. Here and there, lower stems have been tied together: two columns bound with a knot of braided tendril so that they form an arch, or a lintel, or a simple doorway leading from one part of the gallery to another identical part. The knots hold. The plant hasn't absorbed them. They seem to be the only permanent thing that isn't the plant itself.

In one gallery there is a ring of seeds on the floor. They're huge, dark, stone-hard seeds that you won't find anywhere else in the Underleaf, since nothing here ever seems to fruit. Someone brought them, or found them, and laid them in a circle nine paces across. Inside the circle the root-felt is exactly as it is outside.

Near the eastern wall is a hammock, woven of filament, hanging between two columns, still perfect. Beside it, on a cup-plant's rim, sits a small bowl, folded from a single broad leaf and pinned with a thorn. It still holds a little water.

There are no bodies, no bones, no graves. The people who came here left, or were forgotten along with every scratch they made, or lie in the hammock with no weight at all.

## The Shape of It

If you could see the Underleaf from outside, you'd see a depression in a dry white plain, a long crescent-shaped sink a few kilometers across, filled level to its lip with an unbroken surface of green. From above it looks like a pond. The green does not ripple. At midday it shines.

Up there, the ground is salt-crust and heat, and water is precious and far. Down here, a little below the surface of the world, it's warm and bright and damp and still, and every leaf is the first leaf, and every drop of water is waiting to fall and never will.

You can live here. You can drink from the cups and sleep in the galleries. You'll never be cold, and never be in shadow, and nothing will ever change. People who stay a long time say that's exactly the problem. People who leave say they sometimes wake in the upper world in the dark, and miss it so badly they can't breathe.
