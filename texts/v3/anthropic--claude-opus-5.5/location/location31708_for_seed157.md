# The Stillwell

High on a limestone plateau, above the last trees but below the permanent snow, the ground has rotted from underneath and fallen in. What's left is a shaft, roughly round and perhaps forty paces across at the lip, that drops through the bedrock into a chamber no bigger than a village hall. It has no name. If it needed one, the Stillwell would do. It holds a great deal of life, a great deal of water, and almost no change.

## The Lip

The plateau is open ground of heather and wet stone, cold and windy. At the edge of the shaft the wind stops. The air coming up from below is still and heavy, and it smells of wet chalk, leaf-mould, and something faintly animal, like a fox's den or a sheepfold in winter.

The vegetation begins right at the rim and grows thicker as it descends. Along the top, bilberry and crowberry lean over the drop. A foot below them come the ferns: hart's-tongue, broad and glossy, in overlapping tiers, with their fronds laid across one another so completely that no rock shows. Below the ferns is moss. The walls of the shaft are wet everywhere. They do not flow. They weep, and the water collects in a skin so even that the stone seems to sweat. The moss drinks this water and swells into cushions the thickness of a forearm, then a thigh. Liverworts grow over the moss, and filmy ferns, each leaf a single cell thick and translucent as wet paper, grow over the liverworts. Nothing grows directly on stone because every surface already has something on it. Every plant here stands on another plant.

## The Descent

The light fails quickly. At the lip it is a grey mountain day. Ten metres down it is dusk. By twenty the shaft opens into the ceiling of the chamber, and the sky is a pale coin overhead that lights nothing.

Just before the dark becomes total, one moss catches the last of the light: *Schistostega*, goblin's gold. Its cells are shaped like tiny lenses, and they gather whatever light falls on them and return it as a dim emerald glow. A band of it circles the throat of the shaft like a ring of dull coins, and for as long as the plateau has had a sky that band has marked the edge between the world above and the world below.

Beneath it the ferns thin out and disappear. The moss continues a little further in darkness, pale and etiolated, living on almost nothing. After that there are no more plants, only animals.

## The Chamber

In the dark the Stillwell turns out to be very crowded.

**The ceiling** is alive with small lights. Fungus-gnat larvae, each no longer than a grain of rice, hang in countless numbers from the rock. Each one lowers a curtain of silk threads beaded with sticky droplets and lights its own tail with a cold blue-green glow. They are packed so densely that the ceiling looks like a sky full of stars far too close together, and the light from all of them together is enough to show outlines but not colours. The threads hang motionless in the still air. Now and then a midge drifts up from the pool and sticks, and one point of light dims slightly while the larva draws its thread in. That is almost the only movement up there.

Among the larvae, in the dark gaps, hang moths. They are herald moths, rust-orange by any daylight they will never see, with their wings folded flat against the stone. They cover the rock like roof tiles, so many that the ceiling seems to have grown scales. They are not quite dead and not quite waiting. They are overwintering, although in the Stillwell winter never ends and never starts, so they simply stay.

Deeper in, where the ceiling rises into a dome, there are bats: thousands of small brown bats in torpor, packed shoulder to shoulder in a single hanging mass. The dome is furred with them. Their breathing has slowed almost to nothing, and their bodies have cooled to the temperature of the rock. They make no sound. Condensation beads on their fur and grows until it drips, and a fine, constant rain of it falls from the colony onto the floor.

**The walls** are covered in harvestmen. There are no spaces between them. They gather in mats, thousands of bodies the size of lentils held up on legs as fine as hair, and the legs interlock so thoroughly that each mat moves as one thing. From a distance a mat looks like a patch of dark fur. Up close it is a woven fabric made of animals. When something disturbs one of them (a drip, a breath of air, a lantern if anyone were ever foolish enough to bring one) the whole mat rises and falls together. It is a single slow pulse that crosses the wall like wind through grass, and then everything settles again. This is the strangest thing in the Stillwell, and the most characteristic of it: thousands of separate bodies behaving as though they shared one mind, and choosing, together, to stay still.

Cave crickets wedge themselves into the cracks between mats. Their antennae are three times the length of their bodies and wave very slowly, sensing the air. Pale snails, their shells worn thin as eggshell, graze the biofilm on the wet rock. Springtails crowd the moist places in their millions, so small and so numerous that the stone looks dusted with pepper.

**The floor** slopes down to a pool that fills most of the chamber. It is fed by drip from every surface and drains through fissures too narrow to see, so its level never changes. The water is very cold and very clear, and in the glow-worm light it is almost invisible. Its surface is broken only where drips fall, and the drips always fall in the same places, so the surface carries a fixed pattern of ripple-rings. These are not a sign of change. They are part of the pool's permanent form.

The pool is full of salamanders. They are pale and blind, their skin pinkish-white and so thin that their blood shows through, and they have the feathery external gills of animals that never finished growing up. They lie on the bottom in heaps, draped across one another, and move perhaps once in a day. Each one may live a century. Some of them may be older than any tree on the plateau above.

Among them move caddis larvae, and here the Stillwell shows its second strangeness.

## The Knapping Floor

On the far side of the pool the floor levels into a shelf of fine silt, sealed under a skin of moonmilk, the soft white calcite paste that forms in cold wet caves. Beneath the moonmilk, if anyone ever looked, they would find flakes of flint.

There are hundreds of them. They are thin, sharp-edged, and bear the scalloped scars of a hammerstone. Among them lie a few finished pieces: a scraper, a blade with its edge still bright, and a cracked cobble of river quartzite, the hammer itself, carried up from a valley now far below and never carried back. Beside them is a smear of charcoal, all that remains of a fire, preserved under a sheet of calcite as clear as glass.

On the wall above the shelf are hands. They are red ochre, stencilled by blowing pigment from the mouth around a palm pressed against the rock. There are seven of them. Two are small enough to belong to children. Time has glazed them over with calcite, so they now sit a finger's depth inside the stone, like hands pressed against the far side of a frosted window. The harvestmen avoid them, and the mats part around the ochre as though it were still warm.

That is all the made world amounts to here: a handful of chipped stone, one fire, and seven hands. Nobody built anything. Someone came down, sat, worked flint by the light of a small flame, left their marks, and climbed back out, and the Stillwell closed over the traces like water.

The caddis larvae have found the flakes. In the shallows at the edge of the shelf, where knapping debris has washed into the pool, the larvae build their protective cases from it. Each one silks together tiny chips of worked flint into a tube and drags its armour across the bottom. Their cases glitter faintly in the glowworm light, a sharper kind of glitter than natural gravel gives, because every fragment was struck by a human hand. The larvae pupate, the adults fly up to die in the glowworm threads, the eggs return to the pool, and the flint passes to the next generation. The same flakes have probably been passed down this way thousands of times.

## Sound

The Stillwell is almost silent, and the silence has a shape.

Its main sound is the drip, or rather the drips, since there are hundreds of them. Each falls from its own point on its own interval: some every few seconds, some every few minutes, one near the bat dome only about once an hour. Where they land on water they make a small hollow *tok*. Where they land on stone they make a flat *tap*. Where they land on the mats of harvestmen they make no sound at all, only the slow ripple of the mat rising and settling.

The drips never synchronise, because none of their intervals ever varies. Their overlapping rhythm is so complex that it would take longer than a human lifetime to repeat, but it is entirely fixed. Anyone who sat here long enough would begin to hear it as a single enormous piece of music, always playing and always the same.

Under the drips there is almost nothing: the faint whisper of springtails, the creak of a salamander shifting on the bottom, and, very rarely, when the bat colony dreams, a single high click too sharp for most ears, followed by silence again.

The chamber absorbs sound. Its walls are too thickly covered in living bodies to echo. A voice spoken here would come back dull and swallowed, as if the room were upholstered, because in effect it is.

## The Law of the Place

What governs the Stillwell is that it does not change.

It has changed once, of course. It formed, the roof fell in, and water carved the chamber over a span of time too long to picture. But that happened long ago, and since then the place has reached a balance so exact that it no longer moves.

The temperature is six degrees, day and night, summer and winter, and has been for longer than there have been people to feel it. The air is saturated, so nothing dries and nothing evaporates. The pool's inflow equals its outflow. The drip that falls on the floor below the dome has built a stalagmite that grows a hair's width in a century and has been doing so, at the same rate, since before the ice last retreated from the plateau.

The population holds steady too. The glowworms eat the midges, the midges breed in the pool, and the pool is fed by the droppings of the bats, whose numbers stay constant. When a harvestman dies, it drops from the mat and another takes its place, in the same place, holding the same legs. A salamander dies after its hundred years and another has already grown into its hollow in the silt. Individuals come and go, but the arrangement of the whole never changes. Seen at any one moment, the Stillwell looks as though a vast and busy community has been caught in a photograph. Seen across a thousand years, it looks exactly the same.

Above, the plateau goes through its seasons. Snow comes and goes, the heather flowers and browns, storms cross the sky. None of it reaches past the ring of goblin's gold. The Stillwell has closed itself off from all of that, and inside, in the dark, wet, and crowded quiet, it simply remains as it is. The seven hands hang behind their glaze of stone, the caddis larvae carry the flint, and the drips continue on their unchanging schedule.
