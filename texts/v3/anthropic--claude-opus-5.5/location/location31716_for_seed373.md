# The Cirque of the Held Chord

It is heard before it is seen. Before the last switchback, before the pass even shows as a notch against the sky, the air on the approach begins to tremble. It is not wind, though wind is always here too. It is a pressure you feel in your sternum, a hum in the fillings of your teeth, a low roar that grows louder with every step. By the time you crest the lip of the bowl it is no longer a sound. It is the medium you are standing in.

## The Bowl

The cirque is small, perhaps four hundred paces across, a hollow scooped out of the mountain's shoulder well above the treeline of any ordinary range. The air is thin and cold, with the dry cold of altitude, not the damp cold of winter. There is no snow, and no snowmelt, because nothing here has ever been wet. The rock is pale grey granite, and the rim is so dry that lichen, if there were any, would crumble. The sky overhead is an ordinary, generous blue, and the light falls on everything evenly and without drama.

The strangeness is in the shape of the stone. The walls have been shaped by something other than ice or water. Over a span of time long enough to seem geological, they have been polished into curves: long fluted ribs, shallow concave dishes, and horn-mouthed hollows that flare out from narrow throats. The whole bowl has been worn into acoustics. Every surface either focuses the sound or throws it back. Stand in one of the dished alcoves and the roar sharpens into a single piercing tone. Step half a meter aside and it collapses into a churning bass that loosens your knees. The erosion is ancient and settled, and the grain of the rock has long since agreed to it. This stone has been sung into its present form, and it has stopped resisting.

## The Chord

At the center of the bowl, on a low shelf of bedrock, stands the thing that is making the noise, or the thing that used to.

It is not large, perhaps the height of three people. It looks like a column of dark metal-glass wrapped in a sleeve of fine vertical vanes, thousands of them, each thinner than a fingernail. There are no seams and no visible intake. It does not vibrate the way a speaker vibrates, because nothing on it appears to move. The sound seems to leave it the way heat leaves a stove. Around its base, set into the rock at uneven intervals, are a handful of smaller objects: squat housings like milestones, a few leaning pylons capped with discs, and one half-buried ring that might once have been a doorway. That is all the building there is. No roads, no shelters, no inscriptions anyone has read. Whoever set the column here either needed nothing else or left nothing else behind.

The column produces a chord, not a single note. It is a stacked, unresolved mass of frequencies, some too low to hear and felt only as a slow push and pull in the chest, some so high they register as a faint ache behind the eyes. The chord is never still. It swells and shears, and whole registers drop out and slam back in. Every few minutes a pressure front rolls outward across the bowl. You can see it pass as a ripple through the vegetation, like a hand dragged through fur. Sometimes the chord climbs for an hour toward a pitch that feels intolerable, and then it breaks, and the break is a kind of detonation. After that, briefly, the loudest thing in the cirque is your own blood.

## The Listening Growth

There is no water here, but the bowl is lush.

The vegetation is thin and airy, and it fills the cirque the way frost fills a windowpane: intricate and everywhere, yet almost entirely empty space. The plants are made of glass, or something close to it. Silicate stalks rise from cracks in the rock, hollow and pale green, some no thicker than a hair and some as thick as a wrist. They branch into fans, lattices, and open cages of filament. Some reach the height of a small tree. You could walk through most of them without touching a strand, and a dense stand of them contains more air than matter. Seen from the rim, the bowl looks misted with green. Seen from inside, it is a sparse cathedral of threads.

They feed on the chord. Their tissue is piezoelectric, and it flexes under the constant pressure of sound, turning each shudder into a trickle of charge. That charge drives a slow, dry chemistry that pulls silica and trace metals from the rock dust. The growth is shaped by the frequencies that reach it. In the focal alcoves, where the tone is sharp and pure, the plants are tall and regular, tuned like reeds, and each stalk is exactly the length that resonates with the note it lives in. In the churning low zones they sprawl into squat, wide-meshed domes that ride the bass. The whole garden is a map of the sound, drawn in green glass.

Because it is glass, it breaks constantly. When the chord detonates at the top of a climb, whole stands shatter at once. There is a bright, silvery collapse, and green dust hangs in the air like pollen. Then, within hours, the dust begins to settle into new growth. The fragments charge, cling, and recrystallize, and fresh threads rise out of the ruin of the old ones. By the next morning a thicket that was flattened is already knee-high. The cirque is never the same garden twice, yet it is always recognizably itself. Its shape is held by the sound rather than by any particular set of plants.

## The Animals

Life crowds the empty air.

Moths come first, in drifts of thousands, with wings ribbed like the plants and dusted with the same green glass. They are deaf, as nearly everything here must be. They navigate by the pressure fronts, riding each wave outward and then spiraling back toward the column in the lull. They feed on the charged dust released by shattering stands. Where the plants break, the moths arrive within seconds, a sudden grey-green blizzard of wings.

In the lattices live beetles with hard, resonant carapaces. They cannot hear one another over the roar, so they speak by touch. They press their shells against a vibrating stalk and let the plant carry their message down its length. A single tall reed may hum with the courtship of a dozen of them. Some species appear to have learned to mute a stalk by clamping it, dampening its note for their own quiet.

There are birds, small and dun, with hollow skulls and no external ears at all. They nest in the flared stone horns and time their flights to the troughs of the chord, darting across the bowl in the brief seconds of lesser pressure. Hunters follow them: long, low creatures like ferrets made of cartilage and fur. They hug the rock and move only when a pressure front passes, using the ripple through the vegetation as cover. Everything here keeps time with the chord. Predator and prey move in the same pulse.

At the edges, where the bowl breaks open toward the rest of the mountain, grazers come in from the outer slopes. They are shaggy, big-lunged animals that crop the soft new growth after a shattering. They never stay long. They eat quickly with their heads down, and then they stagger back out over the rim, dazed, their coats glittering with dust.

## The Turn

Spend long enough in the cirque and you begin to notice things.

The chord does not quite come from the column. Press your palm against its dark surface and it is cold and still. Put your ear near it, if you can bear to, and you hear less than you expected. The sound is louder in the garden than at its supposed source. The tall tuned reeds in the alcoves do not merely vibrate when the chord reaches them. They lead it. Their notes reach the stone a fraction of a moment before the swell arrives. The squat bass domes throb just before the pressure fronts begin.

Perhaps the column ran for a very long time, long enough to carve the bowl into an instrument and to teach the glass its shape. Perhaps at some point, unnoticed, it stopped, or faltered, or simply grew quieter than the thing it had made. The garden, tuned to the chord and feeding on it and rebuilding itself around it, kept playing. Every stand that grows back grows at the right length. Every shattering is a crescendo the plants have built toward together. The machine's engineering is beyond anyone's reading, but its most successful work may have been this: a sound that learned to sustain itself in living glass, rock, moths, and nerve.

The housings around the base may still be listening. Sometimes one of the leaning pylons turns its disc a few degrees, very slowly, toward a stand of reeds that has gone off pitch. Within a day that stand has shattered and regrown. No one knows whether this is maintenance or conversation.

## Leaving

When you climb back over the rim, the roar does not stop. It recedes the way a fever recedes, leaving you shaky and too light. For a long way down the mountain the silence feels like an absence, like a missing stair. Your ears ring with the ghost of the chord, and you catch yourself timing your steps to the swells, waiting for a pressure front that no longer comes. Somewhere behind you, in the small cold bowl, the glass is breaking and growing and breaking again, and the air is full of wings.
