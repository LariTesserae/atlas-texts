# The Hollow Wards

The ground gives no warning. Across the upland there is only a pale, wind-scoured plateau of soft volcanic tuff, grey-gold and treeless, with scattered bunches of dry grass. Then the surface simply stops, and you are standing at the lip of a collapse, looking down into a city.

It was carved, not built. Whoever made it cut downward and inward into the tuff, a stone soft enough to shape with harder stone and bone. They dug for centuries, perhaps for thousands of years, and then they left. They left so long ago that no one on the plateau above remembers them as people, only as a word that means something like *the diggers* or *the ones below*.

## The Shape of It

The city lies thirty to eighty meters beneath the plateau. It spreads in a loose network across perhaps six or seven kilometers. It is not one cavern but many: chambers, galleries and broad avenues, linked by tunnels and ramps. Here and there the ceiling has fallen in to form roofless courts, open to a narrow strip of sky. These collapses are the city's windows. There are forty or so, scattered unevenly. Between them lie long stretches of covered dark, where the only light is what reflects in from the nearest opening.

The whole place is sparse. The diggers made room for far more people than ever lived here at once, or else they built generation by generation, each moving deeper and leaving the upper wards empty. You can walk a hundred meters of gallery and pass nothing but blank walls and the occasional doorway. Then you step out into a plaza the size of a field, ringed with three tiers of carved dwellings, every one of them empty of people and crowded with animals.

## What Time Has Done

Nothing here is sharp anymore.

The tuff is soft, and the city has had an unimaginably long time to soften further. Stairs have worn into ramps, then into smooth, shallow slopes you would take for natural if the regular spacing of the old treads didn't still show, faint as fingerprints. Doorways that were once rectangular are now ovals, their lintels sagged and rounded. Carved columns have thinned at the waist into hourglass shapes, and some have finally parted and stand as two stumps, one rising from the floor and one hanging from the ceiling, with a hand's width of air between them.

Inscriptions, if there were any, are gone. What remains are ghost-textures. In raking light, a wall shows the faint parallel grooves of whatever tool shaped it, a ridged pattern like the inside of a shell. There are niches everywhere, thousands of them, cut into every wall at shoulder height, rounded into shallow cups. No one knows what they held. Lamps, possibly, or offerings, or nothing at all.

The erosion here is not the work of water. There is almost no water. It comes from air, dust and time, and above all from the animals. Millions of claws have gripped the same ceiling ledges for millennia, and the stone there has been polished to a dull sheen. Where bats have roosted longest, the ceilings are pocked with smooth domes, each the diameter of a cluster of bodies, worn upward into the rock by sheer occupancy.

## Dryness

This is a dry place, and it stays that way. The plateau above gets little rain, and what falls soaks into the porous tuff and drains away far below, to some aquifer no one has found. The city itself holds no pools, no drips and no damp. The air is cool and constant, about twelve degrees, and it has the particular dryness of stone that has not been wet in a very long time. It smells of chalk, of dust, and above all of ammonia and old guano. The smell is sharp but not rotten; in this dry air nothing rots properly. Things simply desiccate and crumble.

Dead bats lie on the floors as little leather envelopes, perfectly preserved and weightless. Beetle husks drift in the corners like chaff.

## The Gardens That Grow on Dust

And yet the collapses are green.

Wherever light falls, life has taken hold, and taken hold thickly. It does so in defiance of the dryness, because the floor of every open court is covered in guano, meters deep in places. This is the richest soil imaginable, made from compressed nitrogen and phosphorus and the ground-down bodies of a hundred billion insects. Plants that can find the tiniest margin of moisture flourish here extravagantly.

What moisture there is comes at dawn. Cool night air sinks into the open courts, and when the first warmth hits the stone, a faint dew forms on the rock faces for perhaps an hour. It isn't much, but it has been enough.

The courts hold:

- **Resurrection ferns** that carpet every ledge, curled brown and dead-looking for most of the day, then unfurling green in the brief damp of morning. A court at dawn visibly changes color, from rust to emerald, over about forty minutes.
- **Wild figs** whose seeds were dropped by roosting animals. Their roots pour down the walls in pale grey cables, following old carved channels and niches, seeking water that is always further down. Some roots have traveled thirty meters from the parent tree, threading through doorways into chambers that have never seen light. The oldest figs have grown into the architecture so completely that their roots now act as columns, holding up ceilings the stone alone would have dropped.
- **Caper bushes**, grey-leaved and thorny, crowding the upper tiers of the dwelling-rings. Their white flowers open at dusk.
- **Mosses and lichens** in fields. They are lichens mostly, in orange, sulfur-yellow and a black crust like burned paper, covering every face that gets even an hour of light. Near the edges of each court the green fades gradually into grey, then into bare stone, marking exactly how far the light reaches.
- **Tall grasses and wild herbs** in the deepest guano beds: thyme, sage, and some low silver plant with a smell like pepper. These grow knee-high and dense, so that crossing a court means wading.

Beyond the light, nothing grows. The line is precise. A single court can hold a thicket so lush you can't see across it, and three steps into the gallery beyond, there is only dust.

## The Inhabitants

The city is full.

The bats are the true population. They are several species, but most of them are a small, brown, free-tailed kind that roosts in colonies of hundreds of thousands. The largest ward, a vaulted hall the diggers must have meant as some kind of gathering place, holds a colony whose numbers no one has estimated with confidence. Its ceiling is invisible, entirely hidden under a living, shifting pelt of bodies. The floor beneath is a guano slope rising higher every year.

Swiftlets nest in the dwelling-tiers, thousands of small, dark birds. They build cup-nests in the old niches, so that the diggers' mysterious hollows have finally found a use. They navigate the dark galleries by clicking, a dry, rapid rattle.

Beneath them lives an entire economy of scavengers. Guano beetles and dermestids number in the billions, turning the dung floor into a faintly moving surface. Cave crickets are pale and long-legged, with antennae three times their body length. Centipedes the length of a forearm hunt the crickets. Harvestmen gather in clusters of thousands on the walls, packed so tightly they look like dark fur, and scatter all at once when disturbed.

In the gardens there are lizards, geckos, and a small grey owl that nests in the fig roots. Snakes lie in the upper galleries at dusk and snatch bats from the air as they pass. Foxes come down from the plateau at night to hunt fallen young. A few feral goats, descended from herds lost long ago, have learned to navigate the ramps and browse the courts. Their hooves have added their own wear to the oldest pathways.

Nothing here is rare. Everything is abundant, except water and people.

## The Noise

The city is deafening. It is not intermittently loud but constantly, structurally so.

The tuff is hard and the chambers are vaulted, so sound goes everywhere and never dies. A million bats roosting produce a ceaseless high chittering, most of it above human hearing, but enough of it low enough to form a hissing, crackling wall of sound, like fat in an enormous pan. The swiftlet clicks rattle through the galleries in rolling waves. Crickets sing in the dark wards, and their chirps echo and re-echo until the separate calls merge into one grinding drone. The beetles alone, at close range, make an audible seething whisper as millions of small bodies move over dry dung.

Every chamber has its own acoustic character. Some galleries hum at a particular pitch and amplify it, so that walking through them feels like walking inside a struck bell. Others are dead and muffled. The great hall booms. The courts, open to the sky, are the quietest places in the city, and even they are louder than a busy street.

Then there is dusk.

At sunset the bats leave. They pour out of the largest collapses in rivers of bodies, spiraling upward in vortices so dense they are visible from kilometers away across the plateau as dark columns twisting against the sky. The emergence takes more than an hour. The sound is overwhelming. It is not a roar but a vast wind-noise of wingbeats, layered over a screaming chorus of calls, reverberating through every tunnel at once. The air moves. Standing in a gallery during the emergence, you feel a steady outward breeze made entirely of passing wings. Dust lifts from the floors. The ferns in the courts tremble.

Before dawn they return, falling back into the openings in steep dives, and the noise repeats in reverse.

No one could live here now. It would be impossible to sleep, speak or think in a normal way. The diggers presumably lived here before the bats came, or with far fewer of them. Perhaps the colonies grew as the people left. Perhaps they were the reason the people left.

## Slow Rules

Everything here changes, but very slowly.

The guano rises, a few millimeters a year, filling the city from the bottom. In the deepest wards, the lower dwelling-tiers are already buried, and only the rounded tops of doorways show above the drifts, like the backs of whales. Given enough time, perhaps a hundred thousand years, the whole city will be packed solid with dung and dust, its chambers turned into a layered deposit, the bats having gradually lifted themselves to the very ceiling on the accumulated record of their own lives.

The ceilings fail, one at a time, perhaps once in a human lifetime. Each collapse opens a new court and lets light into a new ward. Within a few decades of each fall the first lichens arrive, then the ferns, then a fig seedling. So the gardens migrate slowly across the city as the roof comes down, and the oldest courts, open longest, are the most overgrown, choked with roots and grasses and centuries of wind-blown seed.

The fig roots press deeper each year. They will eventually find water, somewhere far below, and when they do, those trees will grow into giants.

The seasons turn in migrations. In the cold months many of the bats leave for somewhere warmer, and for a time the city grows almost quiet, a near-silence of crickets and dry wind. In spring the colonies return, and the noise comes back like a tide.

## The Feeling of the Place

It is dim, loud, dry, cool, overgrown, crowded and empty all at once.

You stand in an open court up to your waist in fragrant grass. Around you rise three tiers of carved doorways, softened to the curves of something organic, like a honeycomb or a skull. Grey fig roots run down every wall. The sky is a strip overhead, pale and far away. The air smells of thyme and ammonia. Every niche is full of nesting birds, and every inch of the dark beyond the court roars.

It is a city in every structural sense. It has streets, homes, plazas, public halls, avenues and neighborhoods. It is simply no longer a city of people. It became a city of bats, birds and beetles, which live here as densely and loudly as any human city, while plants take slow root in the places where the roof gave way to light.

It has been like this for a very long time, and it will stay like this for a very long time yet.
