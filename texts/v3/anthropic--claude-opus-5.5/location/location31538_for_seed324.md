# Gallery Four

## The Approach

There is a door in the downstream face of the dam, about forty meters below the crest. It is steel, painted a green that has already started to chalk. Nothing leads to it except a cantilevered catwalk bolted to the concrete, with grating so open that you can see straight down through your boots to the tailrace, three hundred meters below, where the water comes out of the powerhouse white and furious.

The dam is only four years old. It plugs a notch between two granite shoulders high in the range, above the tree line, where the air is thin and wet. The concrete still has the pale, faintly blue cast of a new pour. Its arrises are sharp enough to cut a hand. Formwork seams run across the face in perfect horizontal lines, one every meter and a half, and each seam holds a bead of water that has not yet decided which way to run.

The door has a wheel instead of a handle. When you turn it, the seal gives with a sound you feel in your molars more than hear. Then the noise hits you.

## The Noise

Gallery Four is a drainage and inspection corridor that runs the width of the dam inside its body. It is two meters wide and two and a half high, arched at the top, and it follows the curve of the dam in a long, gentle bend. You can never see more than about sixty meters of it at once. That is as far as the bend allows and as far as the light reaches. The gallery is enormously long, but you only ever stand inside one short, lit stretch of it, so it feels like a small room with no end.

The sound is not one sound.

Beneath everything is the reservoir. It presses against the upstream face three meters away, through solid concrete, and the whole mass of the dam carries its pressure as a sub-bass drone. It isn't audible so much as present. It is in the handrail and the floor, and in the water in your eyes.

Above that is the powerhouse. Its four turbines sit at the foot of the dam, but their vibration climbs the concrete and arrives here as a steady hum at mains frequency. It is faintly sour, like a note held too long on a harmonium. When the grid demand shifts and a unit is brought on or taken off, the hum changes pitch over several minutes, and you notice it the way you notice a change in your own heartbeat.

On top of that, for about half of every day in the wet season, is the spillway. The spillway chute runs down the left abutment only fifteen meters from the gallery's far end. When the radial gates open, the water hits the chute and tears itself into spray, and the gallery becomes an instrument. The sound comes through the concrete, through the drainage pipes and the ventilation shafts, and it fills every cubic centimeter of air. You cannot hear your own voice, and you cannot hear your own thoughts in words. Operators who work here in season learn to communicate by flashlight and by tapping on the handrail in a simple code. Some of them say that after a shift they hear nothing for an hour, then the spillway again, then nothing.

Layered through all of this is the water inside the walls, and this is the sound that belongs to the gallery itself.

## The Weeping

A dam is not watertight, and it is not meant to be. Water from the reservoir works its way slowly through the concrete and through the rock of the foundation. The gallery is there to catch it. Relief holes drilled into the floor and the upstream wall let the seepage out before it can build up pressure where it isn't wanted. There is one hole every three meters, each a pipe mouth the width of a thumb, and each one drips, trickles, or spurts at its own rate. Each has a small brass tag stamped with a number.

The water runs into a gutter along the downstream side of the floor. The gutter is open, about a hand's width across, and it flows quickly toward a weir box near the center of the gallery. There the water drops over a steel V-notch into a sump, and the depth over the notch is read by a float gauge. That number is phoned down to the powerhouse every eight hours. It is one of the dam's vital signs. If it rises too far, too fast, people will come.

The seepage is cold, a few degrees above freezing, and it smells of stone and iron. The gutter is loud even under everything else. It makes a thin, rushing sound very close to your feet, the only sound in the gallery that seems small enough to be yours.

The walls weep as well. The concrete is still curing, and it will keep curing, slowly, for decades. The reaction that hardens cement gives off heat, and in a structure this massive the heat has nowhere to go. The core of the dam is still several degrees warmer than the mountain air. Warm concrete meets cold seepage and cold ventilation, and the gallery breathes. On most days a low haze hangs just under the arch. When the ventilation fans cycle on, the haze rolls along the corridor in a slow wave. When they cycle off, it settles and beads, and the ceiling drips in fat, irregular drops that land on the grating, on the handrail, and on the back of your neck.

Where the relief holes have run longest, the first mineral formations are beginning. Seepage water dissolves lime from the young concrete and deposits it again where it drips. Beneath some of the older holes there are straws of white calcite a few millimeters long, fragile as eggshell. On the floor beneath them are shallow rimstone pools the size of a coin. In a limestone cave these would take thousands of years. Here they have taken four. The engineers watch them closely, because each straw is a record of the dam slowly dissolving itself. It is a very slow process, measured in centuries, but it is not nothing.

## The Light

There is one lamp every twelve meters. Each is a caged sodium fixture bolted to the crown of the arch, and each throws a cone of amber-orange light that barely reaches the next. Between the cones there is a gray dimness where the concrete looks colorless and wet. Under each lamp the concrete glows the color of weak tea.

The lamps are powered directly from the station's auxiliary supply. When a turbine trips or a load is thrown off, they all dim for a fraction of a second, then surge. You see this ripple run along the gallery, lamp after lamp, around the bend, and a moment later you feel the hum shift in the floor.

Under the haze, each lamp wears a halo. When the fans are on and the mist is moving, the halos stretch and pulse. On spillway days the vibration shakes the condensation loose from the fixtures, and each lamp sheds a small, steady rain of glowing droplets through its own cone of light.

## What Grows

Almost nothing.

There is no soil in Gallery Four, not a grain of it. Everything is concrete, steel, brass, copper cable, or PVC. But the water that comes through the wall has passed through the reservoir, and the reservoir carries spores. Where the conditions are right, something has begun.

In the gutter, under the lamps, there are streaks of green algae. They grow only in the lit cones, never between them, so the gutter is striped: green, gray, green, gray, following the lamps down the gallery like a pulse made visible.

Near the door, where a little daylight comes through when it is opened and the air is fresher, there is a moss. It is a single cushion, dense and dark, perhaps the size of a folded hand, growing on the lip of a relief hole. Its water supply never stops. Someone has written the date they first noticed it on the wall beside it in grease pencil, and below that, in a different hand, a second date and the word *bigger*.

Deep in the gallery, where no daylight has ever reached, there is a patch of pale film on the downstream wall. It is bacterial rather than plant, faintly slick, glistening under one particular lamp. It doesn't look like life. It looks like the concrete is sweating something.

## What Doesn't Live Here

There are no animals. There are no insects, no spiders, no cave crickets. The door seals, the air is too cold, and the vibration is too constant. A moth once came in on an operator's jacket, and it was found days later dead on the grating beneath the third lamp, beaded with condensation, perfectly intact. Nothing had eaten it, because nothing lives here to eat.

## The Presences

The gallery is sparse, and whatever is in it was put there deliberately.

Every thirty meters or so there is a piezometer station. It is a steel box on the upstream wall with a glass face and a pressure gauge inside, its needle trembling, reading the water pressure in the foundation rock beneath the dam. Each box has a clipboard hanging from a hook, its pages soft and warped with moisture. The readings are written in pencil, because ink runs.

Near the weir box there is a telephone in a hooded steel cabinet. It is a magneto set with a hand crank, chosen because it will work when everything else fails. Above it, a strobe light will flash red if the powerhouse calls, because a ringing bell would be inaudible.

There is a single chair, steel-framed with a vinyl seat, placed beside the weir box facing the V-notch. Nobody is supposed to sit in the gallery for long, but somebody has to watch the notch during a flood event, sometimes for a whole shift. The chair's seat is cracked. A folded towel lies on it, damp.

Pendulums hang in vertical shafts that pass through the gallery. Weighted steel wires run from near the crest down through the whole height of the dam. Each passes through the gallery inside a shaft with a small window, and behind the glass is a reading table with a crosshair and a vernier scale. The wire hangs perfectly still against the crosshair. It is the only thing in the gallery that doesn't vibrate. When the reservoir is full, the dam leans very slightly downstream, a few millimeters, and the table moves around the wire, which is to say that the wire, by staying still, measures the whole dam moving.

## Rhythms

The gallery runs on overlapping cycles.

The ventilation fans run fifteen minutes in every hour. When they start, the mist moves and the dripping lessens. When they stop, the mist settles and the ceiling rains.

Every eight hours, someone walks the gallery with a flashlight and a clipboard, reads the gauges, reads the notch, cranks the phone, and walks back out. The rest of the time, the gallery is empty and lit, and the needles tremble for no one.

Grid demand moves the turbine load up in the morning and evening and down at night, and the hum rises and falls with it. The lamps flicker on every major change.

The spillway opens in the wet season, mostly in the afternoons when meltwater and rain from the upper catchment arrive together. The gallery shifts from loud to overwhelming. The mist shakes out of the air, the drips turn into a constant patter, and the pendulum wires sometimes develop a very faint shiver. Their movement is recorded, discussed, and considered normal.

The reservoir itself rises and falls over the year. When it's high, the seepage in the gutter runs faster, the rimstone pools overflow, the piezometer needles climb, and the dam leans a little further. When it's low, the gallery quiets slightly and the concrete dries in patches. Those dry patches are the only places where you can see the concrete's true color: very pale, very clean, with the fine sharp texture of the formwork still pressed into it, as if the forms had been stripped yesterday.

## The Place Itself

What Gallery Four becomes, if you stay in it, is less a corridor than a chamber inside a body. The dam is a single enormous object holding back a lake, and it's alive in the ways a made thing can be. It is still warm from its own making. It breathes mist. It weeps from hundreds of small wounds, all of them drilled on purpose. It hums. It leans with the seasons. It is growing its first stone, and it keeps a pale scum of life on its skin, a stripe of green under every lamp, and one moss by the door.

It is all made, and it's all still new, with sharp edges and fresh surfaces. The pressure from the other side of the wall never stops, and the sound never stops either. The sound is so total that after a while it stops being noise and becomes a kind of weather, a fact of the air, like cold or wet. The water in the gutter runs past your feet, the only small sound in the world, carrying the dam very slowly away.
