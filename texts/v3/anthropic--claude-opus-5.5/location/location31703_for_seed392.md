# The Wet Afternoon of the Made Plain

**First impression**

The plain is low and wide and very bright, and it is still damp. It looks like the ground after a hard rain, dark in the hollows, glistening on the ridges, steaming a little where the sun lies on it. Then you kneel and put your palm down, and the ground is clay. It is not soil or mud. It is worked clay, smoothed flat by something broad and patient, and it still carries the faint parallel scoring of a tool that was drawn across it in long overlapping strokes. The strokes run to the horizon in every direction, like the grain of an enormous board.

Nothing here happened. Everything here was *done*.

---

**The ground**

The clay is the red-brown of a river bank, cool a finger's depth down and warm on top. It gives slightly underfoot and holds a heel-print perfectly, sharp-edged, the way fresh clay does. The print doesn't slump and doesn't dry. Hours later, if hours are the right measure, it is exactly as crisp as when you made it. Nothing here softens. Every edge in the landscape has the keenness of work finished moments ago: the lips of channels, the shoulders of terraces, the rims of basins. Rain has never rounded them, because rain has never fallen. The water was brought.

You can see how. Across the plain, spaced so far apart that they are dots at the edge of sight, stand jars. They are tall, unglazed, taller than a person, each tipped slightly on a wedge of fired clay so that its mouth leans toward the ground. From each mouth hangs a thread of water, poured in the same instant forever. The thread is not falling in any ordinary sense. It is a clear rope, rippled and glinting, joining lip to ground, and where it meets the clay it has cut a small, perfect pool with scalloped edges. From the pools, channels run outward. Someone scratched them with the end of a stick, and you can see where the stick skipped on a pebble and left a little stutter in the line. The channels fill shallow basins, and the basins spill into lower basins, and so the plain is laced with standing water no deeper than a hand, bright as foil under the sky.

The water is warm as blood, and it doesn't evaporate. The air above it shimmers and is heavy with moisture, and droplets bead on every cool surface. Yet the basins never drop by a hair. The wetness here is not weather. It is a material, like the clay, placed where it was wanted.

---

**The light**

The sky is white-gold and cloudless. The sun stands a little past the highest point, where it would be in the first hour after noon, and it does not move. It is too bright to look at. But if you shade your eyes and glance at its edge, the edge is not quite round. It has a slight flattening on one side, like a disc of beaten metal where the hammer came down a little too hard, and a faint ring around it that might be glare or might be the burr of a rim.

Because the sun doesn't move, shadows here are fixed. They are short, dark, and hard-edged, and they point the same way everywhere, all slightly northeast of straight down. The creatures of the plain know this. Things live in the shadows as if the shadows were solid furniture: under jar-lips, under the overhangs of terraces, in the slot on the shady side of every ridge. Step into one and the temperature drops like stepping into a cellar. Step out and the heat lands on your shoulders with weight, a dense wet heat that makes skin shine within moments.

---

**The groves**

The vegetation is dense. It stands in islands, with great open reaches of wet clay between them.

The nearest grove rises out of the flats like a green bruise. Walk toward it (it takes longer than it looks; distances here are generous) and the green resolves into leaves, and the leaves are *cut*. Each one is a blade of some broad, pliant material, green-black and glossy, snipped from a larger sheet. Some edges are clean. Some show the tiny bitten serration of a knife that needed sharpening. The leaves are bound to stems with twists of fibre, and the stems are split cane, lashed in bundles to make trunks. The trunks are tied to one another at the height of a raised arm, so that the whole grove is a single structure, a basket the size of a village.

And it is alive.

The leaves are cool and have a pulse. They turn slightly toward the fixed sun and sweat water from their undersides, which drips, one bead at a long interval, into the clay below. Where the lashings wrap the cane, the fibre has sunk in and swollen, the way a vine grips a fence post over decades. But there have been no decades. The lashings were tied exactly this way, already swollen, already gripping. Fruits hang in the grove: fist-sized, heavy, rust-orange. Look closely and each is a hollow form of pressed leaf, stitched along one seam with a single running stitch. Split one and it is full of wet pulp that tastes of salt and something like honey, and seeds that are small fired beads, each with a hole through it for stringing.

No grove has grown. None is growing. Every leaf is at the size it was cut. Pluck one and the gap remains forever, and the plucked leaf stays green in your hand, pulsing faintly, as long as you care to carry it.

The groves are far apart, often an hour's walk between them across the bright wet flats. Each is different. One is all fronds, fringed with a comb and flaring like cut paper. One is low, a knee-high thicket of knotted cord, each knot sprouting a single pale blossom of shaved wood curled with a blade. One tall grove, visible from almost anywhere, is made of long ribbons of green bark hung from a lattice of poles. It moves, very slightly, though there is no wind. It sways the way a curtain sways after someone has passed through it, a motion left over from its hanging.

---

**The ridges**

Here and there the plain is broken by ridges of stone, low, never more than a few times a person's height, and they are the sharpest things in the world. They are knapped. Each is a spine of dark glassy rock, shaped by blow after blow into overlapping conchoidal scallops, like the edge of a hand-axe grown to the scale of a hill. The flake scars are fresh and lustrous, the edges thin as leaves and translucent where the light comes through them. Walk along the crest carefully. Run a fingertip along an edge without thinking and it opens you.

At the base of each ridge lie the flakes that came off in the making, a scree of thin knives heaped like fallen leaves. They have never moved. Some still lie at angles that shouldn't be stable, balanced on their edges.

The ridges are where the heat gathers. The dark stone drinks the sun and gives it back, and the air above the crests wavers so hard that the far side of the plain seems to swim. Water beads on the shadowed faces, from condensation or from something weeping out of the stone. It forms rivulets that run down the scallops in fixed paths, cut no deeper by their running.

---

**The animals**

There are a great many of them, and nearly all are made.

In the basins live frogs of plaited grass, olive and gold, tightly woven. Their throats are thin enough that you can see light through the weave when they swell. They sit in the warm shallows with their eyes just above water. The eyes are polished seeds. When they swim, you can see the little tail-ends of grass where the plaiting was finished off, trailing from their haunches like the ends of a bracelet.

Birds cross the open reaches, low and unhurried. Their bodies are carved from pale wood and their feathers are split reeds fanned and bound at the root. Their wings make the only regular sound on the plain: a dry, soft *fff*, *fff*, like someone riffling a bundle of straw. They land on jar rims and drink from the poured threads, dipping their beaks into the frozen rope of water, and it gives, and closes behind.

In the grove-shadows there are things like lizards, shaped from clay that was pinched rather than modelled. You can see the finger-dents along their flanks, and on some the whorled print of a thumb pressed into the soft of the back. They lie flat against the cool undersides of leaves and do not blink, because nobody gave them eyelids.

There are insects. There are more insects than anything else, and they are the most astonishing. Beetles are ground from seed-husks, polished to a shine, with legs of bristle. Dragonflies have bodies of thin twig and wings of flaked mica so delicate that the sun shows every lamination. Tiny bees are pellets of wax with feathered edges, and they move between the shaved-wood blossoms with great seriousness, though the blossoms have no nectar. Or the bees carry none away. It is hard to tell, and nothing accumulates.

The animals eat. Frogs take beetles, birds take dragonflies, and something unseen takes the clay lizards, because once in a long while you find a patch of shadow with only a lizard-shaped depression in the clay and a scatter of pinched crumbs. And yet the numbers never change. Stand by a basin and count the frogs, and look away, and count again, and there are always nineteen. The one that was eaten is still there, sitting where it sat. Or the one that ate is also the one that was eaten. The plain does not seem troubled by this, and the animals don't either.

---

**The quiet**

It is so quiet. The heat seems to press sound flat. There is the *fff* of reed wings. Very rarely, one of the grass frogs makes a sound, a short creak like a basket being lifted. There is the drip under the groves, one bead into clay, a sound so small you hear it with your teeth. The poured threads of water make no noise at all where they meet their pools, which is the most unsettling thing on the plain once you notice it. Water should talk. This water arrives in silence.

Your own footsteps make a soft sucking press in the clay, very loud by comparison. You find yourself walking gently.

---

**The rule under the rules**

The longer you stay, the more one fact settles over everything. The world was made, and the making is not finished, and also it is over.

Every surface carries tool marks at the freshness of the moment they were made. The clay is damp the way clay is damp while the potter is still working it. The lashings are tight the way cord is tight just before the knot is pulled. The water is caught mid-pour. The grove of ribbons is caught mid-sway. The sun is caught just past noon, at the hour when a worker straightens up and looks at what they've done.

This is not a ruin. No one abandoned it, and nothing has decayed, eroded, or grown over. It is a single held instant of work, stretched to the size of a plain. Inside that instant, life goes on in loops that return to where they began: eating without depletion, dripping without draining, swaying without wind. The ecology is real. The frogs are hungry and the lizards are afraid. But it runs on a stage that never changes its set.

Some things are not quite finished, and those are where reality thins. At the edges of certain groves, leaves fade from green-black to a pale, unfilled outline, like a leaf sketched with a scratch in the clay, waiting to be cut. A dragonfly has one mica wing and, on the other side, only a notch where a wing should seat. It flies anyway, lopsided, perfectly steady, with the missing wing somehow understood. Out on the flats there is a basin scratched but not filled. Its outline is crisp, and inside it the clay is dry, the only dry clay anywhere. When the reed-birds fly over it they rise, just slightly, as if over a wall.

And there are places where the grain of the ground changes. The long tool-strokes stop and a different stroke begins, at a different angle, a little shorter, as if the tool changed hands or the hand grew tired.

---

**The far side**

The plain is open, low, and wide, but it has an end. If you walk toward the sun for long enough, past four groves and two ridges and more jars than you bother to count, the clay begins to rise in a long, gentle, slightly uneven curve, like the inside wall of a very large bowl. The tool-strokes run up it. On the rise the strokes become fingermarks: broad, dragging grooves, four side by side, then five, a hand pulled up through wet clay to lift the wall.

At the top of the rim, very small against the white-gold sky and too distant to see clearly, there is a figure.

It stands with its back to the plain and one arm raised, holding something that catches the light. It might be a blade, a stick, a jar. It hasn't moved. The raised arm is held at the top of its motion, just before whatever comes next: the cut, the stroke, the pour. The long, faintly flattened sun hangs above and a little behind it, and from the rim of the plain it is hard to say whether the figure is looking at the sun or holding it up.

The birds do not fly that far. The frogs face away from it. In the shadows under the jars, the clay lizards lie with their thumbprinted backs toward the rim.

Everything here is waiting for the arm to come down, and the arm never does. So the water stays warm, the leaves stay cool, and the heel-print you left at the edge of the first basin stays exactly, sharply, freshly as you made it.
