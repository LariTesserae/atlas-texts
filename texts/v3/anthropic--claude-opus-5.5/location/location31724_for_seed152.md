# The Breathing Hole

From above there's almost nothing to see. The lava field runs out in every direction, low and nearly flat, a few dozen meters above a sea you can smell but not hear. The rock is young. It still has the shapes it had when it was liquid: ropes and folds of pahoehoe, the wrinkled skin of a pudding that set too fast, glassy in places, rust-bruised in others where iron has begun to oxidize. Nothing has worn it down yet. No edge has been rounded by rain. Step on a crust and it may ring hollow, or it may crack like a sugar shell over a cavity the size of a fist. Very little grows here. A few pioneer ferns sit in cracks, and a lichen that looks like spilled ash.

Then there is a hole. It is roughly oval, perhaps six meters across, where the roof of a lava tube fell in. Warm air comes up out of it. On a cool morning it steams faintly, like a mouth in winter. Birds cross the field toward it in low straight lines and drop in without slowing.

## The Room

Below the rim the walls fall about twelve meters to a floor of broken roof-slabs, then the tube runs off in both directions into dark. The pit itself is the only lit part, a single room with the sky as its lamp. The light comes down gray-green and diffuse, filtered through moisture. It is never bright. Even at noon the corners hold a brown dusk, and the bottom of the pit feels like the inside of a closed hand.

It is warm down here, warmer than the surface. The rock remembers heat. Somewhere below, the flow that made this tube is still cooling, and the warmth rises through the basalt the way heat rises through a stone that has sat by a fire. Touch the lower walls and they are the temperature of skin. Near the floor it is closer to a bath.

That warmth meets the damp sea air pulled in through the hole, and the result is the pit's water. There is no stream, no pool to speak of, no real rain. The water condenses. Every surface sweats. Beads gather on the underside of every ledge, swell, and let go. The floor is dry in some places, soaked in others, depending on where the drips have chosen to fall. Where they fall steadily they have begun, only just, to make a mark: a shallow dimple in a slab, a faint pale ring of mineral. These are the first scars of erosion on rock that has never been eroded. In a thousand years they will be basins. Now they are fingerprints.

## The City

What makes this place a settlement, and not just a hole, is on the walls.

The upper third of the pit, the part out of reach of anything that climbs from the floor and sheltered by the overhang of the rim, is covered in nests. They are small translucent cups, pale as candle-wax, each glued to the rock by one edge. Cave swiftlets build them from their own saliva, laid down in strands and hardened in the damp air, with a few feathers and fern fibers worked in. There are thousands of them. They sit in rows along the natural bands of the lava, where each successive flow left a slight shelf, so the colony reads like stories of a building, floor above floor. Where the rock is steepest the nests stack directly on one another, new cups cemented to the rims of old ones. The oldest have gone amber and brittle. The newest are nearly clear and catch what little light there is.

It is a real city, with density and districts. The prime addresses are the dry ones under the overhang. The outer rows, closer to the drip lines, are newer and more often abandoned, their cups softened by moisture until they slump. There is a gradient of age and quality running down the wall. Nobody planned it, but everyone built it, and it has the look of something organized: roads of guano streaking pale down the rock beneath the busiest rows, and gaps where a section failed and fell and has not been rebuilt.

The swiftlets are nearly silent. In the lit part of the pit they fly by sight, and they come and go with a faint rush of air, a soft ticking when they land. Only when they pass into the dark tube do they begin to click, a dry rapid sound like a fingernail on a comb. It's a sound for finding walls. It is so quiet you notice it mostly as a texture in the silence. Most of the day the pit is hushed enough that you can hear individual drips land. Each makes a small flat *tap*, and there is one every few seconds, somewhere.

## The Floor

Down on the broken slabs, the pit is a garden, though a scattered one. The rock is too new for soil. What little there is has come from the city above: guano, fallen nest-wax, feathers, the husks of insects, blown dust. Wherever enough of this has gathered in a crack, and wherever the drips keep it moist, something grows, and it grows fast in the heat.

Ferns, mostly. There are tree-fern seedlings with fronds already longer than an arm, unrolling from brown-furred crosiers. Delicate filmy ferns only one cell thick, which live nowhere but air this wet, cling to the shaded walls like green tissue. Mosses lie in cushions so saturated they squelch. There are a few flowering things whose seeds arrived in birds: a shrub with glossy leaves and small red berries, a vine that has started up one wall toward the light and is already a third of the way there.

But between these there is a great deal of bare black rock. The green comes in islands. Each is dense and vivid, and the spaces between are raw. You can walk from one to the next and see exactly where life has taken hold and where it hasn't yet. The pit is lush in its pockets and empty everywhere else, and the emptiness is as much a part of it as the ferns.

## Who Else Lives Here

The swiftlets are the most visible residents, but they are not the most numerous.

On the walls among the nests live geckos, pale and half-translucent themselves, flat against the rock. They wait below the nest rows for the moths and small flies drawn to the guano. They hunt with small sudden lunges, and the only sound is the faint click of a jaw.

In the moss live pale crickets with enormous antennae. There are springtails in their millions, too small to see except as a shimmer when the moss is disturbed. There are isopods, and slender centipedes that come out after dark. Spiders string webs across the gaps between slabs, and every morning the webs are beaded with condensation so heavy they sag. The spiders shake them dry, and a little fine rain falls from each one.

In the dark stretch of tube just beyond the light there are cave-adapted things: blind, white, slow. They come to the edge of the pit to feed on what drifts down from the colony, then retreat.

Everything here is fed from above. The swiftlets hunt insects over the lava field and the sea all day, bring the energy home, and drop it as waste onto the floor, where it becomes soil, which becomes ferns and moss, which become crickets, which become centipedes and geckos. The pit is a funnel. It concentrates a wide, thin, poor landscape into one small rich room.

## The Cycle

The pit has a day, and it is a busy one, only quiet about it.

At dawn the air above the lava is cool, the pit is warm, and the hole breathes out. A slow column of mist rises from the opening. The swiftlets leave in it, pouring out in loose streams, and for an hour the city is nearly empty except for the nestlings. The drips are heaviest now, because the condensation formed overnight is all letting go.

Midday the surface bakes, the air equalizes, and the breathing stops. The pit goes still and close and humid. Geckos hunt. The spiders rebuild. The ferns hold their fronds out toward the gray light.

In late afternoon the sea wind picks up, pushes damp air down into the hole, and the walls start sweating again. Now and then a brief shower crosses the lava field. Rain falls straight through the opening in a single shaft, visible as a column of silver in the dimness, and lands on the central slabs. It hisses faintly on the warmest rock. For a few minutes the floor has puddles. Within an hour they are gone into cracks.

At dusk the swiftlets come home. They come all at once, a river of them dropping out of the sky into the hole. This is the loudest the pit ever gets: thousands of wingbeats making a sound like wind through dry grass, the clicking starting up as they vanish into the dark end of the tube and then return to find their cups. Then it settles. By full dark it is quiet again, and the centipedes come out, and the drips resume counting.

## The Rules of the Place

Everything here is recent. The rock, the soil, the colony, even the light: all of it is new, and most of it is still being made. The walls are unweathered. The first drip-basins are only now being cut. The soil is being laid down one feather at a time. The swiftlet city grows by a row every few seasons. The vine climbs toward the rim.

Nothing is finished. In a century the pit may be a fern-choked grotto with a stream running from its floor, its walls softened, its city ten times the size. Or the next flow may fill it. For now it is in the first chapter: a warm, damp, dim, quiet room in raw new stone, where one population's architecture has become everyone else's ground.

From the surface, all you see is a little mist rising from a hole in black rock, and birds going in.
