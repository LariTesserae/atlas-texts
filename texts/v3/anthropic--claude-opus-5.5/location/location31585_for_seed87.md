# The Pickle-Lake Warren

It begins with a crack you would never notice: a hairline seam in the mud of a cold continental slope, far below where daylight gives out. A little warmer than the surrounding sea, a little saltier, the water seeping from it carries a faint smell of struck matches and rot. Follow the seam down through a throat of soft grey sediment no wider than a hand, and it opens into a room.

The room is about the size of a small chapel, a hollow inside the seafloor rather than on it. There is no single ceiling. The walls, floor, and roof are all one continuous surface of pale, lumpy stone, and that stone is still being made. This is the first thing to understand about the place: nothing here is finished, least of all the ground.

## The Rock That Is Still Arriving

Methane and brine seep up through the sediment. Where they meet the seawater, microbes eat the gas, and the chemistry of their eating makes carbonate fall out of solution. It settles as a crust, then a shell, then stone. The chamber exists because this crust formed a roof over a gas pocket, and the pocket slowly filled with water. The walls are only a few decades old. In places they are still soft enough to dent with a fingernail, chalky and faintly warm, like a bone that hasn't finished setting.

Up close the stone isn't smooth. It grows in blisters, knuckles, and frilled lips where seep currents run, and in fine hair-thin needles where the water is still. Run a hand along a wall and you'll find places where it gives slightly, crumbling into a paste that re-hardens within days. The geology is embryonic: a place that has not yet decided its final shape, and is being argued into one.

## The Lake Inside the Sea

The floor of the chamber is not floor. It is a lake.

Brine is heavier than seawater, so the densest, saltiest seep water has pooled in the lowest part of the hollow and lies there with a true surface, a skin, a shoreline. Under the full weight of the ocean there is a second, smaller sea, and it has waves. When something disturbs the chamber, slow ripples cross the brine surface in lazy, oily undulations, rolling in slow motion and lapping the stone banks in near silence.

The surface is faintly mirrored. Seen from above, the lake holds an upside-down reflection of the chamber in dim, warped greens, and the boundary shimmers like heat haze where the two waters refuse to mix.

The lake is death. It holds no oxygen, and its salt is ferocious. Anything that sinks through the mirror suffocates, then is pickled and held nearly perfect in the cold, sometimes for decades. The bottom of the lake is a gallery of the preserved: curled shrimp, a drifted eel, sprays of worm tubes, all hanging or lying in the gloom exactly as they fell. The inhabitants of the chamber avoid the surface the way we avoid cliff edges.

Around this lake, on its shores and up the walls to the ceiling, stands a city.

## The City

There are no tools here and never have been. There is not even a single species you could call the builder. The city is built by bodies.

It is a dense, layered warren of structures, most of them no taller than a finger, crowding every surface of the chamber in tiers. Some are tubes: parchment-grey worm casings cemented from mud and their own secretions, clustered in their thousands like organ pipes, leaning together into colonnades. Some are cells: honeycombs of glued sediment grains, each chamber the size of a fingernail, stacked forty deep along the shore. Some are bridges, strands of mucus-stiffened silk strung between towers and hardened over months into pale threads of mineral. Some are domes: blisters of carbonate that grew around bacterial colonies and were then hollowed out and occupied.

Look long enough and the organization appears. There are thoroughfares, channels between the tube-forests along which traffic moves at the same heights and in the same directions. There are districts. The shoreline belongs to scavengers and the dense honeycomb of amphipods. The mid-walls are the tube-worm quarter, stiff with tentacled crowns. The ceiling is a pendant city of hanging structures, where the most delicate architecture clings upside down to the youngest, softest rock. There are thresholds too: narrow gates in the worm colonnades where larger animals must squeeze through in single file, and where predators wait.

### The Masons

The most prolific builders are small pale crustaceans, each about the length of a rice grain. Divers might call them lathe-shrimp. They carry mud in their forelimbs, work it with a glue from glands in their legs, and press it into walls grain by grain. Masses of them work in shifts, crowding every construction face so tightly that a new wall seems to extrude itself out of a shimmering, twitching fringe.

They do not build only for themselves. Their cells are occupied by worms, snails, juvenile fish, and small translucent brittle stars, and they don't seem to care. They build, and whatever arrives moves in.

### The Rule of the Stone

Here the place becomes strange.

The carbonate that makes the chamber's walls precipitates wherever the chemistry is right, and the masons' glue makes it right. So stone forms preferentially along their joints and seams. Every wall the lathe-shrimp build becomes a template, and the rock grows over it, thickening it into permanence.

It does not stop when the builders do. In districts the masons abandoned years ago, the stone keeps going. It extends walls along the lines they were heading, closes arches they left open, and adds new tiers in the same proportions, with the same slight lean, the same cell size. Whole quarters of the city were never built by anything alive. They are the rock's continuation of an idea it absorbed. These ghost districts are smoother and more regular than the living ones, a little too perfect, like a handwriting imitated by someone who never understood the words. When the masons return to them, they move in without hesitation, and after a while it becomes impossible to tell which walls are theirs.

The chamber is slowly learning to be a city on its own. The seam in the mud above it is gradually filling with that pale, patterned stone.

## Light

There is no daylight. What light exists belongs to the living, and it is dim, never bright enough to read by, but everywhere.

The bacterial mats glow faintly: white, sulfurous films on the stone near the seeps, giving off a cold, milky luminescence like moonlight through wet paper. These mats are the closest thing the chamber has to vegetation. There are only a few of them, in patches and ribbons, tended and grazed. They grow in long, feathery filaments that wave in the seep currents like a sparse meadow, and in the larger colonies they form pale tussocks that the snails crop in slow, even rows.

The animals make the rest of the light. Worm crowns pulse violet when touched. Tiny swimming copepods leave blue sparks when startled. Along the thoroughfares, some species flash in rhythm, and the flashing spreads like a rumour, one tube-cluster to the next, so that waves of faint light run across whole walls of the city and die out on the far side of the lake.

The lake surface catches all of it and sends it back doubled and wavering. On the busiest nights, if the word applies, the chamber seems to float between two cities of dim sparks.

## Silence

The chamber makes almost no sound. The water is still; the seeps hiss too faintly to hear. Nothing here has lungs or a voice. The inhabitants communicate by light, by touch, by chemical traces in the water, and by the fine pressure-waves of moving bodies, felt rather than heard.

Occasionally there is a noise. A bubble of methane escapes the sediment and rises through the roof with a small wet tick. A fragment of soft ceiling gives way and falls through the water, turning slowly, and settles on the lake surface without a splash, resting briefly on the mirror before sinking. These are the loudest events in the city's life. Otherwise there is only the enormous weight of the ocean pressing down, a silence so complete it feels structural.

## Life in Motion

Though quiet, the chamber is never still. It is crowded and busy, with many processes running at once.

**The seep tides.** Gas and brine rise in pulses, some daily and some irregular. When a pulse comes, warmer water spreads along the ceiling, the mats brighten, the worm crowns bloom open, and the whole city leans toward the vents like a crowd turning toward a sound. When a big pulse overfills the lake, the brine creeps up the shore by a finger's width. The lowest cells of the shoreline flood, and their inhabitants either flee upward in a scrambling tide or are drowned and pickled where they sit. The lake's edge is a tidemark of preserved bodies, glinting faintly.

**The shoreline scavengers.** Along the brine edge work patient animals: pale hagfish-like eels, long, eyeless, and slow; and small ruddy crabs. They fish at the boundary, reaching tentatively into the lake to snatch pickled carrion, then withdrawing before the brine can take them. It is dangerous work. Some reach too far and join the gallery below.

**The hunters.** Predators patrol the thoroughfares. A translucent ribbon-worm, several times the length of a hand and thin as a thread, glides along the colonnades and slips its head into cells to pull out sleeping amphipods. Small ghostly octopuses den in the ghost districts, where the walls are strongest, emerging to hunt in bursts of cold colour. At the colonnade gates there is always something waiting: a tube-anemone, a patient fish, an arrangement of glassy spines.

**The builders' cycles.** Construction comes in surges tied to the seeps and to the mason swarms' breeding. After a spawning, countless new lathe-shrimp swarm the walls, and whole new tiers appear in days, shimmering with activity. Between surges the city is maintained, repaired, and slowly overgrown by its own stone.

**The drift.** From the seam above, marine snow sifts down into the chamber in slow, fine flakes of organic matter, food falling from a world the inhabitants will never see. Worm crowns catch it. Mats absorb it. The lake receives what's left.

## Arriving and Leaving

The chamber is almost sealed. Its only openings are the seam above and a few porous places in the walls where sediment still meets stone, and they are narrowing. New arrivals come as larvae drifting in on seep currents, or as small animals that wriggle down the seam and find themselves in the city. Few leave.

In time, perhaps a few centuries, the rock will close the seam. The chamber will seal. Inside, the city will go on in its silence: the masons building, the stone continuing their walls, the lake keeping its dead, the dim light rolling across the tiers in waves no one outside will ever see. And it will become harder and harder to say where the rock ends and the city begins.
