# The Held Country

## I. The Height That Remembers

It is high, and there is nothing underneath to explain why.

There were mountains here once, so long ago that the word has lost its meaning. The mountains wore down grain by grain over ages that do not count properly, and while they wore, something grew into the spaces the stone left behind. Roots followed the cracks. Where the rock crumbled, root took its place, thread by thread, until the living mass was holding the shape the stone no longer could. Then the stone was gone, and the height remained.

The Held Country is a plateau of plant. It is a tableland the size of a continent, lifted two or three miles above whatever lowlands lie beneath the cloud. Its bulk is root, pressed and braided and grown through itself so many times that deep down it has the density of hardwood and the grain of slate. There is almost no rock. What there is appears as smooth pebbles, rare and pale, carried slowly upward through the mat over thousands of years the way a splinter works its way out of skin.

The land is nearly flat. Long, low swells run across it, sometimes a hundred miles from crest to crest, and a walker would not notice them except by how the fog pools. These are the ghosts of ridgelines. The whole country keeps the faint memory of a range, the way a pillow keeps the shape of a head.

## II. The Roof and the Breath

The Held Country is large enough to make its own weather, and the weather it makes is mostly a lid.

A permanent ceiling of cloud hangs perhaps a hundred feet above the tallest growth. It is thick, warm, and pearl-grey. The sun is only a brightening somewhere overhead, never a disc. Light comes down diffused and green-tinged and without shadows, so that a leaf and the space behind it seem to sit at the same distance. At midday it is about as bright as a room with the curtains drawn. At night it is fully dark, except for what glows.

The cycle is simple and it never breaks. Each evening the cloud lowers. It settles into the canopy, then into the undergrowth, until the country is submerged in fog so saturated it is almost liquid. Every surface beads. Water runs down stems in threads, and the whole land drinks. Each morning the plants exhale. The warmth held in the mat rises, the fog lifts back into its ceiling, and the country surfaces again, dripping.

It rarely rains in the ordinary sense. There is no need. The air here is already mostly water that hasn't decided to fall.

## III. The Layers

What the surface looks like depends on where along the long swells you stand, but it is always green on green on green. There is no bare ground anywhere, because there is no ground that isn't alive.

**The sward.** Underfoot is moss, deep and springy, in a hundred shades from bronze to near-black. It gives under weight and slowly returns. Where many feet have passed, the moss grows back paler, and these pale tracks last for centuries. The surface of the Held Country is scribbled with them: thin trails of silver-green crossing and recrossing, faint as frost patterns. It is a record of every animal that ever walked in a line.

**The stem-forest.** Above the sward rise clubmoss-like towers, some thirty or forty feet tall. They are scaled and unbranched and grow so close together that walking between them means turning sideways. They are not trees, since nothing here grew wood the way trees do. They are columns of tightly spiraled leaf. Tree-ferns open between them in slow fans. Everything is draped in something else: hanging mosses, filmy ferns thin as wet paper, lichens in beards and cups. Nearly every plant carries other plants on it, and those carry smaller ones in turn.

**The sponge.** Push an arm down through the sward and you reach the sponge: a waterlogged layer of dead and half-dead fiber, warm as bathwater, several yards thick. This is where the country stores its water. Squeeze a handful and it runs clear and faintly sweet.

**The weave.** Below the sponge lies the living root, the true body of the land. It runs for miles downward, braided so tight a knife would skid off it. Channels thread through it, some as fine as hair and some as wide as a throat, and water moves through them slowly and constantly. It wicks upward toward the surface and seeps downward toward the edges. The whole plateau is a single vast capillary system.

Whether it is one organism or many that have forgotten the difference is not a question the place answers.

## IV. The Eyes

Here and there, usually in the shallow troughs between swells, the surface opens into round pools. These are the eyes.

They are perfectly dark, walled with root, and very still. Their edges are ringed with pale flowers that grow nowhere else. Dropped stones don't audibly land. Some eyes are a few yards across and some are the size of lakes. Their water is the color of steeped tea and so clear that for the first several fathoms the braided walls can be seen going down and down, until the dim light gives out.

The eyes breathe very slowly. Over a span of days the water level rises and falls by an inch. Occasionally, perhaps once a year somewhere in the whole country, an eye's walls give way. A section of weave softens and collapses inward, and a deep, soft *whumph* travels through the mat for miles. It is felt in the soles more than heard. Then the moss begins to close over the wound, and in a few decades there is only a slight dimple where it was.

## V. The Slow Tide

The Held Country rises and falls.

It does not happen in seasons. The land has none worth mentioning, being warm and wet always. It happens across decades. In wetter spans the weave swells with stored water and the entire plateau lifts, by the height of a person or two, across its whole breadth. In drier spans it settles. Nothing living here would ever perceive this directly. But the old pale paths show it. They buckle faintly, stretching and wrinkling as the surface beneath them expands and shrinks. The flowers at the eyes' edges mark it too: rings of them at different heights up the walls, like tide lines on a pier.

There is one other rhythm. Roughly every thirty-some years, a single plant flowers across the entire continent at once. It is a low, creeping thing, ordinarily invisible among the moss. For three weeks the whole Held Country turns a faint, dusty violet, and the air fills with a smell like wet pepper and honey. Then the flowers close, the seed falls into the sward, and the country goes back to green for another generation. Animals born just after a flowering will likely see only one more in their lives, if they see it at all.

## VI. The Rule of Stillness

The Held Country has one law, and every living thing obeys it or is absorbed.

**The mat grows over whatever does not move.**

It is not fast, but it is certain. A fallen feather is threaded with root-hair in a week. A dead animal is covered in a season and gone into the sponge within a year. Its bones are carried slowly downward and softened, their minerals taken up and drawn toward the surface again in the leaves. Nothing is buried here. Things are taken up, sideways and downward, into the body of the land.

So nothing large rests for long. The animals of the Held Country sleep in shifts, or sleep walking, or sleep on the move. The larger ones drift through their rest at a shuffle, heads low, and the mosses that begin to fur their flanks are shed with the next shake. An animal that lies down sick and does not rise will, within a month, be a green shape the size of itself. Within a year it will be a slight swell in the sward, flowering a little brighter than the moss around it.

This gives the whole fauna a certain restlessness. It is a quiet, unhurried, permanent motion.

## VII. The Many Small Presences

The land is quiet, but it is not empty. It is crowded in a way that takes a while to notice.

**The grazers** are the largest things. They are low, broad animals, heavy-bodied, with coats of coarse bristle in which small ferns take root and are tolerated. They move in loose herds of dozens along the old pale paths, cropping the sward as they go. Their broad feet press the moss down and stir the sponge beneath. They are the country's gardeners, though they don't know it. Where grazers have passed, the moss grows back richer. Where they haven't passed in a century, the stem-forest thickens toward impassable. They make almost no sound. Their only call is a long exhaled hum, felt as much as heard, which they use to keep the herd together in the evening fog.

**The walking birds** have forgotten flight. There is nowhere to fly to under a roof of cloud and among columns spaced a sideways step apart. They are long-legged, soft-feathered in moss colors, and step carefully through the stem-forest turning over the filmy ferns for grubs. Their wings are small and folded. They use them only for balance, and in displays. A male fans them wide in the dim light to show the one patch of pale feathering on each, which glows faintly, catching what little light there is and seeming to hold it.

**The root-eels** live inside the weave itself, in the capillary channels. They are thin as bootlaces, pale, blind, and innumerable. Nobody sees them except where a channel opens into an eye. What one hears is their clicking: a faint, dry, constant ticking that rises through the mat at night, as though the land were a clock slightly out of tune with itself. Lie with an ear to the moss and it is everywhere.

**The fog-moths** hatch in the sponge and rise each evening as the cloud comes down. They are pale, broad-winged, and slow, in drifts of thousands. They feed on the beaded water on leaves and seem to navigate by the faint glow of certain lichens. That glow is the only light of the Held Country's nights: a scattered blue-green phosphorescence on stems and stones, like a sky seen from underneath.

**The water-spiders** string their webs between the clubmoss towers. The webs catch no insects worth mentioning. They catch dew. Each web by morning is hung with a thousand beads, and the spider drinks from them one by one and then eats the silk and spins again.

Frogs are everywhere. They are small, translucent, and live their whole lives in the cupped leaves of epiphytes, never touching the ground. Tiny snails graze the lichens. Flatworms sheen the undersides of fronds. Every square yard, looked at closely, is inhabited many times over.

## VIII. The Scattered Marks

There are signs of others. They are not many, and they are hard to read.

**The stone rings.** Scattered across the country, sometimes a day's walk apart and sometimes a month's, are small circles of pale pebbles. These are the last stones of the old mountains, the ones the roots pushed to the surface over millennia. Someone gathered them and set them in rings a few paces across, on the crests of the long swells. The mat is slowly swallowing every ring. Some show only a few pebble-tops through the moss. Some are fresh and clean, as if placed this century. Stone is the rarest material in the Held Country. The rings are made of the rarest thing there is, laid out on the highest ground, and left.

**The bent houses.** In a few places, the stem-forest has been shaped. A ring of clubmoss towers has been drawn inward at the top and tied, and the tie was grass-rope, long since rotted. The towers have kept the curve, grown into it, and fused, so that they now make a living dome with a door-gap on one side. It is dry-ish inside and dim, with a floor of compressed moss. It would have taken generations to make one: bending young growth a little, tying it, returning years later to bend it a little more. No tool marks, because there were no tools. There was only patience and hands. On the lintel of one, pressed into the living scale when it was young and since stretched by growth into long, strange shapes, are handprints.

Whoever made them isn't here, or is here so rarely and so quietly that the difference doesn't matter. Some of the bent houses are old enough that their domes have split and regrown into something unrecognizable. Some are young enough that the ties might have been replaced within living memory. Grazers sometimes shelter in them during the evening fog. The walking birds nest in their eaves.

**The offerings.** At the edges of the eyes, sometimes, a single pale pebble sits on a flat leaf, or is pressed into the root-wall just above the waterline. Some of these are old enough that the tide-rings of flowers have grown up past them.

## IX. The Edge

Far out, many hundreds of miles in any direction, the Held Country ends.

It doesn't slope down. It stops. The mat simply runs out over nothing, and a cliff of braided root drops away into cloud. The cliff is miles tall, vertical and dripping, scaled with ferns and hung with beards of moss that sway in air currents rising from below. Water seeps out of the weave all along the face. It gathers into threads, then into falls. The falls never reach the bottom. They thin into spray a thousand feet down, and the spray becomes cloud, and the cloud rises back up along the cliff to join the lid above. The country drinks back its own water, endlessly.

It is warmer at the edge, and louder: a continuous soft hiss of falling water becoming air. The walking birds do not go near it. The grazers never have. The pale paths all bend away before the rim, as if something in the long memory of the herds has marked the place where the ground runs out.

Standing there, with the moss giving underfoot and the whole country behind, one could look down into the white. Somewhere under it, presumably, is the old ground, whatever the mountains stood on before they wore away. The Held Country has been up here so long that it has no memory of it. It holds itself up by root and water and the habit of height. It will stay until the last pebble works its way to the surface, and perhaps long after.

## X. Night

The fog comes down. The moths rise. The lichens light, faint and blue-green, on every stem. The grazers hum once, low, and gather on the crest of a swell around a ring of stones they did not place. The eels tick in the weave. A frog shifts in its leaf-cup, and a single drop falls, and another, and the whole country begins, very slowly, to drink.

Somewhere, a hundred miles off, an eye's wall softens and gives. The sound comes through the ground long after, the land's own heartbeat felt through the moss.

Then it is quiet again, and the mat grows, a hair's breadth, over everything that is still.
