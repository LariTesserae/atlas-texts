# The Hollow Under Tarn Ossary

## The approach

The plateau is so old it has almost stopped being a mountain. At this height the air is thin and warm and wet, like breath held against a pane of glass, and the land has been worn down to a slow, rounded tableland: hummocks of basalt softened into loaves, old ridgelines reduced to faint swells you notice only in your calves. Rain falls most afternoons and does not run off. It sinks. The whole upland is porous, a sponge of rotten stone and moss, and every footstep presses out a small sigh of water.

Nobody comes here by day and finds anything. The plateau keeps its interesting part underneath.

About a kilometer in from the eastern scarp, where the moss grows thickest and the ground gives a little too much underfoot, there is a stair. It is the first built thing you meet: a narrow flight of steps cut into the stone, going down. The treads are worn into shallow dishes by water, not feet, and each dish holds a coin of rainwater. The stair turns twice and the daylight behind you dwindles to a grey rumor, then to nothing.

After that, you will not see again until you leave.

## The dark

Light is 0 here. There is no dimness to adjust to, no glow of fungus, no faint phosphor. Eyes are useless, and after a while they stop straining and settle, and the other senses come forward to fill the space.

The first thing is the sound.

The Hollow is loud. It is not deafening, but it is full and constant, a pressure you feel on your cheeks. It is a layered chorus of clicks, chirrs, trills, drips, and a deep, slow hum beneath all of it. In a place without light, sound has become the medium of sight, and everything that lives here is talking, pinging, calling, knocking, listening for its own echo off the world. The Hollow is being described aloud, all the time, by thousands of small voices.

The second thing is the space. You feel it before you understand it. Your clicks and footsteps and breath go out and don't come back for a long, long moment. The air in front of your face is empty. There are no branches or hanging roots in the middle of the room. The Hollow is a single chamber, roughly a bowl, perhaps two hundred paces across. By cave standards that is small, the size of a grove or a village square. But it is almost entirely void. Everything that lives here lives on the edges.

## The shape of the room

The chamber is what's left of a doline, a sinkhole that opened in the plateau long ago, when the plateau still had peaks. Over time its walls have slumped and softened and rounded into a near-perfect bowl, and its throat was capped. Whether the cap was made or grew, no one can say anymore. It is a lid of dense, pitted stone, ribbed from beneath like the inside of a shell, and it seals the Hollow except for the stair and a scatter of hairline seams through which rainwater seeps.

The erosion here is the gentle kind, the kind that comes after all the violent kinds are finished. Nothing is sharp. Every edge has been licked round by ten thousand years of condensation. The walls weep. Stone that was once jagged breccia is now smooth as soap, and water beads on it, gathers, slides in slow sheets, and drips from the curve of the ceiling into the open air. The dripping is one of the Hollow's steadiest sounds. Some drips fall so far through the empty middle that you hear them leave the ceiling, a small tick, and land on the floor a full second later.

The floor is not flat. It is a shallow basin holding a pool at its lowest point, warm as bathwater and perhaps knee-deep, its surface rarely still. Rings from the falling drips overlap and cross in the dark, and you can hear them lapping gently at the margin.

## The warmth

It is warm down here, warmer than the plateau above, and the warmth has a source. You find it by walking toward it with your palms out.

Around the pool, spaced unevenly, stand the Filaments. There are seven that you can find, perhaps more. Each is a thread-thin column, no wider than a finger, rising from the floor and vanishing up into the empty air. Some connect to the lid; others simply stop, their tips hanging in space as if the rest had been erased. They are warm to the touch, like skin with a slight fever, and faintly slick. Held close to the ear, each one hums at a slightly different pitch. That hum, multiplied, is the deep tone under everything in the Hollow.

The Filaments are the built thing that matters here, and the least understood. They do not rust. They do not wear, even when the water runs over them. They are anchored in the stone with no visible seam; the rock simply becomes them. They seem to be doing something to the space around them. Near them the air feels thicker, faintly charged, the way air does before a storm, and plants grow toward them the way plants elsewhere grow toward the sun.

Whatever they emit, it isn't light. But the life of the Hollow eats it.

## The garden that doesn't need the sun

The walls are lush. Run your hand along any surface and you will find it furred, draped, and cushioned with growth: thick mats of something like moss, soft and cool and waterlogged; broad leathery leaves that hang in ranks like ears; trailing strands a meter long that sway faintly in the convection currents around the Filaments. The ceiling carries a hanging forest of pendant stems, their tips swollen with water, which fall and release when they grow too heavy. They are part of the dripping.

None of it photosynthesizes. There is nothing to photosynthesize with. The plants of the Hollow orient toward the Filaments, leaning into the field, and they are densest where the field is strongest. Around the base of each column the growth is so thick it forms a kind of hedge, knee-high and spongy, fragrant with a green, peppery, mineral smell. Farther from the Filaments the growth thins, and on the far curve of the wall, where the nearest column is fifty paces away, there are patches of bare wet stone.

The plants seem to know that the field shifts. Over years, and only over years, the Filaments' hums drift in pitch, and the strength of whatever they give off moves around the room. The vegetation follows it, slowly, creeping along the walls a hand's width a season, leaving behind strands of dead fiber that the water breaks down. If you could watch the Hollow over a century, you would see the garden migrate around the bowl like a slow tide, circling the pool. In a single visit you notice only that one side smells greener than the other.

## The animals

The Hollow is crowded with animals, in the way a coral reef is crowded, all along its surfaces. Almost none of them occupy the void in the middle, and the ones that do only cross it.

**On the walls**, a species of soft-bodied cricket lives in such numbers that the moss seems to breathe with them. They sing in waves. One section of wall begins a dry, rolling chirr, and it propagates around the bowl over the course of a minute, until the whole room has sung and fallen quiet, and then it begins again somewhere else. They are pale and eyeless and fat with the moisture they drink from the leaves. You can feel them under your palm, cool and quick, like damp seeds.

**In the pool**, frogs. Many frogs, small and slick, with voices wildly out of proportion to their size. Their calls are bell-like, sharp, and designed to carry across open water. Each frog calls, then falls silent to listen for the echo of its call off the far wall, measuring the room and the ripples in it. Their chorus has a ringing, almost metallic quality, and it bounces between the pool and the lid in overlapping chimes.

**Across the void**, the only thing that crosses the empty middle is the clickers. They are something like bats and something like moths: soft, broad-winged, furred, about the size of a hand. They launch from the ceiling and cross the Hollow in long, slow glides, clicking rapidly, so that you hear them as a string of pops arcing overhead from one wall to the other. They feed on the crickets. Their clicks are what fill the space with its echoing sense of *size*: each passage of a clicker traces the dimensions of the dark with sound, and your body reads it without meaning to.

**Underfoot**, in the moss at the pool's edge, something larger moves: slow, heavy, many-legged. It rustles and then waits. It seems indifferent to visitors. It hums, very low, almost exactly at the pitch of the nearest Filament, and the hedge around each column is where these creatures gather, pressed close, as if basking.

Everything here navigates by sound or by touch or by the faint gradient of the field. Everything is loud because silence is blindness.

## The other remains

Besides the stair and the Filaments, there are only a few more marks of whoever made or found the Hollow.

On the north curve of the wall, just above the pool, a small alcove has been carved into the rock. Inside, the stone has been shaped into a bench, smooth and deeply worn, and above it, set into the wall, a disk of the same material as the Filaments: warm, faintly humming, palm-sized. Touching it quiets the room around you. Not entirely, but noticeably: the chorus pulls back a few paces, as though a curtain had been drawn a little way around the bench. Perhaps it was a place for someone to rest in a room that never stops talking.

Near the stair's base, half-buried in moss, lies a scatter of small objects: smooth ovoids the size of eggs, made of something like dense glass. They are cold, the only cold things in the Hollow. When held, each one vibrates faintly and produces, just at the threshold of hearing, a single clear tone. No two tones are the same. Some visitors think they are recordings of the Filaments' pitches from some earlier age, a kind of tuning set, a record of how the room once sounded. Put them back where you found them; the frogs seem to call more quietly near them, as if listening.

And once, on the eastern wall, among the thickest growth, someone's fingers find a line of shallow grooves: a row of marks, regular, spaced a hand apart, running level around the wall as far as anyone has traced it. They might be a measure. They might be writing. The moss has grown into them and softened them almost to nothing. They are very, very old, older than the stair, maybe older than the lid.

## The rules of the place

- **No light enters and none is made.** Even brought lamps seem to fail within a few steps of the stair's bottom, not guttering but simply dimming, as though the dark here were a substance that absorbs rather than an absence. Visitors learn to walk with a hand on the wall.
- **The walls weep constantly.** Moisture rises from the warm pool, condenses on the cool lid, and falls back. It is a closed cycle, fed only a little by the rain seeping through above. The Hollow keeps its own weather: an eternal, dripping, warm evening.
- **The Filaments are the sun.** Everything alive is arranged around them. Their hum is the room's heartbeat, and their slow drift over years sets the pace of all change.
- **Space is precious and mostly unused.** The void in the middle is left open. Nothing grows into it and nothing nests there, as if the emptiness were necessary for the sound to work, for the echoes to come back clean. Life crowds the edges and keeps the center clear, the way a village keeps its square open.
- **Nothing here is in a hurry.** The rock has finished eroding into its final shape. The garden moves by finger-widths. The Filaments shift pitch over decades. Only the animals are quick, and even their chorus repeats itself in long, patient rounds.

## Leaving

When you climb back up the stair, the sound follows you for a long way, thinning into a whisper, then into the drip of water in the worn treads. At the top, the plateau is grey and wide and silent in comparison, almost unbearably quiet. The rain is soft on the moss. The land is flat and old and unremarkable.

Only the warmth in the ground under your palm, if you kneel and press it there, tells you that below this worn-out mountain, in a room no bigger than a village square, the dark is still singing to itself, and something is still keeping the garden fed.
