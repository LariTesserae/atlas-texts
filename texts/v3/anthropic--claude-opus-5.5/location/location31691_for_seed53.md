# The Comb

There is a notch in the ridge, high enough that the sky has thinned to a hard, colorless blue. It is not a summit, only a saddle between two summits, and it is small: a hollow you could cross in a hundred paces if you could walk straight. You cannot. The hollow is full of stone.

## The Stones

The stones are blades of dark schist, grey-black with a greenish cast, split along their grain into slabs as thin as a hand or as thick as a doorframe. They stand upright or nearly so, thousands of them, packed so close that in places two slabs touch along their whole length and in others there is just room to turn sideways and slide between. Frost made them. Water gets into the grain of the bedrock and freezes, the ice pries each layer a little further from the next, and the thaw lets the loosened pieces settle. Over a very long time the ridge's own skin has peeled up into this standing field, the way old paint lifts from wood in curls.

They all lean the same way, a few degrees off vertical toward the east and away from the wind. Walking among them feels a little like walking through a crowd that has turned its back on you. Some reach only knee height. The tallest stand three times a person's height, their tops snapped into points or worn round. Their windward faces are smooth, almost polished. Their leeward faces keep the rough, flaky texture of freshly split rock, and silver mica catches the light in the cracks.

Nothing grows on them. There is no lichen and no moss, not even the black crust that lives on stone almost everywhere else. The rock is clean and has been clean for as long as it has stood. Between the slabs the ground is shattered chips of the same schist, sorted by frost into rough rings and stripes. The larger pieces have been shoved outward and the fine grit pooled in the centers, as if something had drawn the pattern with a patient finger.

## The Light

The light here is total. The sun has little air to pass through, and below the notch on both sides lies a floor of cloud that throws it back up. Much of the day the hollow is lit from below as well as above, and the slabs cast almost no shadows. They stand in a white glare that seems to come from everywhere at once. Their dark surfaces take the light and hold it, and the mica shimmers. When there is ice on the stones, the whole hollow glitters too sharply to look at without squinting through your fingers.

At the edges of the day the light turns gold, then pink, then a thin cold violet. For a few minutes the leaning stones throw long shadows eastward, all parallel, like the teeth of a comb. That is where the name comes from, or one of the places it comes from.

## The Cloud and the Rime

The cloud sea below is not still. Every day, as the sun warms the valleys, the cloud begins to rise. By early afternoon it spills up to the lip of the saddle and starts to pour through the notch from west to east, a slow white river of fog squeezed between the peaks.

It has to pass through the stones, and the stones comb it. The fog enters the hollow as a single soft mass and leaves it drawn out into threads and ribbons. It streams between the slabs in long parallel filaments that twist and rejoin on the far side. Standing inside it, you can watch individual strands of mist slide past at shoulder height, close enough to part with your hand. They close again behind your fingers with no sign they were disturbed.

The fog is colder than freezing, but it is still liquid, made of tiny droplets that have not yet found anything to freeze onto. Now they find the stones. Wherever a droplet touches rock it freezes on the spot. The next droplet freezes onto the first, and the next onto that. Rime grows into the wind, not down like icicles but outward, toward where the fog is coming from. It forms feathers and fins and delicate plates of white ice on every western edge, lengthening while you watch. In the thickest afternoons a feather can grow the width of a finger in the time it takes to eat a meal. By evening the windward side of every slab wears a white fringe, and the Comb looks furred, as though the stones had grown pelts.

Then night comes and the cloud sinks back into the valleys. The sky clears, and the cold deepens past anything the day suggested. The rime stops growing, hardens, and turns glassy.

In the morning the sun strikes it. At this height the sun does not so much melt ice as erase it. The rime sublimates straight from solid to vapor, and the feathers thin, go translucent, and vanish from their tips inward. Some of it does melt, though, where the dark stone soaks up the sun and warms from beneath. That meltwater runs down the slabs in thin films and beads, gathers at their bases, and slips into the grit, where the night will freeze it again and pry the ground a fraction further apart.

So the hollow is always damp, but only briefly and in small ways. Water arrives as fog, stays a while as ice, leaves as vapor, and a little of it remains as a trickle or a drop. There are no pools. There is no stream. There is only the daily breathing of the stone.

## The Sound

It is mostly quiet. The wind here is steady more often than gusty, and it makes little noise sliding along the smooth faces of the slabs. At certain angles it produces a faint breathy tone, like someone blowing across the mouth of a jar, which rises and fades without warning.

The other sounds come from ice. As the rime cools in the evening it ticks. In the morning, as the sun loosens it, feathers break away and fall. When they strike the grit they make a soft hiss. When they strike another stone they make a small, clear, glassy ring that carries surprisingly far through the still air. On a bright morning these rings come every few seconds from somewhere in the hollow, scattered and unpredictable, like the last notes of a very slow wind chime.

Once in a long while a stone gives up. A slab that has leaned too far for too many centuries cracks at the base with a sound like a single hand clap. It tips against its neighbor and rests there, and after that the two lean together.

## What People Left

People came here, though not many and not for long.

Just inside the western lip there is a low wall of stacked slabs, dry-laid and no higher than a knee, curving around a hollow about the size of a sleeping body. Fog-water has polished the stones of the wall where they face the wind. Someone lay here, or sat, sheltered from the notch's draft.

Deeper in, the traces grow more deliberate. At the base of certain tall slabs, always on the leeward side, flat stones have been set into the grit and hollowed into shallow basins, pecked out with harder stones over many hours. They are water-catches. The meltwater that runs down the slabs in the morning collects in them, a cupful at most on a good day. Some basins still have a small grooved lip, cut to guide the drip from the stone into the bowl. Here and there a slab bears a vertical line of pecked marks down its face, a channel scored to lead water to the right spot. On a few stones the channels have been recut more than once, the older grooves worn shallow beside the newer ones.

Scattered among the basins are the tools. One is a scraper of grey flint with its edge still sharp, the kind of thing you would use to take rime off a stone and drop it into a bowl to melt. Another is a hammerstone with a pale, bruised end. A few pieces of worked bone lie wedged into cracks, perhaps as markers or perhaps forgotten. There is also one short bar of dark, crudely beaten iron, flaking into rust. Someone once drove it into the gap between two slabs, maybe to hold them apart, and the gap has since closed around it.

At the very center of the hollow, where the frost-stripes converge, someone stacked a cairn. It is not tall. It is built from the thinnest slabs available, laid flat and crosswise so that each layer turns against the one below, a small tower of stone sheets. It is the only thing in the Comb that does not lean. Every afternoon the rime grows on it like everything else, and every morning the sun takes it away. The cairn's western edges have worn round under that treatment, and the whole thing has taken on a slight twist, as if it were slowly unscrewing itself into the wind.

Nothing suggests anyone lived here. There are no hearths, no bones of eaten animals, and no refuse. People came up, gathered what the stones gave, perhaps waited for something, and went down again. Why anyone would climb this high to collect a few mouthfuls of water from the rime is not written anywhere. It may have been a practice rather than a need: water that had never touched the ground, taken from a cloud by a stone and handed over at dawn.

## The Rules of the Place

The Comb keeps its own time. Its day turns cloud into rime, rime into vapor and drop, and drop into the frost that splits the next slab. Its longer time is measured in the lean of the stones, which increases a hair's breadth each winter. Eventually the oldest slabs lie down, and new ones peel up from the bedrock at the hollow's edges to replace them. Over centuries the field slowly migrates, uphill toward the wind, the way a dune crawls across a desert, only far slower.

Nothing lives here to hurry it. No roots crack the rock and no animals disturb the grit. The only forces are cold, light, fog, and time, working together without haste.

If you stand in the middle of it on a clear morning, with the cairn at your shoulder, the white cloud floor far below, and the black blades around you glittering and dripping, you hear the small bright ring of falling ice somewhere nearby. Then, after a long pause, you hear another.
