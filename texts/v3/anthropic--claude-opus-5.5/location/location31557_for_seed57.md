# The Undercroft of the Fallen Bank

It begins with a river that moved.

Not long ago (a handful of winters, no more) the river shouldered sideways in a flood and took a meadow with it. A bank of dark loam and alder roots slumped into the new channel, folded over on itself, and sealed. Beneath that slump, where the old turf now lies face-down against the old riverbed, there is a pocket of water no larger than a small room. Nothing on the surface marks it. The river runs over it, cold and brown, and never notices.

This is where the place is.

## The Shape of It

The pocket is irregular, like a fist closed around something. Its ceiling is the underside of the drowned meadow: a mat of grass roots, clover roots, the pale threads of fungi, all hanging down into the water like the inside of a fleece. Its floor is river stone, freshly broken. When the bank fell it cracked the bedrock shelf beneath, and the fractures are still sharp. Slate edges stand up like the spines of closed books, unrounded, catching at anything that drifts past. The water hasn't had time to soften them yet. It is working on it. Every edge wears a faint grey bloom of silt where the slow current rubs.

It is completely dark. The river's light doesn't reach through two arms' depth of soil. Whatever seeing happens here happens some other way.

The water is cold, just above the temperature at which the roots would stop growing, and it is very still. There is a current, but it moves the way a sleeper's chest moves. A thread of silt released at one wall might take a full day to reach the other.

## What Fills It

There is no empty water here. The pocket is packed.

The meadow didn't die when it went under. It went on growing, confused, downward. Grass blades that were reaching for the sun now reach into the dark, long and colourless, drifting in sheaves. Watercress took root in the crevices of the broken slate and grew into tangled pale towers. Moss covers every surface in layers so thick they have become a kind of soft architecture: cushions on cushions, domes on domes, until the stone beneath is only a rumour. Between the moss and the roots, fine lace-weed spreads in sheets, and between the sheets are the soft bodies of freshwater sponges, green-white, porous, each holding a small quantity of darker water inside it like a held breath.

If you could move through this place, you would be moving through fabric. Every gesture would part one curtain and meet another. There are no clearings. Where something dies, something else is already there, pressed into the gap. The only open volumes are the insides of things.

## The Rule of the Pocket

The first thing wrong with the undercroft is that it is larger inside than the bank that contains it. Not by much; it is not a cathedral. But the far wall is always a little farther than it should be. A root-strand reaching from the ceiling toward the floor will grow for a season and never arrive. The distance keeps quietly accruing, the way debt does.

The second thing wrong is that the water keeps what falls into it.

When the bank collapsed it caught a portion of an evening: a certain wet dusk, a certain slant of weather, the last of a meadow's afternoon. That evening did not end. It was folded into the water along with the turf, and it is still in there, stratified. The pocket is layered like a cake of nights. Near the ceiling the water holds that final dusk, faintly warmer, with a residue of the smell of cut grass that shouldn't survive underwater and does. Lower down are colder, heavier layers, each one a night the river has run over the bank since. Every year the undercroft takes in another night and lays it down, and the lower layers thicken and press together like pages.

The layers don't mix. A strand of weed that grows down through them grows differently in each. It is broad and soft in the dusk layer. In the winter-night layers it turns narrow, stiff, and nearly transparent. There is one thin, black band near the floor where it grows backward, shortening, returning to its own base. Nobody knows which night that band was. It may be a night that hasn't happened yet.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet. The river overhead is not heard so much as felt: a low, even pressure in the water, like a hand resting on a door.

But sound behaves strangely here. Noises arrive before their causes. A stone ticks against another stone, and only afterward does the silt shift that should have moved it. Occasionally, deep in the lower layers, there is a faint patter like rain on leaves. It is the rain from the dusk the meadow drowned in, still falling somewhere in the folded water, never quite reaching the ground.

Once a season or so, the whole pocket gives a single soft note, like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass. The moss trembles all at once and then is still. This happens when a new night is being laid down.

## Light

There is no light, except once.

At the turn toward spring, when the river runs coldest and clearest, the sponges release their held water all together. It comes out luminous, a faint blue-green, like breath on a cold window lit from behind. For the length of perhaps three heartbeats the whole undercroft is visible: the hanging meadow, the book-edged stone, the moss domes stacked like loaves, the drifting sheaves of pale grass. And in that moment you can see that it is all slightly misaligned, as if it were two rooms laid over one another and drawn by different hands, their walls not quite matching.

Then the water darkens, and the sponges begin, slowly, to fill again.

## Who Lives Here

Almost no one.

There is an eel. It is old, far older than the pocket, which suggests it came in with the collapsed evening rather than finding its way here. It is blind, or its eyes have become something else; they are milky and faintly ridged, like shells. It lives in the dusk layer and moves along the underside of the meadow so slowly that the root-hairs have grown around its path, forming a tunnel exactly its shape. When it moves along that tunnel, it is also, somehow, still where it was. The tunnel is never empty.

There is a crayfish, or there was. On the floor among the slate lie seven of its shed shells, arranged in a loose ring, each a little larger than the last. The seventh is not empty. The first isn't empty either. It's possible the crayfish is all of them, growing and outgrowing itself in every direction of the folded time at once. If you watched long enough (years) you might see one claw of the smallest shell open, very slightly.

Beyond these, only the tiny things: water fleas that drift through the layers and change colour at each boundary, and the eggs of something that has not yet hatched and may be waiting for the night it belongs to.

## What Was Built

The meadow was not wild before the flood. Someone used it, lightly.

Under the moss there are traces. A line of fitted slate stones runs across part of the floor, flat-topped, deliberately set: a threshold, or the edge of a path, broken now by the fresh fractures. It leads from one wall to the other and stops at both, going nowhere.

In a crevice lies a small cairn of five river pebbles, stacked largest to smallest. It is still standing, though the water has had every chance to topple it. The moss grows around it and never over it, leaving a clean ring of bare stone. It is the only uncovered thing in the entire pocket.

Tangled in the ceiling roots is a knot of braided grass. Someone twisted it by hand, a child's plait or a marker tied to a fencepost. It hangs down into the dusk layer, and in that water it is still green. It has been green for years.

There is nothing else: no tools, no walls, no metal. Just these few gestures, made by a hand that was here before the water, and held by the water exactly as they were left.

## The Slow Work

Nothing happens quickly in the undercroft. The water rounds the slate edges at the pace of fingernails growing. The moss adds a thickness of leaf each year. The distance between ceiling and floor lengthens by the width of a root-hair. One more night is folded in and pressed down.

In a few hundred years the broken stone will be smooth. The layers of night will be so many and so thin that they will blur into a single deep dark with the old dusk floating on top like cream. The eel's tunnel will have become a solid thing of woven root, a hollow sculpture of an animal that may or may not still be inside it.

And the river overhead will still be running, cold and brown and unaware, over a small drowned room that is keeping an evening nobody else remembers. The grass braid will still be green, and the five stones will still be standing, marking something, for someone, who has not come back.
