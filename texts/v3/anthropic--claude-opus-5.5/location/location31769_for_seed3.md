# The Ringing Heights

## First approach

The cable car comes up out of the cloud, and the first thing you learn about this place is that the noise was there before you could see anything. For the last hour of the climb, inside that grey wet fleece, you heard it: a slow, enormous, irregular hammering, like a dozen giants shoeing horses in separate rooms. Now the car clears the cloud-top and the mountains appear, and they are wrong in a way that takes a moment to name.

They are too sharp.

Mountains are supposed to be old. They are supposed to wear down, round off, and collect scree at their feet. These have not had time. They rise out of the cloud-sea in blades and splinters, fresh-faced and glassy, their edges keen enough to cut rope. The rock is dark, somewhere between slate and obsidian, and it has no sediment, no weathering rind, and no lichen on the highest parts. Near the summits it still has the faint conchoidal ripple of something poured and cooled, though nothing here was ever hot. The ridgelines have none of the tired geometry of age. They branch like frost on a window, like crystal growth, because that is what they are.

There are perhaps forty peaks visible from the cable line, scattered across a region you might cross in two days if the cables ran straight, which they do not. Most summits carry a building, and most of those buildings are making noise.

## The rule

Here, sound becomes stone.

Nobody who lives here thinks of this as strange, any more than a coastal town thinks the tide is strange. A struck note hangs in the cold, wet air for a moment, and some fraction of it precipitates, the way frost precipitates out of fog. Quiet sound precipitates almost nothing. A shout leaves a grain. A steam hammer the size of a chapel, dropped onto an anvil the size of a barge, leaves a sliver of new rock across the surrounding ridges, a few microns thick and spread over a quarter mile. The hammers do not stop. They have not stopped in something like nine hundred years.

That is why there is no erosion. The mountains are not being worn down, because they are still being made. They grow at roughly a hand's breadth a year at the busiest summits and far less on the outlying crags. The growth is patient and almost invisible, but you can see it in the doorframes. Every old building in the Heights has stone creeping up over its threshold, and the oldest have been buried to the second storey and entered through what were once their upper windows.

The corollary is the thing everyone fears. **Silence erodes.** Stone made from sound slowly remembers that it was sound. Where a foundry falls quiet, its peak begins to soften over decades, then slump, then shed into the cloud below in long grey sighs of dust. Lean close to the lower slopes and you can hear it happening: a faint, unending murmur coming out of the rock as it gives back what it was made of.

## The city

It is called, depending on who you ask, the Heights, the Anvils, or simply the City. It is a single city, though its districts are separated by miles of empty air.

Each inhabited summit is a foundry-spire: a vertical tangle of iron galleries, brick chimneys, riveted boiler-houses, and dormitories stacked like swallows' nests against the rock. At the heart of each spire is the Strike, the great hammer, driven by steam from coal hauled up by cable from somewhere far below the cloud. Nobody in the Heights has been down there in living memory, and the coal simply arrives. Around the Strike cluster lesser noise-works: trip-hammers, stamping mills, bell-towers, and the long iron organ-pipes called *bellowers* that moan across the valleys in shifts. Different noises make different stone. Bells make a clear, brittle rock that rings when you walk on it. The bellowers make something softer and darker, almost felted, which the builders prize for foundations. Human voices make the pale, fine-grained stone that the most expensive houses are carved from, and there are districts where choirs work in rotation like mill-hands, singing a single sustained chord for generations to thicken the ground under a cathedral.

Between the spires runs the cable network: iron cables thick as a man's waist, strung across gulfs a mile wide and sagging into the cloud at their midpoints, so that every journey dips briefly into grey blindness before rising again. The gondolas are ribbed iron, lamplit, and loud, because everything here is loud on purpose. Cable wheels are left unoiled so they shriek. Children are taught to whistle before they are taught to read. A quiet person is regarded the way a wasteful person is elsewhere.

The city is sparse. There are long stretches where you can stand on a gallery and see no other human structure, only razor peaks, the soft plain of cloud, and a single foundry glowing orange twenty miles off like a lantern on a far shore. The spires are few because each one is expensive to keep alive. When a district dies, it dies loudly at first, and then with increasing quiet. The dead spires are the most haunting things in the Heights: iron frames standing on peaks that are slowly melting out from under them, the girders tilting, the chimneys cold, the whole mountain murmuring as it unmakes itself.

## Light

It is dim here, always.

Partly this is height. The Heights sit so far up that the sky overhead is closer to indigo than blue, and at midday you can see the brighter stars. Partly it is the smoke, which hangs in slow brown banners from every chimney. But mostly it is the stone. Sound-rock drinks light. Fresh rock is darkest, and a newly grown ridge looks like a crack in the world. Only as the stone ages and begins to leak its sound does it pale, so that the lower, older slopes are ash-grey while the summits are black. The sun, when it clears the smoke, is a white coin with no warmth to it.

The people live by furnace-glow and by lamps, and the ecology has adapted the same way.

## The green

Vegetation is the second surprise, after the sharpness. For all their newness, the slopes below the summits are green, and in places the green is lush.

It begins with the fog. The cloud-sea is not static. It breathes up and down the mountains on a slow daily tide, and wherever it touches, it leaves water. There is almost no rain, but there is endless condensation: beads on every cable, dripping galleries, iron that is always slick. The damp gathers in the fine fractures of new stone and gives life its first hold.

First come the hammer-mosses, which grow only on sound-rock. They are dense cushions of dark green, velvet to the touch, and they vibrate faintly with whatever noise made the rock beneath them. Their spores ride the fog. Within a generation, a fresh ridge is furred with them. After the mosses come ferns, tall and feathery, their fronds uncurling in slow spirals that are said to follow the rhythm of the nearest Strike. There are no trees, as the roots cannot yet penetrate the stone deeply enough, but there is a shrub called *belljack*, a tough, twisted thing with waxy leaves and small cupped flowers that open only when there is noise and close in silence. The fog-slopes below a working foundry are a garden of belljack, thousands of small grey-white cups turned toward the hammering like faces toward a speaker. Below a dead foundry, the belljack is shut, and the hillside looks bruised.

Along the undersides of the cables, where the drip is heaviest, hang long curtains of pale grey-green moss, so that from a distance the network looks like the web of an enormous old spider. The cable crews cut it back seasonally. Left alone, it would drag the lines down.

## The animals

The Heights are full of animals, more than seems possible on such new stone, and nearly all of them are loud.

The **anvil-swifts** are the most important. They are small, black-and-rust birds that nest in their hundreds of thousands on the undersides of foundry galleries, and their calls precipitate stone just as the hammers do: a thin, bright mineral that they use to build their nests, cup by cup, chirp by chirp. An old swift colony is a sagging chandelier of pale stone cells, several tons of it, grown entirely from birdsong. The foundries encourage them. The swifts eat the moths drawn to the furnace-glow, and they add their small, constant voices to the work of holding the mountains up.

The moths are enormous and slow, with dusty wings a foot across in shades of soot and bronze, and they drift through the dim air in the thousands toward every lit window. On still nights the sound of their wingbeats against glass is a soft, persistent drumming across the whole city.

On the crags live **sheergoats**, long-legged and silver-coated, with split hooves hard enough to stand on the razor-edges of new stone. They are browsers of fern and belljack, and they bleat constantly in deep, bronze tones that carry for miles. The herders say the goats understand the rule of the world better than people do. They will not graze on a dead mountain, and a herd abandoning a ridge is the first sign that the rock beneath is going quiet.

In the fractures and crevices live the **clicklings**. These are small crustacean-things, somewhere between a woodlouse and a crab, pale and translucent, and they gather in vast numbers wherever the stone is youngest. They communicate by rapping their claws against rock, and a clickling swarm on a cold morning sounds like rain on a tin roof. Their tapping thickens the fissures they live in, sealing the cracks shut behind them as they move, so that the mountains are always being quietly mended from within.

Down near the cloud-top, where the stone is oldest and palest, live creatures that nobody likes to talk about. They are long, blind, and pale, and they feed on the murmur leaking out of dying rock. They are rarely seen. You hear them instead: a slow, wet, contented humming from somewhere just inside the fog.

## Sound

Put it all together and the Heights are never quiet.

Nearest, there is the rhythm of your own spire's Strike. It comes about once every eleven seconds, a thud felt in the ribs as much as heard, and between strikes the air fills with lesser noise: trip-hammers, the clang of shovels, men and women shouting over the din, swifts in their thousands, the whistle of steam. Farther off, other spires keep their own tempos, and across miles of open air the rhythms drift in and out of phase, so that there are moments of near-silence and then moments when a dozen distant hammers land together and the whole range seems to shudder. Beneath everything, the bellowers moan their long low notes across the valleys, and the goats answer.

It is not unpleasant once you are used to it. The people describe it the way sailors describe the sea. At night, lying in a stone-walled dormitory, the noise becomes a kind of weather, and children who have been taken down to the cloud-top on excursions come back frightened and sleepless, unnerved by how much quieter it is there.

## Where it breaks

The rule that sound becomes stone is the one everyone knows. It is not the only thing that has gone strange here, and the longer you stay, the more you notice the edges of it fraying.

Echoes sometimes arrive before the sound that caused them. Stand at the edge of a gallery and shout across a valley, and now and then the valley answers a half-second *before* you open your mouth. Old foundry hands say this is stone that was made from your voice at some later time, releasing its sound early. They say it matter-of-factly, and they do not like it.

Distances are unreliable. The spires drift, very slowly, relative to one another. Cable-wardens re-survey every decade and find a line that was a mile long now a mile and forty yards, or a peak that was due north now slightly east. The rock is growing, yes, but not in ways that should move whole mountains. The prevailing theory is that the Heights are still deciding what shape they are.

In the oldest, palest stone, pressed flat against a wall at night, you can hear things. Hammering, mostly, from strikes that fell centuries ago. But sometimes voices, and the voices are sometimes saying things that have not yet been said. There is a trade in this. Listeners sit with their ears to the ancient bedrock and sell what they hear, and the city half-believes them.

And there is the matter of the summits. If you climb above the highest foundry on the tallest peak, past the last gallery and up onto the raw black rock that is only months old, the air grows very cold and very still, and the noise from below grows strangely muffled, as though the sound itself is being used up. At the very top, on the newest stone in the world, there is a place about the size of a room where there is no sound at all. None. Not your breath, not your heartbeat, not the wind. The rock there is so dark it hardly seems to be there, just a hole in the indigo sky shaped like a mountain. Some people believe this is where the stone comes from, not from the hammers but from the silence, and that the hammers are not building the mountains but *holding them back*, keeping them from rising faster into whatever is above.

Nobody knows. The hammers keep falling, eleven seconds apart. The swifts keep calling. The moss creeps over the fresh rock, the belljack opens its grey cups to the noise, the goats bleat on the razor-crags, and far below, just inside the fog, something pale hums contentedly as another dead mountain gives its voice back to the air.
