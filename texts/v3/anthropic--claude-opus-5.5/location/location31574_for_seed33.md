# The Upturned City of Hesk

## The Approach

There is no path up, only the mountain's shoulder. It is a long ramp of broken slate that goes on for days, tilting past the point where anything grows. The last lichen gives out somewhere below. After that the slope is bare: grey stone, black stone, stone the color of old bone, all of it cracked into plates by ten thousand winters of frost.

Then the ramp flattens, and the city is there.

It does not rise from the plateau so much as lie across it, the way snow lies across a field. It is enormous: towers, arcades, colonnades, stairways, domes, streets wide enough for processions. It fills the high tableland from rim to rim and spills over the edges in terraces that drop into cloud. And it is almost entirely empty. You can stand in the middle of a plaza the size of a lake and see no moving thing at all except the dust.

## The Air, and the Light From Below

The air is so thin it feels like being pressed between two sheets of glass. It is cold, though not a killing cold: the steady, dry chill of a cellar, of a stone kept out of the sun. There is almost no water here. The only moisture is a rime that forms on the northern faces of buildings in the hour before dawn. It lasts perhaps twenty minutes, and then the wind takes it, not as vapor but as a faint glitter that blows sideways and is gone.

Hesk sits above the weather. Below the plateau's rim lies a sea of cloud, white and slow and unbroken to every horizon, and this is where the light comes from. The sun is real, and it does cross the sky, but at this altitude the sky is nearly black, a deep bruised indigo even at noon. The sun is a hard white coin that seems to illuminate nothing. The city is lit by the cloud sea, by sunlight thrown back up from beneath.

So the light in Hesk falls upward. The undersides of arches glow. The bottoms of balconies are bright, and the tops are in shadow. Shadows stretch toward the sky, thin and dark, lifting off the tops of towers like smoke that doesn't move. Walking through the streets, you carry your own shadow above your head. It is always dim, a grey, shadowless-seeming dimness, because everything is lit from the wrong side and the eye keeps failing to make sense of it.

## Materials

The city is built from the mountain itself, but reworked. Most of it is a pale mortared stone the texture of a wasp's nest: thin laminations, layer upon layer, each no thicker than a fingernail, pressed into a material that rings faintly when struck. In older districts it has weathered to a smooth, satiny grey, rounded at every corner, the carved details worn soft as soap. There are no seams, no joints, no tool marks. Nothing here was cut. It was all laid down, grain by grain.

In places the laminations are threaded with mica, and where the upward light catches them the walls shimmer. There are also veins of a darker material, glossy and almost black. The masons use it for thresholds and lintels, and it stays cold to the touch even at midday.

The streets are paved with the same laminated stone, worn into shallow troughs down their centers. No feet made those troughs. The dust did. It moves along the streets like slow water.

## The Masons

They are the only builders, and there are very few of them.

A mason is roughly the size of a heron and built like one: long jointed legs, a narrow body, a long neck ending in a head that is mostly mouth. Their hide is the same grey as the stone, finely textured, and when they stand still they vanish against a wall. They move slowly, placing each foot as if testing whether the ground will hold. On an ordinary day you might see one, or none. Across the whole city there may be forty or fifty.

They build with their mouths. A mason gathers dust. It licks it from the troughs in the street, from the lee of walls, and above all it licks the dawn rime, the only moisture in Hesk. In its throat the dust and rime and something else, a secretion, become a paste. It lays the paste along the top of a wall in a thin, even bead, pressing it down with the flat underside of its jaw. Then it moves a step and lays another. A single mason, working a full day, adds a band of wall perhaps a handspan long and a fingernail thick.

The city is vastly larger than anything forty masons could build in any span of time you could name.

They do not live in it. They sleep standing, in the open, in the upward light. They do not use the doors or climb the stairs. The staircases are cut for creatures with feet shorter than a mason's and longer than a human's, and the doorways are proportioned for something taller than either. The masons build them anyway, precisely, adding their thin bead of paste to the edge of a step whose measurements they have never needed. Watching them, you have the uneasy sense that they are not designing anything. They are following something. Filling in lines that are already there.

## The Other Animals

Besides the masons, there are a few other creatures.

**Dust-moths** are pale, tattered things with wings like shavings of paper. They drift in the streets at dusk, riding the slow currents of the dust-rivers, and seem to feed on the rime. They make no sound at all, even to each other. You notice them only when one lands on your sleeve and weighs nothing.

**Stilt-ravens** are rarer: black birds with absurdly long legs that walk rather than fly, because the air is too thin to hold them well. They pick through the upper terraces for dead moths and flakes of mason-paste. They are the only thing in Hesk that makes a deliberate sound, a single dry click of the beak, spaced far apart, like someone tapping a fingernail on a table in another room.

Something else hunts the stilt-ravens. No one has seen it clearly. Now and then there is a scatter of black feathers on a high balcony with nothing else near, and the feathers lie in a perfect spiral.

## Sound

Hesk is very nearly silent. The air is too thin to carry much, and there is nothing here to make noise: no water, no leaves, no engines, no voices. The wind moves, but it is a slow, steady wind, and the rounded buildings give it nothing to catch on. Your own footsteps sound muffled and far away, as though you were hearing them through a wall.

Now and then, in the deepest quiet, the laminated stone rings. It is a faint, high, almost subliminal tone, set off by temperature changes as the walls contract at dusk. A whole district will do it at once, a long, low chord that swells and fades over several minutes. The masons stop working while it happens and stand very still, necks lifted.

## Where It Breaks

Walk long enough in Hesk and the city stops agreeing with itself.

**Distance is unreliable.** A tower that stood a few hundred steps away may take an hour to reach, or you may turn a corner and find yourself at its base. The plateau is perhaps twenty miles across, but you can walk a straight avenue for three days and not arrive at its end. The city is larger on the inside of its streets than on the outside of its rim.

**Streets repeat.** You pass a doorway with a particular crack in its lintel, a particular spill of dust on its step. Walk on, turn twice, and you pass it again. Only now the crack is wider, the dust deeper, and the carving above the door worn nearly smooth, as if the second time you passed it a century had gone by. Sometimes the second pass shows it newer, sharper, the crack not yet formed.

**Some buildings exist only from certain angles.** Stand in the right place and there is a dome, perfectly solid, the upward light pooling on its underside. Take a step to the left and there is open plaza where it stood, and dust blowing through the space. Step back, and the dome is there again. The masons build these too. You can watch one lay its bead of paste along the rim of a wall that, from where you stand, is not there. The mason is licking the air.

**The sky has a floor.** On certain evenings the black of the upper sky seems to turn solid. The shadows rising from the towers do not fade into it. They touch it and pool there, flattening out against something, like smoke gathering on a ceiling. At those times it becomes very hard to tell which way is up. The cloud sea is below, and bright, and lit. The sky is above, dark and flat and near. Standing in the plaza, you feel the pull to fall toward the light.

## The Two Tides

The strangest thing about Hesk is not visible at first. It takes weeks of watching.

The city is weathering. Everywhere you look is the evidence of age: rounded corners, softened carvings, wind-scoured faces, stairs worn into shallow bowls. The eastern half of the city is plainly ancient, far older than the masons could account for. Towers there lean slightly. Arcades have collapsed into long drifts of laminated rubble, and the rubble is itself worn smooth.

But in the western districts, the weathering runs the other way.

There, the ruins are growing into buildings. Cracks close over months. A tumble of rounded rubble slowly rises, reassembling into a wall. Soft, eroded carvings sharpen, their edges emerging from the smooth stone like faces surfacing from water. The wind there blows dust *into* the walls rather than off them. You can watch a single leaning column over a season and see it straighten.

Where the two regions meet, a seam runs through the city from north to south. It is not a line exactly but a band perhaps a quarter mile wide, where buildings flicker between old and new, and the dust stops, swirls, and hangs in the air, unable to decide which way to fall. The masons gather along the seam more than anywhere else. They work there constantly, laying their paste along walls that are simultaneously crumbling and assembling, as if trying to hold the two halves of the city together.

The masons, it seems, are not building Hesk from nothing. The city already exists, complete, in some fold of time. The western tide is it assembling itself backward out of its own ruin, the eastern tide is it wearing away forward into ruin, and the masons are the stitching that keeps the two tides from tearing apart. The doors are made for inhabitants who have not arrived yet, or who left long ago, or who will come and go in the same instant at the seam.

## Seasons and Motion

Nothing in Hesk is still for long, even when it looks motionless.

**Daily:** the dawn rime forms and is licked or blown away; the stone chimes at dusk; the upward light brightens and fades with the cloud sea. When the clouds below part, the city goes suddenly black, its light cut off from underneath, and everything freezes for minutes until the white closes again.

**Seasonally:** the cloud sea rises and falls like a slow tide. In the high months it laps at the lowest terraces, and those districts go dim and damp, the only damp in Hesk. The masons descend to them then and work furiously, with more moisture than they have all year. In the low months the cloud sea drops away and the whole city sits exposed to the black sky, lit only faintly, and the dust-rivers run fast.

**Over longer spans:** the seam drifts. Very slowly, over what might be centuries, it moves eastward. Where it passes, the ancient ruins begin, reluctantly, to heal. The masons follow it.

## Standing There

If you stand in the great plaza at dusk, you can see it all at once. There is the dark sky pressing down, and the glowing sea of cloud underneath. Upward-streaming shadows rise from a thousand empty towers. A single mason stands at the edge of a wall that may or may not be there, laying down one more fingernail's width of stone. A dust-moth drifts past without a sound. Far off, a stilt-raven clicks once. A long, faint chord rises out of the stone around you and fades.

No one lives in Hesk. No one ever will, or everyone already does. The city goes on finishing itself, in both directions, in the thin cold air above the world.
