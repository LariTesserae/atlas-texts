# The Shelf-Town at Weeping Scar

Some centuries ago, which is recent for a mountain, the high face broke. A slab the size of a village sheared away and went down into the valley. It left behind a ledge about three hundred paces long and never more than forty deep, a raw step cut into the mountain's shoulder just below the line where anything woody gives up. The rock there is still sharp. Its edges have not had time to round. You can put your palm against the back wall and feel the crisp lip of a fracture that has barely begun to soften, grey-green schist split along planes that still catch the light like knife-flats.

On that ledge, something has grown that behaves very much like a town.

---

## First Sight

You come up from below through cloud, which is the usual weather here. The fog is not a blanket. It is a slow river pouring over the lip of the shelf from the valley side, thinning and thickening in breaths. Through it you see what at first looks like roofs: steep, dark, crowded shapes stacked against the cliff, jostling each other, leaning together at the tops. There are lanes between them, narrow and crooked, climbing in steps. There are low walls. There is a thing that looks unmistakably like a gate, two dense pillars of dark needle-growth with a gap between them just wide enough for a person turned sideways.

There are no people. There have never been people. No tool has touched any of it.

Every roof, wall, stair and lintel is a living plant, or the dead body of one holding up the living. The town is made of dwarf fir, crowberry, cushion-moss, a creeping willow no taller than your ankle, and above all a low, dense conifer that grows here and almost nowhere else. The fir has done the building. It has had help from the cold, the water and the rock, and the architecture is theirs as much as its.

---

## How the Houses Are Made

The shelf fir cannot grow tall. The wind at this height would strip it, and winter would crack anything that stood up proud of the snow. So it grows outward, low and stubborn, and does what mountain conifers sometimes do: wherever a lower branch presses against the ground long enough, it roots. That branch becomes a new trunk, which sends out its own low branches, which root in turn.

Left to itself on open ground, this makes a slow wave. Over decades, a ring of green grows outward while the old centre dies back. Up here the fir did not have open ground. It had a narrow shelf with a cliff behind, a drop in front, and the rock between broken into blocks and troughs by the fall.

So the rings could not spread. They collided. Where one clonal ring met another, both piled upward, their branches interlacing into walls of astonishing density. You cannot put your hand through them. You can barely push a finger past the outer needles before meeting a lattice of wood as close as woven basketwork. Where a ring's centre died, it left a hollow enclosed by these walls: a room. Snow loads bent the upper growth inward over the hollows year after year until the branches met and knitted, and the rooms got roofs.

Those are the houses. Some are the size of a small kitchen, some no bigger than a wardrobe. A few near the back wall are large enough to stand in, domed and dark, smelling of resin and old needles, with floors of rust-brown duff so deep and springy that your foot sinks to the ankle.

The town is also very old in its lower layers. When a wall dies, it does not fall. The dead wood is hard, resin-soaked and slow to rot in the cold, and it becomes a foundation for new growth. The living town sits on the skeleton of earlier towns. Cut a section through any wall and you would see storeys: silver-grey dead wood at the bottom, twisted and hard as bone; then a band of half-dead brown; then the living green crust on top. In places near the cliff the stacked generations reach twice a person's height. These are the tenements, the oldest quarter, where the rooms are small and tall and interconnected through gaps where a dead branch finally gave way.

---

## How the Lanes Stay Open

A town needs streets, and the fir would fill every gap if it could. Three things stop it.

**Water.** The back cliff weeps. It is still new rock, fractured through, and meltwater from the snowfields above works down through the cracks and seeps out along the whole length of the wall in dark glistening streaks. The seep runs out across the shelf in hundreds of threads, and where water runs constantly over the ground in winter, it freezes. Ice-bound soil kills fir roots. So wherever a seep-line crosses the shelf, a lane stays open. The lanes follow the water, running roughly perpendicular to the cliff, crooked where the water was deflected by a fallen block, branching where it split.

**Frost.** Where the lanes are wet, the ground heaves. Every freeze pushes stones upward, and every thaw lets fine soil sink around them. Over the centuries this has sorted the lane floors into rough cobbles, larger stones lifted and set into a mosaic by the ice. It looks almost laid. In the steeper lanes the frost has stepped the ground into terraces, each riser held by a line of cushion moss. These are the stairs.

**Wind.** Air drains down off the snowfields at night, cold and heavy, and pours through the gaps in the town toward the drop. Where it runs strongly it scours new growth and blasts the tips of branches with ice crystals, so the lane edges are clipped flat and vertical, like hedges. The major lanes, the ones that feel like streets, are wind-channels as well as water-channels. They are cleaner-edged than the alleys, straighter, and a few degrees colder.

The gate is where the largest seep reaches the front of the shelf and the night wind gathers into a single throat before spilling over the edge. The two pillars on either side are clonal fir stems that have been sculpted by that wind for three hundred winters.

---

## Materials, Up Close

Everything is soft to touch and hard underneath.

The walls are dark bottle-green on the outside, where the needles are short, stiff and crowded, silvered at the tips with frost most mornings. Running your hand along one is like stroking a bristle brush that has been left out in the rain.

The lane floors are cobble and moss: grey stones, some still sharp-edged from the rockfall, bedded in black wet peat. Between them grow cushions of a pale sulphur-green moss, dense as felt, holding water like a sponge. Press one and cold water wells up around your fingers.

The roofs are thick with crowberry, a mat of tiny, needled, heather-like leaves studded in late summer with berries black as tar. Lichen climbs over everything that has stopped growing: grey-green foliose rosettes on the dead wood, orange crusts on the cliff, and long pale beard-lichen hanging from the undersides of the roof-eaves in tangled fringes. The lichen drinks fog directly and drips.

There is no bare ground anywhere except the cliff face itself, and even that is only half bare, streaked with liverworts along the seep-lines, black and glossy, like wet paint that never dries. Life fills the shelf entirely. It is less a town built on the land than a town that is the land.

---

## Light

It is always dim here. The cliff faces north-east, so direct sun only touches the shelf in the first hour of summer mornings, and then only the front edge. Most days the cloud sits right on it. Inside the lanes, walled by dark fir, the light is grey-green and diffuse, like light seen through a bottle. Inside the rooms it is darker still, a brown dusk where you can see the outlines of things but not their colours.

When the fog thins you get moments of strange clarity: the whole town suddenly sharp, every needle-tip beaded with water, the wet cobbles gleaming. Then the cloud pours back over the lip and it all goes soft again.

At night there is nothing. There is also one odd light. In certain rooms deep in the old quarter, the rotting heartwood of the oldest dead fir carries a fungus that glows very faintly, a cold blue-green you can only see once your eyes have adjusted fully. It is not bright enough to see by. It traces the grain of the old wood like a map of veins, and it is the only thing about the shelf-town that feels truly uncanny.

---

## Sound

Mostly the town is quiet. The walls are dense and absorbent, and the moss floors swallow footsteps. Sounds inside the lanes die within a few paces.

What you hear is water: the continuous, overlapping tick and patter of dripping. Fog condenses on the needles, gathers, falls. Every eave drips at its own rhythm. Under that is the thin trickle of the seeps running between cobbles, and near the back wall a wet whisper where the cliff weeps across a broad face of liverwort.

At night, when the air drains down, the wind makes a low sound in the main lanes, less a howl than a long exhalation, rising and falling. In hard frost the old dead wood cracks sharply, sudden reports like knuckles popping, echoing once and gone. In spring you sometimes hear a deep, muffled slump from somewhere in the town as a roof finally gives under wet snow. The fir will close the hole within a decade.

---

## The Few Who Live Here

Animals are rare, but they are here, and like a real town the place has its residents, its visitors and its dangers.

**The wren.** A tiny, dark-brown bird, almost tailless, lives in the old quarter. There may be only a handful of pairs on the whole shelf. It moves through the town the way a mouse would, creeping through gaps in the walls, appearing in one room and vanishing into another, picking springtails and frozen gnats out of the needle-duff. Its song is the loudest thing on the shelf: a startling, rattling trill, far too big for the bird, that bounces down the lanes and then stops abruptly. It sings in the grey half-hour before dawn, and then not again all day.

**The voles.** Under the moss floors and in the dead-wood tenements live mountain voles, grey-backed and short-eared. They have their own town beneath the fir's town, a network of tunnels through the duff and the soft rotten wood of the oldest foundations. In winter, under snow, they tunnel along the lanes too, and when the thaw comes the cobbles are scribbled with their packed-earth runways like faint handwriting. They eat crowberry, moss capsules, the tender tips of willow. You rarely see them. You see where they have been.

**The stoat.** Something hunts them. A single slender predator, white in winter and brown-and-cream in summer, moves through the town every few nights. It is sinuous enough to follow the voles into their tunnels and quick enough to take the wren if it catches it on the ground. After it has passed you can sometimes find a small scatter of grey fur on a cobble, or a tiny, perfect set of paired prints in fresh snow running straight down the middle of the main lane, never deviating. It does not live here. It passes through, a stranger on the high street. The voles know the rhythm of its visits better than anything else about their lives.

**The visitors.** Occasionally a raven flies along the front of the shelf in the fog, silent except for the slow creak of its wing-feathers, looking down at the roofs. In late summer, pipits come up from the valley for the crowberries and move in little quarrelling bands across the rooftops for a week or two, the only time the town is noisy, and then they are gone.

That is nearly all. The town is intensely, completely alive, but almost all its life is green, and the animals are a thin occasional thread through it.

---

## The Town in Motion

It looks still. It is not.

Every year, somewhere along the shelf, a wall dies. The needles brown from the inside out, and the room behind it opens up to the lane. The following spring the fir on either side begins sending low branches across the gap, rooting into it, closing it. The town reshapes itself constantly and slowly. If you could watch it at a thousand times normal speed you would see rooms open and close, lanes shift as seeps change their course in the young rock, whole districts of the tenements subside and regrow. It would look like a settlement being endlessly rebuilt by residents who never appear.

The rock itself is still moving. The cliff is fresh, and every spring thaw a few flakes break free and fall. Most are small. Sometimes a block the size of a table comes down, crashes onto the roofs of the old quarter, and settles there. The fir absorbs it within a generation, root and branch closing over it until it is a hump in the roofline. Several of the town's odd high domes are old boulders, clothed.

The seeps shift too, as ice opens new cracks in the cliff and old ones silt up with fine rock-flour. When a seep moves, its lane begins to close and another begins to open. The street plan of the town is a record of where water was last century, slowly overwritten by where it is now.

In winter, snow fills the lanes to the rooftops and the whole town becomes a smooth white slope with dark tips showing through, the rooms below hollow and blue-dim and silent, with the voles alive and busy in the space between snow and moss. In spring the snow drains away over weeks, the lanes reappear full of rushing meltwater, cobbles rolling and clicking underneath, and every room smells sharp and green as the resin wakes up.

In high summer, for perhaps ten days, the cushion moss along the stair-risers flowers. Each tiny white bloom is smaller than a grain of rice, but there are millions. The stairs turn faintly silver, and for a few mornings, if the sun reaches the front of the shelf, the whole town seems to have been dusted with frost that isn't cold.

---

## The Edge

At the front of the shelf the town simply stops. There is a last row of low, wind-flattened growth, then sharp rock, then a drop you cannot see the bottom of because the cloud is always pouring over it. Stand in the gate, between the two wind-carved pillars, and you feel the night air sliding past your legs toward the void. Behind you, the town rises in steps toward the weeping cliff, dark and dense and dripping, full of rooms nobody made and lanes nobody walks, every surface alive, every sound swallowed.

It has the shape of a place where people might live. The water, the frost, the wind and one patient tree made it for no one at all. It is very young, and it is still being built.
