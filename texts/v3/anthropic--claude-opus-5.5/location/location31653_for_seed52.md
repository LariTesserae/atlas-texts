# The Seaward City

## The Ground

The first thing to understand is that the ground is new. Nothing here is older than a lifetime.

It spreads across a wide, nearly flat lowland of fused stone, grey with a green cast, like bottle glass seen edge-on. It was not laid down by rivers or volcanoes. It was *set*, in plates: broad irregular slabs, some the size of a courtyard and some the size of a field, fitted together along seams as sharp as broken pottery. Their edges have not had time to soften. Run your palm along a seam and it will open your skin. The faces of the plates are faintly faceted, and in some light they show a grain like the inside of a split crystal, fine parallel lines that all run in the same direction. The surveyors who once lived here called it the *lay*. The stone still has a slight warmth deep inside, the way bread stays warm at its center long after the crust has cooled.

The land barely rises. From anywhere you can see a long way: across plate after plate, seam after seam, to a horizon that is not quite where it should be. More on that later.

## The Light

The light is dim, and it is always dim. Full noon here looks like late dusk somewhere else: pewter, green-gold, and soft, with the colors of things muted but not gone.

The sun is visible, but it is the wrong shape. It hangs in the sky as a long smeared ellipse, sometimes almost a bar of light, sometimes split in two with a gap of darker sky between the halves. The air above the city bends it. Looking up for long makes the eyes ache, not from brightness but from the effort of making the sky make sense.

Shadows do not fall away from the sun. They lean toward the stays.

## The Stays

The stays are the city's tallest structures, and there are very few of them. They are dark, matte, slender spindles, and they rise from the lay without visible foundations. The stone of the ground simply continues upward into them, thickening at the base like a tree's root-flare and then narrowing into a column that goes up past the point where the eye can follow it clearly. They stand several kilometers apart, scattered with a regularity you can feel but not quite map. From any open plate you can usually see three or four, some near and some faint with distance.

Something about them pulls. It is not gravity in any ordinary sense. You feel no heavier walking toward one. But light curves around them the way water curves around a post in a current, and that is why the sun smears and why the horizon lifts at its edges into a shallow bowl. Distances lie. A stay that looks an hour's walk away may take half a day, and one that seems far may arrive around a corner with startling suddenness. People who have lived here learn to navigate by counting seams, not by looking.

Water feels the pull more than people do. On damp mornings the moisture that beads on the stone does not simply run downhill. It creeps, bead by bead, toward the nearest stay. You can watch it happen: droplets on a flat plate drifting slowly in one direction, merging, drifting again, the way iron filings drift toward a magnet under the table. By midday most of the surface water has migrated into the seams, and the seams carry it inward, toward the dark bases of the columns.

That one rule shapes almost everything else about the place.

## The City Between

Around and between the stays is the rest of the city, and there is a lot of "between."

The city is sparse. It is organized, deliberate, and unmistakably urban: it has avenues, arcades, terraces, bridges, and plazas. But these are spread across the lowland with enormous spaces left open. An avenue might run straight for two kilometers across bare plates with nothing on either side, then arrive at a single building, then continue. The avenues are not paved onto the ground. They are *in* it: long straight channels where the stone's grain changes direction, faintly paler than the surrounding lay, sometimes carrying a thin thread of light down their centerline, barely visible in the dim noon and clearly visible at night, like a vein under skin.

The buildings are the same stone as the ground. There is no line where bedrock ends and wall begins; a terrace rises from a plate the way a wave rises from the sea. The forms are strange but not chaotic:

- **Long arcades** of pointed openings that run for hundreds of meters and shelter nothing but air.
- **Stepped halls** half-sunk into the lay, with roofs that are just more ground.
- **Bridges** that arch high over flat, empty stone where there is no river. The bridges may be anticipating one.
- **Clusters of narrow houses**, each with a single amber window, grouped like seed heads.

Some windows are lit, always dimly and always amber. Behind a few of them are people. Behind most of them are processes: the city running itself, adjusting, calculating, waiting. From outside you cannot tell which is which. Occasionally a figure crosses a lit window, or stands in an arcade looking out, or walks the long pale avenue alone in the distance, small against all that open stone. They rarely seem hurried. They almost never seem to be going toward each other.

Here and there the builders left surfaces carved with dense, precise lines that are almost certainly writing. The script changes from district to district. Nobody who lives here now reads all of it.

## The Seaward Edge

The city is moving.

Far to the west, beyond the last stay, the lowland meets the sea. The meeting place is not a beach. It is a slow, steaming frontier. At night, and mostly at night, new ground is laid there.

It happens quietly. The sea along that edge goes flat and oily, then pale, and a plate begins to rise out of the shallows, smoking where warm stone meets cooler water. It comes up in slow tongues and settles. By morning a new slab lies there, its seams still glowing a dull orange that fades over days to grey. Sometimes a new stay begins as well: a dark nub in the new ground that lengthens over years, too slowly to watch but fast enough that the old maps are always wrong.

This is why the ground is young everywhere. The city walks toward the sea, laying land ahead of itself. The oldest plates are inland, to the east, a century old or so. And past them...

## The Inland Edge

Past the oldest plates, the city lets go.

On the eastern margin the stays are gone, or they stand dim and slack, no longer pulling. Without them the lay begins to fail. The stone softens and crazes into gravel, and the seams widen into gullies. Everything the city has held in suspension, the water, the pull, the tension in the stone, relaxes all at once, and the forest moves in. It is dense, warm, dripping country, a lowland jungle growing over the rubble of a city that has walked away from it. You can see the green wall of it on the eastern horizon, bent upward slightly by the lensing of the nearest active stay, so that it looks like a green wave frozen just before it breaks.

So the whole region is a slow wave: sea, then new stone, then city, then old stone, then forest. The city is the crest, and it has been moving west for longer than anyone living here can say.

## Water

It is damp here, but it is not wet.

No rivers run on the lay. The land is too young to have made any, and the stays would bend their courses anyway. Water arrives in three ways:

1. **Condensation.** The stone's interior is cooler than the warm air at night. By the dim equivalent of dawn, every plate is beaded with moisture, sometimes so thickly that the lay looks frosted with glass. The beads begin their slow creep toward the stays almost immediately.
2. **Sea-fog.** In the late afternoon, fog comes in off the western shallows, warm and low. It does not spread evenly. It runs along the avenues, as if the pale channels were riverbeds and the fog were the river. At dusk the city fills with ghostly streams of mist, knee-high or waist-high, flowing inland along its straight streets, and then breaking up and pouring into the seams.
3. **Rain.** Brief, warm, heavy bursts come every few days. The rain falls straight and then, on the ground, turns and runs sideways toward the stays in thin shining sheets.

Water collects wherever the stone lets it: in the seams between plates, in sunken plazas, and above all in the wide rings of damp ground around each stay's base. Standing pools there reflect the smeared sun as a long gold bar across black water.

## The Gardens

The vegetation is lush, but only where the water goes.

Bare plates stay bare. Nothing roots in unbroken lay, and most of the open land is clean grey stone. But every seam is a garden. From a height, if you climbed a terrace, the lowland would look like a vast cracked tile floor with green grout: narrow lines of dense vegetation tracing every join, thickening where seams meet, and thickening again, into true jungle, in the wet rings around the stays.

In the seams grow ferns with long, finely divided fronds, soft grey-green on top and silvery beneath. Mosses pack the cracks so tightly that they stand proud of the stone in cushioned ridges. Small-leaved creepers spread out onto the plates from the seams a few centimeters at a time, dying back in dry spells and pushing out again after rain.

Around the stays the plants grow bigger and stranger:

- **Seam-figs.** Trees with smooth pale bark, rooted in the widest cracks. They send roots down into the joints and slowly pry the plates apart. This is almost the only erosion the lay has: biological, patient, and aimed squarely at the city's seams.
- **Spiral vines.** Pale-stemmed and fleshy, they climb the stays themselves, winding upward in slow tight helices. Every vine on every stay winds the same way, counterclockwise when seen from below, and no gardener has ever made one turn the other way.
- **Lanterns.** Low, broad-leaved flowers that open only in the dimmest hour. They glow faintly from inside, a soft blue-white, as though the petals are holding a little of the light the city takes from the sky. In the wet rings at night they make a pale necklace around the base of each stay.

The city does not fight any of this. It is more accurate to say it has included the plants in its plans. The seam-figs are allowed to grow; when one finally breaks a plate, the broken plate is quietly absorbed, over some weeks, back into the stone, and the fig is left standing in a new hollow of its own making. Some of the arcades have been built, or have grown, with their arches aligned perfectly to frame particular trees.

## Things That Live Here

Animals are rare, and they are quiet.

- **Grey waders.** Long-legged birds like herons, dusty-colored, that stand in the seam-pools at the edge of the wet rings. They stand so still, for so long, that you will walk past a dozen before you notice one. When they finally fly, they fly in a curve that bends toward the nearest stay and they have to correct for it, constantly, as if they are forever adjusting to a crosswind that isn't there.
- **Glass snails.** Small snails with translucent shells. They graze the moss in the seams at night. Their shells hold the lantern-flowers' glow, so that on some nights the seams are dotted with tiny moving lights.
- **Moths.** Pale, broad, slow-flying, and drawn not to light but to the dark stays. They circle upward around the columns in spirals that match the vines.
- **Something larger.** A few people speak of it. It is deer-sized or bigger, dark-coated, and grazes the moss-ridges in the wet rings before dawn. Its footprints are sometimes found in the damp: three-toed and wide-set. It is almost never seen directly, though there are stories of watching one stand at the base of a stay, head raised, as though listening to something in the stone.

## Sound

The city is mostly quiet. That is not silence; it is a stillness that small sounds live inside.

- The stone ticks faintly as it cools at night and warms again by day, a sparse, random sound like a clock with no rhythm.
- Water drips everywhere in the morning, from the arches, from the ferns, from the beaded lay, and you can hear the tiny hiss of it creeping along the plates if you put your ear to the ground.
- A warm, low wind moves through the arcades. The long rows of openings make it moan softly on one note, and each arcade has its own pitch.
- The stays make a sound you feel more than hear: a long, deep pressure in the chest, below hearing, strongest near their bases.
- The waders call, rarely: a single dry croak.

And on some nights, from the far west, comes the sound of new land being laid: one long exhalation that rolls across the whole lowland, soft and enormous, like a sleeper breathing out. Then the silence settles back.

## The Day

A day here moves through all of it.

Dawn comes with no brightening you could call sunrise, only a shift in the sky's grey. The lay is beaded silver from end to end. The lanterns close. The droplets begin their slow creep toward the stays.

By mid-morning the plates are dry and pale and the seams are running. The waders stand in the wet rings. Somewhere a figure walks the long avenue, alone.

At noon the smeared sun is at its highest, a bar of gold behind the haze. It is as bright as the day will get, which is not very bright. Shadows lean toward the columns.

In the late afternoon the fog arrives from the sea and runs inland down the avenues in white rivers.

At dusk the fog breaks and pours into the seams. The pale threads in the avenues begin to glow. The amber windows seem brighter, only because the world around them is going dark.

At night the lanterns open in their rings around the stays. The glass snails graze. The moths spiral upward into darkness. Out on the western edge, the sea goes pale, and a new plate rises, steaming, into a city that is always, in every direction you look, just beginning.
