# The Sorted Town

## Approach

The mountain is almost gone. Whatever it once was, whatever height or violence it had, has been ground down over an unreckonable span into a broad, tilted shoulder of land. It is still high. The air is thin and the sky has the hard, washed blue that comes only near the top of the weather. But the peaks have been lowered into long, soft swells, like a sleeper's shoulders under a blanket. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Even the boulders are rounded like river stones, though no river has run up here in a very long time.

You come up over the last swell expecting more of the same: turf, lichen, scattered stone. Instead there is a town.

It sits in a shallow bowl perhaps four hundred paces across. Its edges are clear. The walls stop where the bowl's lip begins, and beyond that the open fell resumes as if nothing had happened. Inside the bowl, stone has been arranged into walls, and the walls enclose rooms, and the rooms open onto lanes, and the lanes meet in small irregular squares. There are a few hundred rooms. None has a roof. None has ever had a door.

Nobody built it.

## The Walls

Up close the walls are made of stones, and only stones: no mortar, no dressed faces, no marks of any tool. The large stones sit at the base and the small ones ride on top in a gradient so even it looks deliberate. The walls run from knee height to shoulder height and are about as thick as a forearm is long. Their tops are rounded and furred with black-green moss that has probably been growing for longer than any human language has existed.

The rooms are rough polygons, mostly five- or six-sided, a few four or seven. None is quite like another, but they share a family resemblance, the way cells in a leaf do. They are sized for living in: a small house, a large kitchen, a byre. Some are big enough for a gathering hall. Between them the lanes are floored with fine grit and gravel, the stones that were too small to lift into the walls and so were left behind in the channels.

This is what frost does, given enough time. Each winter the ground freezes and heaves. Each thaw it settles. Stones creep outward from the centers of the freezing cells toward their edges, sorted by size, the large ones shouldering to the margins and the small ones sifting down. On most mountains this makes low rings of rubble you would step over without noticing. Here it has gone on longer, in some way that did not stop when it should have. The rings became walls, the walls became rooms, and the gaps where cells failed to close became doorways.

The doorways are the first strange thing. They are too regular. Nearly every room has one or two, the width of a person's shoulders, set low. Standing in the central square and turning slowly, you notice that many of them line up. Sight lines run straight through five or six rooms at a time, doorway beyond doorway beyond doorway, to the open sky at the far edge of the bowl. The lanes do the same, bending gently toward the same few bearings.

Those bearings are the prevailing winds.

## The Sound

You heard it before you saw it. You may not have realized what you were hearing.

The town is loud. On a calm day, and there are few, it murmurs: water ticking, grit settling, a kind of low breathing at the edge of hearing. When the wind comes over the lip of the bowl, as it does most hours of most days, the town plays.

Each room is a vessel, open at the top, pierced by its doorways. Wind crossing a doorway sets the air inside moving, and each room answers at its own pitch according to its size and shape. The small rooms whistle and keen. The middle rooms hum like a thumb drawn across the rim of a bowl. The big halls give a sound felt more in the ribs than the ears, deep enough to rattle loose grit off the wall tops. Because the doorways align with the winds, the air runs through whole chains of rooms at once, and they sound together. What comes out is not noise. It is a chord.

The chord changes with the wind. A westerly strikes one set of rooms, a southerly another, and the harmony shifts as the weather turns. Gusts make it swell and fall. When a storm front crosses, the town does not so much howl as roar: a vast, many-throated organ note over clattering gravel and the shriek of the smallest rooms, so loud you cannot hear yourself speak even pressed flat against a wall. People who have sheltered here through storms, and a few have, say it is like lying inside a lung.

Under the wind there are other sounds. Meltwater runs in the lanes and chuckles in the gravel. Condensation drips from overhangs into standing pools, plink by plink. In the cold seasons there is the sound that is the town's real voice, if it has one: the cracking. On deep freezing nights the ground heaves, and stones shift in the walls with sharp reports like green branches breaking. They come singly, then in runs, a bang and then a long grinding settle, then silence, then another bang from across the bowl. A night of hard frost sounds like something enormous and patient moving its joints.

## The Damp

The town is almost never fully dry, and almost never truly wet.

Fog is the main supply. Most mornings a cloud bank climbs out of the valleys below and pours over the lip of the bowl. It fills the rooms one by one, as if they were cups, and lingers there after it has drained from the lanes. The stones are always colder than the air, so the fog condenses on them. Every wall face beads with droplets, every moss cushion swells and darkens, and by midmorning the whole town is running with small cold water that no one saw fall.

Rain is occasional and fast. Snow comes in the long winters but is thin; the wind strips most of it into the corners, where it drifts against the windward walls and survives as grey banks deep into summer. Those drifts feed the rills. In the lanes water gathers in shallow basins, pale and clear, floored with sorted grit so fine it looks like flour. These pools freeze and thaw a hundred times a year, and every freeze cracks their skin with a sound like glass under a foot.

Beyond the lip of the bowl the fell is drier: thin turf, wind-scoured, tan most of the year. The town is a pocket where the water stays.

## The Gardens

Every room is a garden, and no two gardens are alike.

The walls do what walls do. They cut the wind, hold the fog, trap a little of the sun's warmth in their stones, and shed water toward their bases. Inside them, in the thin air and the cold, things grow that could never survive on the open fell.

The mosses come first and are everywhere. They lie in deep cushions on the wall tops and in carpets on the floors, some so old and thick they are springy underfoot like a mattress. In sunlight they are acid green, in fog nearly black, and wine-red at the edges where the frost has touched them. Among them grow the cushion plants, hard domes of tiny tight-packed leaves that look like mossy stones until they flower. Then, for a few weeks, they erupt in white and pale violet stars. Tufted grasses stand silver in the corners. Along the bases of the south-facing walls, where the stone radiates a little stored warmth, there are low woody plants: dwarf willows no higher than a hand, creeping over the ground like spilled rope, and a heather-like shrub with bells so dark a red they read as brown.

What grows in a room depends on the room. Its size, the direction its doorways face, how long the fog pools in it, how much snow drifts against its walls: each makes its own weather in miniature. A sheltered, deep room might be a soft green bowl of moss and willow, dense and silent, its floor squelching. A windswept room on the western edge might hold nothing but crustose lichen in orange and chartreuse rosettes and a few tufts of grass bent permanently leeward. One hall near the center is a meadow in high summer, knee-deep and humming. The room beside it is bare gravel and a single frost-split boulder.

So the town is lush, but in patches, cell by cell: a honeycomb of separate gardens, each alone with its own conditions.

## The Emptiness

For all the rooms and lanes and gardens, the town is empty in a way that takes time to feel.

There is nothing in the rooms. No hearths, no middens, no bones, no bits of broken pot. There never were. Every room is made to hold something, the proportions say so insistently, but none holds anything but moss and air and sound. After a while you notice yourself stepping carefully through doorways, keeping to the lanes, lowering your voice in the halls, as if someone might be home. No one is. No one has ever been.

The rooms are hollow in the most literal way, resonating chambers, and the sense of absence may be partly that. They are shaped to ring, and a thing that rings is a thing with nothing inside it. Even the lushest garden rooms feel vacant. The green lies against the stone like a lining. Between the moss floor and the open sky there is a room-sized volume of cold, damp, singing nothing.

Stand in the central square at dusk, in a lull between gusts, and the emptiness becomes almost oppressive: hundreds of open rooms in every direction, every one of them waiting.

## The Animals

Few creatures come here, and the ones that do seem to have made arrangements.

The most common is a small dark bird, sooty brown, about the size of a wren, with a pale bar over the eye. It nests in the walls, in cavities between the large base stones, where the frost heave has left gaps. There may be twenty pairs in the whole town. They forage in the moss cushions for springtails and midge larvae, and they sing. Their song is short, rising, and remarkably pure. They sing only in the gaps: in the lulls between gusts, when the town's chord dies back. Listen long enough and you realize they sing in key. Each bird sings the note of the room it nests in.

Mountain hares pass through in winter, white as the drifts, and shelter in the lee of walls during storms. They do not stay. A pair of ravens visits some days, riding the updrafts over the lip of the bowl, and sits on the tallest wall tops working at something, calling back and forth in their clicking, gargling voices, which the rooms pick up and throw around. Once in a while a fox's tracks cross the lanes in fresh snow, always in a straight line from one edge of the bowl to the other, never turning into a room.

There are no grazers. Nothing eats the gardens but insects and weather.

## How the Town Works

The longer you stay, the more the place reveals its rules, and the less sure you are of which rules are its own.

**It is still being built.** The frost has not stopped. Each winter the ground heaves and the stones move. Each spring the town is subtly different. A wall has lifted a fingerwidth. A doorway has narrowed. A room on the eastern margin has, over a few dozen winters, sealed itself shut, its doorway filling stone by stone until it became a closed cell full of undisturbed moss. Elsewhere a wall has leaned and fallen in, and two rooms have become one larger hall with a new, deeper voice.

**It builds toward the wind.** This is harder to believe but easy to see once you look. The rooms that close are the ones whose doorways fall out of line with the winds. The doorways that widen are the ones the air runs through. It may be that wind-driven snow and scouring tip the frost's sorting one way or another. It may be something less explicable. Either way, the town seems to be tuning itself. Over enough time it arranges its stones so that the wind passes through, and in passing, sounds.

**The sounds shape the gardens.** Where the chord is loudest, where the air moves hardest, the plants are low and tough, lichen and grass. In the quiet dead-end rooms the mosses grow deep and the willows thicken. The sound map of the town and its green map are the same map drawn twice.

**Frost is the builder, wind the player, fog the gardener.** Each works on its own schedule. Frost moves fastest on long winter nights, with its bangs and grindings. Wind works nearly always, hardest in the equinox gales. Fog comes in the mornings and in the long grey stretches of late summer. They overlap and interfere and set each other going. A freeze shifts a stone, the shift changes a doorway, the doorway changes a room's note and how long the fog lingers there, and the lingering fog changes what grows. The town is never still. It is only slow.

## The Ending

It is old, and it is ending.

This is the last thing the mountain is doing. The peaks have gone, the cliffs have gone, the ridges have slumped into these long swells. What remains is this: a thin skin of loose stone on a nearly flattened summit, sorted and resorted by the cold, over and over, into the only shape the process knows. Given enough time the whole shoulder will be ground down to gravel. The town will shrink. Its walls will lower. Its rooms will shallow. Its voice will rise in pitch as the vessels get smaller, until one day it plays only the thinnest whistle, and then nothing, and there is a gravel plain under the high blue sky with the fog coming over it and nowhere to pool.

That day is very far off. For now the town is at the height of its making, or near enough. The walls stand, the gardens are green and deep, and the dark birds sing in the gaps.

On a clear afternoon, after the morning fog has burned off, the whole bowl steams faintly in the sun. Every moss cushion glitters. The wind comes up out of the west, and the town takes a long breath and begins to play: hundreds of empty rooms sounding together, a chord no one wrote, tuned over ages by frost and weather, loud enough to feel in your teeth, for no one to hear.
