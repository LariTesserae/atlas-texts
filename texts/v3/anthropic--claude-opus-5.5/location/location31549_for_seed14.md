# The Deep Fan

## The approach

Everything here is under something. Four thousand meters of ocean sit above, and the floor itself sits below what the floor used to be. This is a basin inside a basin: a broad, shallow sag in the abyssal plain, several days' walk across if walking were possible, where the seabed slumped long ago and never rose again. There is no edge to see. The dark ends your sight at arm's length, and the water ends it even sooner.

The water is the first thing to understand, because it is barely water at all. It is thick. Silt hangs in it so densely that it behaves like a thin broth, a column of suspended clay and powdered shell and the ground-down remains of mountains carried off the continent over tens of millions of years. Nothing moves through it without pushing a hundred billion grains aside. Pushed grains don't fall back. They join the drift.

## The floor

Under the broth, as far as anyone could reach, the floor is paved with nodules.

They are black-brown lumps of manganese and iron, fist-sized to melon-sized, grown a millimeter per million years around a shark's tooth, a fish's ear-bone, a flake of pumice. They lie shoulder to shoulder with no gaps, the way cobbles lie in an old street that has settled for centuries. When your hand presses between two of them it finds only a third, and beneath that a fourth, half-buried, and beneath that the soft grey clay. Some are older than the continents are in their present shapes. Their surfaces are finely botryoidal, clustered like grapes or cauliflower. That texture is the only record of ten million years of patience.

The ground itself has no relief left to speak of. Whatever hills were once here have been planed away. The low fault scarps of the old slump have softened to swells so gentle that you would sense them only as a slight lean in the current. This is the flattest kind of land there is. It is not new and smooth like a salt pan. It is old and smooth, the way a step is worn down by feet.

Here and there, faint raised lines cross the nodule field. They are lithified burrows, the tracks of animals that died before there were flowers. They have turned to stone and been exhumed by scour. The floor keeps giving up its dead like this, a millimeter at a time.

## The storms

The plain is ancient, but it is not calm.

Benthic storms come down the continental rise in pulses, and they never fully stop. Some start as eddies in the surface currents thousands of meters overhead, their energy spiraling downward until it reaches the bottom and starts dragging. Others are turbidity currents. Somewhere up-slope, a ledge of sediment gives way, perhaps shaken loose by a distant earthquake, or perhaps simply overloaded, and a slurry of mud and water pours downhill faster than a river. It spreads across the fan in sheets many meters thick and many kilometers wide.

Inside a storm, the broth becomes a gale. Grains that hung still now stream sideways. Nodules that sat unmoved for an ice age rock in their sockets, grind against their neighbors, and settle again a finger's width over. Ripple marks form in the clay between them, then vanish, then form again facing a different direction within the same hour. Sediment is lifted from one part of the basin and dumped in another. A patch that was bare nodule at dusk, had there been a dusk, can be buried under a hand's depth of fresh ooze by morning, and scoured back to stone a week later.

This is the paradox the place lives inside. Its forms are final and its surface is frantic. The deep structure hasn't changed in geological ages, while the skin of it changes by the minute. The storms can move everything but can alter nothing that matters. They churn the same old grains over the same old stones, forever.

Currents arrive from every direction. The basin traps them, and they collide, fold, and double back. There is no lee anywhere.

## Light

There is none, or almost none.

Sunlight gave out three and a half kilometers overhead. What remains is occasional and self-made. A blue smear appears when some drifting creature is jostled by the turbulence and flashes in alarm. The flash is swallowed at once by the silt, so it glows like a lamp seen through fog, and then it is gone. In the thickest storms even these flashes are blotted out, and the dark becomes total, a dark with texture and weight.

## Sound

It is quiet, but the quiet is full.

The silt deadens everything. A rock falling a meter away would make no report. What you would hear, if you could hear here, is the hiss: the endless sibilance of grain sliding past grain, so constant it stops being a sound and becomes the condition of existing. Under the hiss, during the larger storms, comes a very low grinding. It is felt more than heard, the sound of nodules shouldering each other. And sometimes, from very far off, comes the deep dull thud of a slope failing somewhere up the rise, carried through the rock more than through the water. That thud is the only warning the plain gets that a new flood of mud is coming.

## What grows

"Vegetation" is the wrong word for anything here, but there are things that take the place of plants.

The nodules carry a thin rind of life: bacterial films in rust and cream, living on metal and the slow chemistry of the clay. In the rare lulls, faint mats spread across the sediment in the hollows, pale as frost on a windowpane, and the next storm tears them away. They are always beginning.

Rooted things are few. Here and there a glass sponge stands on its stalk, anchored to a nodule by a tuft of silica fibers, its lattice body filtering the broth. Most have been knocked down, and their skeletons lie among the stones like dropped lace. A handful of stalked crinoids, sea lilies, cling to the higher swells, arms furled against the current. When a storm eases they open like slow hands to catch whatever the flow brings.

What it brings, mostly, is marine snow, the continual fall of dead matter from the sunlit world far above. In calm water it would drift down gently. Here the storms seize it and mix it into the broth, so the snow never quite lands. It is suspended and resuspended, turned over for weeks, and finally buried. This one fact feeds everything in the basin. The storms that make life hard also keep food aloft where filter-feeders can reach it.

## What moves

Animals are rare, and each one is an event.

A holothurian, a sea cucumber the color of a bruise, trundles across the nodules on tube feet, swallowing ooze and leaving a looped trail of cast behind it. The trail lasts perhaps a day. A brittle star wedges itself between two nodules and waits out a storm with arms coiled. Once in a great while a rattail fish passes, long-tailed and large-eyed, nosing along the bottom against the current, hunting by smell and by the pressure of other bodies in the water. It is gone into the silt within a body-length.

A small, pale octopus lives somewhere on the western swell. It has been seen wedging stones around the mouth of a hollow under a large nodule, dragging smaller nodules into a rough ring, repairing the ring after every storm. This is the nearest the place comes to building, and the storm always wins.

## What has been dropped

There are, very rarely, things among the nodules that were made.

They are stones with holes bored through them: flat pieces of basalt and sandstone, pecked and ground by hand, the drilled holes worn smooth by a cord that rotted away long ago. They are net-sinkers and line weights, lost from boats of hide or hollowed log on the continental shelf, perhaps thousands of years ago. The turbidity currents carried them here in the same slurries that bring everything else down from the edge of the land. A few are knapped flints. One is a grinding stone, dished in the middle from years of use.

They lie among the nodules as equals. They are younger, but already a nodule's rind has started to form on the oldest of them, a faint dark film of manganese, the first millimeter of a coat that will take the rest of time to thicken. The plain does not distinguish between what was made and what was found. Given long enough, it will turn every human stone into one of its own.

## The rules

The rules of this place are simple, and nothing breaks them.

Everything that falls in, stays. Nothing leaves the basin except as dissolved chemistry, and even that slowly. The storms rearrange but never remove.

Everything is touching. Nodule touches nodule, grain touches grain, and the broth pushes on every body in it from every side. There is no open water, no empty ground, no clear air. To be here is to be pressed.

Everything is old except the surface, and the surface is always new. Every hour a fresh skin of disturbance lies over foundations that have not changed since before the Himalaya rose.

And it is cold, a steady two degrees above freezing that never varies with season, because there are no seasons here. There are only storms and the short intervals between them, which the few living things spend opening their arms and their gills to feed before the next wall of mud arrives out of the dark, hissing, and puts everything back into motion, and leaves it exactly where it was.
