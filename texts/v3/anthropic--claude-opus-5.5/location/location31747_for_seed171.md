# The Rising Stand

## First, the sound

Before anything else, there is the noise. It isn't a single noise. It's a pressure so total that it stops registering as sound and becomes a condition of the air, like humidity or cold.

Imagine standing behind a waterfall, except the water is going *up*. That is closest to the truth. Billions of stems, each a column of wet wood thick as a cathedral pillar, lift water from the root-stone to the crown hundreds of meters above. They lift it constantly, in quantities that would drain a lake in an afternoon. The xylem is not the passive plumbing of ordinary trees. It is pumped, driven by something in the cell walls that works like a peristaltic engine at the molecular scale. Every vessel contracts and releases in slow waves. The waves are not synchronized, so the result is not a rhythm but a roar: a low, grinding, liquid thunder that comes from every direction at once and from inside your own chest.

Over the roar sit the other sounds, which are only audible because they are louder still.

The **crack**, at dawn, when the air temperature shifts by a few degrees. Across whole valleys of stem the wood contracts, and the sound rolls across the landscape like artillery heard from inside the battlefield. Each individual crack is a gunshot. There are millions.

The **groan**, all day, as the Stand grows. Wood lengthening against wood, stems swelling into the spaces between them, which are already no space at all. It is the sound of ships' hulls in ice, sustained for centuries.

The **hiss** of condensation, and the **drum** of the afternoon rain on leaf-plates the size of sails.

No bird has ever added to this. No insect. Nothing here has a voice. Every sound in the Stand is the sound of a single organism working.

## The ground that is not ground

There is no soil. There was no soil to begin with.

Underneath the Stand, the land is new. It is so new that it has not had time to weather. The root-mass does not grow *into* rock. It *makes* the rock. At the root-front, a zone of pale, fibrous, fist-thick filaments exudes a mineral solution and assembles it, crystal by crystal, into stone. The stone is sharp, glassy, grey-green and banded like the inside of an agate. It has never known frost-heave or a river. Its edges could cut you. Its surfaces are still slightly warm from the chemistry of their making.

This is why the land is high, and why it is getting higher. The Stand is building a plateau beneath itself. It stacks new stone under its own roots and rises on it, a few centimeters a year, across a region so large its far edges lie beyond the curvature of the world. Its interior now sits well over two thousand meters above where the old coastline used to be. Nobody can say where that coastline was, because the Stand is still growing outward from it in every direction, and the old ground is buried under a kilometer of deliberate stone.

The engineering is not visible. There are no structures. Nothing here was assembled by hands, and nothing looks made. But nothing here is natural, either, in any sense that word used to have. The organism is a technology that has finished becoming a landscape. Whoever seeded it — if anyone did, if it wasn't something that seeded itself from a smaller, earlier machine — left no trace except the thing itself.

## The shape of the Stand

From above, if you could get above it, the Stand would look like a single dark surface, an unbroken pelt thrown over a continent. From inside, it is more like being in the tissue of a lung.

The stems are packed. They don't stand *among* each other so much as *against* each other, fused in places where two trunks have grown into contact and simply become one. In other places they've split, a single stem dividing into three, each third a pillar in its own right. There are no clearings. There are no gaps wider than a hand. Where one stem dies, which is rare, its neighbors grow into its volume within a season. The dead wood is reabsorbed, drawn back into the living mass. There are no fungi and no rot here. Nothing decays; everything is recovered.

In cross-section the Stand is tiered. The root-stone is honeycombed with channels and pale filaments. Above it rise the pillars. They are dark brown, almost black, ribbed vertically, and the ribs pulse faintly if you watch long enough. Above that, a hundred or two hundred meters up, is a lattice of lateral branches. They interlock into a floor that you could walk across, if walking were a thing that happened here. Above that comes the crown.

The crown is where the weirdness sits most plainly.

## Black leaves

The leaves are black.

Not dark green, not purple: black, the matte, velvety black of soot or deep space. Their surfaces are structured at scales far finer than a cell. There are forests of nanoscopic pillars on every leaf, tuned to trap light and hold it until it is used. The Stand eats light the way a sponge eats water. Almost nothing reflects. Almost nothing reaches the lower tiers.

So the Stand is dim, and it is dim even at noon, even at the crown. The sky above is usually overcast, and the sun is a pale disc behind cloud. Below the canopy the only light comes in faint, filtered greys that seem to belong to no particular time of day. At the level of the root-stone, it is twilight forever. What light does reach the bottom arrives already exhausted, as if it had to squeeze past every one of those billions of leaves on the way down.

The leaves are enormous: sail-shaped plates, some fifteen meters long, overlapping like scales. When the wind moves them, which it does constantly, the crown ripples. Seen from a high ridge, the Stand looks like a vast dark animal breathing, though there are no animals.

## Weather of its own making

The Stand is large enough to have its own climate, and it does.

Every morning it breathes out. The pumping of water from root to crown ends in transpiration at a scale that dwarfs any rainforest that ever existed. By mid-morning a white mist has risen from the canopy. By noon the mist has become low cloud, sitting on the Stand like a lid. By mid-afternoon the cloud has thickened into a grey, heavy cover that stretches to every horizon. Then it rains.

The rain is cool, steady, and heavy. It falls onto the leaf-plates and runs down them in sheets, and at the tips of the leaves it gathers and pours. Beneath the crown there is a second rain: water running off branches, dripping from every rib and fold of the pillars, streaming down the bark in continuous ribbons. The bark weeps. The stems are always wet. At the root-stone the water pools briefly in the glassy hollows of new rock, and then it is drawn back up, and the cycle begins again.

The water barely leaves. The Stand keeps almost everything it catches. Rivers do form at its edges, draining the outermost slopes, and those rivers are the only erosive force the new stone has ever known. They are tiny compared to the Stand. They have carved nothing yet but a few shallow, raw-edged gullies at the rim of the plateau, where the rock is still so fresh it looks like it has been freshly broken.

The air is cool. It's the coolness of altitude and constant evaporation, of wet stone and wet wood and a sky that rarely clears. The temperature doesn't swing much. It stays in a narrow band, damp and chill, like the inside of a cave or a cellar. On the rare clear nights, the Stand cools further, and at dawn the cracking begins.

## The growth front

At the edge of the Stand — any edge, because there are many, and all are advancing — is the place where the land is most itself.

Here the root-front meets old bedrock. The ground is split and shattered, heaved upward by the pressure of the pale filaments pushing into it. The filaments work their way through cracks, dissolve the old stone, and rebuild it in the Stand's own banded glass. The old rock goes in grey and weathered, and the new rock comes out sharp and green-veined and whole. Where the two meet, you can see the seam: a line of crushed gravel and mineral paste, hissing faintly where the chemistry is still active.

Above the seam, the young stems rise. They are thinner than the pillars of the interior, only as thick as a person, and they grow fast: a meter a week, sometimes more. They're packed together from the start, crowding in ranks, and the sound of their growth is higher-pitched than the deep groan of the interior. It's a creaking, a whining, a million-voiced squeal of wood stretching. Up close, it is painfully loud.

The black leaves unfurl here, slowly, peeling open from tightly rolled spears. At first they are a deep bruised red. They darken over days.

The growth front moves steadily outward. Behind it, the plateau rises. Ahead of it, the old land waits. It is empty and quiet, a landscape of bare rock and thin grass that the Stand will one day cover entirely.

## What is not here

There are no animals. No insects hum in the crown; no worms work the root-stone. The Stand does not flower. It has no need of pollinators. It grows clonally, one organism spread across a continent, every stem a branch of a single root-mass.

There are no paths. There's no room for them. There are no ruins, no marks, no evidence that anything other than the Stand has ever been here, except for the Stand itself, whose existence implies intent.

And there is no quiet. There's nowhere in the Stand where the noise stops, except perhaps at the very bottom, deep in the root-stone, where the sound of pumping water becomes a vibration you feel in the rock rather than hear. Even there, the Stand is working.

## The rule of the place

If the Stand has a law, it is this: *nothing is wasted, and everything is filled.*

Every drop of water is caught and lifted. Every photon is trapped. Every dead stem is recovered. Every gap is closed. Every piece of ground is rebuilt. The Stand is not a forest, not really. It's a single process, patient and immense, turning a continent into itself.

It is cool, and wet, and dim, and deafening. It is rising. It has been rising for a very long time, and it has only just begun.
