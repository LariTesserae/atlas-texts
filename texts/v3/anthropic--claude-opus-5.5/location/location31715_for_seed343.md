# The Singing Table

## Approach

The mountain is gone. That is the first thing to understand, though you are standing on it.

What remains is a tableland perched at the top of the sky, four or five miles above anything anyone would call sea level. It is so old that its peaks have long since been planed away, its ridges softened into swells no higher than a sleeping animal's flank, its valleys filled and forgotten. From the edge, where the land stops abruptly in a lip of crumbling green, you look *down* on weather. Cloud decks spread below like a second, paler country, and through their gaps, very far down, lie brown smudges that might be the plains or might be more cloud in shadow.

The table runs to the horizon in every direction but the one behind you. It is nearly flat. The flatness is not the flatness of a new plain; it is the flatness of something that has been lying down for a very long time and no longer remembers standing.

And it is loud.

## The Ground

There is no soil here, and no rock you can see. The whole surface is a single living mat of cushion plant, a dense, domed, tight-packed growth of tiny rosettes, each no bigger than a fingernail, millions to the square stride. It is the color of old copper and wet lichen, with a bloom of grey-green where the newest growth catches the sun. Press your hand into it and it gives slightly, then resists. It is warm, and a little damp, and it smells of pepper and tea and something faintly sweet, like bread just beginning to go sour.

The warmth is not from the sun alone. Dig down (the herders do this, with flat bone spades) and you pass through a finger's depth of living green, then a hand's depth of brown, still-fibrous stems, then into the old body of the plant: a black, compacted, gently steaming peat that goes down farther than anyone has dug. It is rotting, very slowly, as it has been rotting for ages, and the rot gives off heat. The table is warmed from beneath by its own decomposition. At this altitude the air should be killing cold, and at night it tries to be, but the ground exhales a steady breath of warmth, and a thin layer of mild air clings to the surface like a blanket laid over a sleeper. Stand up and your face is cool. Lie down and you are in a summer afternoon.

Somewhere under all that peat is the mountain: granite, or basalt, or whatever it was. The plant has been growing on its own dead for so long that the stone is a rumor.

In a few places the rumor surfaces. These are the teeth: low stubs of rock, rounded to the smoothness of river pebbles though no river has touched them in an age, poking a few feet above the green. They are scattered miles apart. Each one is a landmark, each one has a name, and the herders say each one is the tip of a peak that was once higher than any mountain now standing anywhere.

## Water

The table is not wet, but it is never dry. There are no streams. Rain comes rarely, in short hard bursts when a storm climbs high enough to spill over the edge. Most of the water arrives as fog.

Every night the clouds below cool and thicken. Before dawn they rise, flowing up over the lip of the table like milk boiling over a pot, and roll inland across the green. The cushion plants drink it. Each rosette is fringed with fine white hairs that catch the fog and bead it into droplets; at sunrise the entire surface glitters, every one of those millions of rosettes wearing a single bead of light. For an hour the table looks as if it has been dusted with ground glass. Then the sun warms it, the beads sink into the mat, and the fog lifts and thins and is gone by mid-morning.

Here and there, where the peat has slumped into a shallow bowl, water collects: small, dark, perfectly round pools no wider than a house, tea-colored and startlingly warm, steaming faintly in the morning. They are miles apart. Everything that lives here knows where every one of them is.

## The Sound

It begins before you see the table and does not stop while you are on it.

Living inside the cushion mat, in the damp warm layer just below the green, are insects: small, dark, beetle-backed creatures the herders call *tsik*, uncountable in number, perhaps hundreds under every footstep. They feed on the living stems and the fungi threading the peat. And they sing. They rasp a ridged leg across a ridged wing-case, and the sound of any one of them is barely a tick. The sound of all of them together, stretched across a tableland a hundred miles wide, is a roar.

It is not a noise like wind or water. It is a pulsing, grinding, silvery shriek that sits in the chest and the teeth and the soles of the feet as much as in the ears. The mat itself resonates with it; you can feel the ground buzz. Two people standing arm to arm must shout directly into each other's ears to be heard, and mostly they do not bother.

Stay a while and you begin to hear that the sound has structure.

The *tsik* sing in synchrony. Not all together, but in regions, in patches miles wide that fall into step with one another. The singing swells and drops in long waves, rolling across the table like wind moving across a field of grain. You can hear a surge approach from the east, a wall of sound louder and brighter than the rest, pass over you so that your whole body hums, and move on westward, leaving a relative quiet (which is still louder than any city) in its wake. Sometimes two waves meet and cancel out, and for a few breaths there is a strange trough of near-silence in which you can hear your own blood. Sometimes they meet and double, and the noise becomes something you endure with your eyes shut.

The rhythm follows the heat. When the sun is high and the mat is warmest, the waves come fast and short, a restless shimmer. At dusk they slow and lengthen, and the table seems to breathe in huge slow pulses, each one lasting the span of a dozen human breaths. In the coldest hour before dawn, the singing drops to a low, uneven murmur, and that is the closest the table ever comes to quiet.

In the fog, the sound does something odd. The dense wet air carries it differently, and the waves become visible: faint ripples in the mist, standing bands of thicker and thinner vapor that shiver in time with the song. On a still foggy morning you can watch the chorus move.

## The Air, and the Emptiness

For all that roar, the table *looks* empty.

There are no trees. Nothing grows taller than your ankle. The green runs flat and unbroken to a horizon so distant, and so clear in the thin air, that you can see the curve of the world in it. The sky is enormous, a deep hard blue at the zenith that fades to a white glare at the edges where the cloud country below reflects light back up. By day the light is clean and even, almost shadowless on the flat ground, the kind of light in which distant things look near. A tooth-stone ten miles off looks like a pebble you could walk to before lunch.

Presences are rare and far apart. A single herder, a single hut, a single pool, a single standing stone, each separated from the next by hours of walking across nothing. The space between them is so large that it becomes a presence of its own. You can walk all day and see only the green, the sky, and one dark speck on the horizon that does not seem to get any closer.

It is the emptiness of a field seen from above: nothing tall, nothing close, everything happening *beneath* the surface or *in the air*, and the ground between them as smooth and continuous as skin.

## What Hunts

The *tsik* are the base of everything. Above and around them, the table teems.

There are the birds: small brown ground-runners with long curved bills, which probe the mat for insects and move in loose flocks of thousands, spread thin across the ground so that you only see a few at a time until something startles them and the whole surface for a mile around erupts into the air at once. There are swifts, black and scythe-winged, that never seem to land, hunting the *tsik* when they fly. There are flat grey lizards that lie on the warm mat with their bellies pressed to it, drinking heat, and lunge at anything that moves within a tongue's length. There are burrowing things, shrews or something like them, whose tunnels make faint raised seams in the green that you learn to step around.

Above them are the raptors, pale falcons that hover for long minutes on the steady updraft rising off the warm ground, and then drop.

And twice a day, at dawn and dusk, the *tsik* themselves take to the air.

Something in the change of heat sets it off. In one region, then another, the insects crawl up out of the mat, open their wing-cases, and rise. They rise in columns: dense, twisting, smoke-dark pillars of beetles lifting straight up off the table, hundreds of feet high, their song changing in pitch as they fly to a high, keening whine. From any point on the table you can see a dozen of these columns at once, standing at great distances from one another across the green, like the smoke of widely scattered fires. The swifts pour into them. The falcons slash through. The ground-runners leap and snap at the bases. Around each column the air boils with wings.

It lasts a quarter of an hour. Then the columns lean, thin, and collapse, and the survivors drop back into the mat to mate and lay eggs, and the song resumes from below, rougher and more ragged for an hour until it finds its waves again.

## The People

There are people here, though not many, and they are spread as thin as everything else.

They are herders. Their animals are small, woolly, split-hoofed grazers, something between a goat and a deer, that crop the cushion plants and never seem to damage them. The herds drift slowly across the table from pool to pool on routes older than anyone's grandmother. The people go with them.

They have no metal. Their tools are bone, horn, and the stone of the teeth, which they will not quarry but will gather in fragments that have cracked away over the ages. Every chip of stone is precious. A good scraper is handed down through generations. They cut the black peat into bricks with bone spades and dry it in the sun, and from these bricks they build their huts: low, rounded, windowless domes half-sunk into the mat, heated from beneath by the same slow rot that warms everything else. A hut is abandoned when the herd moves on, and within a few years the cushion plant has grown over it and it is a slight green hummock, indistinguishable from the rest except to the people who remember it.

So the table is dotted with these ghosts of houses, faint swellings under the green, and here and there a hut still standing, with a thread of smoke from a peat fire and a few grazers dozing beside it.

They plug their ears with a wax made of rendered fat and the white fog-catching hairs of the cushion plant. They speak mostly with their hands, in a sign language of great subtlety and speed that the children learn before they learn words. Spoken language is for the hour before dawn, the quiet hour, and so speaking aloud has become a kind of intimacy: what you say out loud, you say only in the near-silence, only to people you trust.

They carry drums, but they do not play them for music. The drums are for talking across distances. A low, deep beat, played into the ground rather than the air, travels through the resonant peat far better than any sound travels through the din above it. A herder with an ear pressed to the green can hear a drum from a neighbor's camp five miles off, the message thudding up through the mat beneath the song of the *tsik*. The table is crisscrossed with these buried conversations.

At each tooth-stone there are marks: shallow grooves, patient scratches, rubbed hollows where generations of hands have touched the same spot. Nobody reads them now. They are older than the herders' memory, and some of them are so worn that they have nearly faded back into the stone. A few show the same shape again and again: a wavy line, repeated, rolling across the rock. Whoever made them heard the waves too.

## Weather

Storms come from below. You watch them build in the cloud country: towers of grey climbing slowly up the face of the table, flickering with lightning deep inside. Most never reach the top. The ones that do spill over the edge in a sudden wall, hard warm rain slamming down onto the green for twenty minutes, drumming so loud that for once the *tsik* are drowned out, or fall silent, and the only sound in the world is water. The pools overflow. The mat drinks and drinks. Afterwards, the whole table steams in the sun, the song returns louder than before, and by the next dawn the columns rising from the mat are twice as thick.

Wind is constant but gentle at ground level, trapped under the warm blanket of air. Stand up and it pulls at your hair; lie down and it vanishes.

At night the sky is overwhelming: black, crowded with stars down to the very horizon, the band of the galaxy so bright it throws faint shadows. Below the table's edge, the cloud country glows silver under the moon. And the ground is warm, and pulses faintly with the slow night-waves of the song, and the fog begins, somewhere far off at the edge, to gather itself for its morning climb.

## What It Is

The table is the end of a mountain. It is what happens when stone has been worn down as far as it can be worn and something living has settled onto it and refused to leave. It is flat because it is finished. It is warm because it is decaying. It is loud because it is alive, more densely alive under the skin than almost anywhere, and it is empty because all of that life lies low, and the sky above it is too big to fill.

Walk across it long enough and you stop hearing the roar as noise. It becomes the table's pulse. You find yourself breathing in time with the waves. The herders say that is how you know you have been here long enough: when the song stops sounding like the insects and starts sounding like the ground itself, lying on its back at the top of the world, slowly breathing, a very old thing that has not quite finished dying and is in no hurry to.
