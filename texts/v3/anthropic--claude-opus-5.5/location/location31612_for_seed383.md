# The Chalk Undercity of Sessile

## The Roof

You enter it the way water once did: downward, through a crack in a desert plateau, along a stair worn so smooth it has become a ramp, its steps now only faint ripples underfoot. The crack widens, and then there is no rock above you at all. There is light.

The roof of Sessile is selenite: sheets of gypsum crystal, some as thin as a fingernail and some as thick as a door, grown across the top of a collapsed basin over an unguessable length of time. Sun from the desert above falls through it and arrives changed. It is white, total and shadowless, the light inside a cloud. It is brighter here, forty meters underground, than it was on the plateau. The crystal gathers the sun and spills it evenly in every direction, so that a hand held up casts no shadow, only a faint grayness in the air beneath it.

The roof is not still. It is growing and failing at once. Along its seams, where moisture wicks through from the few rains that reach the plateau, new crystal spreads in feathered blades. Elsewhere, old panes loosen, and every few minutes, somewhere in the basin, one lets go.

They do not crash. A pane of selenite two meters across, thin as glass, falls the way a leaf falls. It tilts, slides sideways on the rising warm air, catches the light and flares like a struck match, then tilts again and drifts on. Some take a full minute to reach the ground. From anywhere in the city you can usually see three or four at once, turning slowly through the white air, throwing moving blades of brightness across the terraces below. Where a pane has fallen, a patch of raw sunlight comes through, hard and gold and shocking. Where two panes have grown together, the light thickens to milk. The brightness of Sessile never holds a single shape for more than an hour.

## The Floor, and the Silence

Everything that has ever fallen here is still here, ground to flour.

The basin floor is gypsum dust, meters deep in the low places, fine as talc and pale as bone. It swallows sound entirely. A falling pane lands in it with a soft exhalation and disappears to half its width. A dropped stone makes no noise. Your own voice, if you speak, seems to stop a hand's width from your mouth. The dust coats the air as well. It hangs in slow, turning curtains wherever the convection moves it, and it drinks every echo the stone might have given back.

So Sessile is a place of enormous motion and almost no sound. The loudest thing most visitors ever hear is their own pulse. Beneath that, if you wait, there is a faint continuous whisper: dust settling onto dust, the sound of a place being very slowly buried.

## The City

The city fills the basin, and the basin is large. From the entry ramp you can see terraces descending in every direction until they fade into the white haze, perhaps four or five kilometers across. It is a real city, with streets, stairs, cisterns, arcades, towers and courts, all cut from the pale limestone the gypsum sits on and built up from blocks of that same stone.

It is also impossibly old. Nothing in Sessile has a corner anymore. Ages of condensation and dust-scour have rounded every edge. The doorways are ovals. The windows have become soft-lipped holes, like the openings of shells. The towers taper and lean, and their tops have melted into rounded knobs, so the skyline looks like a field of worn teeth, or of candles burned low. Carvings survive only as ripples: a relief of figures on a wall has become a row of gentle swellings you can feel with your palm but cannot see. The streets are troughs, and the stairs are slopes with a memory of rhythm. Whoever first cut this place, it has been so long since they left that the stone has almost forgotten them.

The city is sparse. It was built wide, with great open plazas and long empty avenues between clusters of dwellings, and the dust has deepened that emptiness. Whole districts lie half-buried, with only the rounded tops of their walls breaking the white surface like the backs of surfacing animals. You can walk a kilometer in Sessile without passing anything but pale ground and the slow drift of a falling pane.

And yet the city is being built. Constantly, feverishly, it is being built.

## The Masons

They are the size of a large hand, flat-bodied and many-legged, with shells the exact gray of wet limestone. There are millions of them. People who have visited Sessile call them masons, and the name fits. They quarry, carry, stack and mortar without pause, and they are the reason the city still stands at all.

A mason works with tools. Each carries a hammerstone, a pebble of harder rock it has chosen and keeps tucked beneath its forelegs. With it, it taps loose flakes from buried walls or from fallen blocks, knapping them into rough bricks. It carries these one at a time, clamped against its underside, to wherever the colony is working. It mortars them with a paste it makes by chewing gypsum dust with the dew it has sipped. You can watch a single mason for an hour and never see it rest.

What they build is not quite the city that was here. They follow its lines, raising a collapsed wall along its old footing or roofing a gutted tower, but they build in their own way: in thin courses of flake-brick, curved, porous, faintly scalloped, like a wasp's paper nest made of stone. Many of the old buildings are now cased in mason-work, the ancient rounded limestone showing through gaps in a crust of newer, rougher, living construction. In places the masons have gone further and built things with no ancient precedent. There are bridges of flake-brick spanning dust-filled avenues, and spiraling chimneys that reach up toward the selenite and catch the warm rising air. Near the center of the basin stands a tower of pure mason-work, taller than anything the original builders made. It is still growing, and no one knows what it is for.

Their work is endless because the dust is endless. Every fallen pane buries something. The convection currents carry dust in drifting storms from one district to another, and a street that was open at dawn is a pale dune by evening. The masons dig it out and build it up again, and the dust falls again. The city is always half-lost and half-made, a vast slow turmoil in which nothing is permanent but the effort itself.

They make no sound. Their feet are soft and their hammerstones tap against stone muffled by dust. Standing in a working district, you can see ten thousand small gray bodies in motion across the walls, streams of them carrying brick, clusters of them mortaring an arch, and hear nothing at all.

## Dew

The only water in Sessile comes from the air.

Each night, as the desert above goes cold, the selenite roof cools with it, and the air in the basin, which has been warm and rising all day, sheds its moisture. By the hours before dawn, condensation beads on everything. It runs in fine threads down the tower-walls, gathers in the rounded hollows of old windows, drips from the undersides of the mason-bridges, and darkens the dust into a crust. For a few hours the whole basin smells of wet chalk and faintly of cold iron. The ancient cisterns, which are round and deep and still watertight after all this time, fill by perhaps a finger's depth.

This is the dew hour, and it sets the rhythm of every living thing in Sessile. The masons work hardest then, because their mortar sets only when the dust is damp. They swarm the walls in tides, laying more in those few hours than in the whole dry day. When the sun climbs, the roof warms and the dew burns off into haze. Then the new mortar cures hard, the crust on the dust cracks and lifts, and the dust storms begin again.

The dew hour is also the one time Sessile makes something like music. Drips fall from the high places into the cisterns, and those few, small, clear notes carry farther than any other sound in the city, because for that brief time the damp dust has stopped drinking them.

## The Other Animals

The masons are the most visible life in Sessile, but not the only life. The place is crowded with animals, and almost none of them are plants or anything like plants. Nothing green grows here. The only thing resembling a crop is a thin mineral film, faintly rose and faintly gold, that forms on damp stone in the dew hour. It is a crust of something too simple to call a lichen, and a multitude of creatures graze it.

**Chalk lice.** These are translucent isopods the size of rice grains, so numerous that damp walls seem to crawl. They scrape the mineral film at night and bury themselves in the dust by day. The masons do not eat them, but they tolerate them in their mortar, and old mason-work is often faintly speckled with their tiny, perfectly preserved shells.

**Lantern salamanders.** These are long and nearly transparent, so you can see their organs as faint gray shapes inside a body of clear jelly. They live in the cisterns and hunt the chalk lice at the water's edge. In the bright light of midday they climb onto sunlit walls and lie spread flat, and the light passes straight through them. From a distance a wall covered in basking salamanders looks like a wall covered in patches of rippling glass.

**Dust swifts.** These are the only flying animals, small slate-colored birds that nest in the upper chimneys of the mason-towers. They never call. They feed on the moths that rise from the dust at dusk, and they fly in enormous silent flocks through the white air, weaving between falling panes of selenite. At the moment a pane lets go, the swifts nearby scatter from it and then close in behind, following its slow fall and snatching the insects it disturbs. Watching a flock follow a falling pane down, the birds like a loose gray scarf trailing a turning sheet of light, is one of the defining sights of Sessile.

**Pallid moths.** These are wide-winged, dust-colored, and born in the dust itself. Their larvae tunnel through the gypsum flour eating something microscopic. When the warm air rises at dusk, the adults come up by the thousands, lifting in soft spirals on the convection toward the brightness of the roof. Many reach it and cling to the underside of the selenite, so that for an hour after dusk the roof is spotted with small dark shapes against the fading light.

## The Rules of the Place

People who spend time in Sessile learn a few things.

You do not stand in open ground beneath a raw patch of sunlight, because it means a pane has just fallen, and the panes around a gap are the weakest. The panes fall slowly, but a heavy one landing on you will still break bones.

You learn to read the dust storms by the light. When a district goes milky and its towers fade, the dust is moving there, and you wait in a doorway until it passes.

You do not take water from the cisterns during the dew hour, because that is when the salamanders are at the edge, and their skin carries something that numbs the mouth for a day.

And you do not interfere with the masons. They are not aggressive, but they are single-minded. Anything left still for long enough, whether a pack, a coat, or a sleeping traveler who does not wake, will eventually be incorporated. There are mason-walls in Sessile that hold, sealed in their curved courses of flake-brick, the rounded shapes of things that were once left in the wrong place. There are rope-coils, clay jars, and things harder to identify.

## What Sessile Is

Stand long enough on one of the high terraces and the whole of it comes into focus at once. Below the white vault of the roof lies a pale city, older than memory, softened into something like a landscape of its own. Across it runs a ceaseless gray tide of small builders, restoring what the dust takes. Sheets of light turn slowly down through the air, trailing flocks of silent birds, and each one leaves a hole of raw gold where it has gone. Storms of chalk move across whole districts and pass on. Everything is changing, all the time, everywhere you look.

And none of it makes a sound. The dust takes the noise of the collapse and the noise of the rebuilding alike, so that Sessile's endless turbulence reaches you only as light and movement, like a storm seen through a window, or a city seen in a dream.

The oldest walls are almost gone now, and within a few more ages the original builders' work will be entirely replaced. Then Sessile will be wholly a mason-city, built by small gray animals with stones in their hands, in a place that was once built by someone else for reasons no one remembers. Perhaps the masons will remember, in their way. They keep building along the old lines.
