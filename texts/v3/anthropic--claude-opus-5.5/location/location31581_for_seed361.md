# The Cistern of Voices

## The Tableland

Nothing about the plateau suggests anything is hidden in it.

It sits high, about two thousand meters, though you only learn that from the thinness of your breath and the hard, dry blue of the sky. The land is old enough to have forgotten it was ever mountains. Whatever ranges once stood here have been worn down over hundreds of millions of years into a peneplain, a surface so flat and so finished that the horizon looks ruled. Its skin is duricrust: a rind of iron-cemented laterite, rust-red and purple-black, pitted like a sponge left too long in the sun. Where it has cracked, the pale kaolin beneath shows through, soft as chalk and full of ghost-shapes of quartz grains that were once sand, then stone, then sand again, many times over.

The heat is steady and thorough. Wind moves across the tableland with nothing to stop it, carrying a fine dust that smells of hot pennies. Spinifex grows in rings, each clump dying from the center outward, so the plain is marked with hundreds of low grey circles. A kestrel hangs in the air. There is very little else. It is the kind of dryness that has settled into itself and stopped expecting anything.

Then you hear it, faint, from somewhere ahead. At first it seems like a pressure in the ears more than a sound.

## The Hollow

The ground opens without warning.

It is not a canyon or a valley. It is a single collapse, a doline where the duricrust gave way over some ancient cavity in the kaolin. It is small, perhaps forty meters across and fifteen deep, roughly oval, with rims that overhang like the lip of a jar. You could walk past it a hundred meters to either side and never know it was there. From the edge, looking down, you see green: a green so saturated it looks almost wrong after the red plain.

The sound comes up out of it like heat from an oven.

It is loud. It is not loud like a waterfall or a crowd, but loud like a living organ played by thousands of hands at once. There is an insect drone underneath everything, a cicada-like shrill pitched so high it seems to vibrate the fillings in your teeth. Over that comes a clattering, gurgling percussion of frogs. Over that come birds: whistles, rattles, liquid falling notes, alarm chips. Beneath all of it there is a low, patient hum that is not animal at all.

The locals, the few who come here, call it the Cistern. There is no open water in it. That is the first strangeness.

## The Descent

A path goes down along the eastern wall, cut into the soft kaolin as a series of shallow steps. The steps are worn into saddles by feet, but the path itself is older than the wear. Its edges are too regular, and at intervals small hemispheres of a glassy grey material are set into the wall at knee height. They are cool to the touch, always, even at noon. Each is beaded with moisture.

Temperature and humidity change as you descend. The dry heat of the plain gives way to a warm, close dampness, like a greenhouse at the edge of evening. The rock walls are furred with moss and liverwort and the silver scales of resurrection ferns, which open and close with each passing wave of moisture. Fig roots come down the walls like poured wax, feeling for crevices, splitting the kaolin with slow patience. The vegetation is lush, but it is not dense. The plants grow in clusters, with space between them. Each clump of ferns or each tree seems to have claimed a particular patch of dampness and held it for centuries.

At the bottom, the floor of the hollow is soft red earth covered in leaf litter and mushroom caps. A few trees grow here: a strangler fig that has long since eaten its host and now stands alone as a hollow lattice cage, two tall palms with fronds that scrape the rim, and a kind of cycad with a trunk like a stack of plates, old enough that it may have been growing when the plateau above still had hills.

In the center, on a low mound, sits the Lens.

## The Lens

It is about three meters across, a shallow dish lying on the ground like a saucer set down on a table. Its material is hard to describe. It seems dark grey, but the color shifts with viewing angle. It has no seams and no marks of manufacture, and it does not appear to have weathered at all. Lichen will not grow on it. Leaves that fall on it slide off within minutes, though the surface is not tilted enough to account for that.

The Lens is cold. Not dramatically cold. It is simply always a steady, exact coolness, about fourteen degrees, whatever the air above it does. It pulls moisture from the air. That is what makes the Cistern possible. The wind across the plateau carries a small amount of humidity, almost imperceptible, and the hollow's shape funnels a sliver of that air downward. Near the Lens, water appears.

It does not appear the way dew normally forms. The air above the dish thickens with a faint shimmer, and droplets form in midair, suspended, each hanging for a second or two before beginning to fall. When the humidity is high, in the late afternoon, there is a kind of slow rain that starts a meter above the Lens and nowhere else. The droplets fall onto its surface, bead there, and then run, not toward the center, but outward, to the rim, where they spill into a ring of moss.

The water then moves through channels in the earth, fine as capillaries, that radiate from the mound. You can see them only where the ground is scraped: threads of the same grey material, thin as hair, woven through the red soil like mycelium. They carry the water out to the walls, up into the glassy hemispheres along the path, and into the roots of every plant. The whole hollow is irrigated by something that cannot be seen, from a source that makes water out of dry air.

The hum comes from the Lens. It is felt more than heard, a vibration around forty hertz that you notice first in your sternum. It rises and falls. After a while you realize it rises and falls with the animals.

## The Chorus

The fauna of the Cistern is overwhelming, not in bulk, but in number and in noise.

There are few large animals. A wallaby-sized creature sometimes comes down the path at dusk to drink from the moss rings, delicate-footed, with enormous ears. Some small nocturnal marsupials live in the fig cage. The birds and bats are many but not dense. They nest in holes in the soft kaolin walls, which are riddled with burrows like a sponge.

The overwhelming part is the insects and frogs.

The frogs are tiny, no bigger than a fingernail, colored in shades of copper and moss green. They live in every damp crevice, in the curls of ferns, in the channels of the moss ring, under every leaf of the cycad. There are tens of thousands of them. They call in waves. A single frog in one corner begins a clicking pulse; the pulse spreads outward through the hollow like ripples, until the entire bowl is ticking in near-synchrony, then breaks apart into cross-rhythms and chaos, then gathers itself again.

The insects are worse. There is a cicada-analog here, brass-colored, with wings like smoked mica, which spends years underground in the kaolin, feeding on the grey threads, and then emerges. The emergences are staggered, so every day hundreds are hatching somewhere in the walls. When they sing, the sound is physically uncomfortable: a shrill, sawing whine that echoes from the rock and seems to come from everywhere at once. There are beetles that stridulate in slow grinding clicks, crickets that chirp in the dark, and moths whose wings make a faint whirring when thousands of them lift off the walls at dusk.

And the birds join in. Swifts scream around the rim at dawn, diving into the hollow and out again, feeding on insects. A black-and-yellow honeyeater sings a song of falling notes that the frogs seem to answer. Something like a lyrebird lives in the fig cage and mimics everything: the frogs, the cicadas, the swifts, and, unnervingly, the hum of the Lens itself.

The Lens responds. When the chorus rises, the hum deepens. When a wave of frog calls sweeps through the hollow, the Lens pulses in time. The air above it shimmers harder, and the midair rain thickens. The people who study the place argue about what this means. Some think the Lens uses sound as an input, that it was built to measure the richness of life around it and adjust its water output accordingly: more calls, more water; a silent hollow, a dry one. Others think it is the reverse, that the animals have learned over millions of years to call in rhythm with the Lens because doing so brings water.

Neither explanation quite fits. What is certain is that silence here is rare and brief, and that when it comes, it comes all at once, and it feels like the whole hollow is holding its breath.

## The Remains

The builders of the Lens left little else.

There is the path, with its glassy hemispheres. On the western wall there are marks cut into the kaolin: rows of shallow grooves, some straight, some curved, arranged in patterns that almost repeat. They are worn so soft that you have to run your fingers over them to feel them.

On the rim to the north stands a single upright column, about the height of a person, made of the same grey material as the Lens. Its top is broken off, or perhaps it was never finished. The kestrels perch on it. It is warm, unlike the Lens, always slightly warmer than the air. The ground around it is bare of plants in a perfect circle two meters across.

Later visitors left their own traces. A drystone shelter sits on a ledge halfway down the eastern path, half collapsed, its roof long gone, its interior full of fern. There are cairns on the plateau pointing the way, piled by people who knew the Cistern mattered and wanted to be able to find it again. In a crevice by the moss ring there is a small collection of offerings: a bead of red glass, a carved bone fishhook, a coin so worn its face is gone, and a plastic bottle cap bleached white. The frogs sit on them.

## The Day

The Cistern has its own cycle, driven by the sun and the Lens and the animals.

At dawn the swifts arrive first, screaming around the rim. Then the honeyeaters begin. Then the frogs wake in a rising tide of clicks. Condensation is heavy after the cool night; every leaf drips, and the air above the Lens is thick with suspended droplets that catch the first light as it spills over the eastern rim. For a few minutes the hollow is full of tiny floating points of light.

By midmorning the sun is high enough to reach the floor. Heat builds. The frogs retreat into crevices. The cicadas take over, and the sound becomes a wall. The plants turn their leaves. The resurrection ferns on the upper walls curl shut. Up on the plateau, the air shimmers with heat, but down in the hollow the Lens keeps a column of coolness, and animals gather around its mound: skinks lying in the shade of ferns, butterflies sipping moisture from the moss ring, a lizard with a blue tongue lying perfectly still at the edge of the dish.

In the afternoon, wind picks up on the plateau, and more moist air spills into the hollow. The Lens hum deepens. The midair rain begins, first as a few drops, then a steady shower over the mound, no more than three meters wide. The frogs erupt. The whole hollow roars. For an hour or so it is almost impossible to hear yourself think.

At dusk the bats come out of the kaolin burrows in a stream, thousands of them, pouring up over the rim into the orange sky. The moths lift off the walls. Something hunts them: a small owl with enormous eyes, and a large spider that spins webs across the fig cage and catches moths by the dozen. Predators here are numerous, but so are prey; the hollow is so rich in insects that the balance never seems to tip.

At night it is loud still. Crickets, frogs, owls, the occasional thump and rustle of the marsupials in the leaf litter. The Lens glows faintly, not with light, exactly, but with a subtle brightening of the air above it that you can see only when you look away. The stars are extraordinary. The wind on the plateau dies down. The dew begins to form again.

## The Edge

Climbing out is strange. The sound falls away step by step. At the rim, it drops almost to nothing, as though the hollow's walls hold it in like a bowl holding water. You step out onto the plain and the dry heat hits you, the smell of iron dust, the grey rings of spinifex, the flat endless horizon.

Behind you, a crack in the earth, invisible from ten paces away.

In it, a device older than the landscape it sits in, pulling water out of the sky, drinking in the songs of a hundred thousand creatures, and humming back.
