# The Undercrown

## The First Impression: Light Where There Should Be None

You descend expecting darkness and find the opposite. Somewhere beneath the surface of a cold world, below the frozen soil and the stone under it, there is a hollow larger than any sky you have stood under. It is full of white light, even and shadowless, like the inside of a cloud at noon.

The hollow is not in rock. It is inside a plant.

It takes a while to understand this, because the scale defeats the eye. The walls curve away for hundreds of kilometers and fade into bright haze long before they meet. They are made of pale, fibrous, faintly translucent tissue, the color of a peeled stem or a bean sprout grown in a cellar. From every surface, strands cross the open air. Some are as thin as hair and some are as thick as rivers. They run from wall to far wall, or from floor to ceiling, or out into the brightness and out of sight. Between them lies empty space. Mostly the Undercrown is emptiness, held open by living mass.

## Where the Light Comes From

Far overhead, on the surface, the plant sends up stalks. From above they must look like a scattered forest of glassy reeds across a frozen plain. Each stalk is hollow and lined with a tissue that bends light inward and downward, the way still water holds a reflection. Sunlight enters at the tips and falls, kilometer after kilometer, down the long throats of the stalks. It emerges into the Undercrown as bright columns, each one widening as it goes.

The pale walls do not absorb this light. They scatter it. Every fiber diffuses a little of it, so the whole cavity fills with a glow that has no single direction. Nothing here casts a hard shadow. A person standing on a strand looks lit from all sides at once, strangely flat, like a figure in an old painting.

The light arrives without its heat. The stalks carry the brightness down and leave the warmth behind in the thin surface air. So the Undercrown is bright the way a snowfield is bright: dazzling and cold.

## The Rule of Cold Growth

There is a stranger reason for the cold.

The plant grows by drawing warmth out of its surroundings. Growth here is endothermic. Wherever new tissue forms, the air around it chills. A growing tip, a strand pushing across the void, is always wrapped in frost. Its surface is rimed with needles of ice, and it trails a faint mist where the surrounding air has cooled below its dew point.

This is how the people who live here read the place. **Where it is coldest, something is happening.** A sudden chill on the back of the neck means a new root is coming through nearby. A patch of wall furred white with frost is about to split, swell, and send something out. Warmth means stillness, and stillness in the Undercrown usually means death: tissue that has stopped growing and will soon be resorbed.

The overall temperature hovers just above freezing, drifting below it wherever growth is fastest. Breath is always visible.

## Turbulence: The Plant Does Not Hold Still

Nothing in the Undercrown is old. That is the second thing you learn, and the most unsettling.

The walls look sharp and fresh because they are. They have none of the rounding, pitting, or layering that time leaves on stone. Fractures show clean white faces. Newly split tissue has edges that could cut, and they do, until a few days later they soften into ordinary living surface. No structure in the hollow is more than a few decades old, and most are younger than the people walking on them. The plant builds and unbuilds itself constantly.

**Strands shoot across the open air in hours.** A tip leaves one wall and gropes forward into the void, trailing frost. It moves slowly enough to watch and fast enough to be frightening. Sometimes it reaches the far side and roots, becoming a bridge. Sometimes it droops, withers, and falls. A falling strand the width of a road tumbles for long minutes through the bright empty air before it strikes something below, too far away to hear.

**Chambers open and close.** Parts of the wall bulge inward, thicken, and pinch off whole regions. Elsewhere, sealed tissue thins to a membrane, tears, and reveals new hollows beyond, sometimes still misted with the breath of their making. Maps are useless. The people here do not make them. They keep something closer to weather knowledge: where the growth has tended to go this season, and which regions have been restless.

**The whole hollow breathes.** This is the largest motion and the hardest to perceive. The Undercrown seems to be, among other things, the plant's lung or air bladder. Over cycles of several days it slowly expands and contracts. On the inhale, air pours down the light-stalks from the surface. Cold surface air sinks into the hollow as great slow winds, carrying dust, pollen, and occasionally snow, which falls glittering through the columns of light. On the exhale, the air pushes back up. Pressure shifts. Strands sway like the rigging of a ship. Thin membranes bow and flutter, and loose fibers stream sideways.

When breaths come irregularly, the hollow has storms: long gusting currents, sudden reversals, eddies that spin through the empty space between strands and pull the frost-mist into slow spirals kilometers wide. The Undercrown is large enough to have its own weather, and the weather is the plant breathing.

## Quiet

For all this motion, it is a hushed place.

The soft, fibrous walls swallow sound. There are no echoes; a shout goes out and does not come back. The winds make almost no noise, only a low steady breath, closer to feeling than hearing, like standing near a large sleeping animal. Growth itself is silent.

The exceptions are rare, and they carry. Sometimes frozen tissue cracks as it fractures, a sharp sound like lake ice on a winter night that travels a surprising distance through the still air. Sometimes a far-off strand gives way and there is a long creaking tear, then nothing. Water drips in some places. Now and then there are voices, and they seem too small.

## Water

Water is scarce but never quite absent. The Undercrown is damp rather than wet.

Condensation beads on every cold surface. Growing tips wear coats of frost that melt into droplets once growth moves on. In the mornings of the long breath-cycles, when warmer surface air meets the chill of growing tissue, dew forms along the strands in rows of beads that catch the light.

True pools are rare. Where the plant has been wounded, by a falling strand or a collapsing chamber, it weeps clear sap-water that gathers in cup-shaped hollows of torn tissue. These are the only standing water most inhabitants ever see. The water is cold, faintly sweet, and it does not last. Within weeks the wound seals, the tissue drinks back its own water, and the cup closes.

## Life That Is Not the Plant

Almost everything here is the plant. Animals are rare, and what exists has adapted to a world with no ground, no dark, and no permanence.

**Frost-moths** are the most common. They are small, white, and nearly invisible against the walls. They cluster at growing tips, feeding on something in the mist that growth exhales, and they follow the cold. A swirl of moths is often the first sign of a new strand.

**Longwalkers** are much rarer. They are slow, many-legged grazers the size of a small house, with bodies held high on stilt limbs and feet spread wide to grip the strands. They move along the thick bridges, scraping at the soft outer tissue. They are pale and silent, and they appear and vanish into the brightness like ships in fog. No one knows where they go when a bridge they are walking falls.

**Something else.** Travelers report glimpses in the open air, far from any strand: dark shapes that seem to glide or drift on the breathing winds. They are the only dark things in the entire hollow. They never come close. Some believe they are animals. Others believe they are seeds.

## People

There are people here, not many.

They call themselves **threaders**, or something like it. They live on the strands in small, roaming groups and follow the growth, because growth brings everything they need: frost-moths to eat, fresh tissue to cut, and wound-pools where strands fall. They move constantly. A threader settlement that stays in one place for a season is taking a risk.

Their technology is simple and almost entirely made from the plant. There is no stone to knap and no metal to smelt, so everything is fiber, husk, and bone.

- **Cords.** Threaders strip and twist the plant's long fibers into rope, their most important material. It is strong, light, and pale. They use it for everything: safety lines, carrying nets, lashings, and the long, swaying rope-ways they string between strands.
- **Husk-knives.** When old tissue dies and dries before resorption, it hardens into brittle, glassy sheets. Split correctly, these sheets hold an edge as keen as obsidian. Every threader carries several, and they break often.
- **Shelters.** A threader shelter is a pocket of dried husk-plates lashed with cord to the underside of a strand, shaped like a wasp's nest or a seed pod. It keeps out the breathing winds and holds a little body warmth. Inside there is often a small oil-lamp burning rendered fat from a longwalker. It is the only flame in the Undercrown and it gives the only warm light, a small orange point against all that cold white.
- **Knots.** Threaders mark their passage with knotted cords tied to the strands: warnings, directions, messages, names. The code is intricate. The knots never last. Within a season or two the plant grows around them, over them, into them. Old threader-knots sometimes appear embedded deep in cut tissue, pale fiber inside pale fiber, like insects in amber.

## Remains

Scattered through the hollow, rarely, there are older things.

Now and then a strand splits open and reveals, embedded inside it, part of a shelter, or a tangle of cords, or a husk-knife in a style no living threader uses. More rarely, there is something that was never threader work at all: a flat, carved plate of an unfamiliar material, or a spiral of what might be writing grown over by tissue. The plant seems to absorb whatever it touches and carry it slowly through itself, turning the Undercrown into a disorganized archive. Nothing stays where it was left, but some things are never fully destroyed. They circulate.

Threaders treat these finds with a mix of reverence and practicality. A good old knife gets used. A strange carved thing might be tied to a shelter as a charm and then lost again when the shelter falls.

## What Kind of Place It Is

The Undercrown is not a cave and not a forest. It is closer to the inside of an organism too large to know it is inhabited. It is a lung and a lantern and a lattice, continental in scale, almost empty, flooded with cold light, and never still.

Standing on a strand in the middle of it, you see white in every direction. The far walls are lost in haze. The strands cross the void like the lines of a vast, unfinished drawing. Somewhere a frost-tip inches forward into the air. Somewhere a bridge is falling. A column of sunlight hangs down from nowhere, snow drifting slowly through it. The wind breathes, low and patient. The air is cold enough to hurt your teeth.

Everything around you is alive, young, and in motion, and almost none of it will be here in fifty years. The place will still be here: bright, hollow, quiet, rearranging itself in the cold.
