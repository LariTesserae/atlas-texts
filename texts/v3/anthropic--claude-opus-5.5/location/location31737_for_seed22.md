# The Held Basin

## First, the ground

It is a lowland, barely above the sea that made it, and it is new. The basin floor is a sheet of volcanic glass a few centuries old, black-green and translucent at the edges, broken into plates by long straight fissures that still breathe warm air. Nothing here has had time to soften. The ridges that cross the plain are pressure-folds of cooled lava, and their crests are sharp enough to part a boot sole. Small cinder cones stand in loose rows like unfinished kilns. Their slopes are clinker that rings when it shifts.

Walk far enough and you notice the plain is too clean. There is no drift of grit in the fissures and no fan of sand at the foot of the ridges. Weathering needs things to fall and settle, and in this basin almost nothing settles. The dust is all still in the air, held there. The land stays young because its debris never comes home.

The ground is warm the way a stone is warm an hour after sunset, everywhere and constantly. Heat rises through the glass from somewhere not far below. Barefoot, you would feel faint differences, warmer seams and cooler plates, that the animals clearly know by heart.

## The sound

You hear the basin before you see it, and then you cannot hear anything else.

Across the plain stand the Throats. They are hollow columns of pale fired ceramic, each a few hundred meters tall, spaced so far apart that from the base of one the next is a grey pencil-line at the horizon. There are perhaps forty across the whole basin. Each one sounds continuously. Part of the tone is felt rather than heard, a pressure in the sternum and the back teeth. Part of it sits in the middle of hearing as a vast unwavering chord. Part of it climbs above hearing, and you know it only by the ache behind your eyes and the way small glass flakes on the ground tremble and walk.

Near a Throat the sound is an environment, not a noise. Speech is impossible, and so is shouting. Your own heartbeat disappears. Your voice box vibrates in sympathy without your permission. Farther out, in the wide spaces between columns, the chords of different Throats cross and interfere, and the sound gains texture: slow beats, swells, pockets where two tones cancel and a strange flat near-silence opens up, like stepping into a cellar. Those pockets are rare and precious. Everything alive in the basin knows where they are.

What the Throats are made of, who fired them, and what drives them is not clear. They take in nothing visible. They have run, by every account, since before the basin rose. Some people who live here say the Throats raised the basin, singing the seabed upward over centuries until it broke the surface and spilled its melt. The glass floor is ribbed in long concentric patterns around each column, like the sand in a vibrating dish. The claim is at least not absurd.

## The lattice

The Throats do not just make sound. Between them, the interfering tones build a standing structure in the air, a three-dimensional grid of pressure nodes where the sound compresses and releases in perfect balance. At the nodes, small things are held. They float, quivering, exactly in place.

At first you see only a haze of hovering dust in faint regular rows, like a diagram of itself. Then your eyes adjust to the scale and you see what else is held. Seeds hang there, and beads of water, and loose flakes of glass that catch what little light there is. Farther in and higher up hang larger things: clods of soil, then terraces of it, then whole suspended fields, each resting on a lattice of nodes the way a blanket rests on the points of a bed of nails.

This is the city.

## The city

It is a city in organization, not in mass. It has streets, districts, levels, maintenance, habits, and a population. But it is almost entirely air.

The buildings are light. They are woven from grass fiber and thin fired shell, and they are shaped to sit on nodes: broad, flat-bottomed, perforated so the sound passes through them instead of shoving them aside. They float at fixed heights in strata that follow the harmonic layers of the chord, a low tier a few meters off the glass, a middle tier higher, and a thin high tier near the cloud. A single dwelling might sit alone for a kilometer in every direction, tethered to nothing, wobbling slightly when the chord beats. Clusters of three or four are called a neighborhood. There are perhaps a few thousand people in the whole basin, spread through a volume the size of a valley.

The streets are the node-lines themselves, invisible paths along which a light object can be passed hand to hand, or pushed gently and left to drift from node to node. People travel them on wide woven rafts, nudging from one held point to the next, and they learn the lattice as other cities' children learn alleys. Down on the glass floor there are also roads: long ribbons of re-fused, polished glass laid along the ribbing, where heavier things move on runners. The roads are built with extraordinary precision and used by almost no one.

There is infrastructure everywhere, and it is quiet in the only sense that matters here: small. Ceramic pegs are set into the glass to pin the lattice locally. Thin resonant plates, tuned to nudge a node a hand's width up or down, hang like wind chimes beside every dwelling. Channels in the suspended soil carry water sideways. The deepest work, whatever maintains the Throats, is out of sight, and residents speak of it the way people elsewhere speak of the weather.

## Water

Water is the one thing the basin lacks, and the one thing the sound provides.

It rarely rains in the ordinary sense. The sea is near, though, and the warm air is humid. Wherever the sound's pressure falls lowest, in the troughs between compressed layers, the air cools slightly and moisture condenses out of it. So the fog in the Held Basin comes in horizontal bands, flat as floors, sitting at fixed heights. From a distance the whole basin looks shelved: dark glass, a grey stratum of mist, clear air, another stratum, clear air, a ceiling of cloud.

Everything that grows here grows toward those shelves. Dew beads on the held soil and runs down dangling roots. Beads of free water hang in the nodes themselves, trembling spheres the size of peas or plums, and they are harvested with nets. The glass floor is mostly dry, but in the fissures, where warm air meets a fog band sagging low, there are damp black runnels lined with moss.

Pockets of wet in a mostly dry country, then. The difference is that here the pockets are in the sky.

## Vegetation

Given water, warmth, and held soil, the plants have gone extravagant.

The suspended fields are dense with tall grasses whose stems resonate at particular pitches. Each species tunes itself, and a stand of it hums faintly under the great chord if you can get your ear close enough. Vines spill off the edges of held terraces and hang in long green curtains, sometimes down to the glass. Some of them have taken root at the bottom, so that a floating field is tied to the ground by living ropes. These are the oldest things in the basin, and residents do not cut them.

Some trees grow in the air entirely. They have no soil, only a root-mass spread across a dozen nodes like a hand spread on a tabletop. Their leaves are broad and pale for catching what little light arrives. Their fruit is light and gas-filled. When ripe, it detaches and drifts sideways along node-lines, sometimes for days, until something eats it or a fog band soaks it heavy enough to fall.

On the floor, in the fissure-runnels and on the lee sides of sharp ridges, there is a second, lower flora. It includes black-leaved succulents that drink ground heat and drop moisture, cushion mosses, and pale fungi that grow in the cancellation pockets where the sound goes flat. That flora is delicate and seems unable to tolerate vibration at all.

## Light

The basin is dim. Sunlight has to pass through the high cloud, then each fog shelf, then the suspended dust of a thousand unsettled years, then the undersides of floating fields and airborne forests. By the time it reaches the glass it is a pewter glow with no clear source. Noon and late afternoon look much alike. Shadows are soft and come from above in layers.

At the edges of the fog bands the light does odd things. The held water droplets sit in exact rows, and in certain directions they diffract the light into faint geometric halos, as regular as a printed pattern. Residents navigate partly by these. Each Throat has its own halo figure, visible only from the correct bearing.

At night the basin goes nearly black. The only lights are small lanterns in dwellings, the dull red breathing of the deepest fissures, and whatever the animals choose to show.

## Fauna

For a place so empty, the basin is thick with life. It is simply spread through a vast volume, so that you are always seeing something and rarely seeing it near you.

**Birds** are everywhere and have learned the lattice better than people have. The commonest are long-winged grey fliers called nodewrens. They are not wrens and are larger than gulls. They lock their wings and let the standing pressure carry them, sliding from node to node without a wingbeat for kilometers. Flocks move in lines and grids instead of clouds, and when a flock turns it turns at right angles.

**Insects** hatch in the suspended soil in astonishing numbers. Many have wings stiffened to ride the lattice like tiny rafts. Some have gone further and use the chord for power: wing membranes tuned to resonate, so they fly by vibrating in sympathy with the Throats and hardly spend energy of their own. Near the columns, these swarms are so dense they read as shimmer in the air.

**On the glass**, broad flat lizards the color of the floor bask on warm seams and graze the succulent patches. They are completely deaf, having no ears at all, and they are the calmest animals in the basin. Burrowing things live in the fissures. Small blind mammals eat fungi in the cancellation pockets and panic if carried out of them.

**Predators** here hunt with sound. The most feared is a slender, long-necked, gliding animal that residents call a *mute*. Its throat can produce a counter-tone. It hangs near a floating perch, finds the pitch of a local node, and sings against it, cancelling the node for a moment. Whatever was held there falls: a roosting nodewren, a sleeping insect raft, a beaded bunch of water, occasionally a child's toy. The mute folds its wings and dives after it. In the ordinary deafening roar you cannot hear the counter-tone. You notice it as a brief ear-popping hollowness and a sudden small fall.

Other animals have adapted to the mutes in turn. Some birds roost straddling two nodes, so that no single cancellation drops them. Certain insects sing continuous small corrective tones, patching any hole that opens. The whole ecology is conducted in pitch, and to human ears nearly all of it is lost inside the chord.

## Weather: the retunings

Every so often, with no schedule anyone has pinned down, a Throat changes pitch.

It happens over minutes. The tone slides, the beat patterns across the basin shift, and the lattice reorganizes. Nodes move. Everything held at an old node either migrates to the new one, drifting smoothly up or down, or it falls.

This is the basin's real weather. After a retuning comes a rain of held water, loosened seeds, dust released after decades aloft, stray fruit, insects, and the unlucky. Fields tilt and slide into new positions. Dwellings rise or sink a tier. Fog shelves re-form at new heights within the hour. The dust that falls finally lands on the glass. For a few days after a big retuning there is fine grey sediment in the fissures, the first erosion, and succulent seedlings sprout in it by the thousand. Then the lattice settles, the sound lifts the dust again, and the plain is clean.

Residents feel retunings coming as a pressure in the skull and a restlessness in the birds. They lash light things down, bring children to the tethered vine-fields, and wait. The animals begin to move a quarter-hour before any person notices anything. Mutes gather near a retuning Throat the way hawks gather at a grass fire.

## How people live

People in the basin speak with their hands. Their sign language is fast, spatial, and full of terms for heights, pitches, and directions along the lattice. Touch carries weight too: a hand on the shoulder with a specific pressure, a tap pattern on a raft. Children learn to read the halo figures before they learn to count.

Most residents are somewhat deaf by middle age and consider this normal. The few who come from outside usually cannot bear it for long. They say the sound gets inside the thinking, that thoughts start to fall into the chord's intervals, and that after a month you cannot remember what silence was for.

A few rules hold everywhere:

- You never stand at a node on the glass floor. Things fall on nodes.
- You never build heavier than your node can bear, and you never ask a neighbor what their node's pitch is. That is private, like asking about someone's blood.
- The cancellation pockets are common land. Anyone may go there to rest their bones, speak aloud, or grieve, and no one may be turned away.
- No one touches a Throat.

## What the place is becoming

Watch long enough, across a lifetime or across the stories of grandparents, and you notice that the tiers are slowly descending. The Throats are dropping in pitch, a fraction of a tone per generation, so the lattice sinks toward the glass and the upper strata thin out. Some say the basin is being finished and set down: the sound raised the land and is now lowering the city onto it, and one day the chord will fall silent, everything will settle, the first real rain of dust will stay put, and the place will finally begin to age.

Others say it is breathing and will rise again.

For now the basin holds. It is a dim, warm, roaring emptiness shelved with fog and green, and it is full of animals riding invisible grids across kilometers of air. Far below all of it, a sharp black floor that has never been allowed to wear down waits for the day its city lands.
