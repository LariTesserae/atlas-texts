# The Blister

High on the flank of a young mountain, above the last scrub and below the bare summit cone, there is a place where the rock swelled while it was still molten and trapped a breath of gas inside itself. The lava cooled around that breath, and what remains is a room. From outside you would never find it. The slope there is a ropy black crust dusted with ash, and the only sign is a crack at the base of a pressure ridge. The crack is a little wider than a forearm and exhales warm, wet air that fogs in the cold wind and vanishes a hand's length out.

Inside, there is no light at all. Not dim light, and not the grey that eyes eventually find in most caves. The walls are glass and fresh basalt, and nothing has ever illuminated them except the eruption that made them. Anyone who comes here learns the place by skin, ear, and nose.

## The approach

The crack narrows before it opens. You go in on your side, one arm ahead. The rock is warm, the warmth of a sleeping animal's flank rather than a hearth, and it grows warmer as you go. The surfaces are unworn. Wind and water have had no time to round anything, so every edge is exactly as it cooled: glass lips thin as fingernails, frothy pumice that crumbles and cuts at once, drapes of lava that stiffened mid-drip into points. People who crawl in here bleed a little. There is no avoiding it. The rock is too new to have learned to be gentle.

The air thickens. By the time the passage turns downward it is saturated, so wet it condenses in the throat and beads in the eyelashes. It smells of wet stone, flint struck against flint, and faintly of sulfur, more a memory of it than the smell itself.

## The room

Then the walls fall away on every side, and you are in the blister.

It is not large. Someone lying on the floor with arms outstretched could almost touch both sides. The ceiling curves over at roughly the height of a kneeling person. It is shaped like the inside of an egg that has been pressed slightly flat. But it does not feel empty, because the room is full.

From the ceiling hang thousands of small things. The first impression, reaching up, is of a beard or a pelt. Pull back and feel again, slowly, and they separate into kinds:

- **Lava straws.** Hollow tubes as thin as grass stems, formed when the last molten drips ran down and froze. They are brittle, and they chime faintly when brushed, a sound like distant, tiny bells being put away.
- **Glass hairs.** Finer still, pulled into threads as the gas escaped. They lie in soft tangles along the upper walls. They feel like wool and cut like wool would, if wool were knives.
- **Mineral beads.** Along every ledge and seam, white crusts of salt and sinter have grown where vapor meets rock: little cauliflower clusters, needles, rounded warts. They are the only things in the room still growing, and they grow so slowly that a lifetime would not show it.

Everything is wet. Every straw, hair, and bead of salt carries a skin of water, and every downward point has a drop at its tip.

## The drops that do not fall

This is the first thing visitors notice and the last thing they stop thinking about. In the blister, the water does not drip.

The drops form at the tips of the straws, swell to a certain size, and stop. They hang there. You can cup your hand beneath one and wait as long as you like, and nothing lands. Touch one and it comes away on your finger, warm as blood. The straw does not grow a new one while you watch, but the next time you come it will be there again, the same size, as if it had never been taken.

The room holds its balance exactly. The heat rising from the mountain's core, the moisture seeping up through the rock, and the cool air leaking in from the crack meet here and cancel. The air is always saturated but never oversaturated, the stone always warm but never hotter, the water always present but never moving. There is no drip, no trickle, and no pooling on the floor, which is dry beneath its film of dew. The temperature has not changed since before anyone came. Wind does not reach here, and neither do seasons. Nothing in the blister is on its way to becoming anything else, except the salt beads, and they are hardly on their way.

Some of the drops, in the deepest part of the ceiling, are joined to the floor by threads of water. They are thin, vertical, and unbroken, finer than spider silk. They do not flow. They simply stand, like the strings of an instrument no one has tuned. If you walk through one, it parts and wets your face, and it will be back on your next visit.

## The sound

The blister is nearly silent. Rock this young has no voice yet: no settling, no seeping, no creaking. What sound there is comes from three sources.

The first is the ticking. Somewhere in the walls, at intervals too irregular to count, the glass cools one last fraction and a microscopic stress lets go. It sounds like a fingernail tapping once on a bowl.

The second is the chime of the straws, which only happens if something touches them.

The third is your own body: breath, heartbeat, and the wet click of your eyes blinking, which is loud here, louder than anywhere.

After a while some visitors report a fourth sound, a very low hum felt more in the teeth than the ears, which comes when a palm is laid flat against the largest glass wall. It may be the mountain. It may be the listener's own blood, returning through the stone.

## Roots

Life comes into the blister from above, reluctantly.

In a few places the ceiling is not quite solid. Hairline cracks lead up through several body-lengths of rock to the thin soil on the slope, where tough alpine cushion plants grow among the ash. Their roots have followed the moisture down. They emerge through the cracks as pale, bare threads that never saw light, and dangle among the lava straws, fringed with root hairs as fine as the glass. They drink the hanging drops. There are perhaps a dozen of these root clusters in the whole room. Their tips are soft and cool, the only cool things in the chamber, and anyone reaching up can tell them from the straws at once: they give.

On the floor, around the base of one wall, there is a skin of something that is neither moss nor mold, a slick, faintly gelatinous film that feeds on the minerals and the warmth. It smells like cut cucumber and iron. It covers perhaps a hand's breadth and has not visibly spread.

## The one who lives here

There is an animal. Most visitors never meet it.

It is a cricket, or something like one: pale, eyeless, with antennae several times the length of its body. It lives in the tangles of glass hair, where its weight is too slight for the threads to cut it. It moves only when it must, and it must very rarely. It drinks from the drops. What it eats is unclear, perhaps the root hairs, perhaps the film on the floor, perhaps nothing for long stretches.

Anyone who sits still long enough may feel it: an antenna, impossibly light, drifting across the back of a hand, reading them. Then it withdraws. It never chirps. There is only one, and there has only ever been one. No one knows how it came here or whether it is the same one each time.

## Traces

People have come here. Not many, and not for a long time, but they came.

The marks they left are small and all made by hand:

- **A ledge worn smooth.** On one side of the room a shelf of rock at sitting height is the only rounded surface in the blister. It was not rounded by water. Bodies did it, sitting in the same place, over and over, until the fresh edges gave up.
- **Knapped glass.** Near the ledge, pushed into a crevice, lie three blades of obsidian flaked from the room's own walls. Their edges are sharper than anything outside. One is wrapped at its base with plant fiber, now so saturated with moisture that it has gone soft as wet paper but has not rotted. Nothing rots here; there is not enough change.
- **Missing straws.** Along one arc of the ceiling the lava straws have been snapped off cleanly, a whole swath of them. Whoever did it took them away. Perhaps they were threaded, or blown through, or given to someone.
- **Fingerprints in sinter.** In one soft patch of mineral crust near the entrance, before it hardened further, someone pressed a whole hand. The print is there still, filled now with a thin layer of new crystal, like frost. Anyone who finds it will put their own hand into it. Everyone does.
- **A niche.** At head height, a small hollow has been deliberately chipped into the wall, shaped to hold something round. It holds nothing now. The rock around its lip is faintly greasy to the touch, as if a lamp of fat once burned there. That would have been the only light the room ever saw, and it was taken away again.

There is no writing and there are no bones. There is no sign of why people came: to sleep in the warmth, to hide, to drink the water that never falls, or simply to kneel in the one place on the mountain where nothing is happening.

## What the place is

The blister is not a cave in the ordinary sense. Caves are carved; they are records of long erosion, of water patiently removing stone. This place has not been carved at all. It is exactly the shape it had on the day it formed. It is a hollow the mountain made in itself and then closed, still warm from its making, still wet with its own exhaled breath, holding its balance so precisely that the water hangs in the air waiting for permission to fall.

Outside, the mountain will weather. Storms will round its ridges, ice will split its crust, and one day the slope will collapse and let light into this room for the first time. When that happens the drops will fall all at once, the glass hairs will dull and break, and the cricket will die or leave.

That has not happened yet, and in here it does not feel as though it ever will. In here it is always the first night after the mountain was born: warm, dark, full, and utterly still.
