# The Cleft Under the Line

## Darkness first

The first thing is that you cannot see. This isn't the clean darkness of a starry night on a high peak. It is a cloud-dark, the kind that sits on a mountain's shoulder and doesn't leave. The cloud has thickened past fog into something you breathe in and feel settle on your tongue. Above it, somewhere, there may be a moon, but nothing of it gets through. What light there is comes in fragments: a faint smear of grey where the cloud thins over the ridge, and, at intervals, a sickly blue flicker overhead that crawls a short distance and goes out. You learn to read the place by sound and by hand.

## The sound

It is loud, and the loudness has layers.

The wind is the base layer. The cleft sits just below a col, a notch in a knife-edged ridge above three and a half thousand metres. Air from the wet side of the range forces itself through that notch all night. It doesn't blow steadily. It surges: a long rising roar, a pause that feels like the mountain holding its breath, then another surge. In the pauses you hear the other sounds.

**The cable.** One high-voltage line crosses the col overhead. It is a single span of three conductors strung from pylons that stand well outside the cleft, on the ridge shoulders to either side. You never see the towers. You only hear what the wire does. In the wind it sings, a deep aeolian drone that rises and falls in pitch as the gusts change. When the cloud is thick and wet enough, the air around the conductors ionises, and you hear corona: a dry, frying crackle like fat in a pan, loud enough to cut through the wind. That is the blue flicker. It is the only light the place makes on its own.

**The dripping.** Everything drips. Water hangs in the cloud rather than falling as rain. It collects on every needle, frond, and moss-tip and falls in fat drops that patter on leaves, tick on stone, and thud into the deep moss. Under the wind and the cable there is always this dense, sourceless ticking, like thousands of clocks slightly out of step.

**The mountain.** Stones fall. Some are small, a clatter and skitter down the raw slope. Some are not, a grinding crack followed by a long rumble that you feel through your feet before you hear it. And every so often, from far below and inside the mountain, comes a deep, muffled *whump* that isn't thunder. Thunder comes from above. This comes from beneath, and the whole slope shivers after it.

## The shape of the place

The cleft is small. You could walk its length in a few minutes if walking were possible, which it mostly isn't. It is a steep ravine torn into the side of the ridge, perhaps forty metres long, narrowing from the width of a village square at its lower end to a slot you could touch both walls of at its head, just under the col. The walls rise twenty or thirty metres on either side and lean in at the top. On calm nights, if there are any, it must feel like a nave. Tonight it feels like the inside of a throat.

It is also new, and it hasn't finished being made. The rock is young: dark, fine-grained metamorphic slabs, fractured along sharp planes. Nothing here has been rounded. Edges cut the hand. A large part of the northern wall is fresh scar, a pale, clean-faced scarp where a slab the size of a house let go not long ago, maybe a season, maybe a few years. Below it the floor of the cleft is a chaos of angular blocks, jammed and tilted, with voids between them deep enough to swallow a leg. Some of these blocks still rock slightly when the wind surges. Some creak.

This is what "young" means here. The cleft is not an old, settled valley worn down by millennia of water. It is a crack the mountain opened recently and is still widening. Uplift, frost, wind, and the rhythmic thumping from below keep prying at it. It changes from week to week. A route through the boulders that exists tonight may not exist next month.

## Water, and where it hides

There is almost no standing water. No stream runs down the cleft floor. Whatever rain falls sinks straight into the broken rock and disappears into the mountain's fractures. The water here is almost all suspended: in the cloud, in the air, and above all in the plants.

The moss holds it. Press your hand into the moss that sheets the older rock faces and it gives like a soaked sponge. Cold water wells up between your fingers and runs down your wrist. The moss is thick enough in places, a hand's width or more, that it is less a covering than a second soil, a spongy skin draped over the stone and saturated like a bog stood on end. Tiny seeps drain out of its lower edges and bead on the rock below, then vanish into cracks. In a few hollows between boulders, water pools briefly, a palm-sized mirror you'll never see because there is no light to show it. Within an hour or so it drains away.

So the place is damp rather than wet. It is humid, beaded, and soaked at the surface, and dry beneath. The water is in transit, always passing through.

## The growing things

What surprises you, if you have the light to be surprised, is how much is growing. The cleft is lush, packed tight, layered, and near-suffocating in its density, in a way that seems impossible for this altitude and this cold.

The answer is the cloud. This slope sits in the condensation band of the range. The cloud catches on the ridge almost every day, and the plants have learned to drink it straight from the air. They don't need rain. They need fog, and they get it without end.

**Mosses and liverworts** cover everything that has had time to be covered: the old walls, the upper surfaces of the older boulders, the trunks of the trees. They grow in cushions, pelts, hanging beards, and long dripping curtains that sway in the gusts and slap wetly against the rock.

**Ferns** crowd every ledge and crevice, from tough little rock-ferns in hairline cracks to broad-fronded species whose leaves are the length of an arm. They fill the gaps between boulders so completely that the gaps become invisible. You feel for footing and find fronds, then more fronds, then a hole.

**Shrubs and dwarf trees** fill the lower, wider end of the cleft in a thicket you can barely push through. They are wind-bent and elbowed, twisted by constant gusts into low, dense, interlocking shapes: a gnarled heath-like tree with leathery leaves, a rhododendron-type shrub with thick, rubbery foliage, something with small hard needles. Their branches overlap and knit together, and every surface is furred with moss and hung with lichen. There is no clear space between them. You move by bending, squeezing, and crawling. Wet leaves press against your face on every side.

**Epiphytes** grow on everything else. Small orchids, bromeliad-like rosettes, and trailing fern-strands cling to branches with nothing but air to feed them. There is life growing on life growing on life. The trees are hard to find under their tenants.

At the upper end, near the col, the growth thins. There the new scar and the fresh rockfall are still mostly bare, pale stone. But even there, in the cracks, the first colonisers are already arriving: crusts of lichen, a fine green film of algae, tufts of the hardiest mosses. The vegetation is chasing the rock. The cleft opens, and within a few years green floods in to close it. That is the other half of the place's instability. The mountain breaks, and the plants heal it almost as fast as it breaks.

## No animals

In all this growth, nothing moves that isn't moved by the wind. There are no birds; they can't hold a line in air this violent, and there is nowhere safe to nest on stone that shifts. There are no rodents in the boulder gaps and no insects in the moss. It is too cold at night, too loud, and too unstable. The deep vibration that comes up through the rock seems to drive out even the things that could otherwise survive the cold.

It's an odd kind of stillness under all the noise. The place is crowded, but not with anything that breathes. Nothing will startle at your approach. Nothing will call out. The cleft is plants, stone, water, wind, and one wire.

## What's underneath

Here the industrial part of the place lives, mostly out of sight.

Deep below the cleft, a tunnel is being driven through the mountain for rail or water, it doesn't matter which. The work runs day and night. The *whump* that shivers up through the rock is blasting: charges set in the tunnel face, fired on a schedule. Each blast sends a pulse through the whole mountain. Up here, hundreds of metres above, it arrives as a dull jolt. The boulders shift. A few stones break loose from the scar and clatter down. The moss curtains shake off their water in a sudden brief downpour. Then the wind fills the silence again.

Between blasts, when the wind drops in one of its pauses, you can sometimes hear something even deeper and steadier: a faint mechanical grind carried through the rock, perhaps a boring machine or a ventilation fan somewhere inside the mountain. It sounds more like a vibration you imagine than a noise you hear.

And overhead, always, is the line: the one piece of human making in the cleft, suspended across it without touching it, carrying electricity from a turbine hall somewhere far away to cities somewhere else. It sings in the wind and crackles in the cloud. It doesn't care about the cleft, and yet it is the cleft's only lamp.

None of this was built *here*. There are no structures in the cleft, no path, no marker, no footing, no bolt in the rock. But the cleft is not free of industry either. Industry runs through it and over it: the blasting reshapes it from below, and the wire lights it from above. The fresh scar on the north wall may be natural, or it may be the mountain's answer to a charge set too large on some night last year. There's no way to tell. The cleft doesn't record the difference.

## The rules

**Nothing stays put.** The rock moves, the plants move, the water moves. The cleft is a place of constant rearrangement at every scale, from the drop falling off a fern to the slab peeling off a wall.

**Growth races collapse.** Stone breaks, and moss and fern pour into the break. The cleft is always partly raw and partly smothered, and the line between the two shifts every season.

**Water lives in the air and in the plants, not in the ground.** It arrives as cloud and leaves through cracks. It is borrowed and never kept.

**Light is almost absent.** What there is comes from the machinery, not the sky.

**The loudness never ends.** Wind, wire, drip, rockfall, and blast take turns, and there is never a silence long enough to hear yourself think.

## A single moment

Stand at the lower end of the cleft, wedged between two moss-thick trunks, soaked to the skin by water that never fell as rain. The wind builds in the col above, roars, and strips a curtain of water off the moss so that it hits you like a thrown bucket. The cable howls. Then the wind drops away. In the pause, the ticking of ten thousand drips rushes in, and above you the corona starts to fry. A pale blue flicker walks along the wire, and for a fraction of a second you see the cleft: black walls leaning in, the white wound of the fresh scar, the jammed boulders, and everywhere, crowding and dripping and pressing in, the green. Then it goes out. Somewhere deep in the mountain a charge fires. The ground jumps. A stone breaks loose from the scar and bounds down the slope, knocking into other stones as it goes, until it lands somewhere in the ferns. The wind comes back, the moss drinks, and the cleft grows and breaks, and grows.
