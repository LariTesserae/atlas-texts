# The Cold Rise of Asker

You hear it long before there is anything to see, and for a long time there is nothing to see at all.

The approach climbs through cloud. Somewhere below the plateau's rim the air thickens to a wet grey that stays grey when the last light fails, so night comes on as a slow drowning rather than a sunset. At that height, in that season, night lasts most of the year. By the time the path levels out onto the Rise there is no light to speak of, only a faint pewter sheen where water films the rock. Everything you learn about the place, you learn by ear, by skin, and by the soles of your feet.

The first thing you learn is that it is loud.

---

## The Stone That Is Still Arriving

The plateau is new. That is its central fact.

Asker is not weathered down from anything older. It is being pushed up from below, a finger's width a year, by engines nobody alive fully understands. The rock comes out of the ground raw and unfinished: dark, fine-grained, glassy in places, split along clean planes that have never known frost or rain. Run your palm across a wall and it feels almost sharp, with crisp edges and no grit. Nothing here has had time to erode. Where older rock would be softened and pocked, this rock has the hard newness of a broken plate.

It arrives slightly warm, only a few degrees above the freezing air, and it exhales as it cools. Every surface smokes faintly with condensation. The cold cloud drifting over the plateau meets the warmth of the fresh stone and gives up its water, so the whole city weeps. Walls run with thin sheets of meltwater that never quite become ice. Ledges drip. The seams between blocks carry little silver threads of water that braid and part and braid again on their way down. Underfoot there is always a skin of water a few millimeters deep, shivering with vibration.

The rock's color is hard to name in the dark, but bring a lamp (the few people who live here mostly don't) and you'll see the near-black of fresh basalt shot through with greenish glass where the engines ran hot. The glass seams are the best places to drink. The water that runs over them tastes of iron and struck flint.

---

## The Shape of the City

Asker is a city. Anyone arriving expects ruins or a natural formation, so this takes a while to accept.

The engines do not push the rock up in a single slab. They lift it in sheets and blocks, and they cleave it as it rises along planes laid down with total precision: right angles, long straight galleries, stacked cells, stair-stepped terraces, arches with a keystone that was never set because the arch was lifted whole. The result is a mountain-top built like a hive, or like a very old city that has somehow just been poured. It is dense. The streets are ledges barely wide enough for two people to pass. Buildings, if that's the word, are honeycombed cliffs with thousands of openings each, about the size of a fist, a head, or a crouching body, rising tier on tier into the fog.

It spreads out. From the eastern rim, if you could see, you would see miles of it: a broken, terraced upland laid out in blocks and canyons, a regional sprawl of stacked stone lifting unevenly out of the cloud. Some districts are higher than others. Some are still low, their walls only waist height, sharp and wet and smelling of the deep earth. Others tower. A newcomer takes the variation for accident. It isn't.

The canals are the only broad ways. Water collects off ten thousand weeping walls and drains down deliberate channels cut into the rock's grain. The channels widen into black, perfectly straight watercourses between the tiers, cold enough to numb a hand in seconds, running with a steady purling roar toward the plateau's edge. There they fall. You can hear the falls from anywhere in the city, a bass note under everything else.

## The Colony

The creatures are the loudest thing in Asker, and there are more of them than anyone can count.

The keepers call the fliers **skerries**. They are small warm-blooded animals the length of a hand, with soot-colored membrane wings stretched between elongated fingers, dense water-shedding fur, and wide pale mouths. They roost in the city's wall-cells. They roost in all of them. Every one of the uncountable openings in every honeycombed cliff holds skerries, packed in by the dozens, the hundreds. The walls are made for them, or perhaps they are made for the walls. The distinction stopped mattering long ago.

They never stop calling. A skerry navigates by sound, and in a place with no light, sound is the world. Each one clicks and chirrs and buzzes constantly, and the calls stack into a roaring, fizzing, rolling wall of noise, shaped by the stone. The galleries echo. The narrow streets focus the sound into shrill corridors. Open terraces diffuse it into a great hiss like surf. It is loud enough to feel in the teeth. Walking through a dense district is like walking inside a struck bell that someone keeps striking.

They fly in the dark in streams, rivers of bodies moving along the canals and through the galleries, and you feel them far more than you see them: the brush of air, a wingtip's flick against your cheek, the pressure of a thousand small bodies parting around you and closing behind. They do not collide with you. They don't collide with anything. At the evening feeding (Asker's evening is a matter of the colony's rhythm, not the sky) the outflow from a single large wall can last an hour, a continuous torrent of wings pouring out of the cliff face.

Below the fliers there are the crawlers.

The **drip-lice** are pale, flattened, many-legged grazers the size of a fingernail, living in the running water on every wall. They don't mind the cold. They scrape the rock. Where the water sheets over fresh stone, their bodies form a moving film, a faint crackling carpet that shifts under your hand. Put your palm flat against almost any wall in Asker and within seconds you'll feel them walking over your fingers, investigating, moving on.

In the canals live the **longcombs**: eel-shaped, blind, slow, heavy as a forearm, feeding on drip-lice that wash down. Their movement drives little bow-waves up the black channels. They are silent themselves but their prey is not; drip-lice in the water make a faint clicking chorus when disturbed, and at night the canals tick like cooling metal.

And there are the **gleaners**, gangly ground-walking creatures larger than a dog. Keepers argue about whether they are animals at all or some grown thing left over from whoever set the engines running. They pick their way along the ledges on stilt-legs, feeling ahead with long jointed antennae, taking skerries that fall from the roosts. Their call is a low wet rattle. There are perhaps a few thousand of them in all of Asker, which makes them almost solitary by the standards of the place.

## Why There Is Life Here

There are no plants. Nothing on Asker is green, and nothing roots. The cold, the dark, and the raw stone forbid it.

The food chain is built on the newness of the rock.

Freshly lifted stone is chemically hungry. Its unweathered minerals have never met air or water, and when the condensation runs over them it leaches out iron and sulfur and reactive compounds that exist nowhere else on the surface. Invisible mineral-eating microbes bloom in that water, feeding directly on the stone's freshness. They form thin slick films on every new surface, the only thing on Asker you might call vegetation, and only barely.

The drip-lice graze the film. The longcombs and the skerries eat the drip-lice, and the skerries eat the countless tiny flying things that hatch in the canals. The gleaners eat the skerries. Everything is powered by fresh rock.

But fresh rock stops being fresh. Within a few decades of rising, a surface weathers just enough to go inert, and the films thin out. The drip-lice wander off. The walls fall quiet. An old district is a starving district.

That is why the engines never stop.

## The Engines and the Rule of Weight

Nobody in Asker has seen the engines. They lie far below the plateau, in the mantle, and they are not machines in any way a modern engineer would recognize. They don't burn, spin, or hum in any audible way. The keepers describe them in borrowed words: pressure that thinks, stone that is persuaded, heat that is sent where it is asked. The effect is simple. Rock rises, and when it rises it is shaped.

What decides where it rises, and what shape it takes, is the colony.

The keepers learned this over generations of listening. Where skerries crowd most densely, where their weight and their roaring calls concentrate, the engines lift new stone. A district that goes silent sinks in priority and stops rising. A district that grows crowded and loud lifts faster, its walls pushing upward with fresh, weeping, food-rich rock full of new roosting cells. The engines seem to measure the colony by pressure and sound, as if the whole population were a vote cast every night in bodies and noise.

So Asker grows toward its animals. Its uneven skyline, with tall loud districts beside low quiet ones, is a map of where the colony has chosen to live. The city is built to the colony's specifications by an intelligence that treats their roaring as instructions.

Nobody knows who designed this. The keepers' oldest theory is that the engines were built to make a city for people, and that they were built to answer the density and voices of inhabitants, and that at some point the skerries simply outnumbered and outshouted the humans and the engines followed the majority. It is, the keepers note, a very old kind of urban politics.

## Time on the Rise

Everything happens slowly here, except the noise.

A new terrace takes a lifetime to reach full height. A wall visibly taller than it was when you were a child is a common sight for the old. The canals shift course over centuries, re-cut along new planes as the ground lifts beneath them. The skerry population rises and falls in cycles decades long, and the city's shape follows at the pace of stone, always a generation behind.

Within that long time there are shorter rhythms. The drip on any given ledge has a period; keepers can tell where they are by the tempo of the water. The colony's roar swells toward the middle of the long night, when the young are born into the warmest cells deep in the walls, and eases in the brief grey season when a thin light comes over the eastern rim and the fog lifts almost to the height of the tallest districts. In that season, for a few weeks, the city can be glimpsed, a black honeycombed expanse streaming with silver water under a low lid of cloud and swarming with shadow, and nobody who sees it forgets it.

---

## The Keepers

A few hundred people live on Asker. They are not rulers. They are more like tenants or musicians.

They live in the older, quieter districts, where the stone has gone inert and the roar thins to something bearable. They wear close-fitting waxed layers against the constant wet and move through the dark by touch and echo; most carry a clicker, a small bone or glass instrument, and navigate the way the skerries do, reading the returned sound off the walls. They have a vocabulary of wall-textures carved at shoulder height, read with the fingertips, marking junctions and stairs and the places where a ledge ends abruptly over a canal.

They keep the canals clear of the bodies of fallen skerries, which otherwise dam the watercourses. They track the growth of districts. And sometimes, when they want the city to lift in a particular direction (to raise a wall against the wind, to open a new channel), they go out in groups and make noise. They clap stone on stone, whistle, crowd close together on a single terrace night after night, adding their small weight and voices to the colony's. Now and then, over a decade or two, the ground rises under them.

It is a humbling kind of civic participation. A keeper's life's work may add a few centimeters to a wall that a million skerries built. But the keepers say that is how it has always been in cities, only here you can hear it.

## Standing in the Middle of It

Imagine the terrace below the district the keepers call Tallow, one of the loudest and highest in Asker.

It is fully dark. The air is a degree or two above freezing and saturated, so every breath comes back to you as cold mist against your own lips. Water runs over your boots in a thin, quick sheet, and the stone beneath is sharp enough to feel through the soles. Somewhere to your left a canal pours through its channel with a deep steady rush. Far off, the falls at the plateau's edge thunder.

Above you, invisible, the wall of Tallow rises some two hundred meters into the cloud, and every one of its hundred thousand cells is full. The noise comes down on you like weather: clicks and buzzes, chirring and shrieks, a million voices fused into a single churning roar that the stone throws back and forth until the whole terrace seems to vibrate. Wings flicker past your face in unending streams. Your hand on the wall is instantly swarmed with small cold feet. The water tastes of iron. The rock smells like a struck match and deep, clean earth.

And underneath all that, at the very edge of what the body can register, there is something else: a pressure through the soles of your feet, patient and immense, coming from far below. The ground is rising. It has been rising your whole life and it will rise long after you, lifting this dark, wet, roaring city another finger's width toward the cloud, because this is where the voices are.
