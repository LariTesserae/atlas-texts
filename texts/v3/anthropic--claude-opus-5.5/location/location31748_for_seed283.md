# The Mound Country

It is night at the end of the dry season, and the basin has not cooled.

Heat comes up out of the ground in the dark. The soil is laterite, red by day and colourless now, baked for five months into something close to brick, and it gives back the day's sun slowly and steadily, like a kiln left to die down. The air hangs a few degrees below body temperature. Nothing stirs it. The basin sits barely above the sea that lies somewhere far off past the flat horizon. It is a broad, shallow floodplain, so gently tilted that water in the wet months cannot decide which way to run and simply spreads. There are no hills here. There are only the mounds.

## The Mounds

They stand everywhere, and in the dark you find them by touch or by the faint warmth they breathe out. Some reach your knee. Some are twice your height, fluted and buttressed, with chimneys and ridges weathered smooth by decades of rain. The largest are older than any tree around them. They are spaced as closely as the ground allows, a few paces apart, sometimes fused at the base into long walls. Walking among them is like walking among the pillars of some enormous hall whose roof fell in long ago and was replaced by forest.

Nobody built them, in the sense the word usually carries. There are no tools here, no roads, no fires, no marks of a human hand anywhere in the basin. But the mounds are working. That is the right word for it. Each is a ventilated structure, its walls porous, its inner galleries arranged so that the heat of the colony below draws air up through the central chimney and fresh air in through the thin outer skin. Inside, in the dark beneath the dark, the termites farm. They carry dead wood and dry grass down into chambers where they have laid out combs of chewed plant matter, pale and sponge-like, and on those combs they grow a fungus that digests what they cannot. The fungus is their furnace and their kitchen. The colony keeps it at a constant temperature and humidity. The mound is the machinery that does it.

Multiply that by the tens of thousands of mounds across the basin and you have a landscape that is, in effect, an industrial district. It runs day and night. It processes the forest's dead material at a scale no fire could match, and it has done so continuously for longer than anyone could count. It needs no electricity. Its power comes from rot and breath. Put your palm flat against the wall of a large mound at midnight and you will feel it, faintly warmer than the air, humming with a vibration too low to hear, like a factory floor felt through a wall.

## The Forest Between

The forest here is lush in the way dry forests can be lush: crowded rather than tall. Around the mounds, where the termites have enriched the soil, the trees grow thickest. Every large mound carries a thicket of its own: fig trees whose roots grip and split the old earthwork, thorny shrubs, climbers knotted into curtains. Between the mounds grow lower, harder trees with small leathery leaves, many of them half-bare now in the drought, shedding onto the ground the litter the termites will eventually haul underground.

There is no open ground. Where there is no tree there is a shrub. Where there is no shrub there is dry grass, head-high and brittle. The vegetation leaves no gaps, and in the dark every step pushes against it. Stems snap, leaves rasp, spiderwebs break across the face every few paces.

Along the old watercourses the forest changes. It turns evergreen and darker, a dense gallery of broad-leaved trees whose roots reach down to water the surface has forgotten. Here the canopy closes entirely, and the darkness beneath it is total. No star comes through. The air under those trees is a degree cooler and noticeably wetter. It smells of mould, sap, and something musky and animal that grows stronger the further in you go.

## The Water

There is not much of it. The river that shapes the basin in the wet season has broken into a chain of pools strung along its bed, some no bigger than a room, a few large enough to hold a fringe of reeds. Between them the channel is cracked mud curled into plates, and then sand, and then mud again.

The pools are the busiest places in the basin. Every animal that needs to drink comes to them, and in the last weeks of the dry season that means all of them, at night, when the heat is bearable. The mud margins are trampled and pocked with hooves, pads, claws, and the drag-lines of tails. Mosquitoes rise off the water in clouds you can feel before you hear them. Under the dry mud of the channel, frogs lie buried in cocoons of their own shed skin, waiting. They have been waiting for months.

There is one other kind of water: condensation. In the hours before dawn the air under the gallery forest reaches its dew point. Leaves bead, drip, and patter onto the litter below in a slow, irregular rain that falls from no cloud. For an hour or two the forest floor is damp, and a whole second shift of animals comes out to use it, snails and flatworms and slender salamanders that will vanish again at sunrise.

## The Fauna

The animals are what the basin is made of. They outweigh everything else.

Start with the ground. It moves. In the beam of a light, if you had one, you would see that the leaf litter is not still anywhere. Ants run over it in columns. Some columns are narrow and hurried. Others are broad highways a hand's width across, carrying cut leaf fragments back to their own underground fungus farms, a second agricultural industry running alongside the termites'. Millipedes as long as your forearm travel slowly over the mound walls. Beetles push through the litter. Scorpions stand motionless at burrow mouths with their pincers open. The soil itself is threaded with termite galleries so thoroughly that in places it is more tunnel than earth, and a heavy footstep can break through into a hollow.

Then the trees. Every fig is roosting. Hundreds of birds are packed shoulder to shoulder along the branches, asleep: small drab weavers, doves, starlings, the whole day-world folded up and silent. In the gallery forest the higher limbs sag under fruit bats. They hang in colonies of thousands, wrapped in their wings, shifting and jostling, and the smell of them lies over the riverbed like a blanket. Some nights they leave together. You feel the air move, a pressure more than a sound, as a quarter of a million bodies drop off the branches and stream out over the basin to feed.

Then the pools. Antelope drink in nervous groups, lifting their heads between every mouthful. Pigs wallow. Something heavy moves in the reeds and then goes still. Water birds stand one-legged in the shallows. Frogs have not yet begun to call, but toads that never went underground squat at the margins, snapping at insects that fall toward the water. The mud at the edge crawls with crabs no bigger than a coin, hundreds of them, sidestepping away from each footfall.

And the air. It is full. Moths batter slowly through the undergrowth. Mosquitoes whine at the ear. Tiny flies drift in columns over every pool. Here and there, not many, a firefly blinks pale green once, twice, and is gone. That is almost the only light in the basin. There is no moon tonight, and the stars show only in the gaps above the dry forest, not beneath the gallery trees. Otherwise the dark is complete, and the fauna, overwhelming as it is, is mostly a matter of touch and smell: the brush of a moth against your cheek, the tickle of ants crossing your ankle, the thick fecal sweetness of the bat roost.

## The Quiet

For all of that, the basin is quiet.

You expect a tropical night to be loud, and in the wet season this one will be. The frogs alone will make it deafening. But not now. The heat and the dryness have pressed the noise down. Most animals are conserving themselves. What sound there is stays low and continuous, a texture more than a noise: the dry whisper of the ant columns moving through the litter, the tick of a beetle's legs on a leaf, the faint papery rustle of the bat colony resettling. Once in a while a nightjar calls, a single churring phrase, and stops. An antelope snorts at the pool and bolts, and for a few seconds the brush crackles with its flight before the silence closes again.

Under it all, if you lie with your ear to the ground, there is the mound-sound: an almost imperceptible seething, millions of mandibles working the fungus combs, millions of feet in the galleries. It never stops. It is the basin's machinery running in the dark.

## The Waiting

Almost nothing changes from one night to the next. Change here happens on a longer scale.

The mounds grow by a few grains a day, and over a century they rise to cathedral height. When a colony dies, its mound softens in the rains, slumps, and becomes a low fertile hump that a fig or a thicket will claim. A new colony will start nearby. Across generations the pattern of mounds and thickets shifts slowly over the floodplain, the way dunes shift, but in centuries rather than seasons. The river bends a little each wet season. The gallery forest follows it. The whole basin is settled, and has been for a very long time. Nothing here is raw or new. Everything has worn into its place.

The one change that comes quickly is the rain, and even that approaches slowly. For weeks now the air has thickened. Some nights, low on the horizon, there is a flicker of lightning too far off to hear. The pools shrink by a finger's width each day. The animals crowd closer to them.

And inside the mounds, the colonies have been preparing. In chambers near the surface, winged termites wait by the tens of thousands: the next generation of kings and queens, fat, soft, pale, with long wings folded down their backs. They will stay there until the first real rain softens the soil. On that night the workers will open the mounds. From every mound across the basin, all at once, the winged ones will pour up into the wet dark in plumes like smoke. Every animal in the basin will come out to eat them: the birds, the bats, the toads, the scorpions, the ants, the frogs finally woken from the mud. The basin will be loud for the first time in months.

But not tonight. Tonight the heat holds, the mounds breathe, the ants go on with their carrying, and the dark is full of bodies waiting, all of them quiet.
