# The Chorus Vault

## The Approach

Near the top of a mountain that is still being made, the air is thin enough to make the blood hum in your ears. Just below the summit ridge there is a split in the stone.

The mountain is young, and that shows everywhere. Nothing up here has been softened. The rock is dark, glassy andesite, shattered into blocks whose edges are still keen enough to open a palm. Scree lies where it fell, and it has not yet settled into slopes. Every piece sits on its neighbors at angles that look temporary, and when the wind is right the whole flank ticks and chimes faintly as fragments shift. There is no soil to speak of, only grit, a skin of black sand in the hollows and pale crusts of lichen that have had perhaps a few hundred years to spread. Snow lies in the shadowed seams and does not melt. Below, cloud fills the valleys completely, a white floor with the peak standing out of it like something surfacing.

The split is not much to look at from outside: a vertical cleft about twice a person's height and barely wide enough to turn sideways in. It is the only sign on this face that the mountain is hollow. The first thing that reaches you is the smell, before any sound: ammonia, wet fur, old eggs, something musky and sweet. Warm air seeps from the opening even in the cold. Where it meets the outside, it fogs.

Then you hear it.

## The Mouth and the Breath

The cleft breathes. This is the first rule of the place, and everything else follows from it.

Air is rarefied at this height, and the pressure of the thin atmosphere rises and falls with the sun. Through the day, as the rock outside warms, air is drawn into the fissure in a long, steady inhale that pulls at your clothes and hair. After dark, as the outer stone cools faster than the inner chambers, the mountain exhales. A column of warm, fetid, heavily humid breath pours out of the crack. It freezes on the lip of the opening into feathered rime, a white beard of frost around the mouth that grows through the night and sublimates by afternoon.

The exhale carries sound. Near the cleft at dusk you stand in a stream of noise as much as air, a wall of clicks, chirrs, rasps, and high whistles, the massed voices of more animals than you can easily imagine, pushed out of the mountain on its own breath.

## The Vault

Inside, the cleft widens after about thirty paces. It opens into a single chamber about the size of a small church nave. By mountain standards it is modest, and you could cross it in a minute if the floor allowed. In other terms it is enormous, because every surface is alive.

It is dark, the heavy, velvet dark of the inside of a mountain, broken only in two ways.

**The first light** comes for about an hour each day. When the sun reaches a certain angle, one blade of light enters the cleft and lies across the chamber floor, gray-gold and thick with drifting motes of dander and down. It moves slowly across the room like the hand of a clock. Where it falls, the animals nearest it stir, shuffle aside, and complain. As it passes, the path it cleared closes again behind it.

**The second light** belongs to the chamber itself. The floor and lower walls are coated in a deep, soft layer of guano, dark and damp and in places knee-deep, laid down over centuries. Growing in it is a fungus that gives off a faint, cold, blue-white luminescence. It is too dim to see by directly. At the edges of vision, though, the floor seems patterned with faint constellations, clusters and threads of pale glow that trace where the guano is freshest and wettest. When something walks across it, the glow brightens briefly under its feet, so the traffic of small animals across the floor leaves glowing tracks that fade over a minute or two.

The walls are the mountain's youth laid bare. The chamber formed when a gas pocket in cooling lava collapsed, and the interior is still glass. Obsidian flows freeze into curtains, drips, and blades. The ceiling bristles with them: long black icicles of volcanic glass, sharp as the day they hardened, some twice the length of a person, hanging in close ranks like a pipe organ turned upside down. They are brittle and they ring.

## The Multitude

The fauna here is overwhelming. It is less a population than a condition of the space: a living lining several animals deep.

**On the ceiling**, among the glass spines, hang the creatures the locals below the clouds call *hollow-swifts*. They are wrong for any single category. Each is about the size of a sparrow, furred like a bat along the body, but with small, stiff, overlapping plates at the leading edge of the wing, dark and slightly iridescent like beetle shell. They cling upside down in dense mats, packed so tightly that the ceiling looks upholstered. When a mat shifts, a wave runs across it, thousands of bodies adjusting at once with a sound like wind across dry grass.

They navigate by sound, as bats do, but their clicks are not only for them. Every click strikes the glass. The obsidian spines are tuned by their own shapes, and each one answers certain frequencies. A hollow-swift can therefore "ring" a particular spine by calling at its pitch. The roost has organized itself around this. Different colonies claim different spines, and a spine's note functions as a territory, an address, a name. The ceiling of the vault is a keyboard played continuously by ten thousand small mouths.

**On the walls**, in the ledges and pockets of the glass, live nesting birds. They are gray-brown, heavy-bodied, and pale-eyed, with long hooked beaks, and they nest in the cracks in small cup-shaped structures of hair, down, and dried guano cemented with spit. They are fewer than the swifts but louder. Their calls are harsh descending screams that set off cascades of ringing from the glass, and the swifts respond with irritated clicking. The two species maintain a constant, audible border dispute along a band of wall about halfway up.

**On the floor**, the guano supports its own crowd. Pale, soft-bodied beetles plow through it in legions. Long-legged cave crickets with antennae three times their body length stand at the edge of the glow, tasting the air. Blind white amphipods swim in the small, rank pools where condensation collects. There are also the *gleaners*: small, rat-sized animals with dense slate-colored fur and enormous, trembling ears. They run along the base of the walls collecting fallen eggs, dead young, and dropped swifts. Their glowing footprints are the busiest constellation on the floor.

**In the air**, always, something is flying. Swifts are leaving, swifts are returning, birds are circling, moths are drifting. The moths are a large, soft, colorless species that feed on the fungus, and their wing scales pick up its light. They show as faint blue smudges wandering through the dark at head height.

Everything here is in contact with something else: wing against wing, claw against fur, body pressed to body. There is almost no empty space. The warmth in the chamber comes entirely from them, the combined metabolism of a colony that fills a mountain.

## The Sound

It is loud, but not deafening. It is a sustained, textured roar with depth to it.

Under everything is the ringing of the glass: a shifting drone of dozens of tones overlapping and beating against each other, never quite resolving. Over it, the swift clicks form a crackling static, dense as heavy rain on a roof. Through that, the birds' screams cut in sudden bursts. On the floor there is chewing and scratching, the wet sounds of beetles in guano, and the tiny tick of crickets.

The sound is never the same twice, but it has a structure. At dusk, as the mountain exhales, the whole chorus rises in volume and pitch. The swifts pour out of the cleft on the outbound breath in a river that takes more than an hour to pass. For that hour the vault is shaken by the sound of wings, a thick, fluttering thunder. The ringing of the glass swells as millions of wingbeats push air across the spines. Afterward the chamber is quieter, but not quiet. The birds settle and mutter, and the gleaners come out in numbers.

Before dawn, on the first draw of the inhale, the swifts return. They come in on the incoming breath, and the whole process repeats in reverse, faster and more frantic, because the ones who arrive last get the worst places.

## The Damp

The cold outside meets the animal warmth inside, and the vault sweats. Condensation beads on every glass surface and runs down in thin trickles, collecting in the obsidian cracks and dripping from the tips of the spines in a slow, irregular rain. The floor pools are fed by it. The fungus lives on it, and so do the amphipods. The walls weep, the ceiling drips, and a fine mist hangs in the upper air, thicker in the hours when the colony is home.

Outside the vault the mountain is dry. It gets snow but little rain, and the thin air sucks moisture from everything. The chamber is a pocket of saturated air sealed in a desert of cold rock, and its wetness is manufactured almost entirely by breath.

## The Green, Such As It Is

Plant life is sparse, and all of it lives on the margins.

At the mouth of the cleft, fed by the warm, damp, nitrogen-rich exhale, there is a fringe of dense, astonishingly green moss, the only real color for a vertical mile. It grows in a ring around the opening, thickest on the lower lip where the breath pools, and it is often furred with frost. A few tough, low-growing plants with fat, gray-green leaves and tiny white star flowers have rooted in the moss. They are the highest flowering plants on the mountain.

Inside there is only fungus: the glowing mat on the floor, plus pale, shelf-like growths in the corners where the wall meets the guano, and a strange, stringy, gray fungus that hangs from some of the lower spines in long, delicate beards, glistening with moisture. The swifts avoid those spines, which are silent.

## The Traces

Almost nothing in the vault is made, but not quite nothing.

A short distance inside the cleft, in the narrow passage before the vault opens, someone has cut shallow notches into the obsidian. They are a sequence of small grooves, some old and dulled by frost, some fresh. There is also a braided cord of dark hair, perhaps goat or human, knotted at intervals and tied around a projection of stone, leading a short way into the dark and then stopping. And in a crack there is a cache: a few flaked obsidian blades, a gourd, a bundle of sticks bound with sinew.

People come up from below the clouds, probably rarely, at great risk and at particular times. They may come for the guano, which would make extraordinary fertilizer, or for bird eggs, or for something else entirely. It is also possible that they come to listen. The notches in the stone are not evenly spaced. If you run a thumb along them, the intervals are irregular in a way that suggests rhythm, and some of them match the ring of certain spines. Someone has been counting the notes of the ceiling.

## The Long Change

Everything here happens on two clocks.

The fast clock is the breath: the daily inhale and exhale, the tides of animals leaving and returning, the blade of sunlight crossing the floor.

The slow clock is the mountain's. It is still rising, fed from beneath. Every few years there is a small tremor. It is barely felt, but the vault feels it: the glass spines shiver, a few crack and fall into the guano, and the tuning of the ceiling changes. Colonies whose spines have fallen must find new notes. For a season afterward the vault is louder and more quarrelsome as territories are renegotiated. Over centuries the ceiling's music drifts, spine by spine, as the old glass breaks and the stresses of the rising mountain open new cracks.

The cleft itself is widening, by fractions of a finger each century. When it opens further, more light will get in and the breath will change. The glass will weather, the spines will dull, and the chord the mountain has been holding since it cooled will slowly fall silent. That is a long way off. For now the vault is new, sharp, dark, and full to bursting, and it sings every night into the cold high air above the clouds.
