# The Striking Room

You come down to it through a crack, not a door. The crack is new. Its edges are sharp enough to take skin, glassy and unweathered, as though the rock split an hour ago and no air has touched the faces since. It runs down at a slant through pale mineral, a salt-white stone shot through with amber banding. The deeper you go, the warmer it gets. It isn't the wet heat of a jungle. It is the dry, held warmth of a kiln left closed overnight. There is no moisture anywhere. Your lips crack. The air tastes faintly of struck flint and hot iron.

Before you see anything, you hear it.

---

## The Sound

It comes up the crack as a pressure first, a thud felt in the sternum. Then it becomes audible: a single enormous blow, iron on stone, followed by a ringing that spreads out and slowly thins. Then silence. Then the blow again.

The interval is exact. It has been exact for as long as anything here remembers. Between the blows there is a second sound, a continuous low hum like a held breath through a brass throat, and over that a third: a dense, dry chittering, thousands of small voices. They rise in the gap after each strike and cut off precisely before the next.

By the time you reach the bottom, the sound is less something you hear than something you stand inside.

---

## The Chamber

It is not large. You could cross it in forty paces. The ceiling is high, though, maybe as tall as a chapel nave, and it narrows upward into darkness. The walls are not rock so much as growth. Every surface bristles with crystal: needles, blades, fans and fine hairlike fibers, all the pale translucent amber of old honey, all impossibly sharp. Nothing here is rounded or worn. Every edge is new.

At the center stands the engine.

It is iron and blackened brass, about the size of a small house, and bolted directly into the living crystal of the floor. A flywheel taller than a person turns steadily on a greased axle. It drives a cam, and the cam lifts a hammer: a single rectangular head of dark iron on a long arm. At the top of its arc the hammer releases and falls. It strikes a plate of crystal on the floor, the anvil-bed, which is never the same crystal twice.

Each strike shatters the bed. Amber shards leap into the air, catch the light, and fall back as glittering dust. By the time the hammer rises again, new needles have already begun to thrust up from the broken surface. They grow visibly, like frost spreading across a pane. By the time the hammer falls, the bed is whole again.

That is the rule of this place, and everything else follows from it.

---

## The Equilibrium

The crystal grows from the air. Along the base of the walls run narrow vents, seams in the rock where a faint orange glow shows and where the heat is strongest. From them seeps a warm mineral vapor, invisible and dry. It isn't steam, because there is no water here. It is something that sublimes out of the deep stone and condenses the moment it touches a cooler surface. It deposits itself atom by atom into those relentless amber blades.

Left alone, the room would fill. The crystal would close the chamber like a fist within weeks. But the hammer strikes, and each blow sends a shock through the whole lattice. Needles snap all across the walls. Dust sifts down. The growth and the breaking are perfectly balanced. The room is always exactly this full and exactly this sharp. The dust on the floor is always exactly this deep, about ankle height. It is ground finer by every blow, and the finest fraction is drawn back into the vents on the warm updraft, to be re-deposited somewhere else as new crystal.

Nothing here changes. Everything here is happening constantly. The two facts are the same fact.

If you set something down, a coin or a glove, the vapor finds it. Within a few hours it is furred with amber needles. Within a day it is part of the wall. Then a strike cracks it free, and it falls, and it is buried in dust, and slowly it wears down to nothing. The room accepts offerings but doesn't keep them.

---

## The Light

It is dim here, always the same dimness. Some of the light is the dull ember-glow of the vents, low and red at floor level. Some comes from a single electric lamp mounted high on the engine's frame. Its filament burns a steady tired orange behind a cage of wire. No wire leads away from it. No wire leads anywhere. Like the engine, it simply runs.

The crystal takes these two weak lights and scatters them. The whole chamber holds a faint amber luminance, and every needle carries a tiny bead of reflected glow. With each strike, the flying shards throw brief sparks of light across the ceiling like a flock of struck matches. Then they settle, and the dimness closes again.

---

## The Inhabitants

The room is crowded with life, more than a room this size should hold. Every surface is inhabited.

**The interval-mites** are the most numerous. They are pale, eight-legged, and no bigger than a grain of rice. Their shells are made of the same amber crystal as the walls, grown over their bodies as they molt. They live in their millions on the crystal faces and feed on the freshest growth, the thin tips of new needles where the lattice is still soft for a fraction of a second. They move only in the gap. At the strike they freeze and clamp down with hooked feet. The instant the ringing fades, they swarm. Their chittering is the sound of their shells clicking against the crystal as they run. It sweeps across the walls in waves that begin at the hammer and roll outward, and it stops cleanly, all at once, just before the next blow. They have never been wrong about the timing, because the timing has never changed.

**The glass-moths** hang from the ceiling in clusters so dense they look like more crystal. Their wings are thin plates of mineral, clear enough to see through, veined with dark iron-colored lines. They don't fly in the ordinary way. With each strike the shock goes up through the ceiling and shakes them loose by the hundred, and they fall, opening their wings, gliding in slow spirals down through the shower of shards. They eat the dust in mid-air, filtering it through combs on their faces. Before they reach the floor they catch the rising heat from the vents and ride it back up to the ceiling, arriving just in time to be shaken loose again. A single moth may make this circuit a hundred thousand times. Their wings chime faintly against one another as they fall, a sound like tiny bells rung in a closed box.

**The dust-eels** live beneath the floor's surface. They are long and eyeless, their skins armored with overlapping flakes of amber, and they swim through the ankle-deep powder the way fish swim through silt. You mostly see them as ripples, lines of motion crossing the dust. When the hammer strikes, the whole floor jumps, and for an instant their backs break the surface, a hundred glinting coils. They feed on the mites that fall, stunned, from the walls. They also seem to feed on the shock itself. They gather most thickly near the anvil-bed, pressing close to the point of impact, and the ones nearest the hammer grow the largest.

**The tenders** are the strangest. There are perhaps a dozen of them, and they live on the engine. They look like crabs built from the engine's own materials: iron-grey carapaces, joints the dull gold of brass, legs thin as clock hands. They crawl over the flywheel, the cam, and the long hammer arm, and wherever crystal tries to grow on the machine, they scrape it off. Wherever grease has thinned, they smear more from glands along their undersides. No one built them. They may have been born from the engine, or the engine may have been born from them, or they may simply have come to it the way barnacles come to a hull, and over some unimaginable span become its keepers. They ride the hammer arm up and leap clear before the fall. They have never mistimed it either.

---

## The Vegetation

Very little grows here in the green sense. There is no green at all.

Near the vents, where the heat is strongest, a few colonies of **ember-moss** cling to the rock. They are low, dense mats of something like lichen, deep rust-red, feeding directly on the mineral vapor before it can condense. They make faint patches of softness in a room otherwise made of blades. The mites avoid them. The eels sometimes lie curled against them, as though for warmth.

High on the walls, in cracks that the shock never quite reaches, hang a few strands of **thread-root**. They are pale and hairlike, dangling a meter or more into the air, and they sway with each strike. Nobody knows what they root into. They don't seem to be growing. They don't seem to be dying. They have probably hung there, unchanged, as long as the hammer has fallen.

---

## The Remains

Someone was here once, or something like someone.

A narrow iron catwalk runs along one wall about halfway up, bolted into the rock, its railings furred thick with amber needles. At its far end sits a small cabin, hardly more than a closet of riveted plate with a single porthole window. Inside is a stool and a shelf. On the shelf rests a ledger, bound in something that was once leather and is now mostly crystal. Its pages are fused into a single amber block. Through the translucence you can still see columns of figures. They are the same figure, written over and over, down page after page. It might be the interval between strikes, recorded by someone who checked it every day of their life and never once found it different.

On a rack below the catwalk hang tools: long-handled wrenches, a grease-can, a mallet. All of them are sheathed in crystal, and all are part of the wall now. There is also a pair of boots, standing neatly side by side at the foot of the catwalk ladder, filled to the brim with amber needles. Whoever wore them is not in them.

---

## Being There

You can't stay long. The heat builds in you slowly, and the dryness pulls the water from your mouth and eyes. Your ears never stop ringing, and after a while the ringing no longer seems to belong to your ears.

But in the time you stand there, something happens to you. You begin to feel the interval. You start holding your breath at the top of the hammer's arc and releasing it with the chittering of the mites. Your heartbeat, unasked, finds a rhythm that fits neatly inside the gap. You stop flinching at the blow.

The room isn't welcoming you. It is doing to you what it does to everything: fitting you into the equilibrium, making you one more thing that grows and is broken and grows again at exactly the rate that keeps it all the same.

When you climb back up the crack, the hammer follows you for a long way. It fades slowly: first the chittering, then the chiming of the moths, then the ringing, until only the blow is left, a pressure in the chest. For days afterward you wake in the night having stopped breathing, waiting for it, certain it is about to fall.
