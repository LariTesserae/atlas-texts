# The Wax Hollow

It sits just below the summit of a mountain that did not exist a few centuries ago.

You climb to it over ground that still has edges. The slope is black basalt that cooled in ropes and plates and has not yet been softened by anything. The rock is sharp enough to open a palm. Where the flow ran fast it went glassy, so there are sheets of obsidian lying at angles, bottle-green when the light comes through their thin rims. Nothing has rounded these stones. No rain has fallen here, and no river has cut through them. The only weathering is the slow flaking of heat, the rock expanding in the afternoons and contracting in the long nights, until a thin skin spalls off and lies on the ground like a shed scale.

Then the slope folds inward, and you step over a lip of fresh pumice into a hollow no wider than a village square. You could walk across it in the time it takes to say a few sentences. It is full, though. Nearly every surface is occupied or grown over, or has been built on and then built on again.

## The ground and its warmth

The hollow is a small secondary vent, a side-mouth of the mountain that opened, spat briefly, and went quiet. It never went cold. The floor stays warm all the time, about the heat of a sleeping animal's flank, and in a few places the warmth is almost uncomfortable. It comes up through hairline cracks as a dry, faintly sulfurous breath. The air carries no steam and no damp. This mountain has no water in it and never did. The rock is dry to its roots.

Everything alive here has had to work that out.

## The wax-growth

The vegetation is lush, and it takes a moment to understand why that feels wrong. The green is real, a deep resinous green like pine needles in shade, but none of it is wet. Break a stem and no sap beads out. What oozes instead is a clear, slow wax, warm and mobile inside the plant and turning pale and firm within seconds in the open air.

This is the chemistry of the hollow. The plants move oils and waxes the way other plants move water, and they depend on the vent heat to keep those fluids flowing. Near the warm cracks the growth is thick, glossy, and fast by local standards, which means a leaf might unfurl over a season. Toward the cooler rim it slows and stiffens, and the outermost plants are nearly solid. They stand like candles someone forgot to light, their growth rings visible as bands of opaque and translucent wax.

Several forms crowd together:

- **Felt.** A low mat of fine waxy filaments carpets most of the floor. It gives slightly underfoot and springs back slowly, over several minutes. It is where the silence mostly comes from, because it drinks sound the way thick snow does.
- **Lantern-stalks.** These are waist-high to head-high columns with swollen translucent crowns. When the low sun hits them, light pools inside them, so that the hollow at evening is dotted with dim amber glows that are only sunlight held a little longer than usual.
- **Creepers** that follow the heat. They grow along the warm cracks and nowhere else, which traces the vent's hidden plumbing across the floor in green lines, like a map of veins drawn on skin.
- **Cushion-trees.** Along the more sheltered eastern wall grow squat, broad trees whose leaves are thick waxy pads stacked like roof tiles. They are the tallest things here at perhaps three times a person's height. They are also where the city is densest.

The plants pull their minerals directly from the rock. Their roots are acidic and secrete a solvent wax that etches the basalt, so beneath the felt the stone is pitted with fine channels. It is the only erosion the hollow knows, and it is almost entirely biological.

## The city

At first you might take the built part for more vegetation, or for strange rock. Then you see the doorways.

Every surface above the floor is covered in architecture that nothing with hands made. It is a single colonial settlement, dense as a honeycomb, built over many generations by the creatures who live in it. Their building material is the hollow itself: obsidian dust ground fine, bound with wax harvested from the plants, and set hard in the warmth. The result is a dark, faintly glittering paper-stone. It is light, surprisingly strong, and smooth where many bodies have brushed against it.

The structures range from fist-sized to the height of a doorway, and they cluster so closely they touch. There are stacked chambers like swallow nests, one pressed against the next. Narrow lanes run between them, worn into grooves by traffic. Spans of paper-stone arch from one outcrop to another and are strung with waxy cables. On the cushion-trees, cells are built between the stacked leaf-pads, so each tree carries a vertical district that is half plant and half masonry, and you cannot say where the tree stops and the building begins. The trees seem to have adapted to this. Their pads grow in flatter where they bear weight, and they exude extra wax along the seams, as if they were mortaring their own tenants in.

There are no machines and no tools anywhere. Every surface is shaped by mandible and foot and secretion. Yet the place is unmistakably a town. It has main routes and back routes, and districts where the chambers are old, thick-walled, and blackened, and others where they are new, pale, and still slightly tacky. It has what look like communal halls, larger domed chambers where the lanes converge. It has middens where the old wax and spent shells go, and something close to plazas: open patches of felt, kept clear, where nothing is ever built.

## The builders

The masons are about the size of a thumb. They have long bodies with segmented, lacquered backs, the same resinous green as the plants, and soft padded feet. They number in the tens of thousands, possibly far more. At any moment the walls, lanes, and bridges are thick with them, moving in steady files, carrying pellets of wet paper-stone and beads of harvested wax. Wherever you look, something is moving. The hollow never feels busy, though, because they do not hurry and they make no noise.

They are not alone. The city supports a whole ecology:

- **Tallow-moths** sip wax from the lantern-stalks at dusk. Their wings are dusted with pale powder that sheds and settles over everything, so the city is faintly frosted at its edges.
- **Glass-backed beetles** graze the felt. Their shells are made of obsidian flakes they glue to themselves, and when they cluster, they catch the light like scattered broken bottles.
- **Wall-lizards**, slow and heat-loving, lie flattened against the warmest chambers. They pay a kind of rent, and nobody quite understands the arrangement. The masons build little ledges for them, and the lizards eat the mites that would otherwise bore into the paper-stone.
- **Parasitic tenants** move in as well. A smaller, darker mason species builds its cells inside the walls of the larger ones, living in the gaps of the city like people in the attics and crawlspaces of an old town.

Everything here is at least a little dependent on something else, and the dependencies are layered as tightly as the chambers.

## The silence

The quiet is the first strange thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing.

At this altitude the air is thin, and thin air carries sound poorly. The felt absorbs most of what remains. Nothing in the hollow evolved to make noise. The masons have padded feet, the moths fly with a soft, dusty wingbeat, and no creature here calls or sings.

They signal in other ways. The masons carry faint color patches along their flanks and shift them slowly, over the span of a few breaths, from green toward bronze and back. Watch a lane for a while and you see waves of color passing along the files. It is a message moving through the city, and it moves slowly, at the pace of pigment. Scent does part of the work too. The paper-stone of each district smells slightly different, of different resins and different blends of wax, so the city is mapped in odor as much as in structure.

The few sounds that do exist become very noticeable. There is the faint tick of cooling rock at night, and the whisper of a wax cable stretching as the air warms at dawn. Once in a long while a flake of obsidian spalls off the rim and slides down onto the felt with a sound that is barely a sound at all, and every mason nearby pauses its color for a moment.

## The slow drift

Nothing here happens quickly, but everything is moving.

The vent is cooling, and it will take centuries. The warmth is gradually retreating from the eastern side of the hollow toward the western. The plants follow it. Along the cooling edge the wax-growth stiffens, the lantern-stalks stop glowing, and the felt goes chalky and firm. Along the warming edge new creepers push out along fresh cracks.

The city follows the plants. Its oldest districts, the thick black ones, sit on the cooling side. They are emptying slowly. Their lanes go quiet, and their chambers are colonized by beetles and then sealed over by frozen wax, so that they gradually become something closer to geology. On the other side, the newest districts are being laid down pale and bright.

Seen over a lifetime, if anyone had one long enough, the whole settlement would appear to be walking. It moves across its tiny crater at the pace of cooling stone, shedding ruins behind it and raising new chambers in front, following the mountain's slowly withdrawing heat.

Eventually, the masons' color-waves suggest, the heat will reach the far rim and have nowhere else to go. Nobody in the hollow seems concerned about this. Perhaps that is because it is very far off. Perhaps it is because the masons remember, somewhere in the scent of their oldest walls, arriving here from somewhere else.
