# The Sink at Hollin Fell

## The Rim

The land above looks like nothing: a rolling upland of heather and wind-flattened grass, gray under a high, even sky. You would walk across it without suspecting anything if not for the breath. In certain hollows the air is warmer and damp, and a thin mist rises from cracks in the turf. Follow the mist and the ground ends.

It ends abruptly, on edges of pale limestone that broke away in blocks only a few decades ago. The fracture faces are still bright, the color of old bone, with crystal facets that catch the light. Lichen has not yet reached them. Below the edge the ground falls forty meters, and then it keeps going sideways. The Sink is not one hole but a chain of them, collapse after collapse along a buried river, strung out for three or four kilometers across the fell like beads on a thread. Some are open to the sky. Some still have roofs, or half-roofs: tilted slabs, stone bridges, arches that have not decided whether to fall. From the rim you can see perhaps five of the openings, each one full of green, and fog moving between them underground.

## The Descent

The way down is a talus cone of breakdown, a slope of fallen blocks that still shift. Each block has sharp corners. The upper ones are bare. Lower down, moss starts in the angles where two stones meet, and further down the moss covers everything, so the blocks become soft green mounds and you can no longer tell where one stone ends and the next begins. The moss is so saturated that a foot pressed into it fills with water up to the ankle, and the water is cold enough to ache.

The temperature drops as you descend, which surprises most people. Cold air is heavy, and on still nights it slides off the fell and pools in the Sink the way water would. The floor is a frost hollow. Snow lingers in the shaded corners into early summer, gray and pitted, while ferns grow a hand's width away from it. Frost forms in the open chambers at dawn in every month of the year.

Water, meanwhile, comes up from beneath at the steady, mild temperature of deep stone. So the Sink is cold air lying on warmer water. Where they meet, fog forms constantly. It forms low, a meter thick, and moves along the floor in slow rivers, following gradients too slight to see, pouring from one chamber through the arched passages into the next. By midmorning it lifts into a ceiling layer under the rims, and the light passing through it is soft and directionless. Shadows are faint. Everything is visible and nothing is lit.

## The Walls

The walls are where the city is.

On a first look they seem textured, pocked, like a sponge. On a closer look every pock is a nest. They are cups of moss and mud and pellet-mortar, each about the size of two cupped hands, built onto the wet rock wherever it weeps. They are packed rim to rim. Newer cups are built on older ones, and the old ones become foundation, so the nesting face bulges outward in lobes and terraces and overhanging tiers. In places it hangs a meter off the rock. Rough, organic, it looks like the side of a very old town seen from far away, balconies on balconies.

The builders are swifts: small, dark, stiff-winged birds with cold-adapted habits. They need three things, and the Sink gives them all three. They need wet rock, because the nest mortar only sets damp and the mosses they weave in must stay alive, anchoring the cup as it grows. They need a sheer drop below the nest, since a swift cannot take off from level ground. And they need shelter from the wind of the open fell. There are tens of thousands of them. No one has counted. The nesting faces cover every wall that seeps and faces away from the prevailing wind, and in the older chambers they have grown so thick that the rock itself is invisible for hundreds of meters.

They are nearly silent. That is the first strangeness of the place, and the least weird: a colony this size ought to scream. But these swifts do not call at the nest. The cold makes them torpid on the worst nights, sunk into their cups with their heartbeats slowed, and during the day they are away, feeding high over the fell and the farther valleys on midges that rise from the bogs. What you hear on the walls is small: a feather-rustle, a single dry chitter, the tick of a claw on mortar. Multiply that by forty thousand and it becomes a faint, continuous hiss like fine rain, and it is hard to tell from the rain that is often actually falling.

The walls weep. Water emerges along the bedding planes of the limestone in horizontal lines, beading, spreading, and it runs down through the nests and keeps the moss alive. Where it drips free it builds tufa, soft new stone deposited as the water loses its dissolved lime. Tufa is fast stone, geologically speaking, growing a millimeter or so a year. It forms curtains and small lips and fans below the seeps, and it grows over abandoned nest-cups and turns them to rock. In the oldest chambers the lowest tiers of the swift city are now stone, entombed in cream-colored crust, and the living nests are built on the fossil of the city beneath them. The walls are, slowly, building themselves out of birds.

## The Floor

The floor is water, arranged.

The buried river emerges here and there along the chain of dolines, rising up through gravel, and it would spread into shapeless bog if it were left to itself. It has not been left to itself. Beavers came down the talus at some point after the collapse and found cold, clean, constant water with no winter freeze at the springs and thickets of willow and alder already growing on the breakdown. They have been working ever since.

The floor of each chamber is stepped. Dam behind dam behind dam, low barriers of stick and mud and stone, each holding a still pool a hand's width higher than the one below it. The pools are narrow and long. They run between the fallen blocks and they are connected by overflows and spillways and, in places, by canals the beavers have dug to float branches from the thickets to the lodges. Seen from the rim, the floor looks planned, a grid of terraces and channels with the lodges as domed buildings at the intersections. There are dozens of lodges in the larger chambers. The colony is dense by any standard, and they have run out of room to spread, so they build up: lodges on top of abandoned lodges, dams atop old dams, the whole floor rising.

And the tufa gets into this too. The spring water is lime-heavy, and where it pours over a dam it leaves crust on the sticks. The dams are slowly turning to stone. The oldest look like rims of travertine, carved and scalloped, with the shapes of branches fossilized inside them. The beavers keep adding fresh wood on top. Here, as on the walls, a city of animals is being preserved in rock as it is used.

The pools are clear. You can see to the bottom, three or four meters down in the deepest, where the springs rise through pale gravel and the gravel dances in slow boils. The bottoms are reefed with caddisfly larvae in their cases, each one a small tube of cemented sand grains or plant bits, gathered so thickly on the submerged stones that the stones look furred. Freshwater sponges grow on the dam faces, green and knobbed. Small trout hang in the current at the spillways. Crayfish, pale and nearly translucent, come out at dusk.

## The Green

There is almost no bare ground. Everything that is not water or nest is plant.

The mosses first, dozens of kinds, in cushions and wefts and hanging tails, so wet that they drip steadily into the pools. Liverworts, flat and lobed, cover the lowest stones. Filmy ferns, whose leaves are only one cell thick and would die in a day of dry air, grow in the passages between chambers where the fog never lifts; they look like green silk and are as delicate. Hart's-tongue ferns, big-leaved and glossy, crowd every ledge. Saxifrages with small white stars flower on the tufa. Golden-saxifrage mats the spring heads. In the open chambers, willow and alder and birch have grown up out of the talus, low and dense and wind-sheltered, their trunks furred in moss to the tips of their twigs, their branches thick with lichens hanging in gray beards.

It is lush in the way cold places can be: no single plant is huge, but there is no gap anywhere. Plants grow on plants. Epiphytic mosses grow on the willows; liverworts grow on the mosses; tiny fungi fruit on the liverworts.

## The Dark Parts

Between the open chambers are the roofed stretches, where the river runs through passages that have not yet fallen. They are the only true darkness in the Sink, and even they are not fully dark, since fog carries light in with it for dozens of meters. The passages are the lowest, oldest, and wettest parts of the system, and the ceilings are thick with a second population: bats, clustered in the cracks, hibernating through the long cold, or hunting over the pools on the few warm nights. Dippers, small plump brown birds, bob on the stones at the passage mouths and walk underwater along the gravel to feed.

In the passages you can hear the actual sound of the Sink, when everything else is still. It is water, but quiet water: a drip, a drip, a pause, then a small plash as a drop hits a pool. And beneath that, sometimes, a sound almost too low to hear: the deep stone ticking and settling. The system is still young, still collapsing. Every few years another block lets go. Every few decades a roof fails, a passage becomes a chamber, and light falls on stone that has never seen it. The swifts move into the new walls within a season. The beavers follow the new water.

## The Day

At first light the fog is thickest. The pools steam. Frost stands on the moss tips, white on green, and the swift walls are motionless, the birds in torpor.

As the sky brightens above the rims, the first swifts stir. They do not fly out one by one. They leave in a flush: a whole wall-face drops at once, tens of thousands of birds letting go of their cups and falling a few meters to gain speed and then climbing, a dark sheet rising through the fog layer. It is almost silent. The sound is the air: a long, soft rush, like a breath let out, and it lasts perhaps a minute as the sheet spirals up out of the chamber and splits into streams heading out over the fell. Then quiet again.

Midday belongs to the beavers, working slowly and unseen under the water, and to the stoats and martens hunting along the talus for voles, and to the peregrine that lives on the northern rim and takes the occasional swift that comes home early. The pools drop a little as the day warms and melt from the snow patches feeds in; they rise again at night. The fog lifts, hangs, thins.

At dusk the swifts come home. This is the one moment the Sink becomes loud, if it ever does. The streams converge from every direction and pour down through the open chambers in dense rivers of birds, and for a few minutes the air is full of wings and the thin high chittering that is all the voice they have. Each one finds its own cup among tens of thousands, the colony packed so tightly that a returning bird has to land on the birds already home and push. Then the walls go still. The chittering fades to the hiss. The fog begins to thicken again as the air cools.

## What Holds It Together

The Sink works because it is off balance. The air is colder than it should be. The water is warmer. The stone is still falling and still being built. It is new ground, raw at the rims, and it is already full to the brim: every surface claimed, every chamber a settlement, cities of mud and stick and moss laid on top of older cities turning into stone beneath them.

No one designed any of it. It has streets, terraces, dams, towers, canals, foundations, and ruins. It has a population too dense to count. It has not a single tool in it.

In a thousand years, the roofs will all have fallen, the chain of dolines will have become a gorge, and the swift walls and beaver dams, fully petrified, will be part of the cliffs. Someone climbing down then will run a hand over the stone and feel, under the lime, the shapes of cups and branches, the outline of a city that built itself out of water and cold and need, and never made a sound louder than breath.
