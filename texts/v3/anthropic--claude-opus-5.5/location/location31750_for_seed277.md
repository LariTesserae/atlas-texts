# The Anvil Highlands

## The Dark

It is always night here, or near enough. Some slowness in the world's turning, or a permanent veil of high haze, keeps the sky a deep charcoal. A few stars show through, blurred and brownish. Sometimes there is a smear of greater brightness low on one horizon, which may be a distant sun that never rises or may only be the eye wanting one. No one has decided which.

Your eyes adjust slowly, and what they find is not light so much as the faintest differences in black. The ground is darker than the sky. The hollows are darker than the ground. Here and there, very far apart, something glows a dull cherry red, the color of an iron bar an hour after it has left the forge. These are the only fixed lights. There are other, briefer ones, and they come later.

The first thing you notice is not the dark. It is the warmth.

## The Ground

The warmth comes from below. The highland is a tableland of basalt and iron-rich tuff, lifted so high that the air is thin and a little sharp in the lungs. Even so, the rock is warm to the palm, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Heat rises through it constantly and patiently from somewhere deep. The whole plateau is a lid over a slow furnace, and has been for longer than anything alive here has existed.

The plateau is vast, so large that its edges are not a practical question. You could walk for a year and remain on it. At this scale it is not flat but gently swollen: long low rises spaced tens of kilometers apart, like the crests of a frozen ocean swell. Between them lie broad shallow basins. From the crest of a rise you can see nothing, of course, but you can *feel* the distances. The air moves differently over open ground, and sound behaves as though there is nothing to stop it.

The rock has been here a very long time, and it has worn into a settled, final-seeming shape. Nothing is jagged. Old lava flows have rounded into smooth black whalebacks. Old fissures have filled with pale mineral and healed into seams that run straight for enormous distances, like the joins between plates of armor. Ancient lava tubes have collapsed along parts of their length and now form sunken trenches, walled and roofless. In other places the tubes remain intact, and warm air breathes out of their mouths in a steady exhalation you can hear if you stand close.

Iron is everywhere. It rusts in the damp and gives the black rock a rind of deep oxblood. In places it has been concentrated, and not by geology alone. That comes later too.

## The Damp

This is not a wet place, but it is never quite dry.

The water comes from the meeting of warm ground and cool thin air. All night, which is always, a fine condensation forms. It beads on the rock and dampens the grit, then collects in the smallest depressions as a film you notice only when your fingers come away slick. In the basins the moisture gathers into low mist. It lies in shallow pools of vapor a meter or two deep, perfectly still, and it stays warm, so walking into it is like stepping into breath.

There are seeps. Along the old healed fissures, water that has been down near the furnace comes back up, hot and loaded with dissolved metal. It trickles out in thin braided channels no wider than a hand. It runs a few hundred meters, steaming faintly, then soaks back into the rock or evaporates and leaves behind crusts of green and white mineral. A few of the largest seeps form chains of shallow pools, terraced like rice paddies and rimmed with deposited salts. These are the closest thing the highlands have to rivers, and they are the richest places on the plateau.

Occasionally it rains. The great warm updrafts of the basins build clouds too high and dark to see, and they release a brief drizzle, warm as bathwater and faintly metallic on the tongue. It lasts minutes. Then the rock drinks it, and the mist comes back thicker than before.

## The Growing Things

Vegetation is sparse and strange, because there is no light to grow by.

What grows here grows on chemistry and heat. Around the seeps, mats spread across the wet rock: dense, rubbery, black-green crusts that feed on the dissolved metals and sulfur in the water. They form rings around each seep, thickest at the source and thinning outward, and the rings make the landscape legible to anything that can sense them. Between the seeps, on the damp but otherwise barren rock, there are lichens. They are crustose and nearly invisible, patches of rough texture that feel like sandpaper, living on the condensation and whatever the air carries.

And there is wire-grass. It grows in loose, widely spaced tufts on the flanks of the rises, stiff and dark and faintly springy. Its blades are threaded with iron, which the plant draws out of the soil and lays down in fine filaments along its length. A tuft of wire-grass rings softly if you brush it. It serves some purpose to do with the currents in the ground; the roots go deep and the tips are always slightly charged. Touch a blade and you may feel a small, distinct prickle, like touching a doorknob in winter.

That is nearly all. A walker could go a day without passing more than a few dozen tufts. The ground between is bare rock and grit and damp.

## The Current

Here is the fact that organizes everything else.

The ground carries electricity. The heat rising from below is uneven. Pockets of hotter and cooler rock sit side by side, and iron seams run between them. Across these differences, currents flow: slow, broad, constant tides of charge moving through the stone. They are weak at any one point but enormous in total. The whole plateau is a battery, a thermocouple the size of a continent. Its currents shift with the heat, and the heat shifts with nothing anyone can see.

The air carries the smell of it: ozone, hot metal, the dry clean scent of an electrical cabinet. The fine hairs on your arms lift and settle as you walk, responding to fields you pass through. A compass is useless here, or rather it is too useful. It spins, points, reconsiders. Magnetic north on the plateau is a local and temporary opinion.

Life has built itself on this. It has no light to eat, so it eats current. And because the current is industrial in scale, the life here has become industrial in character.

## The Animals

The dark is full.

This is the second great surprise, after the warmth. For all its emptiness of plants and stone, the plateau is crowded with animals. They are rarely seen, because nothing is seen, but they are constantly present: brushing past, moving in the mist, sensed as disturbances in the air and the prickling of the skin.

**The smelters** are the largest and the slowest. They are heavy, low-slung animals the size of a cart, with broad plated backs and short thick legs. They graze the seep-mats and chew iron-bearing grit along with them, grinding it in their gizzards. Inside, something happens that should need a blast furnace: the ore is reduced, and the iron separated from the rock. They are always warm, far warmer than the ground, and some of the dull red glows on the plateau are smelters, their bellies incandescent with the work of digestion. They move in loose herds of a dozen or twenty, kilometers apart. Behind them they leave droppings of glassy slag, and, more rarely, small dense beads of nearly pure iron. Over ages uncounted, these have accumulated. There are fields in the basins where the grit is half slag, and old grazing routes along the seeps are lined with iron beads packed so densely they have fused into ribbons of metal as long as roads.

Nobody made these. They look made. That is the unsettling thing about the highlands: from a distance, if there were a distance you could see across, it would look like the aftermath of a vast factory. Slag heaps, glowing furnaces, rails of iron, steaming pipe-mouths. It has all happened without a single hand.

**The dynamo eels** live in the seep-pools. They are long, pale, and flat as ribbons, and they lie in the hot water in chains, nose to tail, spanning the gaps between pools. Each chain taps the current in the rock and concentrates it. The eels store charge in their bodies the way a capacitor does, and they live on it, and they release it. On certain nights, which is to say at certain times, the whole length of a seep-chain will discharge at once. A line of blue-white light snaps along the pools for half a second, bright enough to show you everything, and then it is dark again. The afterimage stays on your eye for a minute: terraced pools, black rock, mist, and the shapes of animals standing very still around the water.

**The drift-moths** are uncountable. They are small and dark and soft, and they navigate by the magnetic field, which on the plateau is a detailed, shifting map. They move in rivers of millions along the iron seams, following invisible contours. When a stream of them passes overhead you hear it as a faint dry rustle across the whole sky. Sometimes they land on you, drawn by the iron in your blood. They weigh nothing.

**The grazers** are everywhere. They are small hoofed things like goats built lightly of wire, and they crop the lichens and the edges of the mats. They are nervous. They wear their fur charged, standing out in a haze around them. When one startles, the discharge from its coat makes a tiny crackle and a flicker of violet at the tips of the hairs. A herd running in panic looks, for a few seconds, like a scatter of sparks blown across the ground.

**The hunters** are what makes the grazers nervous. There are several kinds, but the one most spoken of is long, lean, and eyeless, with a head flattened like a spade and lined with pits. It sees in current. Every living body on the plateau disturbs the ground's fields in its own way, and the hunter reads those disturbances as clearly as sight. It walks slowly along the rises with its head low, then waits and waits, then crosses a kilometer in a straight silent sprint. The grazers' best defense is to discharge their coats all at once, a flash of static that blinds the hunter's senses for a moment. Predator and prey here fight with electricity. The struggle is quiet. It shows as a crackle and a flicker, and then either the flicker runs on or it stops.

There are also scavengers that follow the smelter herds to gather iron beads, for reasons of their own. There are burrowers that live in the lava tubes and feed on what the warm air carries. There are wire-grass beetles that chew the iron filaments from the blades and harden their shells with them, until they chime when they collide. No one has catalogued it all. The dark hides most of it, and the scale of the place swallows what the dark does not.

## The Sound

For all this life, the highlands are quiet.

It is a working quiet, the silence of a large machine running well. Under it lies a hum, felt more than heard. It is so low and constant that you stop noticing it within an hour, and then notice its absence if you step into a lava tube where the rock damps it. It may be the current. It may be the ground itself, flexing with heat.

Over the hum, small sounds. Rock ticks as it cools and warms. Wire-grass chimes softly. The moth rivers rustle overhead. Somewhere far off, a smelter lets out a deep, steam-filled groan as it shifts its weight. Grazers crackle. Water trickles in a seep channel. None of it is loud. Voices carry strangely and far. In the thin air, sound doesn't die off the way it should; a word spoken at normal volume can reach someone standing a surprising distance away.

The one loud sound is thunder. When the updrafts over a basin grow tall enough, they pull charge up out of the ground. The storm that results is not lit by lightning you can see from the plateau. It happens above, inside the cloud, hidden. But the thunder comes down, long and rolling, and across the whole basin every animal goes still and the fur of every grazer stands straight out, glowing faintly violet. Then the warm rain comes. Then it stops. Then the hum resumes, and after a while you forget you ever heard anything else.

## The Motion

The place is never still, though it is always slow.

The heat below moves on cycles longer than lives. Over generations, one basin cools and another warms. The currents change course. The seeps along one fissure dry up while new ones break through elsewhere, and the mats die back and spread anew. The smelter herds follow the seeps, and the grazers follow the mats, and the hunters follow the grazers. The moth rivers reroute to match the shifting field. The whole ecology migrates across the plateau like a slow tide, over thousands of years, leaving behind fields of slag, iron roads, and dried terraces as the high-water marks of vanished seasons.

On shorter scales there are the storms, the eel discharges, and the hunts, the ordinary pulse of a crowded place. The mist gathers and lifts. Herds pass. A seep swells for a few days and then subsides. It is busy in the way a city is busy at night: most of the activity is invisible, but you can feel it in the air.

## The Rules

A few things are true here that are not true elsewhere.

Iron moves. Leave a nail on the ground and within a season it will have crept toward the nearest seam, dragged by the currents a millimeter at a time. Over longer periods, the iron beads left by the smelters migrate into the seams too, so the fissures slowly thicken with metal. The plateau is concentrating its iron and drawing it into lines, as if wiring itself.

Nothing rots quickly. The dark and the thin air slow decay. The dead lie a long time and slowly mineralize, until the old grazing grounds are scattered with animals turned half to rust, their skeletons outlined in iron.

Everything alive carries a charge, and everything alive can feel the charge of everything else. You cannot be unobserved here. The dark hides you from sight, but the ground knows exactly where you are. So does every hunter within a kilometer.

## What It Is

Stand on the crest of a rise, in the warm dark, with the hum under your feet. Your hair lifts slightly in the field. Mist lies in the basin below. Far off, a single dull red glow marks a smelter at its long work. Overhead, the moths rustle past, following a line you cannot see. Somewhere a grazer crackles, and something that has been waiting for a long time begins to move.

It is not a ruin, and it is not wilderness in the usual sense. It is a works: a foundry, a power station, a mill. It is continent-wide and self-assembled, running in the dark with no one to run it and no one to use what it makes. It has been running for a very long time, and it shows no sign of stopping. The iron gathers. The current flows. The animals go about the business of the place.

Then, all along a seep-chain far down in the basin, the eels let go. For half a second, everything is lit.
