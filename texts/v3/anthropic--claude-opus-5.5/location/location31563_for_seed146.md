# The Hollow Beneath Keel

Start with the dark, because the dark comes first here and lasts longest.

It is not the dark of a closed room. It has distance in it. Stand anywhere on the floor of the Hollow and the air carries a faint pressure of *room*, a sense that the space runs on in every direction far past where any light could reach. It does. The Hollow is a single cavity lying under the crust of the world like a held breath. It is a few hundred meters high at its tallest and a continent wide. No one has walked its edges, and it is unclear whether it has any. The rock above is a lid, and the rock below is a floor. Between them is warm, dry, heavy air, and in that air, and on that floor, and clinging to that lid, live more animals than anyone would think the dark could hold.

## The Floor

The floor is new. That is the first thing a hand learns.

Nothing here is rounded. The Hollow opened, and not long ago in the time of stone. Perhaps a great sheet of molten rock drained away sideways and left its roof standing. Perhaps the crust simply buckled upward and held. Either way, every surface still wears the look of the moment it broke. Plates of dark glass lie tilted against each other in shelves, and their edges are fine enough to part skin at a touch. Between them grow fields of gypsum blades, each as long as a forearm, standing upright in crowds so close that walking means placing each foot in the narrow gaps where the crystals chose not to grow. In some places the floor is a single sheet of fractured basalt, cracked into hexagons that fit together so tightly a hair would not slip between them. In others it is a scree of flakes, conchoidal and glittering, heaped like the leavings of an enormous knapper who worked here once and wandered off.

There is no water to wear any of this down. There is no wind strong enough to carry grit. There are no roots. So the edges stay edges. A shard that fell from the ceiling a thousand years ago still has the same bright lip it had on the day it landed. The Hollow does not age the way surface places age. It holds its first shape and does not let go of it.

It is warm, always the same warmth. The heat comes up from below, steady and even, as if the whole floor rests on the back of some large sleeping body. Lay a palm flat on the glass and it feels like the skin of a living thing a little warmer than you. The air holds that warmth without stirring. There are no cold corners and no hot vents. The temperature is equal everywhere to within a fraction of a degree, and it has been for as long as anything has lived here to notice.

## The Air, and What Hangs in It

The Hollow is big enough to have weather. It has only one weather, though, and it never changes.

About a third of the way up from the floor, the air thickens into a layer. It is not fog, because there is no water to make fog. It is a band of heavier gas, dense and faintly sweet-smelling, bled from the rock over ages and pooled at its own level the way oil pools on water. Above it the air is thin and dry. Below it the air is thin and dry. Within it the air is nearly a fluid, and many of the Hollow's animals do not walk or fly. They float in that band. They drift at its surface, or sink partway in and hang there, and some of them have not touched stone in their lives.

This layer is the Hollow's only cloud. It spreads unbroken across distances that would hold oceans on the surface, perhaps ten meters thick, with a surface smooth enough that a stone tossed onto it skips before it sinks. Its height stays the same, and nothing ever disturbs it. Seen from below by the light of the floor-sparks, it glows back faintly, a pale ceiling under the true ceiling, crowded with the silhouettes of things hanging still.

## Light

There is almost none, but there is some, and it comes from pressure.

Many of the crystals on the floor are triboluminescent. When they are stepped on or crushed or rubbed, they give off a tiny cold flash of blue-white or pale green that lasts a heartbeat and is gone. Nothing crosses the Hollow without making light. A walking animal leaves a trail of small sparks behind it, like footprints written in electricity and then erased. A great crowd of grazers moving across a gypsum field makes a slow, flickering shimmer that can be seen from kilometers away as a smear of faint stars low on the ground.

So in the Hollow, light means motion, and motion means life. Stillness is dark. After long enough there, you stop thinking of darkness as absence and start thinking of it as *rest*. A region with no sparks is a region where nothing moves, and nothing moving is the Hollow's usual state.

A few animals carry their own light: dull red smolders in their joints or along their bellies, weak as coals left overnight. They are rare. Most prefer to be seen only when they choose to move.

## Sound

The Hollow is quiet, but it is not silent.

The dense air carries low sounds far and swallows high ones. Crystal snapping underfoot makes a dull *tock* instead of a sharp crack. Wingbeats, where there are wings, sound like cloth folded slowly. The floating animals of the gas-band make almost no sound at all, only now and then a long, soft exhalation as one of them adjusts its buoyancy. Under all of it, too low to hear and easy to feel through the soles of the feet, runs a hum that might be the rock settling or might be the heat moving through it. It never varies in pitch.

With so many animals, you might expect noise. But nothing here needs to call across distance. Everything that matters is near. The crowding is the conversation.

## The Crowd

The Hollow is full, and not in scattered herds. It is full the way a hive is full, the way a reef is full. Presence is layered on presence, and near-touching is the normal state of things.

**The shelf-grazers** are the most numerous. They are flat, many-legged animals about the size of a spread hand, with backs of fused mineral plates that match the glass they walk on so closely that a still one cannot be told from the floor. They eat the thin crust of salts and metal films that forms, very slowly, on fresh rock surfaces. They move in carpets. On some glass shelves they lie two and three deep, the lower ones feeding and the upper ones waiting, and the whole mass shifts position by a body-length every few hours. Where they move, the floor lights up in slow waves.

**The hangers** live in the gas-band. They are sacs, some small as a fist and some big as a room, filled with something lighter than the heavy gas and wrapped in a membrane so thin the floor-sparks show through it. Fine threads trail down from each, meters long, and the threads touch the threads of their neighbors. The hangers form a single woven net across the whole band, sagging here and tightening there. What they eat is uncertain. Some say it is the heavy gas itself. Some say it is tiny drifting things too small to see. Nobody has seen a hanger die, and nobody has seen one born. There are always exactly as many as there are.

**The ceiling-folk** cling to the lid of the Hollow upside down, in clusters so dense the rock is invisible beneath them. They hang by hooked forelimbs and wrap themselves in membranous wings, and when one drops, it falls through the thin air, strikes the top of the gas-band, glides along its surface, and eventually climbs back up by routes only they know. They are the only animals in the Hollow that travel far. They carry things: seeds of mineral, flakes of metal, and dried bodies.

**The dew-carriers** are the Hollow's most important animals and its strangest. There is almost no water in the Hollow, just the faintest trace of vapor, less than a desert morning's. What little there is, is held inside living bodies. The dew-carriers are slow, round, heavy animals that move from creature to creature and trade water mouth to mouth, taking it from bodies that have too much and giving it to bodies that have too little. They are not predators or parasites. They are something closer to a circulatory system for the whole Hollow. A shelf-grazer that has not met a dew-carrier in a long time grows still and dim. When one arrives, the grazer drinks, brightens, and moves again, and a small pulse of light spreads outward from the meeting. The total amount of water in all the Hollow's living bodies, taken together, never changes. It only moves.

## The Knappers, and What They Build

Among the crowd, sparse and quiet, live the only builders.

They are small, about the size of a child's arm, bipedal when they wish and four-footed when they don't. Their long, fine fingers have pads of something like callus that let them handle the sharpest glass without harm. They go by many names. Here we will call them the knappers, because that is what they do.

They shape stone. They strike flakes from glass cores with hammerstones of dense basalt, and they press the edges with tines of antler-like bone that they grow and shed. They make blades and scrapers and awls. They make thin, bright points that they set in handles of dried hanger-thread wound tight and hardened. In a few places they have found native copper, threads and nuggets of it grown out of the rock. They beat it cold into flat sheets and thin wires, and they bend the wires into shapes whose purpose is not obvious: spirals, rings, and open lattices like the skeletons of small cages.

What they build is scattered and small. A cairn of stacked flakes stands at the edge of a gypsum field. A low ring of fitted basalt hexagons, lifted and replaced so that they rise a hand's breadth above the floor, might be a shelter, a marker, or a place to sit. Copper lattices hang from the ceiling on hanger-threads, turning very slightly in air that otherwise never moves. Here and there a flat shelf of glass is covered in fine scratched lines, as dense as writing though no one has read it.

The structures are a day's walk apart, sometimes several days. Between them there is only the floor and the crowd and the dark.

## The Rule of the Hollow

Here is the strangest thing, the thing that explains the rest.

The knappers do not only make new edges. They *keep the old ones*.

Across the whole continent of the Hollow, as far as anyone has gone, the knappers walk slow circuits and look after the floor. Where a glass edge has dulled, whether from the traffic of a thousand grazers or from some small settling of the rock, a knapper kneels, sets a bone tine against it, and presses. A flake springs away, and the edge is bright and new again. Where a gypsum blade has snapped, a knapper clears the stub and resets the broken piece in its place, or leaves a fresh-struck shard in the gap. Where a pile of scree has spilled, a knapper restores it. Everything goes back to the way it was.

Nobody knows how they know the way it was. Perhaps the scratched glass shelves are records. Perhaps they simply remember, each of them carrying the whole Hollow's shape inside. However they do it, the result is that the Hollow does not change. It is kept young on purpose. Each worn edge is renewed, each fallen thing is returned, and each small erosion is answered and undone.

The rest of the Hollow's life fits into this. The water that moves between bodies never grows or shrinks. The hangers in their net never increase or diminish. When an animal of any kind grows old and still, the ceiling-folk come and carry it up. The body is dried in the warm air, and it becomes something useful: shelter, food, a hanger's new membrane, a knapper's handle. A new animal appears somewhere to take its place. The shimmer of the grazers moving over the floor follows routes that, over long enough time, repeat exactly.

The warmth is constant. The dark is constant. The heavy band of gas sits at its height. The edges stay sharp.

All the Hollow's motion adds up to stillness. It is a place where a great deal happens and nothing changes, where life is abundant and busy and quiet, and every flash of blue-white light from a crushed crystal is answered, sooner or later, by a knapper's careful hand putting things back.

Stand long enough on that warm glass and you start to feel it. You stop wondering where the edges of the Hollow are and start wondering whether the knappers will come to smooth away your footprints, and then you realize they are already on their way.
