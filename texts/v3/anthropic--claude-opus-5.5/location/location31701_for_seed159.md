# The Opening at Kessary

## The Tear

It opened eleven years ago, and it has not finished.

Before, there was a plain of low brown scrub over limestone, and under the limestone something hot that nobody knew about. Then came three nights of noise like furniture dragged across a floor the size of a province. On the fourth morning a crack ran eastward across the plain for nine miles and the land on either side of it had slumped. What remains is a rift, a trench-world sunk two to four hundred feet below the old surface. In most places it is a mile or more across. Its walls are so new they still have the look of broken bread.

From the rim you look down into a basin that goes on past where your eyes can follow. The floor is a jumble of tilted slabs, each the size of a village. Between them are chasms, and further down, in places, the rift keeps opening. Heat rises out of it as a steady breath on the face, like standing in front of an oven someone has just opened. The air above the trench wavers, and it is never empty. At almost any hour it is thick with wings.

## Stone That Has Not Learned to Be Old

Nothing here has been softened. That is the first thing a visitor understands and the last thing they stop noticing. Rock in the rest of the world has been rounded by water, lichened, cracked by frost, and dusted over. Here it has the raw geometry of the moment it broke.

The limestone faces are cream and bone-white where they split. They are so fresh that they still hold fossil shells in cross-section, cut clean in half, their inner chambers open to the air for the first time in a hundred million years. Below the limestone lies a band of dark basalt, then something reddish and glassy that the harvesters call *liver-stone*. It fractures into curved, razor-lipped flakes. People who come down without leather on their hands and knees leave blood on it within the hour. There are no screes of rounded pebbles and no soil at all. Where rock has fallen, it lies in angular heaps that still ring when you step on them, a dull porcelain *tonk* underfoot. Wear has not yet deadened them.

The ground is also still being made. In the deepest parts of the rift, fissures exhale steam and sometimes a sluggish, ropy seep of dark rock that crusts over before it spreads a body's length. The seeps are rare, but the heat is not. The floor of the trench is warm everywhere, blood-warm or warmer. In places the stone is too hot to rest a palm on. On cold nights on the plain above, the rift glows faintly along its deepest seams, like coals seen through a grate.

## Wet Without Rivers

This is not a watered place, but it is never dry.

The warm air rising from the floor meets the cooler air of the plain at the rim and condenses. Most afternoons a lid of low white cloud forms over the trench. Some afternoons it rains, but only *inside* the rift: a brief, heavy, warm downpour that hisses on the hot slabs and throws up a second fog from below. After half an hour it stops, and the steam lifts, and the stone steams dry.

Brine is the other water. It weeps from the seams between the limestone and the basalt, hot and heavily mineralized. It runs down the walls in thin glassy sheets and collects in pools on the tilted slabs. The pools are turquoise, milk-green, and rust. Some of them are hot enough to scald. None of them are drinkable. Where the brine runs and evaporates, it leaves crust, and the crust grows.

## The Saltbloom

This is the strange thing, the thing that brings people down.

The crust does not stay crust. On the wetter walls of the rift, the mineral deposits grow into spiked, feathered, branching formations that look, from a distance, like frost on a window made enormous. The harvesters call it saltbloom, though it is only partly salt. It grows *fast*: a hand's width in a day, a man's height in a season. Its branches are translucent white, faintly pink, and in places amber where iron has run into them. They are brittle. When they grow too heavy they break away from the wall and fall. At any hour you can hear somewhere in the rift the crystalline smash of a bloom shearing off and shattering down the slope. It sounds like a cartload of glass tipped down stairs.

Nothing green grows here. There is no moss, no fern, no weed. What lives on the walls at the bottom of the food chain is a skin of slick, colored films: purple-black near the hot seeps, orange on the brine runs, a pale greasy grey on the shaded stone. They are not plants. They eat the stone and the heat and the brine, and they have spread across the fresh rock like a spill. Everything else here eats them, or eats what eats them.

## The Multitude

In eleven years, the rift has been colonized by more animal life than the plain above ever held.

**The swifts come first.** They are small, dark, sickle-winged cave swifts, and there are so many that numbers stop meaning anything. At dawn they pour *up* out of the rift in a column that can be seen from twenty miles, twisting like smoke, and spread across the plains to hunt insects. At dusk they pour back down in a descending funnel. The sound of their return is a long hissing roar, like surf heard from inside a shell and made gigantic. They nest in the saltbloom, pressing their gummy, spit-bound cups into the crystal branches, and their weight is part of what breaks the bloom. When a formation collapses it takes hundreds of nests with it, and the survivors rebuild on the fresh growth within days. The rift's walls are a perpetual construction site of spit and salt.

**Below them, the crawlers.** Pale crickets as long as a finger coat the shaded walls so thickly that the stone seems to shimmer. When a shadow passes, a whole wall shifts its surface at once with a sound like sand poured on paper. Centipedes hunt among them, banded red and yellow, some of them longer than a forearm. Beetles with iridescent green backs graze the colored films. Ants have come down from the plain and built nothing, because there is nothing to build with. Instead they cluster in living masses in the crevices, and they stream in black ropes along the warm seams.

**In the pools, the crabs.** No one knows how they got here. They may have come in the mud on some bird's feet, or perhaps they lived in the old dark water under the limestone and came up when it broke. They are small, bone-white, and eyeless. They are everywhere there is brine, crowding the pool margins in rustling carpets and scraping the films with their claws. At night they leave the water and climb the walls, and the rattling of their shells on stone goes on until morning.

**Then the ones that eat all of these.** Bats take the night shift the swifts leave, and a second column rises at dusk in the opposite direction. Pale lizards bask on the hot slabs. Snakes have come down to eat the lizards. Rock doves have come for the warmth and the crickets. Hawks and falcons wheel over the rim at dawn and dusk and drop into the swift columns. They strike with a sound like a slap, and a puff of feathers drifts down through the heat haze. Kites follow them for scraps.

Everything is layered and near-touching. There is no bare surface that is not also occupied. Rest a hand on a ledge and it comes away with crickets on it. Sit still for a minute and ants find you, then something that eats ants.

## Noise

The rift is loud the way a market is loud: no single sound dominates, and silence is impossible.

The wall shrieks with swift-calls, which are high, thin, and constant. There is the ceaseless tick-rustle of insect millions. Brine hisses on hot stone, and the steam fissures sigh deeply. The saltbloom shatters at irregular intervals, near and far. Every few days a heavier sound comes: a slab of wall letting go. First there is a crack like a door slamming in an empty house. Then a grinding roar that goes on for longer than seems possible, and a boom that is felt in the chest before it is heard. Afterward, for a minute or so, every creature in the rift falls silent at once. It is the only silence the place has. Then the swifts start up again, and everything else follows.

The fresh rock carries sound strangely. It rings rather than absorbs. A voice can travel improbable distances down the long axis of the rift, and so can a falling stone, a cough, or a chisel blow. Harvesters on opposite walls converse at a half-mile's distance without shouting.

## The Harvesters

People come down for the nests and the bloom.

The swift nests are made of hardened spit laced with fine mineral grit. They are gathered, boiled, and sold on the plains as medicine and delicacy. The saltbloom itself is broken off in chunks and carried up to be ground into a coarse pink salt. The salt keeps meat for a year and tastes, people say, faintly of iron and of lightning. Neither harvest lasts. The nests are rebuilt within weeks and the bloom regrows in months. The rift is an inexhaustible, dangerous orchard.

What the harvesters build is small and temporary, because nothing here stays where it was put. There is no wood in the rift, so they haul poles and rope down from the plain. They lash ladders of it against the walls, and walkways that bridge the narrower chasms. Many are lost each year, crushed under falling bloom, jarred loose by tremors, or swallowed when a crack widens.

At the base of the stable walls they stack low shelters of flat rock without mortar. These are roofless rings, waist-high, meant to turn aside falling stone and swifts' droppings, and to keep the crabs out at night. Some have a scrap of hide stretched over one side. Fire pits sit in the hot seams. The ground supplies the heat, so the harvesters cook by setting clay pots directly into cracks in the basalt.

Their tools are hand-made and few: stone and bronze chisels, bone scrapers for prising nests loose, braided-hide ropes, leather sleeves and knee-guards against the liver-stone, and flat baskets carried on the back. They mark safe routes with small cairns, three stones and a white shell. They mark danger by painting a red handprint in ochre on the rock. Over eleven years the handprints have become the most common human mark in the rift. There are thousands of them, some only a few days old, some already disappearing under saltbloom as the wall grows over them.

There are also shrines of a kind. Where someone died, the others often wedge a nest into a crack, not harvested and not sold, and leave it. Swifts sometimes take these over and live in them.

## The Rules of the Place

The harvesters say the rift has rules, and they teach them to newcomers in the first hour.

*Watch the birds.* When the swifts leave a section of wall all at once, a collapse is coming. They know first. Harvesters say they can feel the stone begin to shift.

*Watch the crabs.* If the crabs leave a pool and do not come back, the water is going to boil or drain.

*The rain is not water.* The warm rains inside the rift are mostly condensed brine-steam. They leave a fine salt on everything, and drinking them makes you sick. All water must be carried in from the plain.

*Nothing is where it was yesterday.* A ledge used for a year can be gone in a morning. A walkway that bridged a crack at dawn may be hanging from one side by nightfall. The rift is still widening, a finger's width a year on average, but in lurches and jolts rather than smoothly. Every morning's route is checked again by the first one down.

*The rift is not finished.* Nobody knows how deep it will go, or how wide, or whether it will one day close again. Some harvesters say it will open all the way to the sea. Others say the heat in the floor is rising, and that one day the seeps of dark rock will turn into rivers and fill the whole trench with new black stone. They mean to make their money before then.

## Noon

At midday the sun stands straight over the trench and light falls all the way to the floor. The fresh rock is pale, and it throws the light back and forth between the walls, so the rift fills with a hard white radiance brighter than the plain above. The saltbloom blazes. The brine pools glow like lamps. Heat shimmer rises from every slab. Through it drift the endless flakes of wings, feathers, and crystal dust, so that the whole trench seems to glitter and swarm.

Then the afternoon cloud forms at the rim, and the light goes soft and pearly. A warm rain comes down and hisses on the stone. The rift steams. The swifts wheel through the steam, calling, and somewhere a bloom lets go and falls.

At the bottom of the world, in a new wound in the ground, everything is still being made, and everything is alive.
