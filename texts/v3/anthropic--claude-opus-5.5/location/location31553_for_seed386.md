# The Kettle Tableland

The first thing is the breath. At this height the air is thin enough that every step costs something, and yet it is *hot*. It's not the dry, bright heat of high deserts but a close, wet heat that sits against the skin like a palm. You expect the cold of altitude and get the opposite. The heat comes from beneath. The stone is warm when you lay a hand on it, faintly and steadily, the way a sleeping animal is warm.

This is a tableland, a raised shelf of old basalt many kilometres across, lifted far above everything around it. Its edges drop away in cliffs so tall the eye stops trusting them. Up here, on top, the land rolls gently: long swells and shallow basins, low domes of rock shouldering up through the green, and everywhere the small white breath of vents. The mountain beneath is not dead. It has simply stopped hurrying.

## The Ground

The rock is black basalt gone grey-green with age. It has been rained on for so long that every hard edge has softened. Columns that once cracked apart in clean hexagons now stand like worn candles, their tops rounded and cupped, each cup holding a little pool of warm water. Where hot springs have run for centuries they have laid down sinter: pale, stepped terraces of mineral crust, cream and apricot and rust. They spread across the slopes like the frozen overflow of a basin left too long under a tap. The terraces are finished forming. They grow now by the thickness of a fingernail in a lifetime, and moss has claimed most of them.

Water is everywhere, and most of it is warm. It seeps from the cliff-faces in dark weeping sheets. It stands in the basins as shallow, steaming meres fringed with sedge. It runs in narrow channels that braid and rejoin across the flats, too hot to wade in some places and bathwater-soft a few metres downstream. The air is so thin that water boils here at barely more than eighty degrees. The hottest springs do not violently erupt. They roll: a slow, continuous turning-over, a lazy seethe you can watch for an hour. Steam lifts from them without urgency.

## The Weather That Lives Here

The steam does not leave. In the morning the basins fill with cloud that has formed from the ground up, white and thick, lying in every hollow like milk in a dish. You walk down into a mere and vanish. You walk up onto a dome and the whole tableland spreads out below you as an archipelago of green humps rising from a white sea. Beyond the edge, far down, there is a second sea, the true cloud deck, grey and flat, stretching to the horizon.

By noon the sun has burned the hollows clear. Then the heat builds, and the rising steam and the updrafts climbing the cliffs meet. By mid-afternoon, nearly every day, a storm assembles itself directly overhead. The rain comes warm. That is one of the strange things about this place that you stop noticing after a day: rain the temperature of blood, falling on warm stone, steaming back up almost as it lands. After the storm the whole tableland smokes. Every rock, every leaf, every branch of every tree-fern gives off a faint haze, and the light goes soft and pearly.

Sound does not carry far in air this thin. Thunder arrives muffled, more of a pressure than a crack. The rain on the moss is nearly silent. Mostly the tableland is quiet: the tick of water, the seethe of the springs, the occasional wet cough of a vent clearing its throat.

## The Green

Everything grows. The heat and the water and the mineral springs have made the high plateau a garden that should not exist at this altitude. The plants carry the look of mountains even so, the giant rosettes and silvered leaves of things adapted to strong light and thin air, but pushed to lushness.

Tree-ferns stand in crowds along the warm channels, their crowns overlapping, their trunks furred with a century of dead fronds and alive with smaller things rooting in them. Giant rosette plants, taller than a person, grow on the drier domes. Their leaves are thick and felted with silver hair, and their flower-spikes rise two and three times higher, crowded with pale yellow blooms. The moss is the true ground here. It lies knee-deep and sponge-soft over the basalt, in fifty shades from lime to near-black, and it holds water like a held breath. Step on it and it sighs.

Bromeliads clasp every trunk and ledge. Each one is a small cistern, and each cistern is its own world: mosquito larvae, tiny crabs, tadpoles, a drowned beetle, a sprouting seed. Orchids hang from the tree-ferns in chains, most of them small and brownish and easily missed. A few are huge and waxy and smell of rotting fruit.

Near the vents the vegetation changes. Close to the hottest ground only mats of heat-loving algae survive: orange, emerald, and a violet so saturated it looks dyed. They lie in concentric rings around each spring, a map of temperature drawn in colour. A little farther out come ferns that grow nowhere else, their fronds curled permanently against the heat.

## The Soot

Look closely at the leaves on the western side of the tableland and you will see it: a fine grey-black film. It rubs off on the fingers. It beads the rainwater on the silver rosettes into dark droplets. In the sinter pools it settles into thin layers, so that some terraces are banded like tree-rings, pale and dark, pale and dark.

It comes from below.

Nothing is built up here. There are no roads, no paths but animal paths, no walls, no marks of a hand. But far down, beyond the western cliffs and under the grey cloud deck, lies a plain, and on that plain there are furnaces. You never see them directly. The cloud deck covers them. You know them by their evidence.

The afternoon updrafts climb the cliffs, carrying whatever the lowland breathes out, and the storms wring it out onto the plateau. The soot is part of the rain now, part of the soil, part of the chemistry of the pools. Some of the moss on the western rim has turned permanently darker, and the insects that live in it are darker too. There is a small moth here, found nowhere else, that comes in two colours. Its pale form lives in the east. Its black form has taken over the west within living memory.

At night the evidence is clearer. The cloud deck below the western edge glows a dull, patient orange, lit from underneath. In certain places the glow pulses, brightening and dimming on a rhythm slower than breathing. Moths rise toward it from the tableland in thin streams and pour off the edge into the dark. Some nights, when the air is still and the wind is right, you can feel rather than hear a low repeated thud coming up through the stone of the cliff: a drop-hammer, perhaps, or a pump, many kilometres down. It is the only regular sound in the whole landscape, and it has no source you could ever walk to. Occasionally a thin, long note rises too, a whistle or horn stretched and flattened by distance, and then it is gone.

The tableland takes no notice. It has its own engines. The vents cough. The springs roll. The ground stays warm.

## The Living Things

The animals are many and mostly quiet.

Frogs are everywhere: in the bromeliad pools, in the warm meres, clinging to fern-trunks. They are small and jewel-bright and call only at dusk, and even then softly, a high dripping trill that thin air swallows within a stone's throw. Hummingbirds work the rosette flower-spikes in the morning, fast iridescent flecks. Their wingbeats are almost the loudest sound of the day. Beetles in armour of green and copper trundle through the moss.

A rock-dwelling animal, round and brown, something like a hyrax, lives in colonies on the warm basalt domes. Colonies sprawl across the stone in the early light, soaking up heat from beneath, and scatter at the shadow of a hawk. The hawk is slate-grey and patient. It hunts from the tops of the tree-ferns, dropping on frogs, on the round brown animals, on anything that crosses open moss.

Snakes winter in the crevices near the vents. Here winter means only a slightly cooler season, and they seek the heat anyway. They lie coiled in fissures where the stone is too hot to touch, eyes half-lidded.

At the rim, where the cliffs fall away, swifts live. They nest in the weeping rock-faces behind curtains of seepage and spend their days in the updrafts, hunting insects carried up from the lowlands, sometimes from the edges of the very smoke. At dusk they come in all at once, a vast sickle-winged cloud pouring into the cliff, and for a few minutes the quiet breaks under the rush of their wings.

Deep in the tree-fern groves, rarely seen, there is a heavy, slow, dark-furred browser. It leaves clear prints in the sinter mud and strips of bark torn from the fern trunks. It is shy, and the tableland is large enough for it to remain mostly a rumour.

## What It Is

It is an island. That is the truest thing about it. A warm, wet, green island lifted into the sky, sealed off from everything below by cliffs no one climbs, running on its own inner heat, making its own weather every afternoon, and changing at the pace of moss.

Everything here has been in balance for a very long time. The terraces are finished, the channels have found their courses, the forests have reached their shape. Only at the western edge does something newer press in, arriving on the wind: a dusting of grey, a black moth, a distant pulse through stone, a glow under the clouds at night. The tableland absorbs it the way it absorbs everything, slowly, taking it into the moss and the mud and the banding of the pools.

In the morning the hollows fill with cloud again. The rosettes stand silver in the first light. A frog calls once from a bromeliad, softly, and the ground beneath is warm.
