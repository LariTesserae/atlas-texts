# The Standing Works

## I. Ground

There is no ground in the ordinary sense. Wherever you put a foot, what holds you is root: fibrous, finger-thick cables braided into mats, the mats layered on older mats, all of it slightly springy and slightly warm, like the back of a sleeping hand. Push down hard and water comes up around your boot. It is not mud. It is clear, and it tastes faintly of iron and sugar. Somewhere below, perhaps a few meters down, perhaps a few hundred, the root-mass rests on whatever this world is made of, but nothing has ever dug that far, and nothing ever will.

The land is low, almost perfectly so. Across distances that would take weeks to walk, the surface rises and falls by only a few meters. Those gentle swells are not hills. They are places where the root-braid is thicker. There are no valleys either, only slight depressions where water stands a finger's depth deep in the weave, black and motionless, holding no reflection because there is almost nothing to reflect.

## II. Dark

It is dark here. It is not the dark of a night that will end. It is the dark of a place that was never lit.

No sun reaches this surface, and it is unclear whether there is a sun at all. Above the vegetation there is a ceiling of mist, and above the mist there is nothing the eye can resolve. Yet the plants do not need light. That is the first rule of this place, and every other rule follows from it.

The only illumination is corona. At the very tips of the tallest growths, and sometimes along the serrated edges of the broadest leaves, a faint violet-blue glow clings like breath on cold glass. It does not flicker or spread. It sits exactly where it sat yesterday, and where it will sit tomorrow, marking the points where the vegetation's electrical potential is highest. If you stood still long enough for your eyes to open fully, you would see these glows scattered to the horizon in every direction: a dim constellation laid across the land, faint but unwavering.

## III. The Growth

Everything visible is plant, and the plant is doing work.

Rising out of the root-mat are stems, and they do not look like stems from any garden. They are straight, ribbed, and jointed at regular intervals, like lengths of conduit fitted together. Some are as thin as a wrist; some are as broad as a house and rise forty or fifty meters before branching. Put your palm against one and you will feel a deep, steady pressure beneath the skin. It is fluid, moving upward, always upward, at the same rate. Put your ear against it and you will hear it: a hiss so faint it could be your own blood.

At the joints, the stems swell into nodes, dense, tuber-like knots wrapped in overlapping scales. These are warm to the touch, noticeably warmer than the air. The nodes are where the current gathers. A leaf held near a large node will lift, very slightly, toward it. Wet hair stands up. Fine droplets of condensation, drifting in the air, bend their paths as they pass.

The leaves are enormous and rigid, laminated like plates of horn. Their undersides carry parallel ridges, finely spaced as the grooves on a record. Their margins are sharp. Truly sharp: fresh, crisp, unfrayed. Nothing has ever torn them. Nothing has ever chewed them. No wind has ever worn them. The whole growth has the look of something just finished, as if it were completed an hour ago and has not yet been touched by time. There is no leaf litter and there are no dead stalks. Nothing is browning or curling. Nothing here has ever died.

Between the great stems, the space fills with lesser growths: coils, ladders, lattices, fan-shaped membranes stretched between uprights like filters. Their density is high but not suffocating. There are always gaps, always a route through. The gaps are regular, as though clearance were part of the specification.

## IV. What It Makes

This is the second rule: the vegetation is an industry with no one to supply.

The stems draw water up from the saturated root-mass. The nodes do something to it. There is heat, and there is charge, and there is a smell like a struck match drowned in honey. What comes out is gathered in bladders: translucent sacs clustered beneath the leaf-crowns, each one swollen with a dense amber fluid. Hold a corona-lit bladder close and you can see that the fluid is layered, heavier bands at the bottom and paler ones above, perfectly separated, refined.

The bladders never burst, and they never drop. They are always full.

The production is not accumulating. It is being held, at pressure, in perfect balance. What flows in is exactly what is stored. What is stored is exactly what is sustained. The whole continent is a refinery running at steady state: every valve open to the exact degree, every reservoir at exact capacity, every line at exact pressure. It does not grow and it does not diminish. It runs.

Some of the output does escape, as heat. That is why this place is warm: not hot, but steadily and humidly warm, like a greenhouse at night, or a machine room after the machines have been running for a long time. The warmth rises from the nodes and from the root-mat itself, and it is the only weather-maker this world has.

## V. Weather of Its Own

Because the Works are so vast, spanning distances where the curve of the world would hide one edge from another, their waste heat does not simply dissipate. It organizes.

Warm, saturated air rises from the growth and meets the colder dark above. At a fixed height, about sixty meters, it condenses into the mist ceiling, a single continuous layer, flat as a table, stretching across the entire expanse. The tallest stems pierce it, and where they pass through, the mist parts in tight collars around them and never closes.

Beneath the ceiling, there is rain, but only in certain places. Far out in the growth there are bands where the air columns meet in a fixed pattern, and in those bands a fine, endless drizzle falls from the mist. It has fallen there since the beginning. It will fall there always. The leaves in the rain-bands are beaded with droplets that slide down channels precisely cut to receive them, returning to the root-mat and to the stems and to the nodes. The droplets flow in the same direction today as they did at the start. The paths are worn, if anything here can be called worn, only in the sense that they have always been exactly the right shape.

Walk out of a rain-band and the air is merely damp. Condensation forms on your skin and on every leaf. Rocks, where they break the root-mat at all, weep: small grey outcrops of sharp, unweathered stone with water beading down their faces from no visible source. The stone is new. Its fracture edges could cut you. It has been here as long as everything else, and it has never been rounded, because nothing here moves with enough force to wear it down.

## VI. Silence

The sound of this place is almost nothing.

There is the hiss inside the stems, audible only by contact. In the rain-bands there is a fine, uniform patter, too soft to carry. Sometimes, near the largest nodes, there is a hum. It is not mechanical, not quite. It is lower and wetter than that, closer to the sound a finger makes circling the rim of a glass, felt more in the teeth than heard. There are no birdcalls, no insects, and no rustle of small things in the litter, because there is no litter and there are no small things. No wind stirs the leaves, because the convection is too slow and too steady to make gusts.

Walking here, the loudest sound is yourself: your breath, the squelch of your boots in the root-mat, the drag of your sleeve against a rigid leaf-edge. The silence does not feel empty. It feels occupied, the way a building feels occupied when every machine in it is running smoothly and none of them needs attention.

## VII. The Rules, Stated Plainly

- **Nothing is lit, and nothing needs to be.** The vegetation runs on its own heat, water, and charge.
- **Nothing lives here but plant.** There are no animals, no fungi, no rot, no decomposers. Without decay, there has never been death.
- **Nothing was built.** The pipes, filters, reservoirs, and transformers are all grown. There are no seams, no fasteners, no tool marks. The architecture of industry has arisen here without an architect.
- **Nothing changes.** The Works reached equilibrium at their completion and have held it perfectly. The mist ceiling, the rain-bands, the corona points, and the fill-level of every bladder are all fixed.
- **Nothing wears.** Because nothing moves with force, every edge is still as sharp as the day it formed. The place is ancient in duration and newborn in condition.

## VIII. The Edge That Isn't

Walk far enough in any direction, for weeks or months, and you will not find a boundary. The growth thins nowhere. The mist ceiling does not break. The corona lights continue past the curve of the horizon, unchanging in their spacing, unchanging in their faint violet.

Some visitors, if there ever were any, might come to believe that the Standing Works cover the whole world. Others might suspect the opposite: that the Works are the world's machinery, and that somewhere, beyond the dark or beneath the root-mat, is whatever all this refined, stored, perfectly pressurized output is *for*.

But the Works give no sign. They do not wait. They do not anticipate. They hold. Across a continent of warm, wet darkness, every valve is open exactly as far as it should be. Every reservoir is full. The current rests at the tips of the leaves. The plant runs, in silence, as it always has, as it always will.
