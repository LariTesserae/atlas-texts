# The Stamp Field at Orrin Low

## The ground

The lava came down forty-one years ago and stopped a little short of the sea. It spread across a low coastal shelf, never more than sixty meters above the tideline, and cooled into a field of black basalt about two kilometers across. It is shaped roughly like a spilled cup, with a raised lip at the northern edge where the flow slowed and piled against itself.

The stone is still sharp. There has been too little time for it to be anything else. The surface is ropy in places, braided and wrinkled where the skin of the flow crumpled as it moved. Elsewhere it breaks into clinker, loose rubble with edges like cheap glass, which cuts boots and cuts the pads of dogs. Where the flow crusted over and then drained from beneath, there are lava tubes. Their roofs have fallen in places, leaving long trenches three or four meters deep with walls that show every layer of the cooling. Nothing has been rounded yet. Frost has only started its work, and hairline cracks run through the pressure ridges. Pick up a stone and it has no weight of age. It rings faintly when it strikes another, like a tile.

The basalt is porous. Rain disappears into it almost as soon as it lands, so there are no streams and no ponds. The field is damp without ever being wet. Water arrives as fog off the cold sea, as a thin grey rain that comes perhaps one day in four, and as condensation. Each morning the dew beads on every black edge. By noon the stone has drunk it.

## The green

Life came into the field quickly, and it came from below.

First were the mosses. A grey-green woolly moss grows so thick on the older ropy flows that stepping on it is like stepping on a mattress. You sink, and the moss exhales cold air that was stored in the stone underneath. Then came the ferns. They root in the cracks and in the collapsed tubes, where shade and trapped moisture make small cellars of humidity, and there they grow shoulder-high. Their fronds are a bright, almost chemical green against the basalt. Birch and alder followed, gnarled and low, their roots working into the fissures and prying at them. There are no old trees anywhere. The oldest birch in the field is younger than the oldest person in town, and everyone knows which tree it is.

The greenery is dense wherever it can take hold, and it cannot take hold everywhere. The fresh clinker is still bare. The result is a patchwork: deep moss-pillows and fern-trenches separated by stretches of raw black rubble, hard edges on every side.

## The town

Orrin Low is a mill town. Its people came for the stone, and the stone is what they mine.

The basalt of the flow is unusually rich in something the townspeople call *grit*. It is a fine, hard, glassy fraction that, once crushed and sorted, is valued inland for abrasives, for grinding wheels, and for the sanding belts of factories far larger than any in Orrin Low. So the town crushes the lava field. It has been crushing it for thirty-four years.

The heart of the town is the Stamp House, a long shed of riveted iron and tarred timber. Inside it, forty-eight iron stamps rise and fall in a staggered sequence. Each is a column of cast iron the height of a man, lifted by a cam on a turning shaft and then dropped onto a bed of broken rock. A steam engine drives the shaft, and the engine's boiler burns peat cut from bogs along the coast. The stamps never stop. They have not fully stopped since the Stamp House was built, apart from three days during a boiler failure. The older residents still speak of those three days quietly, the way people speak of an illness.

The Stamp House does not stay where it is. It stands on rails, iron tracks laid across the basalt and bolted into it. As the stamps consume the rock in front of them, the whole shed is winched forward, a few centimeters a week, into the unbroken flow. Behind it the lava field has been stripped down to crushed gravel and settling ponds of grey slurry. Then the town moves too.

It moves more slowly than the Stamp House does. The houses are timber and iron, and many of them sit on sledges. Every few years a family decides that the walk to the Stamp House has grown too long. The neighbors come, jacks go under the corners, and the house is dragged across the moss on oiled logs to a new spot closer to the work. Other buildings stay where they are and are simply left behind. Because of this the town is thin. Its buildings are spread across nearly a kilometer: a dozen near the Stamp House, a scattering further back, and at the far end, near the original quarry face, a few shells that nobody uses. Moss and fern have climbed those abandoned buildings the way they climb anything that stays still for long enough.

The infrastructure ties it together. Iron pipes carry steam, water, and compressed air, raised on trestles so they clear the uneven ground, and they run in long, sagging lines between the buildings. A narrow tramway carries crushed grit from the Stamp House to the sorting sheds and on to the jetty, where a small coastal steamer calls every eleven days. There are telegraph poles, and a single electric line powered by a dynamo off the main engine. It lights the Stamp House, the sorting sheds, the doctor's house, and nothing else. Everyone else uses oil.

## The water, again

Because the field drinks the rain, the town has to catch its water before the rock does.

Along the northern lip, where the fog comes in thickest, the town has strung fog-nets: tall frames of tarred cord mesh, perhaps thirty of them, each the size of a barn wall. Fog drifts through the mesh, beads on the cords, and drips into gutters, which feed a stone cistern. On good mornings the nets hiss, a sound like soft rain even when no rain is falling. The cistern water tastes of tar and faintly of iron.

The Stamp House supplies the rest. Its engine exhausts steam through a condenser, a tower of stacked iron plates cooled by the sea wind. The condensate runs off warm and distilled, down a trough to the settling ponds. Along that trough, and only there, the town's gardens grow: kale, potatoes, rhubarb, and a tangle of watercress. The gardens move too. When the Stamp House is winched forward, the condenser goes with it, and the trough is extended. Every year or two the old beds dry out and new ones have to be dug. There is a local saying about following the warm water, and it means about what you'd expect.

## The noise

The stamps are the first thing anyone notices and the last thing anyone forgets.

Each stamp strikes with a heavy, flat *thud*, and there are forty-eight of them, each falling roughly once every two seconds. Together they make a continuous pounding. It is too complex to count and too regular to ignore, a rolling sound felt as much as heard. Close to the Stamp House it is deafening. People working inside wear waxed cotton plugs and talk with their hands. At the edge of town it becomes a low, uneven drumming that comes up through the stone into your feet. The fresh basalt carries sound well, better than soil would, and the whole lava field hums with it. Put your ear to a boulder a kilometer away and you will hear the stamps.

On top of the stamps there is the engine's chuff, the shriek of the tram brakes, the clatter of grit falling through the sorting screens, the steamer's horn on the eleventh day, and the dinner bell at the Stamp House. There are dogs, and there are the people, who have learned to speak loudly and bluntly, often in short phrases.

And there are the birds.

## The fauna

The lava field is full of animals. They came for the same reason the plants did: new ground, few competitors, and shelter in the cracks.

Most numerous are the petrels. They are small, dark seabirds that nest in burrows. On the old coast they nested in earth. Here they nest in lava tubes and fissures, and in the gaps under the sledge-houses. Tens of thousands of them come in from the sea at dusk, circling low over the town. In their burrows they make a sound like a cat purring into a bottle, and they make it all night.

There are rock pigeons in the Stamp House rafters, iridescent and fat on spilled peat-grain. There are starlings on the pipe trestles, mimicking the engine's whistle and the squeal of the tram. There are gulls along the jetty. A small grey fox lives in the collapsed tubes, eats petrel chicks, and has learned to steal from the gardens. There are voles in the moss and slow-worms in the fern-trenches. In the settling ponds there is an odd crust of algae, rust-orange and slippery, grazed by snails and by a species of small wading bird that nobody in town knows the name of.

The animals have adapted to the stamps.

This is the strange part of Orrin Low. The rhythm of the stamps has run without interruption for more than three decades, and the life of the lava field has settled into it. The petrels' purring rises and falls in pulses that match the stamps. The starlings sing in phrases that fit between the beats. The voles move through the moss in short dashes, in time. During the three days of the boiler failure, the petrels did not come in from the sea at all. They circled offshore, calling, until the stamps started again.

The moss is stranger still. Near the Stamp House, where the vibration is strongest, the moss does not grow as an even carpet. It grows in patterns: concentric rings, nested curves, and radiating lines, like the figures sand forms on a vibrating plate. They are subtle from the ground and unmistakable from the roof of the Stamp House. The patterns shift slowly as the Stamp House moves forward and the vibration changes. Behind the building, on the ground it has already crossed, the patterns fade over months into ordinary moss. Ahead of it, on fresh rock, faint rings are already starting to form, a meter or two past where the stamps have reached.

Nobody in town thinks about this much. It is simply how moss grows here.

## The light

The light is clear and even. Fog comes in each morning and burns off by mid-morning. On a clear day the sun is bright but cool, and it lies flat across the basalt, catching every sharp edge. The black stone makes the green seem brighter than it is. At midday, looking across the field, you see an intense mosaic: the black rock, the green of moss and fern, the rust of the settling ponds, the grey-white of the crushed gravel, and the dark iron of the pipes. At dusk the petrels come in against a pale sky and the Stamp House windows light up yellow, the only electric light for many kilometers.

## Time

Almost everything here changes slowly.

The Stamp House advances a few meters a year. At that rate it will reach the northern lip of the flow in about sixty years, and then the grit will be gone. The town will either follow the stone into the older rock inland, where the grit is poorer, or it will stop. Nobody has decided.

The lava field itself is aging, but only just. Frost and roots are working at the cracks, and the clinker is slowly breaking down. The oldest moss-beds are deepening. In three or four centuries there may be soil, and a forest, and something like the country that was here before the flow. By then the Stamp House will be long gone. There will be a long strip of crushed gravel and old settling ponds running across the field like a scar, and at its northern end, perhaps, a rusted iron shed on rails.

For now there is the pounding, the fog, the green, and the birds purring in the rock, all of it moving forward a few centimeters a week.
