# The Pump Hollow

You would walk past it. From the marsh-edge it is only another swelling in the green, one knuckle among thousands where the reed-flats give way to the fig-mass. The fig-mass is a single organism, or behaves like one, a braided continent of aerial root and trunk and leaf that runs low along the waterline for miles and never rises more than a few body-lengths above the mud. Its outer surface is so dense a hand can't be pushed into it. But if you put your ear against this particular knuckle you would hear it, and then you would never mistake it for anything else.

It is thumping.

---

## The Room

Inside, where the roots have grown apart from one another the way a lung grows apart from itself, there is a chamber about the size of a chapel nave, though lower, maybe twice a person's height at the centre, curving down to crawlspace at the edges. It is entirely plant. The floor is a mat of root laid over root, springy and damp, with the grain running in long parallel lines toward the centre like the fibres of a muscle. The walls are root. The ceiling is a vault of interlaced stems, and light comes through it in a thousand small pale coins, a steady ordinary daylight that has been sieved and softened but not coloured much. There is no stone here, no soil, no made thing. There is nothing in the room that was not, at some point, grown.

And yet the first word that comes to mind is *machinery*.

At the centre, where the floor-fibres converge, stands the pump. It is a root. It is a single enormous root, thick as a cart-horse, that comes down from the vault and bends into the floor and does not stop moving. It contracts along its length, draws itself shorter and fatter, holds, then releases and lengthens, and each contraction produces a sound like a sledgehammer driven into wet timber. *Thoom.* A pause of perhaps four seconds. *Thoom.* The whole room flexes with it. The floor lifts a finger's width under your feet and settles. The coins of light on the floor shiver and re-form.

It is drawing water up out of the marsh. Beneath the root-mat the water table sits only a hand's depth down; you can press the floor and feel it go cold and wet beneath your palm. The pump root pulls that water up through its own body and sends it outward and upward into the fig-mass, into miles of leaf. The whole continent of green is fed from pockets like this one, hundreds of them, maybe, hidden inside the knuckles, each one beating.

---

## The Noise

It is loud in a way that plants are not supposed to be.

The main stroke you feel more than hear. But around it there is a full industrial register of smaller sounds. When the pump root releases, water rushes back through valves in its flanks, flaps of tissue that slap shut with a crack like a belt snapping taut. Somewhere in the walls, secondary roots take up the pulse and relay it outward, each a little behind the last, so after every main beat there is a rolling *tock-tock-tock-tock* receding into the mass in every direction like a train crossing a long trestle. The vascular channels in the walls hiss. Where the channels run close to the surface you can see them through the bark, glassy and full, and they whine faintly at a pitch that sits right at the edge of hearing and makes the teeth ache.

Then there is the fauna, which is loud in its own right.

You would not call the room crowded. Most of it is empty air and bare root-floor and light. But nearly every presence in it is making noise.

---

## The Workers

Along the upper flanks of the pump root, spaced at intervals so regular they look measured, sit the **stokers**: black beetles the length of a thumb with broad flat heads like shovel-blades. Each one works a single patch of bark. It scrapes the bark with its head, back and forth, back and forth, and the bark there is worn to a smooth pale oval like the step of an old stair. What they are doing is keeping the valves clear. Something in the sap crusts at the valve-lips, a fine orange scale, and the beetles eat it. They rasp continuously. Forty or fifty of them working at once make a sound like a room full of files on iron.

The orange scale is **iron**. The fig draws it out of the marsh water. Iron-rich water seeps from under the reed-flats, and the plant pulls the metal out of solution and lays it down in its outermost bark, so that on the outside of the fig-mass the bark is plated in overlapping rust-coloured scales, each one bossed at the centre like a rivet head. Inside the pump hollow the process runs backward. The iron comes loose at the valves, and the stokers eat it, and their shells are black because they are iron too, very nearly. Press one gently and it is cold and heavy, and it rings faintly if tapped with a fingernail.

Higher, in the vault, live the **bellows-frogs**. There might be a dozen, each one pressed into its own niche in the ceiling-lattice. They are small and green-brown, and their throat sacs are enormous, translucent, and ribbed. They call on the upstroke of the pump, every one of them, in unison: a single flat blaring honk like a steam whistle, timed so exactly with the beat that it becomes part of it. *Thoom*, honk. *Thoom*, honk. No one frog leads. They simply do not miss.

On the floor there are the **runners**, small grey shrews with long scaled tails, which dash along the fibre-lines of the root mat toward the centre and back. They are always on the lines, never between them, as though the lines were rails. Their feet drum. There are perhaps six or seven in the room at any moment, far apart, each one going somewhere with total conviction and then turning around and going back.

And there are the **sparkflies**.

---

## The Current

The air in the hollow is thick and warm, warmer than outside, like the air in a laundry. It condenses everywhere. Every surface is beaded, and the beads gather into runnels that trickle down the walls in long glistening threads toward the floor, where they are absorbed and drawn back into the pump. The room sweats, and drinks its own sweat, and never dries.

The water that runs down the walls is conductive. It carries iron in it, and salts, and something else. The pump root generates a charge. Whether the charge comes from friction, or chemistry, or from the fig's own intent, there is no way to tell, but it is there. On the upstroke the hair on your arms lifts. The runnels on the walls glint, and occasionally, where two runnels almost touch, a tiny blue-white spark jumps between them with a sharp *tick*. The air smells of ozone and green sap and wet rust, the smell of a rained-on railway yard in summer.

The sparkflies live on this. They are long-legged, gnat-like, silver, and they hang in the air near the walls in loose drifts. When a spark jumps they converge on it instantly, a small silver cloud that bursts into existence and disperses again. They buzz loudly for their size, a high electrical drone. There are thousands of them, but they are so small and so spread through the space that the room still feels mostly empty, a big warm cavity with a haze of motes in it.

Deeper, in pools that collect in hollows of the root-floor near the walls, live **eels** no longer than a forearm. The pools are shallow and black, and the eels lie in them nearly motionless, curved in the shape of the hollow as though they had been cast in it. They are charged. If you put a hand near a pool your fingers buzz. The eels give off a soft continuous crackle, like a radio tuned between stations, and when the pump strikes, the crackle surges.

---

## The Stillness Underneath

For all the noise and motion, nothing in the hollow changes.

The pump beats at the same interval it has always beaten. The frogs have always honked on the upstroke. The stokers' worn ovals are worn precisely as deep as they need to be and no deeper. The worn places are not getting worse, and the scale does not build up. The runners run the same lines. The sparks jump between the same runnels at the same points. The light through the vault falls in the same coins, because the ceiling does not grow, and the fig-mass outside, for all its green enormity, does not grow either. It is the size it is. It has reached the size it is supposed to be and stopped, and every part of it now exists only to keep being exactly this.

If you came back in a hundred years you would find the same beetle on the same oval. It would not be the same beetle; they live a season or two. But its replacement would take the same place, crawling up out of some crack in the root and settling onto the vacant oval as if it had been summoned, and it would scrape in the same rhythm. The frogs replace themselves in the same niches. The eels are always the length of their pools. The weathering here is complete. Every edge that could be worn smooth has been, every surface has found its shape, and what is left is a system so balanced it has no history, only a repeating present.

It is not a dead stillness. It is the stillness of an engine that has been tuned so perfectly and run so long that it has become a kind of weather. It is loud, and warm, and wet, and alive in every fibre, and it will be exactly this loud and exactly this warm tomorrow and in a thousand years.

---

## At the Edges

Where the chamber narrows toward the walls, the ceiling comes down to meet the floor in a long curving crawlspace. Here the room becomes more intimate. The sound changes: the main beat deadens and the secondary *tock-tock-tock* of the relay roots becomes clearer, closer, a fast knocking just behind the bark by your ear. In the crawlspace the root-grain runs outward, away from the pump, and if you lie on it you can feel the water moving under you in pulses, travelling out into the rest of the fig-mass toward the thousand other hollows.

There are other rooms. You can tell. Pressed into the crawlspace with your cheek against the root, if you hold your breath between beats, you can hear another pump somewhere far off through the wood, *thoom*, and its frogs, faint as a train whistle across a valley, sounding a fraction of a second after this room's own. And behind that, fainter, another. The whole low green continent along the marsh is full of them. It is a factory floor that stretches to the horizon, with no one working it, hidden in knuckles of root. It hums and knocks and blares in the warm damp shade, forever pumping the marsh up into the leaves, which sweat it back into the air, which rains it back into the marsh.

Outside, from the reed-flats, you would hear none of it. You would hear the wind in the reeds and the water lapping at the rust-plated bark, and see a long, low, silent, impossibly green shore.
