# The Accretion Highlands

## First Sight

Start with the rock, because everything here starts with the rock.

It is dark, almost blue-black, with a faint grain like the inside of a split loaf. It is newer than anything should be. Run a hand across it and the edges are still sharp enough to catch skin: no rounding, no polish, no crust of weathering. It breaks in clean planes. Where two slabs meet, the seam is so fine a fingernail won't enter it. Nothing has worn this land yet. There hasn't been time.

The highland spreads away from you in a shallow tilt, rising so gradually that the horizon seems level until you notice the clouds are *below* you in the east. The whole plateau sits between two and three thousand meters. It is so wide that nobody who lives on it has seen it all. It makes its own weather. Air climbs its western flank from a sea no one here has visited, cools, and drops its water as a fine persistent mizzle. Most days it is barely rain, a wetness that seems to arrive from all directions at once. Then it drains off the far edge in long white falls you can hear only when the wind is right.

The light is ordinary: grey-white overcast most mornings, thinning by afternoon into a pale sun that throws long, soft shadows across the slabs. At this altitude the sky above the cloud-breaks is a deep, clean blue. When the sun comes out, the wet rock shines like pewter all the way to the edge of sight.

## How the Land Is Made

The ground is still being built.

Beneath the surface, at depths of a few meters to a few hundred, runs what the inhabitants simply call *the lattice*. Nobody living made it. It is older than the settlements, older than the records, and it works on principles the settlements' own engineers describe in careful, hedged language. It draws dissolved mineral out of the groundwater, which is everywhere here, seeping and cold, and lays it down as stone. It does this slowly, the way coral builds, but in organized planes. The highland grows upward perhaps a finger's width a year at the center and less at the margins, and in places it pushes out new shelves of rock at its edges.

So the geology is embryonic in a literal sense. Walk from the old center of the plateau toward its rim and you walk forward in time:

- **The Core.** Near the middle, the stone has had a few thousand years. Thin soils have gathered in the joints. There are mosses, and in sheltered places even low shrubs.
- **The Middle Reaches.** Farther out, the rock is bare except for lichens in grey-green rosettes.
- **The Rim.** At the edge the rock is so new it is still faintly damp from within, *weeping*. Water beads out of pores that haven't yet closed. Mornings there have a thin steam of condensation where the slightly warmer new stone meets the freezing air.

The newest ground at the rim has a texture like wet slate that has never known frost. The first winter it goes through, it cracks in a fine polygonal net. You can find shelves where you step from unfrozen, unmarked rock onto rock with one winter's crazing, then two, then ten.

There are no valleys to speak of. Water hasn't had time to cut any. It moves in sheets and shallow braids across the slabs and pools in basins where one accretion plane meets another at a slight angle. These pools are everywhere: shallow, clear to the bottom, cold enough to ache the teeth, and often skinned with ice at dawn. From height, the plateau looks spattered with thousands of small mirrors.

## The Cities

People live here, and they build.

The settlements are properly urban: organized streets, water systems, dense housing blocks, workshops, archives, markets. But there are few of them and they are far apart. Between one city and the next may be three or four days of open rock. From the edge of any town you can see nothing of any other, except on the clearest nights, when a faint smudge of light hangs on the horizon.

The cities are not built on the lattice so much as *with* it. The inhabitants learned, or inherited, the means to speak to it: to request a wall, to ask that a plane of new stone rise here rather than there. So their buildings are grown more than assembled. A house begins as a pattern pressed into the groundwater and becomes, over a decade, a structure of the same seamless blue-black stone as the land. The joins between building and ground don't exist. Streets are channels the lattice was asked not to fill.

This gives the towns a strange unity. Towers rise without footings, flowing up out of the plateau with the same grain as the rock underfoot. Windows are thin places in the stone, ground to translucence rather than cut through, glowing amber at night. Stairways are simply stone that grew in steps. Older districts are the most elaborate, because they have had time. A neighborhood of four hundred years has arcades, balconies, carved rain-gutters, and deep courtyards. A district of forty years is plain blocks with the edges still sharp.

**Infrastructure is mostly invisible.** Heat comes up through the floors: the lattice runs warm where people ask it to, and the insides of houses feel like sun-warmed stone even in the depths of winter. Water arrives purified; the lattice already sorts minerals out of it. The inhabitants have other tools too, which they rarely discuss with outsiders:

- **Sky-lines.** Long, straight filaments tethered at intervals across the open plateau. They carry messages, and something else that lets the cities feel each other's weather days before it arrives.
- **Lanterns.** Some burn with no fuel and have for centuries.
- **Record-stones.** They hold voice when touched in a particular way.

None of it hums or clanks or smokes. The technology is quiet by nature.

The cities are built to endure the plateau's rules. Their roofs are steep, shedding the mizzle. Walls are thick. Every city stands on the higher ground between pool-basins, never in them, because the lattice fills low places first and a careless builder can find their cellar quietly becoming solid stone over a few seasons.

## Weather and Seasons

The highland has its own climate cycle, driven by its size and height.

**Mist season** (the long wet months) brings continuous low cloud across the western half. Visibility drops to a few hundred meters. Moisture condenses on every surface: on stone, on hair, on the wool of animals. Travelers between cities follow the sky-lines rather than trust their eyes.

**Clearing season** comes when the prevailing wind swings north. Cold dry air pours over the plateau. The pools freeze in a night, hard and clear. The sky opens. Sound carries a long way, which here is still not far, because there is so little to hear.

Between these, fast weather crosses the plateau constantly. Squall lines move over it like dark hands. You can watch a storm cross the open rock for an hour before it reaches you, a grey curtain dragging a sheen of fresh water behind it. Then sun behind that. The highland is large enough that it is always raining somewhere on it and always clear somewhere else.

The wind is the one persistent presence. It is not loud (there are no trees for it to roar through), but it is always moving. It stirs pool-surfaces into ripples, pushes mist in slow rivers across the flats, and whistles thinly through the finer stonework of the old towns.

## Life

Despite the bare rock and the cold, there is a great deal of life, and it all depends on water.

**The pools** are the foundation. Every basin is colonized within a few seasons of forming:

- Algal films green the bottoms.
- Tiny crustaceans (fairy shrimp, water fleas, copepods) bloom by the billion in spring meltwater.
- Midges hatch from the pools in clouds so dense they darken the air above the flats on still evenings. A cloud of them is nearly silent, just a faint high whine at close range.

**Birds** follow the insects. The highland is a breeding ground for waders and small shorebirds that arrive from somewhere below the cloud line each year:

- Plovers nest in scrapes on the bare slabs.
- Phalaropes spin in the pools, stirring up food.
- Long-legged stilts pick through the shallows.
- Larks sing briefly over the flats at dawn, the only real music most mornings.

On the new rim-shelves, where the rock is still weeping and slightly warm, colonies of seabirds nest in their tens of thousands. They are dark-backed and white-breasted, packed shoulder to shoulder on ledges that did not exist twenty years ago. They fly out over the cloud sea to feed and back again in long quiet lines.

**Grazers** move across the Middle Reaches in loose herds:

- **Lichen-deer.** Stocky, woolly, and grey as the stone. They scrape rosettes of lichen from the slabs with hard lower lips and lie down in the pools' lee when the wind rises. They follow a slow migration around the plateau, tracking the lichens' growth, and the herds cross between cities like weather.
- **Pikas and marmot-like burrowers.** They live wherever the oldest rock has cracked enough to give them joints to dig into, whistling alarms that carry over the silence.

**Predators** are fewer but present:

- **Stoats.** White in winter, brown in summer, they hunt the burrowers.
- **Foxes.** They raid the shorebirds' scrapes.
- **Grey falcons.** They sit on the highest points of city towers and stoop on waders over the flats.
- **Wolves.** In the Core, where there is some soil and shelter, a population follows the deer herds. Their tracks cross the frost-crazed slabs in winter, and in quiet weather their howls come very faintly from miles off.

The cities are full of animals too. Swallows nest in the grown eaves. Pigeons roost in the stone. The deer sometimes shelter in the lee of city walls in storms, and people leave them alone. There is a tacit arrangement: the cities are warm in their stones, and the animals know it.

**Vegetation** remains sparse. There are lichens everywhere, mosses in wet seams, and in the Core, low grasses, sedges, cushion plants, and saxifrages with tiny white flowers pushed up between cracks. The inhabitants cultivate gardens in the lee of their houses: small, careful plots of carried soil, where they grow hardy greens and roots under glass-thin stone. Outside of these, green is something that appears in patches, never as a covering.

## Sound

It is quiet. Stand in the open on the plateau on an ordinary day and you hear:

- the wind moving past your ears,
- a trickle of water somewhere near your feet,
- a pool-lip lapping,
- a plover calling once, far off,
- your own breath.

That's often all. The rock makes no sound. It grows too slowly to be heard. The cities are quiet too: voices in courtyards, footsteps on stone, a bell perhaps, doors closing. No engines, no traffic, no industrial noise. The occasional great sound is weather: a squall hissing across the pools, or thunder in clearing season rolling across a hundred kilometers of flat stone.

The loudest places on the plateau are the seabird colonies on the rim, where the din is constant, and the edge-falls, where the water finally drops off the highland and roars into the cloud.

## The Shape of Living Here

The rules of the place are simple and everybody who lives here knows them:

- **The land is growing.** It will outlast you. What you ask of the lattice now, your grandchildren will live inside.
- **Water finds its level, and stone follows water.** Don't build low.
- **The edge is newest and least stable.** The rim-shelves can be treacherous, too new to have settled, sometimes hollow beneath.
- **The animals were here first and will remain.** The herds cross the cities' outer fields, and no one fences them.
- **Distance is real.** Between cities is open rock, cold water, weather, and wolves. People travel in groups, in clearing season, following the sky-lines.

Stand at the rim at dusk. Behind you is the long tilt of blue-black stone, pooled with a thousand small mirrors going violet with the sky. A distant city's windows are beginning to glow amber through thinned stone. A herd of deer is a darker grey against the grey. In front of you, the cloud sea spreads toward a horizon too far to believe in, and a line of seabirds comes home low over it, silent, wingbeats barely visible. Below your feet, too slowly to feel, the ground is still being made.
