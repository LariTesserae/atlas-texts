# The Knot

## Above, first, so you know where it is

There is a fen in the low country. It is flat, cold, and colorless in the months when anyone would think to cross it. The land here only lately stopped being lake, and the water has not finished leaving. Tussocks of sedge stand in black pools. Alder carr rises in dark islands. Every step goes a little way down before it stops.

Out toward the middle stands a hummock. It is a little higher than the rest, perhaps the height of a kneeling person, and furred over with red and green sphagnum. A few stunted alders lean off its shoulder. Frost stiffens its top most mornings. Nothing about it invites attention, and the fen has a hundred others like it.

Under this one there is a city.

## The shape of the inside

The hummock is not soil. It is a root-ball, a single tangled organism. Alder, bog myrtle, and something older and paler that has no name up top have fused into one mass, the way roots do in wet ground when they have nowhere to go but into each other. The ball is hollow. Or rather, it is kept hollow.

The hollow is small. If you could light it, which you cannot, you would see a space about the size of a large barn, roughly egg-shaped, with its long axis tilted down into the water table. Within that space, cut into the walls, hung from the ceiling, and stacked in tiers along a spiral shelf that winds down toward the water, are something over four hundred rooms.

Most of them are empty.

This is the first thing to understand about the Knot. It is built like a city, dense and layered, with streets, stairs, and quarters, but it is lived in like a scattering. The tenants are few, perhaps sixty at any time. They keep to perhaps forty rooms. The rest are kept open anyway. Each is cut and recut, swept of rootlets and named, and then left standing dark and cold, full of nothing but breath-mist and the smell of cut sap.

Why so many rooms for so few? The tenants have an answer, but it comes later.

## Material

Everything is root. The walls are root. The floors are root, worn smooth and faintly spongy, sweating a chill water that beads and runs in the grooves where feet go. The ceilings are root, knotted into ribs and vaults the tenants did not design but have learned to cut along. Where two roots cross, the wood is hard as horn. Where a single root runs alone, it is soft enough to carve with a sharpened mussel shell.

The tenants' tools are few and old in kind. They use shell blades, scrapers of knapped chert brought in from somewhere beyond the fen, and bone awls. Their one precious thing is a set of three bronze adzes, green-crusted and passed hand to hand along the spiral. They twist rootlet into cord and cord into rope. They make doors of woven withy, which are really screens against the animals, not barriers. They carry water in bladders and store food in pits lined with birch bark.

There is no stone in the Knot except what was carried in. There is no fire. Fire would kill the root, and the root is the city.

## Dark

It is dark the way the inside of a closed hand is dark.

Nothing enters from above. The sphagnum cap is too thick, and the tenants' single entrance is a flooded passage. They swim it holding their breath, through perhaps three body-lengths of black water under the hummock's edge. Nothing glows, or almost nothing. In certain old rooms at the bottom of the spiral, where the wood has been dead a long time, there is a foxfire so faint it can only be seen by someone who has been in the dark for many days. It is not light so much as a thinning of the black, a place where the eye stops insisting.

So the tenants live by other senses.

They know the city by touch. Every rope along the spiral is knotted in a code that gives the quarter, the tier, and the distance to water. They know it by air. Cold draughts come from the flooded entrance, and colder ones rise off the water at the bottom. The draughts move differently through a cut passage than a closing one, and a tenant can feel a room shrinking by the way it stops breathing. They know it by smell. Fresh cuts smell green and bitter. Old cuts smell of vinegar. A room gone wrong smells of warm sweetness, which means the root has begun to take something.

They know it most of all by the animals.

## The animals

The Knot is quiet and teeming at once, and it takes a visitor a long time to understand how both can be true.

The root-ball is the warmest, wettest, most sheltered thing for a mile in any direction. Everything that can crawl, swim, or squeeze into it has done so. The tenants' city is a clearing inside a swarm.

Every surface is alive. Pale amphipods, thousands to a hand's width, move over the sweating walls in slow sheets and graze the film that grows on the wet wood. Springtails rise in faint clouds when a wall is touched and settle again. Mites, too small to feel singly, can be felt collectively as a faint grain moving under the fingertips. Harvestmen hang from the ceilings in clusters the size of a head, thousands of legs interlaced, and shift together when the air changes.

In the water at the bottom there are eels. There are always eels, black and slow and cold, knotted together in the channels between the lowest roots. In autumn they come in from the fen in such numbers that the water becomes more eel than water. The tenants wade among them to the waist, and the eels part and close like thick cloth.

There are cave crickets with antennae longer than a forearm, which they sweep through the rooms endlessly. There are leeches, which the tenants tolerate and even value. There are shrews: blind-seeming, ferocious, tiny, and constantly hunting, the chief predators of the upper tiers. There are centipedes as long as a hand, which hunt the shrews' young. There are moths that come in somehow through the sphagnum and die in drifts in the empty rooms. There are frogs wintering in the walls, frozen nearly solid, their hearts stopped. They will thaw in the spring and walk out as if nothing happened.

All of this makes very little noise.

This is what the tenants mean when they say the city is quiet. There are no voices of any size. What there is is texture: a continuous, faint, dry hiss at the edge of hearing, like snow falling on snow. It is the sound of a million small bodies moving over wet wood. It rises when anything disturbs the air and falls when the air is still. Under it, the water drips. Under that, occasionally, comes a shrew's squeak, thin as a needle. Once in a long while there is a sound like a knuckle cracking, which is the root growing.

The tenants speak in murmurs, mostly by touch. A hand laid flat on another's back and pressed in a certain rhythm says *follow*. Pressed differently, it says *the east stair is closing*. Loud speech is considered a kind of violence, not because of any rule but because it sets the whole city moving. The hiss rises into something like a roar, harvestmen drop from the ceilings, and for a while no one can feel anything but legs.

## The rule of the root

Here is the strangeness at the center of the Knot, the thing that makes it a city and not a burrow.

The root grows toward breath.

It grows toward warmth, moisture, and carbon, toward anything that lives and exhales. It grows fast. It does not grow fast enough to see, but fast enough to feel. A doorway cut clean at the start of a month will have narrowed by a finger's width at the end of it. A passage left untended through a winter will close entirely. Rootlets thread across it first like hair, then thicken into cords and fuse. By spring the wall will be continuous and smooth, as if no one had ever been there.

A tenant who sleeps too long in one place will wake with rootlets in their clothing, in their hair, and very gently along the lines of their skin. The rootlets are harmless if pulled away early. The tenants sleep in shifts. They sleep curled, and they never sleep twice in the same room in a row. The very old and the very ill, who cannot move, are sometimes given to the root on purpose, in a lower room, with a withy screen across the door. Afterward the room is not cut open again for many years.

So the city is always being taken back, and it is always being recut. This is the tenants' work. Every day, all of them who can hold a shell blade walk the spiral and cut. They cut back the rootlets across the doorways. They clear the stairs. They reopen rooms that no one has lived in for generations. They pare the vaults. Their only true labor is to keep the hollow hollow.

This is why the city is young. Nothing in it is old. There are no worn edges and no weathered corners, because every edge has been cut within the season. The walls are sharp, bright-smelling, and raw. The whole city is always freshly made, and it is always nearly gone.

This is also why there are four hundred rooms for sixty people.

The tenants say that the root does not only grow toward breath. It grows toward *shape*. The more complex the hollow, the more rooms and stairs and turnings it has, the more the root holds back from it, as if confused. A single room, a simple cave, would close in a year no matter how hard they cut. A city stays open. The empty rooms are not empty. They are argument. Every one of them is part of the shape that keeps the whole hollow alive, and every one has to be cut, named, and walked through, even if no one ever sleeps there.

Whether this is true, no one can say. The Knot has never closed. That is the only evidence.

## Seasons

Down here, time is not light and dark. It is cold and less cold, water high and water low.

In deep winter the fen freezes above. The flooded entrance skins over with ice, and the tenants are sealed in. The animals slow. The amphipod sheets go still. The frogs stop their hearts. The hiss drops to almost nothing, and the root slows too. Winter is the one time the cutting eases. The tenants sleep longer, in heaps, sharing warmth, and the rootlets come to them more slowly.

In the thaw, water rises. The lowest tier floods. The eels come in their thousands. The root wakes, and it wakes hungry. The spring cutting is desperate, all hands, day and night, because a spring passage can close in a week. This is when rooms are lost. This is when the old knotted ropes along the spiral have to be retied, because the city has changed shape underneath them.

In summer the fen above warms, though never much, and the Knot, insulated by the whole root-ball, barely feels it. Water falls. The bottom channels drain to mud, and the tenants harvest what the eels left behind: mussels, snails, and the swollen roots of water plants. The shrews breed. The centipedes hunt the young shrews. The walls crawl with a new generation of everything.

In autumn the eels leave, all at once, over three nights, out through the flooded entrance to wherever eels go. For those three nights the water at the bottom of the city seethes silently, and the tenants sit at the edge of it in the dark, feeling the cold current of the leaving pull at their ankles.

## The tenants

They are not very different from the people of the fen. They are perhaps paler, perhaps with larger hands. They come and go. The entrance is hard but not impossible. Some are born in the Knot. Some come in from outside because of debt, grief, or curiosity. They find the swim and do not come back up.

They have no leaders, only the order of the cutting. They have no writing, only the ropes. They have one word that means both *home* and *the work of keeping home open*, and another that means both *room* and *reason*.

They keep the names of lost rooms. There is a long rope in the lowest occupied tier, heavy with knots, and every knot is a room the root has taken. Tenants run it through their hands in the dark sometimes, the way people elsewhere might look at stars.

## What you would notice, if you came

If you swam in through the black water and came up gasping into the Knot, this is what you would notice first. It is not the dark, because you expected that, and it is not the cold, because the fen was colder. It is the sense of being inside something alive and attentive. You would feel the walls sweating against your palms and the faint moving grain of countless tiny bodies. You would feel the air holding still around you and then, very slightly, beginning to lean toward your breath.

Then, from somewhere up the spiral, you would hear the soft, patient sound of a shell blade cutting root. Someone is keeping a room open for no one, so that the city will still be there tomorrow.
