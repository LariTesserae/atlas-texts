# The Cistern Where the Water Lives

## The Room

It begins with a room, and it stays a room. You never get more than that.

It is a chamber about the size of a small chapel, perhaps thirty paces long and half as wide, cut into bedrock somewhere beneath a coastline. It is sealed and entirely flooded. There is no air pocket at the ceiling and no surface to rise toward. The water fills it the way stone fills a mountain. It holds at around nine degrees, cold enough to ache in the fingers after a while but not cold enough to numb them.

The walls are basalt, and they are new. Nothing has softened them. Their facets still carry the conchoidal shine of fresh fracture, and the edges would open a palm. Where the rock meets the floor there is no rounding, no silt drift, no rim of settled time. The room looks as though it was split open this morning, though it has probably been open for a few decades. Here, a few decades is young.

The light is ordinary, and that is the first wrong thing. It is a clear, even white, like an overcast noon, and it has no source. It does not come down from above, and no lamps hang anywhere. The water itself is lit, every cubic centimeter faintly and evenly, so nothing casts a proper shadow. Objects sit in the brightness like specimens in a display case. You can see the whole room from any point in it, and you can see every edge.

There are a great many edges.

## The City

From the floor to within an arm's length of the ceiling, the chamber is packed with a city.

At first it looks like a mineral growth, some drowned geode run wild, because the towers are translucent and sharp and cluster so tightly. Then you notice the regularity. There are streets, if streets can be a finger's width across. There are arcades, stairwells spiraling inside hollow spires, bridges arching between towers at a dozen heights, and cantilevered platforms stacked like shelf fungus. Some districts are dense with thumb-high blocks laid in grids. Others rise into slender needles as tall as a person and hollow as flutes. Cisterns inside cisterns. Aqueducts in a room that is entirely water.

Nothing is spaced out. No building stands free, and no plaza is left empty for long. Every structure leans on, bridges into, or grows out of another. Looking into the city is like looking into the cross-section of a lung, or the inside of a very old coral: architecture compressed until the gaps are part of the architecture. Your hand could not enter anywhere without breaking a dozen rooms.

The material looks like glass with a grain to it, a smoky, faintly blue-green substance layered in laminations thinner than paper. In places it is clear enough to see three or four streets deep. In others it has been built so thick it turns milky, like ice with trapped breath, except there is no breath here. Hold a fragment to the light and the laminations shimmer as if something is being written in them, lines of a script too small to read.

It is still being built.

## The Noise

Water carries sound better than air, and this room is built to prove it.

The city is loud. It isn't a roar. It is a dense, layered, percussive noise that presses on the sternum and the back teeth, like standing inside a bell foundry where every bell is being cast and struck at once. Underneath everything runs a steady low drone, felt more than heard, which seems to come from the bedrock. Over it comes a constant fine ticking, millions of tiny impacts, like sleet on a greenhouse roof or a hall full of watchmakers each tapping in a different rhythm. That is the sound of accretion, the city depositing itself layer by layer.

On top of that comes the loud part. Every few seconds, somewhere in the room, a structure sings. A needle-tower finishes a course of its laminations, and the whole spire rings with a clear tone that carries through the water and sets its neighbors humming in sympathy. The tones are never quite consonant. The chamber stays full of slow, beating interference, chords that swell and sour and resolve and sour again.

Then there are the cracks. The city does not only build. It also breaks, sharply and on purpose. A span of bridge, or a whole district of blocks, will shear off with a report like a rifle shot. The pressure wave slaps the skin and rattles through every tower in the room. For a moment after a crack the ticking stops everywhere at once, as if the city is listening. Then it resumes.

You cannot hear yourself think in here, only the room.

## The Weather

The room has weather, and all of it is manufactured.

Currents run through the city, and they are too regular to be natural: narrow, laminar streams that thread the avenues at walking pace, turn precise right angles at intersections, and spiral up through the hollow towers like smoke up a chimney. You can feel them against your hand, cold ribbons with clean edges. Water a centimeter away from one lies perfectly still. The currents behave less like flows than like traffic.

Every forty minutes or so, a snowfall arrives. It starts as a faint turbidity near the ceiling and thickens into a slow, glittering descent of flakes. Each one is a flat, many-armed platelet of feedstock, the raw laminate before it is laid. The flakes fall into the city, and the currents sort them. They are drawn down specific streets, carried up specific towers, and delivered to the growing edges. For a few minutes the ticking rises to a hiss, and the laminations visibly thicken. You can watch a balcony extend by a hair's breadth. You can watch a stair acquire a step.

After the snow comes the calving: a flurry of cracks, whole sections released. The fallen pieces do not settle. They dissolve over an hour or two, clouding the water with a pale bloom that drifts toward vents in the floor. The vents are cut so cleanly into the basalt that they look drawn with a ruler. The bloom goes down, and somewhere below it is reworked into snow. The city eats itself, grows, and eats itself again, and nothing is wasted.

This is why nothing here is worn. Nothing lasts long enough to erode. The sharpest, youngest thing in the room is always the whole room.

## The Rules

It takes a long time of watching, longer than is comfortable in that cold, before you understand who the city is for.

There are no animals. There never have been. No fish drift through, no worms burrow into the floor, and no crust of bacteria dulls the glass. The water is sterile past the point of cleanliness, empty in a way that feels deliberate, like a guest room made up and kept untouched. The one exception to that emptiness is described below.

The streets are not scaled for bodies. They are scaled for flow. Once you stop looking for doors and look at the currents instead, it becomes obvious. The avenues are shaped exactly to the ribbons of moving water that travel them. The bridges carry currents over other currents. The stairwells inside the towers are the paths the vertical streams take. The arcades slow the flow to a loiter, and the plazas let it pool and turn in slow eddies before it moves on. When the city calves a district, the currents that ran through it do not stop. They reroute, and within a snowfall new streets grow to fit them.

The water lives here. The city is built for it, around it, in answer to it, the way a nest is shaped by the bird that sits in it. The currents do not obey the architecture. The architecture obeys the currents, and the currents seem to have preferences. Some streams take the same route cycle after cycle, and their avenues grow ornate, thick with carved-looking laminations and flanked by the tallest singing spires. Others wander, and the city chases them, laying tentative temporary streets that are calved almost as soon as they are finished.

Whatever machinery does this is not visible. There are no arms, no nozzles, no humming boxes. The engineering seems to live in the water's own structure, in its temperature gradients, in the light it gives off, in the script written through every lamination. It may be one mechanism or a billion. It may be the water, taught to build. Physics as you know it describes the pieces: deposition, resonance, flow. It does not describe why a stream of water should linger in a plaza, turn twice, and leave by a different road, or why the city should mark that choice with a new spire.

## The Garden

There is one plant.

Near the middle of the room, a little off the chamber's long axis, there is a clearing, the only real one. It is a round plaza perhaps the width of two hands, floored in clear glass. From a hairline crack in the basalt beneath the glass, through a hole the city has left open and never closed, grows a single strand of eelgrass.

It is pale, almost colorless, a few blades as long as a forearm, green only at the tips. How it got here is impossible to say. A seed may have come in through the vents before they were cut, or been carried in the feedstock, or just happened, the one uncalculated event in a room full of calculation. It is sparse and not especially healthy, and it is the only living thing in the place.

The city has not removed it. The city has done something stranger. The streets bend around the plaza in concentric rings, like ripples. The currents that pass through slow almost to stillness, and they move the blades with great gentleness, first one way and then the other. The towers nearest the clearing are the oldest in the room, spared through dozens of calvings, and their laminations are thicker and more densely written than anywhere else. When the snow falls, none lands in the plaza. The currents part the snowfall around it like a hand shielding a candle.

The loudest spire in the room stands at the plaza's edge. Every time it sings, the eelgrass trembles.

## What It Is

Maybe it is a machine that was left running. Maybe it was built by someone who wanted to know what water would do if given a city, or a monument to something, or an experiment still generating data for a reader who never returns. It might be a seed, a pocket of something enormous, growing slowly inside the rock the way the eelgrass grows inside the glass.

From inside the room, none of those questions carries much weight. What there is: cold water lit from within, a city packed into a chapel-sized cavity, sharp and ringing and always new. There is a population of currents moving through avenues built for them, and a weather of glass snow and breaking bridges. And in the middle of it all is a pale strand of grass, the only thing here that was not made, which the city seems to have decided to keep.

The noise never stops. The building never finishes. Somewhere in the lamination, too small to read, the city is writing down everything the water does.
