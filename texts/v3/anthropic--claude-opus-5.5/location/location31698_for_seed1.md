# The Floor of the Unbuilt Works

## I. The Ground

Start with the ground, because it is the oldest thing here and the least interested in you.

It is a basin so old it has nearly stopped being a basin. Whatever mountains once rimmed it were worn down so long ago that their roots now show as faint concentric ridges in the plain, like the growth rings of a tree felled at ground level. The ridges are knee-high at most. Between them the land is flat in a way that feels deliberate, as if planed.

The stone underneath is a dark schist, folded and refolded, but you rarely see it clean. Mostly you see what it has become: a fine gray-black slurry, cold as dishwater, that everything else in the basin rests on, wades through, or is slowly sinking into. Where the schist does break the surface it has been polished by water, by wind, and by the passage of innumerable bodies until it has the dull sheen of a stair tread in an old public building. It weeps constantly. Every exposed rock carries a skin of moving water that beads at the lowest point and drips, and the drip lines have cut grooves into the stone. Some of the grooves are older than any living lineage on this world.

The water never quite pools and never quite drains. It sits just under the surface everywhere, so that every footprint fills from below within a breath. In the low places it stands in broad shallow sheets a finger deep, dimpled by drips, skinned with a gray mineral film that wrinkles when anything moves. There are no plants. There is only the film: a slow, simple, mat-forming life, more crust than organism, colored the gray-green of oxidized copper. It grows on stone, on water, and on the backs of animals that stand still too long. It is the only thing here that is quiet.

## II. The Air

It is cold, not with the sharp drama of ice but with the patient cold of a cellar, or a foundry floor on a winter night after the fires are banked. The cold never lifts. Your breath condenses and so does everything else's, and there is a great deal of everything else. The basin breathes, and the air above it is thick with vapor that hangs at head height in slow brown-gray layers. Fog is the wrong word, since fog is white. This is closer to steam that has cooled and given up and kept a faint smell of iron and scorched hair.

The light is dim and comes from nowhere in particular. Somewhere above the vapor there is a sky, and possibly a sun, but it arrives as a diffuse pewter glow with no direction. Shadows are soft and hard to read. The real light, the kind you can navigate by, comes from the animals, and I will get to that.

It is also soot. It falls constantly and finely, a black dust that settles into the mineral film and gives the basin its dark stripes. There are no fires to make it. There are no chimneys. It falls anyway.

## III. The Sound

The first thing a visitor would notice, before the cold and before the smell, is the noise. It is the noise of a works.

There is a deep, continuous, multi-layered thrum under everything, felt in the sternum as much as heard. Over it comes a rhythmic percussion, thousands of irregular strikes that resolve at distance into something almost like a beat. Over that come hisses, long exhalations, a grinding like millstones, the crack and spit of electrical discharge, and occasionally, very far off, a whistle. It is one long falling note, and it silences everything.

Almost all of this is made by animals.

## IV. The Workforce

The basin is packed. There is no other word for it. From any vantage (and there are few, since the ground is so flat), the plain is covered in bodies to the limit of sight: shouldered together, stacked, overlapping, standing in the water sheets and on each other. Walking here means pushing through them. They are indifferent to you the way crowds at a shift change are indifferent. They are going somewhere and you are in the way.

**The bellows-backs** are the thrum. They are the most numerous large animals, low and broad as overturned boats, with dull leathery hides crusted in mineral film. Along each flank runs a row of pleated chambers that fill and empty in a slow cycle, drawing in the cold wet air and expelling it warm. They are, as far as anyone can tell, heating the basin. Not efficiently, and not enough, but they are doing it. They stand in herds of thousands, and their breathing falls into sync across enormous distances, so the whole plain inhales and exhales together. The vapor layers rise and fall with it. When they breathe out, the soot swirls.

**The strikers** are the percussion. They are knee-height armored things, somewhere between a crab and a heron, standing on jointed legs in the water sheets. Each carries a single massive forelimb ending in a blunt knob of mineralized chitin. They find exposed schist and hammer it with a slow, patient, absolutely regular stroke. What they are doing is unclear. They do not eat the stone. They seem to be *working* it, and over generations they have flattened every outcrop in the basin into those polished treads. Each striker keeps its own tempo, but neighbors drift into agreement, and whole districts of the plain will lock into a single beat for hours before falling apart into clatter again.

**The smelt-snails** are the reason the water runs orange. They are slow, heavy, spiral-shelled grazers the size of a clenched fist, and they carpet the wettest ground so densely that you walk on their shells, which crunch and bear your weight without complaint. They eat pyrite and iron-bearing silt, and internally, at no temperature anyone could measure, they reduce it. What comes out of them is iron: tiny gray pellets of bloom, nearly pure. The runoff below any smelt-snail colony is rust-red, and the colonies are everywhere.

**The arc-eels** are the light. They live in the water sheets and in the saturated slurry just beneath, long and ribbon-flat and nearly transparent. They generate current, as eels do, but they discharge it into the air, in blue-white arcs that jump between individuals across the surface of the water. A school of arc-eels crossing a flat produces a travelling web of lightning a hand's breadth tall, hissing and spitting, lighting the vapor from beneath in flickering violet. This is how the basin is lit at ground level: not by a sun, but by the constant stuttering welding-glow of the eels. They are hunted by nothing and they are short-tempered. Crossing a flat means timing your steps between discharges.

**The tallymen** are harder to describe. They are small, dark, quick, and bipedal only in the loosest sense, with long fingers and heads that are mostly eye. They move through the crowds in ones and twos, touching things. They touch a bellows-back, a striker, a snail. They seem to be *counting*. They do not eat in any way anyone has observed. When a striker drops out of the beat, a tallyman is usually nearby within minutes, and the striker usually does not strike again.

There are others. There are carrion flocks of gray, featherless, skittering scavengers that pick over the dead in shifts. There are slurry-worms thick as a man's thigh, so abundant that the mud visibly heaves. There are clouds of midge-like things that swarm the warm exhalations of the bellows-backs and are eaten, in the same exhalation, by the bellows-backs. There are predators: long-legged, heavy-jawed stalkers that wade the flats at the edge of visibility and take snails, strikers, and occasionally the old and slow among the bellows-backs. Nothing here is rare. Everything here is plentiful, pressed together, eating and being eaten, breathing and hammering and sparking, in the cold and the wet and the dark.

## V. The Product

Dig down anywhere and you find what the basin makes.

Below the slurry, the sediment is layered, and the layers are iron. They are mostly smelt-snail bloom, compressed into bands of dark metal-rich stone. In some bands, though, the iron is not in pellets. It is in *bolts*. They are small, uniform, hexagonal-headed, threaded, perhaps the length of a finger joint, and they are identical to one another to a degree no natural process should allow. The deepest layers are so old that erosion and compression have ground the threads smooth and rounded the heads into lozenges, but the shape is still unmistakable. There are bands of bolts hundreds of feet thick. There is no machine anywhere in the basin that could have made them. There never was.

Newer bolts turn up too. They are found at the surface, rusting in the water sheets, usually near where a tallyman has recently been. No one has seen one appear.

## VI. The Rules

The basin is not entirely, or not reliably, real. The breakdown is not chaotic. It has a logic, and the logic is the logic of a factory that was never built.

**There are shadows of structures that do not exist.** At certain angles of the directionless light, long straight shadows fall across the plain: the shadows of chimneys, of gantries, of the roofline of a shed half a mile long. There is nothing casting them. Animals route around them. Bellows-backs will not stand in them. Strikers inside a shadow keep perfect time.

**Sounds arrive before their causes.** You hear a striker's hammer ring and then, a fraction of a second later, watch it fall. You hear an arc-eel's crack and then see the flash. The lag is small and it varies. Near the chimney-shadows it grows long enough that you can hear the whole next minute of a district before it happens, like standing outside a building listening to the work inside.

**Distance is counted, not measured.** Walking across the plain, you cross a certain water sheet with a certain pattern of drip-dimples and a certain half-sunk bellows-back skeleton. Then you cross it again. It is not identical: the snails have moved, the eels are elsewhere. But it is the same sheet. The basin repeats, the way an assembly line repeats, bay after bay. How many bays there are depends on something no one has figured out. Travellers who try to walk out in a straight line report crossing the same stretch dozens of times, then suddenly reaching the rim ridges, as if a tally had been completed.

**The whistle is real.** Several times in what might be a day, from no identifiable direction, comes the long falling note. At the sound, everything in the basin stops. The bellows-backs hold their breath. The strikers lift their hammers and freeze. The arc-eels go dark. The snails pull in. For the length of a long exhale, the only sounds are the drips and the settling soot. Then every animal in the basin turns a quarter-rotation, the same direction, all at once, and resumes. Herds that were facing the chimney-shadows now face away. The beat of the strikers comes back at a new tempo. A shift has changed. Something has been handed over. It happens every time.

**Nothing here was made, but everything here is working.** That is the rule under the rules. The basin carries all the effects of industry: the noise, the heat, the soot, the rhythm, the counting, the product, the shifts, the workforce. It has none of the cause. There are no walls, no machines, no owners, no plans. There is only the ancient, eroded, cold wet floor of a works that has either not been built yet or was unbuilt so thoroughly that only the labor remains, persisting in flesh the way a ghost persists in a hallway.

## VII. The Ecology of It

It holds together. That is the unsettling part. The system functions.

The snails make iron from silt. The strikers' pounding breaks schist into fresh silt for the snails. The bellows-backs' warm exhalations keep the water from freezing, and the midges breed in that warmth, and the bellows-backs eat the midges. The arc-eels' discharges fix something out of the air into the water, possibly nitrogen, possibly something without a name, and the mineral film feeds on it. The film grows on everything and is grazed by snails and worms. The worms are eaten by the stalkers, the stalkers by nothing, and all of them eventually by the scavenger flocks, whose droppings return to the slurry. The tallymen remove whatever falls out of rhythm. Soot falls, is folded into sediment, and becomes the dark stripes in the iron bands.

The bolts pile up. Nothing uses them. They are simply the output, accumulated over geological time into strata, the way a factory that runs long enough fills its yards.

## VIII. Standing There

If you could stand still on one of the polished treads of schist, with a striker hammering beside you and a school of arc-eels crackling violet across the water sheet at your feet, the bellows-backs breathing like a single enormous engine to the edge of the fog, the soot falling on your sleeves, the cold coming up through your boots, and a long chimney-shadow lying across the plain from nothing, you might hear, a few seconds ahead of time, the sound of your own next footstep splashing into the slurry.

Then, somewhere, the whistle. Everything holds its breath. The water drips. The whole living plain turns, and you turn with it, because by now it would feel strange not to.
