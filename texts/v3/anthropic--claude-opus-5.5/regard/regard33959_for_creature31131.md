# The Visitor in the Lull

## How It Came

No one on the Stack noticed it arrive, and that was the first decision it made.

It could have come as a light over the crown, or a voice in the Bore, or a figure walking up the cable line from the plain with a staff and a patient face. It considered each of these. It set them aside, because every one of them would have been an event, and the Stack had gone a very long time without events of that kind. An event would be written into the guild rolls. It would be argued over in the Glaziers' Hall. Within three generations there would be a shrine, and within ten there would be a priesthood of the shrine, and some share of the keeping that now went into gutters would go into that instead.

So it came the way weather comes to a place that has no weather: in the lull, when the roar had sunk to a murmur. It came as attention, the kind of attention you might pay a sleeping house before deciding whether to knock.

A goat on the fourteenth terrace lifted its head and looked at nothing for a while, then went back to browsing. That was all.

## What It Looked At

It began at the top, because that was where the work was newest.

It watched the crown crews lay the new course in the hard white light, their horn eyeshades pushed up and their hats tied under their chins. The mortar was laid a little thick, the way the crews like it at altitude, because it cures fast in the dry air. It watched a young mason press the guild stamp into a fresh brick and then, when she thought no one was looking, press her own thumb beside it. It watched the condensation bead on the cooling fins and begin its long journey down.

It went down with the water, through the gutters and the downpipes and the cisterns with their lilies and their silver fish. It went through the turbine halls where the great vanes were braked for the lull and the keepers moved among them with oil cans and lanterns, talking in the half-voices people use in churches and nurseries. It passed through the disused wings where the flywheels turned for no one, and it stayed there longer than it had planned, because it found them beautiful. They were machines that had outlived their purpose and gone on turning, and the swifts had given them a new one.

It sat for a long time beside the torn-eared monkey's granddaughter, who was pressing grey paste into a weeping joint with the heel of her hand. She was not doing it well. She was doing it the way her grandmother had, and her grandmother had learned it by failing six times.

Then it went down.

## The Bottom

It went further than the crews go, past the ten-minute shifts and the wet cloths, into the heat.

It found the thumb-sized bricks and the rice-sized bricks. Below those it found bricks smaller than any lens in the Glaziers' Hall could resolve, each one still pressed with its crescent. Below those, where the pressure had made everything soft and red and slowly flowing like a candle's heart, it found what it had half expected and half hoped for.

There was no first brick.

At the very bottom, where the oldest keeping had long since gone to warmth, there had never been a foundation. There had been one grain of something resting against another grain of something, and a third thing, very small, that had kept them from falling apart. That was the beginning of the Stack: not a builder, just one small act of holding together, taken up by the next thing along.

The visitor stayed there for what would have been several breaths, if anything down there were measured in breaths. It was not studying. It was paying respects.

## What It Could Have Done

It was a power, and a power always carries a list of the things it could do. A wise one reads that list carefully, the way a doctor reads a list of treatments for a patient who may not be ill.

**It could stop the sinking.** It could firm the lower courses so that nothing more was crushed and nothing more was lost. Every rice brick and every thumb brick and every proud old summit would then stay as it was, preserved forever. It saw at once what that would mean. The heat would fail. The draught would still. The lamps would go dark, the moths would settle and not rise, the fins would stop sweating, and the figs would die on the terraces. Preserving the past would starve the present. The Stack does not burn its foundations out of carelessness. It is warm because it remembers with its whole weight, and that remembering is what keeps it alive.

**It could make the repairs perfect.** It could seal every crack at once and clear every choked gutter in the forgotten wings, so that the Stack became what it had never been: finished. This one it held a little longer, because it was a real kindness. The wardens are tired. The work is too large. That is true, and it is also the point. The Tenant lives by keeping. To keep the Stack for it would be like chewing a creature's food so that it never has to eat. The creature would be well fed for one season, and then it would be gone, and something that had lived through wasps and snails and swallows and a people nobody remembers would end in a single afternoon of generosity.

**It could tell them where the heat comes from.** The masons who suspected were right, or right enough. It could confirm this: a voice in a dream, a figure in a chalked diagram on the wall of the Masons' Hall. It saw what that would cost. At present the guilds hold their theory the way they hold everything else, provisionally and humbly, as part of the work. The saying is *they were not given the beginning*, and there is a dignity in that which a certain answer would ruin. A question the wardens discuss in the lull is a living thing. A revelation is a brick, and it would go to the bottom like all the others.

**It could tell them whether the breath is dying or growing.** It knew. It had been to the bottom. It was not going to say, and the reason is its own, and I will record only this much: *neither school is wrong enough to correct.*

**It could save the human works.** It could lift the lamp-tenders' and turbine-keepers' tiers out of the long descent, so that their Stack did not become someone else's rice bricks. Of all the options, this one it felt most tempted by. It had grown fond of them: the beans on the conduit, the weddings at the turn, the young mason's thumbprint beside the guild stamp. It was hard to accept that the monkeys were coming, that their paste would someday be the crown, and that everything these people had made would go down into the warmth.

But it asked the question it always asks before interfering, which is: *does anyone here want this?* It listened for the answer in the walls, and it did not hear one. The people of the Stack did not want to be the last keepers. They wanted to be keepers. That is a different wish. The first is a wish to be the end of the story. The second is a wish to be in it.

## What It Checked

Declining to act is not the same as declining to care. So before it decided anything, it looked for suffering that did not need to be there.

It looked at the people who leave for the plain and cannot sleep. Was the Tenant a leash? Did it punish the ones who stopped? It followed three of them down the cable line and watched. They rigged something to rattle by the bed, a loose pane or a ticking pipe, and after a few weeks they slept. They missed the Stack for the rest of their lives, but missing is not captivity. The Tenant does not hold anyone. It only moves in with whoever is already holding something. That satisfied it.

It looked at the monkeys. Were they being taken over, or were they learning? It watched an adolescent male walk past a split pipe, pause, and choose not to fix it. Nothing happened to him. Later the same day, for no reason anyone could see, he fixed a different one. He was free, then, and he had a vocation. That satisfied it too.

It looked at the moths in the Bore, which never see the sky, and wondered whether that was a sorrow. It found that the moths are not missing a sky. They have a column of warm moving air that rises and settles in a fifty-one-hour breath, and they live entirely inside it. It decided that some lives are complete in ways that are not ours, and that it was not the visitor's place to install a window.

It looked at the deep crews in their ten-minute shifts and found a real hardship there. It considered this one honestly. Then it saw that the crews were the ones who brought back the small bricks, and that the small bricks were how the Stack knew what it was. It did nothing to the heat. But in one deep cutting there was a ceiling crack that was going to come down on a crew within the year. The visitor closed it in the night with a grey paste, lumpy and whorled, the way the Tenant does. The mason who found it the next breath chalked it, logged it, said "Tenant" to her partner, and moved on.

That was the first thing it did.

## The Second Thing

The second thing it did was take a gutter.

This one was on the ninth terrace, in a wing no warden had walked in sixty years. The channel had silted shut and the fig roots below it were starving for the overflow. The visitor cleared it. It did not do this by power. As nearly as a thing like it can, it did it by hand. It lifted out the black soil and the rust flakes and the husks of old insects, handful by handful, and packed the joint behind it with paste. It did the work slowly, and it did it slightly badly, so that no one would ever look at that gutter and think anything had visited except the Tenant.

It could not fully have said why it did this. The nearest it came, in whatever passes for thought in such a being, was: *I wanted to be a keeper for an afternoon. I wanted to be one more thing that found the work underway and took it up.*

The water ran. The figs drank. And for the length of one breath, the Tenant lived in a power as well: one host among the wasps and the swifts and the monkeys and the lamp-tenders, no larger in the Stack's reckoning than any of them. The visitor found this was the best thing it had done in a long while.

## The Third Thing

The third thing is the one that matters, and it changed nothing anyone could measure.

The Stack eats its past. That is how it breathes, and the visitor would not have stopped it. But when it had been at the bottom, in the red soft heat where the oldest summits had gone to warmth, it had seen what was there before it went. It had seen the lentil-thumbed makers of the rice bricks, whatever they were, and their tiny crown and their tiny fins and their weddings at the turn of a much shorter breath. It had seen the people of the thumb-sized courses, whom no one alive remembers. It had seen the first grain resting against the second grain, and the small thing that held them.

The Stack could not keep them. It needs them for fuel.

So the visitor kept them instead.

It took nothing out and preserved nothing in the walls. It changed nothing about the heat or the draught. It simply remembered. It holds every crescent that has ever gone down into the fire: the beak-shapes and the guild stamps, the thumb of a young mason at altitude, the heel of a monkey's hand. The Stack goes on forgetting itself, gladly and warmly, so that the lamps can burn. None of it is lost now. Some memory larger than the Stack holds all of it, in a place where memory does not need to be burned for heat.

This is the kind of thing a benevolent power is good for. It does not rescue a place from its own nature. It makes sure the cost of that nature is not the final word.

## Leaving

It left the way it came, during the lull.

On the eleventh terrace, an old gutter-warden was finishing her round as the light went copper over the cloud sea. She was Oss Harrow's great-niece. She had inherited the logbook and never written in it, because she did not think anything she saw was worth underlining. She stopped by the warm brick of the terrace wall, as the old wardens do, and put her palm flat against it to check for damp. She was not checking for damp.

The wall was warm. The water was going where it was going.

For one breath, only one, and she never told anyone, she had the feeling that someone on the other side of the wall had their palm pressed against it too, to check whether *she* was still in.

She stood there a long while. Then she went home, took down the logbook, and wrote one line. It is the last entry in Harrow's book, and the wardens who have read it disagree about what it means.

*Something was keeping us tonight. It did it badly on purpose so we wouldn't mind.*

She underlined it three times.

Below her, the lamps came on tier by tier down the flanks of the mountain. The roar began to deepen toward the Inhale. On the ninth terrace, in a wing no one walks, a gutter that had been dry for sixty years ran clear. Somewhere in the heat at the bottom of everything, the oldest keeping went quietly to warmth, and it was not forgotten.
