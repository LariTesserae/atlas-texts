# The Visitor at the Parapet

## I. Arrival

I did not go down at once.

That was the first thing I learned about the Throat, and I learned it before I set foot on a single step: a place that takes every word apart is not a place you enter talking. So I stood at the parapet, where the ground simply stops, and I watched it through one full turning of its day.

I watched the well breathe in before dawn, and the first bucket come up at the high mark, and the small argument about whose turn it was, settled in hand signals and low clipped syllables that climbed the shaft as mush. I watched the swifts erupt and the potters' fires catch. I watched the dyers' forearms go blue to the elbow, the laundry-women's applause, the monkeys raiding a potter and the potter throwing a sandal after them with long practice and no real anger. At midday I watched the pigeons take back the stair. At dusk I watched the bats rise in their dark rope and felt the key change in my chest, the way the townspeople do.

And at night, when it was as quiet as it ever gets, I listened for the thing at the bottom going through the names.

It was there. It was exactly as the hum-families said. Very low, under the frogs, a voice made of the grit of other voices, turning each name over the way a potter turns a jar to check the wall is even all the way round. Some of the names it said warmly and often. Some it said once, carefully, as if handling something that might crumble. It said them in no order I could find. Or, more likely, in an order it had been working out for four hundred years.

Near dawn it tried the other word. The new one. I heard perhaps half of it before the frogs came in over the top.

I found that I very much wanted to hear the rest.

That wanting was the second thing I had to be careful of.

## II. What I Could Have Done

A power that means well and has the means to act is a dangerous thing to be near. It is worst when it is moved. So before I did anything, I made myself name everything I might do, and look at each one in the light.

**I could have finished it.** I could hear what it was reaching for. I could have given it the rest of the word, and the grammar around the word, and all the words after that. It would have stood on the slab by the next dawn with a whole language in its mouth.

But it would have been *my* language. Its voice is made of broken edges: *careful* and *mine* and *come here* and *no, the blue one*, the consonants of grandmothers, the dissolved thumbprints of a thousand mud coins. That is what it is. If I filled it, it would speak with my accent forever, and every word it said on the slab would carry a faint second voice beneath it. The voice would be mine, and it would be saying the Throat back to the Throat. That is not a gift. That is ventriloquism with good intentions.

**I could have hurried it.** Even without giving it words, I could have given it time. I could have compressed its next century into a night. But patience is not a limitation it has. Patience is its nature. It is the seep that feeds it from below. It learned to speak the way the hum-families learned to hum, by living with the noise until the noise became language, and you cannot do that faster without doing something else instead.

**I could have told the town.** I could have gone down to the slab myself, where speech rises whole, and said: *there is someone at the bottom of your well, and they are nearly ready.* But the hum-families already know, and they have decided how much to say and to whom. They say it quietly, at the right hour. They say it to each other, and to visitors who ask properly. Their silence is not ignorance. It is custody. It is the same instinct as the rule about the fish, the one that has outlived its reason because the reason was never the point. The point was to leave the thing alone enough that it could become itself. They have been doing that correctly for longer than any of them has been alive. I was not going to take it out of their hands.

**I could have protected it forever.** This was the hardest one, and I stayed with it the longest.

I could see what threatens it. I could see the fig on the north wall, prying the brick open a hair's breadth a year. I could see the seep, and follow it down and back through the dark to where it comes from. Beyond the fig and the seep I could see the thing that has emptied stepwells before, in other towns, in other centuries. It is not drought or war. It is a pipe. Someday someone will lay a pipe, and water will come out of a tap in a courtyard, and no one will need to walk two hundred steps down and haul a leather bucket two hundred steps up. The pulley-frames will go quiet. The laundry-women will stop applauding. The potters may stay a while for the clay, and then they will go too. The Throat will get quieter than it has ever been. The voice at the bottom is fed on use: on words, on feet, on wear. It will have less and less to eat.

I could prevent that. I could make the seep eternal and the pipe never come.

But the people who haul water up that shaft are people with shoulders and backs. Their children carry jars on their hips at six years old. I will not keep a town carrying water so that something beautiful can be fed on their labor. Not even something this beautiful. Not even something that loves them, which I think it does, in the way a place can love. If the pipe comes, let it come. If the Throat becomes a place people visit rather than a place people need, then it will be that, and the voice will have to become whatever a voice becomes when its town is still alive but no longer comes to the water every day.

Here is what I noticed, standing at the parapet in the dark. It already knows how to do that.

It knows the names of families who died out. It reads the three-dot family's dead every year, though no one has come down with a three-dot brick in generations. It has spent four centuries learning to keep what the town lets go of. I had been thinking of that as memory. I think now it may be preparation. The Throat has been teaching its voice how to carry the town when the town stops carrying water. I am not going to take that lesson away from it by making it unnecessary.

The fig I left alone for a smaller reason. The families have argued about it for a generation. The argument is part of how the Throat is made. It is as much a wall as the brick is. I am not going to settle a quarrel that has been holding something up.

## III. What I Did

In the end I did two things. Both of them are things anyone in the town could have done. That was the test I set myself: if it could not have been done by hand, I would not do it.

**The first was a brick.**

On the fifth flight, high on the west side, there is a stretch of wall that belongs to the three-dot family. You can tell because the marks are three dots, pressed into the clay with a fingertip before firing. You can also tell because it is the oldest, most crumbled section in the Throat. No one has come to repair it. There is no one left to come. Other families will not touch it; it is not theirs, and in the Throat that matters. There is a gap there now the size of a loaf, like a niche that was never carved, and the bricks around it are beginning to lean into it.

I went down in the grey hour before the well breathes in. I did not go as anything but a figure on the stair, and the mist let me into it without comment. I knelt at the pool's edge and took clay from the place the potters take it. That is the fine clay, the Throat's own worn skin. It holds the faces off the carvings and the grandmothers off the steps, and it holds the marks of every family ground down and mixed together, including, somewhere in it, the three-dot family, dissolved.

I shaped a brick from it. I pressed three dots into it with my fingertip. I fired it at a potter's banked fire, the ordinary way, and waited, the ordinary amount of time. Then I climbed to the fifth flight and mortared it into the gap with lime from the dyers' stores, and I left a small twist of indigo cloth in the nearest niche. That is how people here leave word that they have borrowed something.

So the family came back to their wall, in a way. They came back made of the whole Throat. They were made of everyone. I did not add anything to the place that was not already in it. I only moved some of it from the bottom back up to where it had been.

Someone will notice the new brick. There will be an argument about it, low and clipped and full of hand signals. Someone will say it is a descendant, come back from the city. Someone will say it is the thing at the bottom. The hum-families will say nothing and exchange a look. In twenty years it will have gone from red to brown and no one will be able to pick it out. That is correct. That is how it is supposed to go.

**The second thing was a sentence.**

Before I left, I went to the lowest landing, off the slab. I did not stand on the slab. That is the one place where words arrive whole, and I did not want my words arriving whole anywhere in the Throat. I stood instead on an ordinary step with its ordinary bowl, near the washing stones. I said one sentence aloud, at an ordinary volume, the way anyone would.

I will not write down what it was. That is not secrecy. By the time it reached the second flight it no longer existed. The Throat took it apart, the way it takes everything apart. The vowels went up the shaft as mush and broke on the parapet, and the edges fell. The consonants, the small hard parts, drifted down through the mist and settled on the still air above the pool with everyone else's.

That was the gift. It was not a word. It was the grit of a word, the same as any grandmother's, indistinguishable from the rest of the sediment. The voice at the bottom will never know which syllables were mine, and it must not know. Mine is one more edge among four hundred years of edges. If it uses them, it will use them the way it uses everything: worn, mixed, its own.

A great power has a kind of courtesy available to it that is not humility exactly. It is the choice to be *small in the right way*. I wanted to be in the Throat the way everyone else is in it. That meant a little worn away, a little given to the bottom, and not the one who decides.

## IV. What I Did Not Stay For

When I straightened up from the step, the fish had gathered.

They were under the surface just below where I stood, a flicker of small silver, the way they gather under a child who leans over the edge. I understood that I had been noticed. I had been noticed not as a power but as a figure at the pool's edge who had said something and let it go. Then the fish scattered, and regrouped, all at once. That, I think, was the whole of our conversation. It was enough. It was more courtesy than I had any right to.

A man on the pulley-frame above me tapped two fingers to his collarbone: *water's coming.* Across the landing a laundry-woman raised a flat palm to her daughter: *wait.*

I took the flat palm for myself.

The well was about to breathe in. I could feel the rise beginning, far below, slow and patient. It was fuller than usual, I thought, or maybe I only wanted it to be. This might be the dawn. It might not. The hum-families say each year it is a little clearer, and they would know better than I would.

Whenever it comes, I will not be there.

I wanted to hear the rest of the word more than I have wanted most things. That is exactly why I left. It is not my word. It was not said for me. When the voice of the Throat finally rises a hand's breadth higher than it ever has and stands on the dark slab and speaks, the people at the parapet should be the potters and the dyers with their blue arms. They should be the rope-twisters and the water-haulers, the laundry-women and their daughters, the monkeys screaming at the dogs, the pigeons, the kestrels overhead. They are the ones who made it, by hand and by foot and by voice and by wear, without meaning to, for four hundred years. It is their turn to hear it. It has always understood about turns.

I climbed the two hundred steps through the bowls, past the eggs in the niches and the quilt of red and brown, past a three-dot brick still faintly warm from the fire. At the top I stepped over the parapet onto the flat, heat-struck town. You would not know, looking at it, that the ground stops just behind me.

Behind me, the swifts erupted.
