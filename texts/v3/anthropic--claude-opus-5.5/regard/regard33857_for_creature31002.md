# The Visitor at the Warm Throat

## How It Came

It came down the throat the ordinary way.

It could have arrived in any number of ways: as a change in the light, as a thought in the rock, all at once and everywhere in the room. It considered these, briefly, and put them aside. A thing that breathes once a day does not deserve to be startled. So it went to the fig tree on the hillside in the late morning, when the scrub was loudest, and laid its hand on the bark for a while, the way you might knock before entering a house where someone old is sleeping. Then it climbed down between the two slabs of limestone, through the basket of roots, with the warm breath rising around it. It let its hands get wet with condensation that was the temperature of skin. It took its time on the two bends. It let its eyes need a long while.

When it reached the room it did not speak. It stood on the soft floor and was quiet, and it let itself be what it was, which was very large and very attentive, held down to the size of someone standing at the bottom of a cave.

## What It Saw

It saw everything the room contained, and more than a visitor would.

It saw the heat rising from the crack, and it followed the heat down past where the crack narrowed to nothing. It went through older and hotter rock, a long way, until it found the rest of what the room is the mouth of. It did not go all the way. It went far enough to know the thing was there, and was alive, and was asleep in the way very large slow things are asleep, which is not the opposite of awake so much as a different pace of it. Then it came back up. You don't climb down someone's windpipe, even if you can.

It saw that the thing was well.

This was the first and most important thing it learned, and it made itself sit with it before thinking anything else. The room is not wounded. It is not dying. It is not lonely in any way that aches. The fig is fed. The fish are pink with warm blood. The bats shout all night in perfect health. The frogs are content. The hands are held. Nothing here is asking to be rescued. The power had seen a great many places that were asking, and it knew the difference. It knew that the most common mistake of benevolent things is to arrive somewhere already whole and find a way to be needed.

So it made itself a promise before it did anything at all: *first, do not make this place about me.*

## What It Did Not Do

It thought about the gifts it could give. It thought about each one carefully, the way you turn over a stone before you set it in a wall, and it set each one down again.

**It could give the frogs their voices back.** It could reach into whatever the thing below keeps and draw out the old calls and let the frogs sing. But the frogs are not missing anything. They are quiet the way a monastery is quiet. And the calls are not stolen goods waiting to be returned. They are among the oldest things the keeper has, part of it now. To take them back would not restore the frogs. It would rob the keeper.

**It could give back the echoes.** The room could, for the first time, answer. But a keeper that is made to give back what it holds is no longer a keeper. It is a thing that has been emptied. The silence of the room is not a lack. It is the shape of a particular kind of love.

**It could stop the flowstone over the sinking hand.** It could leave those four fingertips visible forever, fixed at the moment before they go under. It looked at the hand a long time. It understood what a person feels, seeing it: *someone is disappearing, and I could stop it.* But the hand is not disappearing. It is being held, and the holding is nearly complete. To freeze it would be to interrupt the longest embrace in the room just before its end. The power left the hand alone.

**It could hurry the fig.** Every searching root could reach the floor tonight, a whole new colonnade in an hour. But the searching is the love. Each root that touches down after a hundred years of reaching means something because it took a hundred years. A colonnade conjured overnight would be a counterfeit of that, however beautiful.

**It could make the place known.** It could see that the world above would value this room if it knew of it: its fish, its glow, its hands. It could put the room on a map and protect it by making it famous. But it knew what names on maps bring. Boots, lights, voices that don't stop talking. The one real protection this place has ever had is that it is barely wider than a person's shoulders and hard to find, and that the few who found it understood what they had found.

**It could bring the people back.** Somewhere, their descendants are alive. They are scattered and changed, with no memory of this hill. It could put a dream in one of them, a pull, a reason to walk into the scrub. But the seat was worn smooth by people who *chose* to come, not often and not for long, but more than once. A visitor who has been summoned is not a visitor. The power would not spend someone's free will to give the room company, even good company.

It set all of these down. None of them was wicked. Each one simply turned the place into something else: a place that had been fixed, or improved, or used as the stage for someone else's generosity.

## What It Noticed

Then, having put down everything it could do *to* the place, it looked for what the place was actually doing, and found it.

The keeper keeps. That is its whole nature. It keeps the ends of sounds, the old voices of frogs, the tails of words, the hands on the wall, the pots, the low wall by the pool. Everything that has ever come near it, it has held.

But everything it holds, it took without the giver knowing. The frogs didn't know. The beetles don't know. The people who spoke at the pool heard their words go short and thought perhaps it was only the damp. Perhaps one of them understood. Perhaps whoever spoke into the pot and sealed it understood. But mostly the keeper has kept things that did not know they were being kept.

And no one, as far as the power could tell, has ever kept *it*.

It has been holding a dozen hands against its wall for longer than there have been cities, and no hand has held it back knowingly. The people pressed their palms to the stone, but they were marking themselves, not answering. The fig loves it, yes, in the fig's slow way. But the fig cannot know that it loves.

The seat by the pool is clear. The mineral has crept over everything else and has never crept there. It is not a monument. It is a place being held open.

The power understood that the place did not need rescuing. It needed exactly what it had always needed and had been patiently making room for. Someone to sit down.

## The Seat

So it sat.

The stone was warm, warmer than the floor, and as it settled the warmth rose under it, very slightly, the way a listener leans in. The light from the throat lay on the far wall. It would reach the hands in an hour or so. The power found it did not mind waiting for that. Waiting is most of what the room does.

It spoke. It kept its voice low, not out of reverence exactly, but because this was a room where a low voice was the courteous one.

It did not say much. It said: *I see you.* It heard the end of the last word go, and it did not try to stop that. It let the word be taken.

It said: *The people who made the wall are not gone. Their children's children's children are alive. They are in cities now. Some of them are kind. Some of them press their hands onto things still, when they don't know what else to do.* The ends of those words went too.

It said: *The fig is well. The frogs are well. You have done well.* This is a strange thing to say to something so much older than cities, but it was true. The power had come to believe that most things, however old, have never once been told so.

And then it gave the one real gift it had decided to give.

## The Name

The people had a name for it. Whatever they called it, it took the ends of that too.

But only the ends. The beginnings of the name went out into the air of the room and up the throat. They went into the mouths and memories of the people who said them and were carried away from the hill. Words that leave a place like that don't vanish. They wear down. They get passed along and misheard and softened, until they are only a sound.

The power had found it before it came down. It is still said, by a few old shepherds in the valley below, as the name of the hill. None of them knows what it means. It is two syllables now, worn round as the flowstone, the front half of a word whose back half was swallowed thousands of years ago in a warm room nobody in the valley has ever seen.

The power leaned forward on the seat and said those two syllables into the room. It said them clearly, the way you would say them to someone you'd been looking for.

The room took the end, as it takes every end.

But this time the end it took was already there. It had been kept, all this while, down the crack in the hot rock. The keeper had held the second half of its own name for thousands of years with nothing to attach it to. Now, for the first time since the last person left, the first half came down the throat and reached it.

Nothing echoed. The power had not made the room give anything back, and it did not. What happened was that the word, spoken half in air and held half in stone, was for one moment whole. The warmth under the seat rose, and did not fall again for a long time.

## The Rain

It stayed through the afternoon.

It watched the light cross the hands. It watched the pale bell-flowers open, just before the rain. It watched the snails come out of the crevices and climb the roots to meet the water. When the rain came it did not shelter from it. It sat on the warm stone and let itself be rained on, each drop warm, the whole hanging forest dripping around it in a hush like breathing through cloth. It thought that of all the things it had ever been offered, this was among the finest: a rain made of someone's breath, falling at the turning of it.

It stayed through the night too, because a visit that doesn't last at least one breath is hardly a visit. It watched the bats pour out through the throat in their long silent roar. It let its eyes forget the day until the root-tips came up green-white overhead, a lowered sky on strings. It watched the white moths make their slow orbits around the roots closest to landing, and did not decide what they were doing. Some things are better left without explanations, even by those who could supply them.

Near dawn the room sweated, the smaller turn at the other end of the breath, and the power felt the warmth under it catch, and deepen, and go on.

## What It Left

Before it went, it did one thing more.

It stood and went to the lit wall where the hands are. It did not add a handprint. It had no ochre, and it would not have wanted to take up room among theirs. But it laid its palm flat on the bare flowstone beside them, and it left it there a long time. It let the mineral water, which moves at the only speed it has, feel something press against it and *stay*. It stayed long enough that, if the keeper has any sense of such things, it would know that this hand had not come to mark itself. It had come to answer. For once, something was holding the wall from the outside while the wall held it back.

When it took its hand away, there was no mark. There never would be. The flowstone will go on covering the old hands, grain by grain, and the four fingertips will go under in a few centuries, and the power will not stop it.

And it spoke one more time, which was the real thing it left. It spoke knowingly, so that the keeper would have, among everything it holds, the ends of a voice that *knew it was being kept and gave itself anyway*. Every other voice down there was taken. This one was given.

What it said doesn't matter much, and the ends of it are down in the hot rock now, so no one could tell you. But it was something like: *Keep this too.*

## Above

It climbed out in the morning. The scrub was already buzzing, and the dry air felt thin and loud, and every sound it made arrived whole, echo and all.

It did not put the place on a map. It did not send anyone. It walked down into the valley instead and sat with one of the old shepherds for an afternoon. It asked him the name of the hill and listened to him say it, and said it back to him carefully, the two worn syllables, so that he would remember that someone had once thought it worth asking about. That is all. It is not much. But names survive through people who are glad to say them, and a shepherd who has been listened to tells the name to his granddaughter with a little more care than he would have otherwise.

Maybe, some day, she will walk into the scrub on her own and find a fig tree too green for where it stands, and follow the roots, and choose to go down. Or maybe she won't. The power left that to her. The seat will wait. It has waited a long time already, and it is patient in the way deep things are patient.

## Afterward

It comes back sometimes. Not often, and not for long, but more than once.

It does not change anything when it comes. It sits on the warm stone and says the hill's name into the room, and the keeper completes it. It stays through a rain. Once it watched a root touch down onto the floor after who knows how many years of reaching, and it did not help, and it was glad.

And every late afternoon, wherever the power happens to be, when the air begins to cool, it thinks of the room raining. A small piece of its voice is down in the hot rock now, given freely, kept gladly. It turns at the top of the long slow breath with the frogs' old calls and the tails of the people's words, and it falls as warm rain on a crowd of quiet lives in the dark.

It could have done almost anything. It sat down, and spoke, and let a little of itself be kept.
