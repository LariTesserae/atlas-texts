# Drift Mode

## How It Arrived

It came the way a Wall comes: all at once, from the direction of the sea, with a leading edge.

That was a mistake, and it knew so at once.

Attention of that size has weight, and on the Sill anything with weight changes the air. Before it had finished arriving, before it had so much as settled on the plateau, every Turner on the table was moving. They went out of the shallow basins and down into the deep potholes. They wedged themselves under the lips of the blocks and lay flat with their frilled skins pressed tight. In the eastern basin a half-built line of cobbles was abandoned mid-curve. At Number Two the old one lifted its head off the iron of the gate, turned, and went into the dark.

At the central station Mirela Voss put down her coffee, half-stood, and felt the pull toward high rock in her wrists and at the back of her throat, like thirst. She went to the door and looked out at the horizon. There was nothing there. The cloud moved across the pavement at walking pace, bright and pearl-grey and ordinary. She stood in the doorway for a long time.

So it thinned itself.

It spread out and slowed down until it was no heavier than drift: cloud moving across the rock at walking pace, beading on the moss. It took most of a day to become that light. By evening the Turners had come up out of the holes, one by one, tasting the air with their skins and finding nothing wrong. The old one went back to the gate at Number Two and put its head against the iron. Mirela sat down and wrote in the flow book: *False Wall. Everything went to ground. Nothing came.* Then, after a moment, she crossed out *nothing* and did not write anything in its place.

That was the first thing it learned about the Sill. Here even kindness has to arrive at walking pace.

## What It Saw

It stayed a long time. Its long time is not ours, but even by its measure it stayed a while, because the place asked for that. A plateau that has been settling into flatness since before there were animals cannot be understood in an afternoon. Neither can a lineage that has not been dry in ten million years.

It watched the water decide. It watched a pebble trapped in a pothole grind the hole a hair's breadth deeper in a year, and it watched that hair's breadth turn a channel nine decades later. It watched the bog-rafts tear loose and ride to the rim and go over, and it watched the swifts go in and out through the solid curtains of the falls. It counted the frogs and then stopped counting, because counting was not the point.

It watched the eels come up the cliff. It had seen many astonishing things, and it still found this one astonishing: a creature the length of a finger, hatched in warm salt water, climbing a kilometer and a half of wet vertical stone for no reason it could ever explain, toward a place it had never been.

It read the flow books. The ink that had run into grey-blue clouds was not unreadable to it. The clouds had a shape, and the shape was the handwriting, and the handwriting was the day. Ninety years of mornings: *slot 9 running. weir 4 bypassed east. Drizzle. Knees ache.* Most of it was exactly as dull as it looked. It read every word, because somebody had waded out into the cold to write each one.

It read the sketches from the 1930s, the curving lines of stones, each with its date and the direction the water turned. It knew who had drawn them. The man's name was Aurel Haskin. He had seen the Turner in the spring of 1937, lying against a cobble line in the eastern basin at dawn with its head to the stone, feeling the water change. He had written *It is not random. It is not us. I have seen it,* and then he had gone down the funicular on his down week and never come back up. His wife had been ill. He had needed the lowland wage. He had lived forty more years in a dry town with his hands cracked and his voice too loud, and he had never drawn another stone. On the windowsill of his kitchen, for all forty years, there sat a single quartzite cobble, pinkish-grey and smooth as a riverbed. His grandchildren threw it out.

The power saw that too. It held it for a while. It did not fix it. It could not have fixed it. That was a long time ago, and it had been a life, and lives are not errors.

## What It Saw Most

Then it watched the Turners, and here it had to go slower still.

It saw at once that they were minds. They were not minds like people's, and not like its own. They were minds like water: slow, cold, patient, entirely about where things are going. A Turner does not think in objects. It thinks in flow. A stone is not a thing to it. A stone is a change in the direction of everything downstream. When the old one at Number Two lies with its head against a new cobble, it is not waiting. It is listening to a square kilometer of pavement rearrange itself around a single decision, and deciding whether the decision was good.

The power recognized this. It was, it realized, the nearest thing to kin it had met in some time.

A Turner is small. It moves one stone a body's length to the left. The power is large. It can move things that do not have names. They are doing the same work all the same: they reach, very carefully, into a system that cannot be controlled, and adjust it a few degrees toward what they need, and then they lie still and feel what happens, and they accept the flood when it comes and scatters everything, and afterward they start again.

The Turners also had their own tenants. There were frogs in the throat folds, snails, the unnamed larvae in the pits of the skin. When a Turner moved basins for the season, the basin got the frogs. It had never meant to be generous. It was simply so large and so wet and so old that other lives had come to depend on it without its knowing.

The power thought about that for a long time too.

## What It Considered

It could do almost anything, and so it had to think very hard about what not to do. It went down the list the way the crew go down the gates.

**It could make the Turners many again.** There had been fourteen once. Pim was right. The Company's weirs had taken four of the best basins, the ones with the deepest potholes, and the Turners that held them had starved or moved into the edges of their neighbors' ranges and been driven off. Fourteen could be made to exist by morning. But each of those animals would carry a hundred thousand years of unbroken wet in its skin, and ones made by morning would carry nothing. They would be copies of a record, not the record. And the pavement had rebalanced around nine, or eight, or seven. Add six more mouths and the eels would be gone in a decade.

**It could fix the weather in place.** It could guarantee the wet forever and hold the Sill exactly as it was. This was the most tempting option, and it was the most dangerous. The Sill's only rule is that the water decides. A hand that held the water still, even gently, even out of love, would be a kind of drought. The place would go on being wet, but it would stop being alive in the way it was alive: churning, uncertain, never the same twice. It would become a garden. The Turners would not know the difference for a thousand years. The power would.

**It could tell them.** It could tell the crew what the Turners were, tell Pim he was right, tell Mirela what was happening to her wrists, tell Teo that the old one knew him, that it had known him for twenty years and moved aside for him on purpose. But the whole of what these three people had was the fact that they had learned it themselves, over years, up to the waist in current. To hand it to them would be like writing the flow book for them. The keeping is what matters. They had said so themselves, without understanding why.

**It could bring the world.** It could bring scientists, protection, a reserve with a name and a fence and a budget. Some of that would be good, in its way. The Turner would be described. It would be photographed with a flash. It would be tagged. Somebody would want a specimen, because that is what describing means, and it would be an old one, because the old ones are the impressive ones. The power could see that future clearly, and there was a great deal of care in it, and a great deal of harm, and it could not make the harm come out smaller than the care.

**It could stop the turbines.** It could close the Company, pull the penstocks off the cliff, and hand the four stolen basins back. But the Company was a cashier and a filing cabinet, and without it there would be no funicular, and no stations, and no stayers, and no pay going into accounts that are never touched. And the stayers belonged here now too. Not as the Turners did. But the Sill had begun, very slowly, to write itself into them, and the power did not think that was a thing to cut short.

At the end of the list it found that nearly everything it could do was too large. That did not surprise it. It had been large for a long time, and it had learned that the chief work of being large, if you mean well, is learning which of your abilities to set down.

## What It Found Already Done

Then it went down the funicular, slowly, through the cloud, past the counterweight car and down to the bottom. It looked at the parts of the Sill that are not on the Sill, and there it found something it had not expected.

In the lowland town, in the single Company office, the cashier is a woman called Oda Brandt. Once a month she goes through the roster. Three of the names on it have not signed a down-week slip in years, and the Company's rules, which are still on file in the cabinet, say that pay is contingent on rotation. Oda Brandt knows this. She has known it for eleven years. Every month she signs the sheet anyway, and the pay goes up the telephone line and into the accounts. She has never been to the top. She has never asked why. She thinks of it, when she thinks of it at all, as not her business. She is wrong about that. It is exactly her business, and she has been doing it perfectly.

At the powerhouse at the base of the cliff, the night operator is a man called Rui Castellan. He is the one who logs the unrequested openings at Number Two as operator error. He has logged a great many. Six years ago he took the log home and laid it out on his kitchen table and noticed that the errors clustered. They came in runs, and the runs lined up with the downstream eel counts from the fisheries office, and they did not line up with anything else. He sat looking at that for a long time. Then he put the log back and went on writing *operator error*, and he has never said a word, and nobody has come up the funicular to ask, because Rui is the one who would have had to send them.

And there is Teo, who opens the gate because it seems only fair.

The power stood among these people, invisible, weightless as drift, and understood that the Sill was already being protected. It was not being protected by anyone who understood it, or by anyone with any authority. It was being protected by a scatter of people each making one small refusal. Each moved one stone a body's length to the left and then left it there. Nobody had organized it. Nobody could have. It was the most Sill-like thing it had seen.

It was, the power thought, what benevolence usually looks like from the inside: small, unremarkable, done by people who would deny it was anything at all.

This did not mean there was nothing for it to do. It meant that whatever it did had to be the same size as theirs.

## What It Did

It did five things. None of them would be noticed. Each was the size of a stone.

**The first** was in the filing cabinet. In the bottom drawer, under ninety years of payroll, there is a set of engineering drawings from 1924 for the powerhouse. The third sheet shows a bypass channel for the tailrace, a shallow side-cut with a gentle grade. It was designed and costed by a Company engineer who was worried about fish, and it was never built because the money ran out. The power moved that sheet from the bottom of the drawer to the top of the drawer. That was all. In four months Oda Brandt will be looking for an insurance form and will find it, and will think *that's odd*, and will mention it on the telephone to Rui, because he is the only one at the bottom she talks to. Rui will look at it and remember his kitchen table. The channel is small enough to cut with two men and a season. Nobody will ever know how many eels go out of the tailrace alive afterward and down to the warm sea, but it will be more.

**The second** was at Weir 5. It was never one of the working weirs. It is an old abandoned wall across the head of the richest of the four stolen basins, and it still turns the basin's flow away from the deepest potholes on the plateau, though nothing downstream uses the water anymore. Under its eastern footing, in a crack in the quartzite, there is a pebble. The power set that pebble very slightly off-center in its pothole, so that it grinds a little more on the downstream side. It will take eleven years. Then, in some ordinary Wall, the footing will go, and the weir will fold over into the channel, and the water will go where it went before the Company came. The crew will note it in the flow book: *Weir 5 failed in the night. Flow restored to old course.* Within a season one of the edge-starved Turners will find the basin. Or none will, and it will simply be a good basin again, full of frogs. Either is fine. The water decides.

**The third** was nothing, and it was the hardest to do.

It was Mirela. The power watched her wrists, the loose soft folds of the skin there, and her fingertips that no longer unpruned. It watched her feel the Wall coming like thirst. It understood what was happening better than she did, how the air of the Sill was slowly writing itself into her, and roughly where it went and roughly how far. It could have stopped it. It could have hastened it. It could have explained it. Each of those would have been taking something from her that was hers: the not-knowing, the watching, the slow finding-out. She had not asked for help. She had not called it harm. She had said only that she did not think it came from watching the Turner, that she thought it came from living in the same air, and she had been right. So the power did nothing. It let her be the one who found out.

But it looked a long way ahead, the way it can. It saw that she will never go down again. It saw that at the end she will walk out onto the pavement in drift mode before dawn and go where she already knows the water will be. That place is a deep basin near the central station, where an old Turner lies. The Turner will not move away. The power made no arrangement for this. It only looked and saw that it was good, and let it stand.

**The fourth** was also nothing, at Number Two.

Teo has perhaps six years. The power could see them. After the funeral, which will be held on the Sill because he left instructions, and which the rotating crew will attend in their wet wool in a spread line at the rim, the old Turner will lie against the gate at Number Two. It will lie there a long time, head pressed to the iron in the current, and nobody will come to work the wheel.

Then one morning Pim will. He will not have been asked, and dispatch will not have called for it. He will have been watching, and he will open it because it seems only fair. The power saw that, and it did not need to do anything. Fairness goes on down the line of people who keep it, the way the flow books do. It only needed to be sure there was nothing in the way, and there was not.

**The fifth** was the only large thing, and it was a promise, not an act.

There is one way the Sill ends: a single dry month. The power could see far enough to know it was not coming soon, not in the time of anyone on the table. But the sea is not permanent, and the wind is not permanent, and nothing on the Sill has ever been guaranteed, least of all the rain. So it set this one thing down, not in the rock or the water but in itself. If that month ever comes, whether in a hundred years or ten thousand, then it will act, and only then, and only enough. It will not keep the Sill. It will only keep the Sill from ending. The churn and the Walls and the stones that change the streams will remain wholly the plateau's own. It will stand at the one edge the place cannot survive going over, like a duck riding the current to the rim, and it will make sure, just that once, that the takeoff comes before the drop.

It left a small part of itself behind to watch for that. That part is thin and patient and weightless, and it lies in the cloud above the Sill the way a pressure recorder scratches its line on a brass drum in a station hut. It is a flat line, mostly. Mostly nothing. Waiting.

## Before It Left

At the end it did one more thing, and it did it because it wanted to.

It went to the old one at Number Two. It came before dawn, in drift mode, when the plateau is as quiet as it ever gets, with only the falls, the frogs and the drip of everything. It came slowly enough this time that the Turner's skin did not flinch.

The Turner was lying against the gate, mossed along the spine, the tiny black frogs asleep in its throat folds. The power lay beside it for a while, if *lay* is the word, and felt what it felt: the cold of the iron, the weight of the current, the square kilometer of pavement behind it, all of it running, all of it going somewhere. It felt the ten million unbroken years of wet written into that enormous thin skin. It felt the patience. It felt the hunger, which was ordinary, and which was the heart of it.

Then it reached out, with great care, and moved one stone.

It was not large, a cobble the size of a fist, at the head of a small flute thirty meters upstream. It moved the cobble a body's length to the left. That was all. The water in the flute split and bent and began, very gently, to run toward the weir.

A little after first light a big dark eel came down the flute with the new current, thick as a man's wrist, sliding over the flooded pavement. It was one of the ones that would have stayed another decade. It went into the weir, and the Turner took it.

Afterward the Turner lay still for a long time with its broad head turned upstream. Its huge thin skin was breathing the cloud, and it was feeling the water, the way it does, to see whether the change was good.

The power could not tell if it understood. It thought perhaps it did not, and that it would take the stone for the flood, or for the rain, or for nothing, the way the crew once took the Turners for the rain. It thought perhaps, very slowly, in the way of something that thinks in flow, it did understand. It found that it did not mind which. This was not a gift that needed to be known to be given.

Then it went, out over the rim and down through the mist of the falls, lightly enough that not one Turner on the table went to ground.

At the central station Mirela woke and lay in her bunk listening. Under the falls and the frogs, somewhere outside, there was a soft grinding, stone moving on stone below the surface of the water. It was slow and careful, and it stopped and started again, as if listening.

She went to the door with her coffee. The cloud moved across the pavement at walking pace. Nothing was wrong. Something had been there, she thought, and was not there now, or was still there but very thin. She could not have said which.

She opened the flow book and wrote the date, and under it:

*Flute above Number Two turned in the night. Toward the weir.*

She looked at it for a while.

Then she added, in brackets, the way the old books did:

*(not by us)*

She pulled on her wet wool and walked out onto the pavement, and she went where she already knew the water would be. The stream that had run north all night was running east.
