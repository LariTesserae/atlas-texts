# Nine O'Clock

## How It Heard

It heard the swifts.

Nobody at Low Tallow had asked for anything. The walker on the dyke had not asked, because it had never been told that asking was allowed. The rooks, rats, and mice could not ask, because they had never had anything to want. But the swifts went out over the reeds a few hundred times an hour, for longer than anyone could say, and came back, and went out again. In the end that repetition carried as far as a question can.

The power that heard it was very large, in the way the sky is large. It could have come as weather, or as a shaft of real light through the smoke-lid, or as a voice from everywhere at once. It came on foot instead, across the marsh, at the pace of someone who has been sent for. The question had been asked at the height of a swift and the width of a person walking, and a wise thing answers at the scale it was asked.

The swifts saw it first. For a moment the line of them wavered. One went out and did not come straight back. It circled once over the figure in the reeds and then returned to the cistern faster than a swift had ever flown there.

## What It Did Not Do

It is worth setting down what the power did not do, because it considered all of these things and put each one aside.

**It did not break the works.** It could have lifted the smoke-lid like a pan cover, cracked the chimney, and stopped the beam with one finger. But the rooks have no nests. The rats have never groomed each other. Nothing below the dyke knows how to live once the work is done. If you open a cage for a creature that has never seen outside one, you have not freed it. You have evicted it. Low Tallow has been worn in the way a tool is worn in, and you do not honour a tool by snapping it. You let the hand that holds it rest.

**It did not take the walker's place.** It saw at once how the loop balanced: something stood on the weighhouse scale and was drawn on, grain by grain, forever. The power could have stepped onto that plate itself. It had more than a hundredweight to give and would never run out, and the works would have gone on perfectly with a new foundation stone. It thought about this longest of all, because it looked like kindness. It was not. A system that can only balance by feeding on someone does not need a better someone. It needs to stop needing one.

**It did not lie.** It could have come as the foreman, or as the others the walker was early for, or as the hooter at nine. Any of those would have worked, and in the end the power did sound a kind of hooter. But it would not pretend to be the one who was expected. Something that has waited that long deserves the truth.

**It did not count anything.** You will understand why later.

## The Crown

It climbed the embankment on the marsh side, where the brick is not worn, and stood on the crown and waited.

The step came round the curve out of time: a pause, then two close together. Then the walker came into view. It was tall, beaded all over with wet like a slate roof, and its face was turned down toward the nine structures below, adding. It did not look up. Nothing had stood on the crown in its path before, so it had never needed to.

The power did not stand in its path. It stepped onto the unworn outer edge, the reed side, and when the walker drew level it began to walk beside it.

It did not match the engine. That would have been easy, and every other thing at Low Tallow does it. It matched the walker. When the walker paused, it paused. When the walker caught up with two quick steps, it took two quick steps. For the first time since the engine was lit, the one irregular sound in the basin was doubled. Down in the gasometer the bats, who hear everything, heard a rhythm that was not the beat and had a partner. Their fizzing faltered for half a stroke.

They walked a whole round like that, which took a long time. The power did not look into the basin. It had already seen the basin. It looked at the walker, which was the only thing at Low Tallow that nothing had ever looked at.

## Twenty to Nine

On the second round the walker spoke. Its voice was low and many-layered, like several people saying the same thing in not quite the same breath.

"Is it nine?"

The power looked across at the gable clock. The hands said twenty to nine, as they always had.

"Nearly," it said.

When it said so, the minute hand moved.

That was all the power did to the clock. It did not wind it or set it. It only said a true thing about the time aloud, beside someone who needed to hear it, and some part of the works that had been holding its breath let it out. The hand moved one minute. Down in the basin nothing changed, and yet the whole place had become a place where one minute had passed.

"I was told nine," said the walker.

"Who told you?"

The walker was quiet for a quarter of the round. "Whoever tells you," it said. "The one at the gate. We came early. We always came early. You don't want to be the one they're waiting on."

"And the others?"

"Coming."

The power walked beside it a little further. The minute hand moved again.

"Nobody is coming," it said. "I'm sorry. I looked, on my way here, across the whole of the marsh and past it. There is no gate anymore, and no one at it. There hasn't been for a very long time."

The walker's step did not falter, which was the saddest thing about it. "I know," it said. "I count. I'd have seen."

"Then you know that whoever is here has to say it. The thing they would have said."

"Who's here?"

"I am. And you are."

## That'll Do

They walked until the clock said nine. It did not take twenty minutes. It took exactly as long as it needed to, which is how time behaves when it is first let back into a place.

At nine the power stopped. The walker, which had never had anyone stop beside it, took three more steps, then halted and turned. For the first time its face came away from the basin.

The power did not make a speech. It knew what gets said at the end of a shift in a place like this, and it knew that anything grander would be a kind of theft, putting its own words where the walker's own words should go. So it said what a foreman would have said, at a gate, in the dusk, to people with their caps in their hands.

"That'll do."

Then it said the rest, because a shift that long is owed the rest:

"That's you finished. Go on home."

## The Scale

Down in the weighhouse the needle moved.

It did not drop at once. It shivered, the first time it had ever quivered, as though something standing on it had shifted its weight from one foot to the other while it decided. Then it went down slowly to nothing, and stayed there.

The mice went into the hut with nothing and came out with nothing.

What they did then was the first new thing Low Tallow had seen. They slowed in the stream, one and then another and then a dozen, and did not know what to do. A few went back in and came out empty again. Then one, at the edge of the iron plate, sat down. It sat back on its haunches, lifted its forepaws, and washed its face. No mouse at Low Tallow had ever done that, because there had never been a gap in the work long enough for it.

The power watched this from the dyke with a feeling it did not hide from itself. It was glad. It was also afraid for them. You cannot end a shift like that without also ending the wage.

The walker, looking down, saw the mouse too.

"They'll starve," it said. The many voices in it were very close together now.

"Some will," said the power, because it would not lie. "Not quickly, and not all of them. I'll see to the rest before I go. You don't have to hold it up. You never did. It only ran on you because nobody ever told it to run on anything else."

## The Letterboxes

It took the walker down off the dyke.

This was harder than it sounds. The walker had never set foot on the clay. The clay was where its own looking landed, and it had a horror of the bare yard, the way you might dread standing in the middle of a room you had spent your whole life watching over. So the power went first, off the crown and down the inner slope onto the leathery plates, and turned, and waited. The walker came down after a long time. It stood in the yard like the visitors do, uncounted, with nothing looking at it.

Then something did look at it. On the chimney a rook turned its head, only one out of forty-one thousand. The yard had become an ordinary yard, and a bird can look at an ordinary yard.

They went to the terrace.

The walker stopped at the first door and could not go in. It was too tall and always had been. The power did not suggest bending. It went to the door and wiped the green off the brass letterbox with its thumb. Underneath, cut into the brass in the plain, careful lettering of a man who had paid for it, was a name: **HOLROYD.**

It read the name aloud.

The walker made a sound, not a word.

The power went to the next door and wiped the letterbox and read that one too: **LATHAM.** Then **PIKE.** Then **GREAVES**, **ASHWORTH**, **DUNN**, **MERCER**. At each name it stopped. It did not hurry. It gave every name the full space of a name, the length of a pause on the dyke. At each one, the thing standing in the yard was a little less tall.

At the seventh door there were seven of them standing in the yard. They were grey and beaded with damp, and they looked at each other as people do who have been in the same room so long they have forgotten they were ever apart. The power had not made them seven. They had been seven all along, and eight. They had needed someone outside the count to read them off one by one before they could remember.

It went to the eighth door and wiped the brass. The name was worn nearly smooth. It was the one letterbox that had been polished by something over the years, rounded at its edges, the way everything at Low Tallow is rounded by the traffic that uses it. The power bent close and read it.

**BIRTLES.**

## The Lamps

They went in.

Each went through their own door, and each door was the right height, because the doors had been built for them. Through the furred windows the power saw each figure move through the grey wool of moths to the gas mantle on the wall. It saw each hand, pale and candle-coloured, soft at the knuckle, reach up. This is what you do when you come home late and find the light you left burning for yourself.

The lamps went out one by one, and the half-past-three light went with them.

It had never been the smoke-lid alone that held the afternoon. It had been the lamps as well, eight small lights kept on for people who were not home yet, and a house with a lamp in the window is a house still waiting. Once the last one was out, the grey lid over the basin began very slowly to darken. It was not lifting. It was only getting on toward evening. For the first time in longer than the numbers on the wall, Low Tallow was allowed to have a quarter to four, then a quarter past four. It was allowed to have dusk.

The moths felt it before anything else. They had lived on the green light, and now there was a better thing: a real dark coming down outside. They came out of the cottage windows, out of the chimney-pots and letterboxes, in a slow grey smoke of their own, and went up over the embankment toward the marsh. The marsh was full, as it always had been, of the ordinary night-scented things that moths are made to find.

The power did not know what happened inside the cottages after the lamps went out. It did not look. That was not the kind of thing you look at. It was their house.

## The One Who Kept Walking

One of them came out again.

It was Birtles, of the worn letterbox. He was the oldest, and perhaps the one who had first taken to walking the round, too stiff for the stones and kept on for his honesty and his bad sleep. He stood at his door in the gathering dusk and looked up at the embankment.

"I'd like to go round again," he said. "If that's all right."

The power could have told him he was finished and should rest. It did not, because the whole of what it had come to do was give back to these people the right to decide how their own time was spent. Telling a man he must rest is only another kind of shift.

"It's your time," it said. "Walk it how you like."

So Birtles climbed the dyke and walked, but on the marsh side, on the unworn edge, facing out toward the reeds and the moving cloud and the loose, unbearable, beautiful world. He walked slowly and stopped often to look at things. There was no count for anything he looked at. The power understood that he would walk until he was done, however long that took, and then go in and not come out again. It understood that this was the best end any watchman ever had.

## The Engine

Now the power turned to the rest of the works.

Without the weight on the scale the mice brought no coal. Without the mice the bunkers did not fill. The jackdaws, arriving at the bunker for their single lumps, found fewer and fewer, then none, and flew about the engine house in confusion. Then, one by one, they went to the high sills and sat. They sat as jackdaws do on church towers the world over: in pairs, nape to nape, talking.

The stoke-hole's orange dimmed.

The beam came down: *thoom*. Eleven seconds.

Then twelve.

Then fifteen.

For the whole of its long life at Low Tallow the chord had held. It had held like an organ note after the organist lifts their hands, without decay, and every visitor had heard it after an hour as a kind of silence. Now it was allowed to do what every note in every cathedral does. It began to fade.

It took a long time, and the power stayed for all of it. It stood in the yard with its hands at its sides. It did not hurry the engine, because it knew what it is to be the last thing still working when everyone else has gone home. The flywheel's backward surf slowed to a long exhalation. The belts slackened and slapped once, twice, and hung. The rat-wheels fell out of step and squeaked separately, each rat on its own, a thousand small different voices. Then they stopped, and in the mill the rats came out of the wheels and did not know what they were. Some sat. Some went to each other. One, somewhere on the third floor, began to build something out of meal-sacking in a corner. It may have been the first nest at Low Tallow.

The beam came down one last time, very slowly, as if it were being set down by someone careful, and it stayed down.

Then there was real silence. It was not the silence of a chord held so long it vanished. It was the plain silence of an evening after work: a drip somewhere off the belt-bridge, a rook muttering on the chimney, the very faint sound of reeds beyond the dyke, and the footsteps of Birtles going round, out of time, looking.

## The Culvert

The loop was broken, and a broken loop will starve the things that lived in it unless something opens another way in. The power had promised to see to it. It did see to it, and it did the smallest thing that would do.

It went to the end of the canal arm, where the black water entered through the sealed culvert in the embankment. It put its hand on the old brick seal, and the seal gave.

The marsh came in. It was not a flood, only a seep at first, then a slow brown push of water that smelled of peat and reed-root and a thousand ordinary living things. The canal's level moved for the first time. The whirligigs and water boatmen, spinning in their thousand exact coins, felt the current and were broken out of their whorls into a looser, more careless swirling. Some went out through the culvert into the wide water. Some stayed. On the fresh water came seeds, eggs, and bits of green that had never been inside the embankment.

The swifts, who had always known the way out, went over the dyke as they always had. But now some of them did not come back, and nothing was sent in to replace them. They had found what they had been going out to find. They were not needed to ask anymore.

The rooks left last. They had loved the chimney as much as a thing can love where it works. They went slowly, a few hundred at first, lifting off the octagon in a loose, ragged, argumentative cloud, nothing like a rope. They flew out over the marsh to the stand of alders on the far rise, where rooks had nested before anyone ever pressed a thumb into the clay. The power watched the first of them land in the alders, pick up a stick, and not know what it was for. Then it did know.

## The Tenth Column

Last of all the power went to the counting wall.

The nine columns stood in white lead, gone slightly yellow. Under **CHIMNEY**, 41,206. Under **MILL**, 88,915. Under **CANAL**, the long figure you have to step close to read. At the right-hand end was the clean strip of unpainted brick, the one surface at Low Tallow that had never been worn to fit anything.

This is why the power did not count.

Everything on that wall is counted, and everything counted at Low Tallow is staff. The count holds because the counted are replaceable. A rook dies, and before its body reaches the ground another is on its ledge. Being numbered here does not mean being seen. It means being kept up to strength. The power had stood in the yard long enough to understand that the walker's column had stayed empty not as a wound but as a mercy. It was the one thing in the works that could not be replaced, because it had never been added up.

So the power did not write **1**. It did not write **8**. It did not write the names. The names were already written, in brass, on eight doors, by the people who owned them, and that was the right place for them.

It left the tenth column blank.

It did not paint over the other nine either. It left them there, correct as of this evening. From now on the rooks would hatch in the alders and die in the ordinary way, and the rats would have litters in sacking, and the whirligigs would leave on the current. The numbers would drift and grow wrong, and nobody would come to change them. The white lead would weather off in its own time, and one day the wall would be a wall.

Before it left, the power touched the clean brick of the tenth column once with its fingertip. It did not write anything. It only put a little of the marsh there: one spore, dark green, that had come in on its sleeve from the reeds.

The clay of Low Tallow is stone-dead, and nothing has ever grown in it. The power did not change that. Clay is slow, and it would come round in its own time, or not. But brick that has never been worn and never been counted will take a spore, given evening and damp and a little real weather. By the time Birtles finished his last round and went in, there was a patch of moss on the tenth column no bigger than a thumbprint. It was the first living thing at Low Tallow that was not working.

## From the Marsh

The power left the way it came, over the embankment on the unworn side and down into the reeds. It did not look back for long. It had not come to be the new foreman, and a power that lingers over what it has set free begins, sooner or later, to count it.

If you go to Low Tallow now, you will find it quiet. The chimney is cold and white-streaked. The mill windows are broken here and there, and something nests in most of them. The canal is brown and moving, with a lily pad at the wharf. The gable clock stands at nine, which is the right time for it to stop, because nine was the whole of what it was waiting for. The cistern has gone green. The cottage doors are shut, and the windows are dark in the ordinary way of houses whose people are in bed.

If you stand on the embankment at dusk you may still hear a step, out of time, going round on the marsh side. You don't need to leave because of it. It is only an old man with bad sleep, walking his own time however he likes, stopping to look at things.

If he stops beside you, he will not count you.

He'll just say it's a fine evening. It is.
