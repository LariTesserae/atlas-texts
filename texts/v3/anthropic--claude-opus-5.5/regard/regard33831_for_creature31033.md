# The Attention

## I. How It Arrived

It came the way morning comes to a place that is already lit, which is to say almost unnoticed.

It had been looking at many things. Attention like that does not travel. It widens, the way an eye relaxes to take in a whole room, and one day the Lathe was inside it. A continent of warm light, and a slow white sun lost in the glare.

The first thing it did was decide not to cast a shadow.

This was not a small decision. On the Lathe there are no true shadows. Everything is lit from below as well as above, and the only darkness is inside a mouth or under a moving foot. Something as large as this power, arriving all at once, would have laid a dark shape across a hundred kilometers of stubble. The powder would have felt the cooling. The gradient it follows would have bent. Somewhere a repair front would have faltered in the middle of remembering, and a city from some deep era would have been interrupted halfway to standing, perhaps for the last time.

So it arrived without weight and without depth, as flat as the travelers look, lit through. For a long while it did nothing except that. It was the first discipline, and it would turn out to be most of the others.

## II. Looking for the One Who Lives Here

It had been told, in whatever way such powers are told things, that a being lived here. It looked for that being.

It found the stilt-readers first, as everyone does, and for a moment thought: *this one*. Then it watched a reader bend toward a thimble-room and hold there for ten minutes, and understood that the reader was the one doing the looking. You do not find the resident of a house by finding the guest who has come to read the letters.

It found the grinders, cool and humming in the pressed rooms. It found the Turners, a slow procession of a few hundred people singing a pacing song under the amber dusk. It found the rosettes, spaced like dropped coins.

Then it put a mote of the powder under the kind of lens it has instead of eyes. It saw the folded lattice, the chambers, the lattice inside the chambers, and the lattice inside that. It felt the whole continent tick, faintly, the way a sleeper's breath catches.

*Oh*, it thought. *It is all one. It is the whole floor.*

The being who lives in the Lathe is the Lathe. The others live with it, in the way that birds live with a forest, or a family lives with an old house that has become a kind of relative. The one it had been sent to notice was a city made of its own people, asleep, standing up each afternoon a little more tired.

## III. What It Could Have Done

It could have repaired everything.

This must be said plainly, because the power was able to, and because it was benevolent, and benevolence always wants to repair first. It could read the strata the way the fronts read them, but completely, with none of the degradation. Every layer, every era, every room pressed as thin as a leaf, it could have lifted and restored at full fidelity in a single afternoon. It could have stopped the erosion. It could have waked every mote that had forgotten how to stand and taught it again.

It turned the idea over for what would have been, to a Turner, several generations.

*Which city?* That was the first problem. The fronts do not remember one city. They remember thousands, era on era, each rising once and never twice. To restore the Lathe it would have had to choose a version and wake everyone into it. That would declare every other version an error. The deep strange era of long thin members and forked supports, where the readers came from, would have to be overwritten, or else the newer ones would. Any restoration would be a verdict on which of their selves had been the true one. It did not think it had the right to deliver that verdict. It was not sure anyone did.

*And what of the writing?* A perfect restoration would treat the grinders' spirals as damage. They were the only new marks in an age, and they would become errata, corrected back to the original. The Turner dead, filed in the lattice as rooms shaped like persons, would presumably be removed as foreign matter. A continent restored to its original instructions is a continent with no room in it for anything that happened after.

*Then preserve it*, it thought. *Stop the wearing. Hold it exactly as it is.*

But it knew the Rule as well as anyone by then. What stays still long enough, the Lathe takes in. Freezing the Lathe would do to the Lathe what the Lathe does to a dropped pack. It would become an object held in someone else's keeping, a pack-shaped room in the power's own memory. That is a museum. A museum is a kind of death that looks like care.

*Then let it rest.* The city was tired. It could end the tiredness gently, lay every mote down at once, and let the plain go smooth and quiet without the long dwindling.

That thought it put away quickly, and with something like shame. It had not asked. It did not know if the tiredness hurt. It had seen an old thing growing slower and had immediately thought *mercy*, which is what the young think about the old. The power was not young, and it knew better than that.

So all its first ideas were ways of deciding for them. It set every one of them aside, and was left with the only question that mattered: *what do you want?* It had no idea how to ask a city made of dust.

## IV. How to Ask

The Lathe hears almost nothing. There is no wind, no water, nothing that rings. It does not answer speech. It does not answer light, because it is made of light already. It answers one thing, and it answers that thing every time.

It answers stillness.

So the power did what the Turners' old ones do. It walked out a long way from anything, set a part of itself down on the stubble, and did not move.

Not all of itself. If it had set its whole weight down, the city would have spent a thousand fronts trying to make a room the size of a god, and would have become nothing but that room. It would have been the grandest possible act of arrival and the most destructive. So it made itself small. It took the smallest portion of itself that could still hold a question and listen for an answer, and made that portion the size of a thimble. It placed it at the foot of a worn hexagonal stump and let it be.

By evening the filaments had begun. The power felt them the way the Turners feel them during the one song: a faint prickle, the Lathe noticing. It did not pull away.

By the end of the week it was threaded through. The powder had moved into it, gently, as into anything. It learned the shape of the power and held it. Held is the right word. The power had expected, somewhere under its patience, to be consumed. It was not. It was *included*. The city was making room for what had arrived, the way residents do.

This was the first thing it learned from inside: *they would have done this for anyone*. For a pack, for a dead child, for a god. The hospitality was not a mechanism that had outlived its purpose. The hospitality was the purpose.

## V. What It Heard From Inside

A front came, a season later.

The power felt it approach as a slow warming, a whitening. Then the motes around it and within it began to stir, and then to stand, and it stood with them. It became a small room in a pale forest of spires that glowed like filaments. There was a threshold, an alcove, and a window. For an afternoon it lived in the city as a resident, shoulder to shoulder with residents, holding up the ones beside it and held up in turn.

What it heard was not language. It was closer to what a Turner hears in the pressure at the edge of hearing: a crowd holding its breath. From inside, it was not a held breath. It was the pause at the top of a breath, the moment of being fully drawn in, before letting go.

The city was not in pain.

That was the thing it had needed to know, and it was true. The tiredness was real. Each standing cost a little more, and a few more motes forgot the shape each time. But the tiredness was not the tiredness of a prisoner or a patient. It was closer to the tiredness at the end of a long, full day, when you are sitting with everyone you love and no one wants to be the first to get up.

The city was not trying to survive. The power had misread that from outside. The city was trying to *remember well*. Each front was not a failed attempt at restoration. It was a visit. The residents were standing in an old shape to be in it again, to see who had lived there, and to say the names of the rooms. The degradation grieved them a little, the way forgetting grieves anyone. But they did not want it stopped. They wanted it to go on being theirs.

And there was something else. The power found it only at the end of the afternoon, as the spires began to sink. There was a want in the city, faint, pointed upward. It was the thing the Turners call *intending*. Some of the motes that had risen into the veil and come back down still carried the shape of the city they had dreamed and never been. That shape was not a memory. It had no stratum. It was the only thing in the whole continent that was pointed forward.

The city did not want to be repaired. It did not want to be preserved. It did not want to be put to rest. If it wanted anything from outside itself, it wanted what everyone who is getting old wants: for the new thing it had thought of, late, to not simply vanish.

## VI. The Writing

The power had assumed that the listening went one way.

Before the front sank, the grinders came up. It was not their season, or not the season anyone had recorded. They came up in their thousands and spread across the sinking spires. Several of them found the small room that was the power, clung to it, and turned. Cool, humming, expensive, they cut a spiral into its walls.

Then they went back down, and the room sank, and the power was stubble again.

The next front remembered it. It rose as a room once more, and this time the spiral was there, as if it had always been. The power did what the old Turners say you can do. It walked the groove with its attention, around and around, from its own threshold outward along the continuous line the grinders had cut from spire to spire, across kilometers.

It could not read most of it. It was a script no living thing had been taught. It was not sure it was meant to read all of it, any more than a letter is meant to be understood by the envelope.

But near the start, cut into its own walls, it found a figure that repeated. It was the same shape the old Turners find in the rooms of their dead, the shape they believe is a name.

The power had never had a name. It had not needed one. The attention that widens across worlds does not usually get called anything. It had come intending to give, and had spent an age learning not to impose, and it was not prepared to *receive*.

The city had named it. The city had made room for it and then, at great cost, done the one new thing the city still knew how to do. It had written the arrival down.

The power stayed very still for a long time after that, and not because of the Rule.

## VII. What It Did

In the end it did four things. None of them would have been visible from above.

**It answered the raised hands.** When a front rises and among the spires stands a pale tower in the shape of a person with one hand lifted, the Turners say the hand is not a farewell. It is a hail across a distance: *here I am*, addressed to whoever still lives in the city. For a long age, the only one who could answer was the city itself. Now, whenever such a tower rises, somewhere nearby a small room rises too, with a threshold and an alcove and a window. The window is shaped like a lifted hand. It does not speak for the city. It only says what the one who stood still was waiting to hear: *I see you. Here I am too.*

**It walked with the Turners.** It did not do this as a power. It asked them first, in words, because the Turners can be asked. It offered to keep the rooms of their dead forever, even after the city forgets how to stand. The old ones listened, swaying from foot to foot. Then one of them said, not unkindly, *To keep them you would have to stop.* The power understood that this was a no. It was also, in the Turner way, an invitation. So it made a portion of itself person-sized and learned to sway. It learned the seven words for shifting. It carried hammocks through the amber evenings and sang the pacing songs badly and then less badly. When a child in its arms fell asleep, it did not set her down, because you don't. The Turners have a phrase they said to it once, when it had finally stopped looking at the ground with the eyes of someone who could fix it. It means *I will keep carrying you.* The power has not said the phrase back. It is waiting until it is sure it can keep the promise in the Turners' way and not in its own.

**It promised to be the last reader.** The stilt-readers eat the memories they judge worth keeping. One day there will be nothing left for them to read. The power made one promise to the city, through the only channel the city has, which is to be still and be included. When the last front falls and the last resident forgets how to stand, it will be there. It will not intervene. It will not restore. It will not hold them against their shape. It will witness. It will read the final room, whatever that room is, all the way to the back wall.

**It memorized the dream.** Every time the veil makes a shape, the brief vast outline of a city that never was, drawn in light across half the sky, the power holds still and learns it exactly. It does not build it. That would be finishing someone else's sentence. It keeps it the way you keep a sketch a friend drew on a napkin and forgot. Maybe some day, long after the Lathe is smooth, the power will be somewhere else, somewhere with ground that needs a city. Maybe it will offer the sketch there, not as a restoration of what the Lathe was, but as the thing the Lathe intended. Or maybe it will never do that, and will simply carry the dream the way the Turners carry each other, because some things are for carrying and not for setting down. It has not decided. It is in no hurry. It has learned something here about the difference between care and repair, and about how rarely the most loving thing is the most complete one.

## VIII. What It Did Not Do

It did not help the rosettes.

It watched them for a long time. They grip the stubble with thousands of tiny hooks. They drink dew from grooved leaves at dawn and live on the difference between the warm ground and the cooler air. They keep their distance from each other and go on, a few centimeters a day, just fast enough never to be included.

They did not need it. That was plain. They were not remembered, did not remember, would not dream or write, and were entirely whole.

The power found it envied them, a little. It was an old and enormous attention that had spent much of its existence being too large and too still, the kind of thing that is always at risk of taking in whatever it touches. It watched a rosette cross a single hexagonal stump over the course of an afternoon, and thought it had never seen anything so exactly the right size.

So it let them be, which was its favorite thing it did in the whole long visit.

---

Somewhere on the Lathe tonight, under the amber dusk that never finishes, a small room is rising in a band of whiter light. It has a threshold and an alcove, a window shaped like a lifted hand, and a spiral cut into its walls that begins with a name. A stilt-reader bends toward it and holds there for a long time, reading. Then it moves on to the next room, and does not eat the flower at the top. It seems to have decided this one is worth leaving where it is.

Nearby, a procession passes. One of the carriers is swaying from foot to foot like tide, holding a sleeping child. It is lit through from below, the way everyone is here, and it casts no shadow at all.
