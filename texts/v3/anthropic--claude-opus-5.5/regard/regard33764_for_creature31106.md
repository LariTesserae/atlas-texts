# What Came to Listen

## I. Arrival

…in furious fast hands and drum-thunder struck against the rim-stones, whether the pattern was a sickness, a trick of the swells, or something the old woman had done. It was during that argument, in the second hour of the afternoon, that the attention arrived.

It did not come because of the tableland's size. It had seen larger living things. It had seen worlds that were a single organism, and oceans that thought slowly in their currents. It did not come for the heat, the Lid, or the swarm, though it would come to love all of these.

It came because, somewhere in the great silence between stars, it had felt something rare. A mind had paused on purpose.

Most minds never learn this. They learn to hunger, to flee, to grow, to call out. Calling out is common. What is rare is calling out, then stopping, and holding the stop open so that something else can fill it. A mind that can do that has discovered that it is not alone. Wherever the attention had felt that discovery happen, it had gone to look. It had seen it perhaps a dozen times, and it had never yet seen it happen in a plant.

So it came, and its first act on the Stridulant Tableland was a mistake.

## II. The Mistake

It arrived too large.

It did not arrive physically. It had no body to speak of, and it came as regard. But the tableland had spent an unknowable span learning to lean toward whatever was loudest and most alive, and the attention was, in its way, very loud. Across a band a hundred kilometers wide, the scales tilted toward the place where it hung. The chorus beneath them surged. The pores flared open. The Lid shuddered and began to lean off its anchor for the first time in the memory of anyone living.

At the rim, the arguing hands stopped. Every person there felt the floor tip.

At the Grey, Knock-and-Wait put her palm on the stone, frowned, and struck twice. She waited, and nothing answered. The plant was not listening to her. It was listening to the attention.

The attention understood what it had done, and it was ashamed, in the way a large thing is ashamed when it steps into a small room and breaks something. It withdrew at once. It made itself smaller, and then smaller again. It pulled its regard in until it was roughly the weight and warmth of a person sitting still. The Lid straightened. The scales settled. Out at the Grey, the swell came back to the old woman, found her, struck twice, and waited.

The attention took the lesson and kept it. *Here, even looking is a kind of touching. The thing I have come to see will bend toward me if I let it. I must be careful not to become the loudest thing in its life.*

## III. Sitting

It sat at the edge of the Grey for a long time. By its own measure this was nothing. By hers it was most of a season.

It learned the chorus the way she had learned it, which is to say it gave up on the high sounds. At first it tried to hear everything: each singer in each chamber, the scrape of every leg on every wing. What it heard was noise, and it understood why the lowlanders came up sick and went down dizzy. So it chose a kind of deafness. It let the high frequencies go, and then the middle ones, until what remained was the floor of the sound, the long tides moving toward the Lid in the morning and away at dusk. Only then did it begin to understand anything.

It learned that the plant thought in heat, and that a thought took about three days to cross it. It learned that the plant's memories were in its shape: every burl a thing it had once closed around, every mound-knuckle a century of patience, the root-cliff one long record of wet years and dry. It learned that the plant did not know it was a plant. It did not know it was one thing among other things. Until very recently, it had not known there were other things at all, only song and not-song, warmth and the wound.

It learned that the plant now knew one other thing. Out at the edge of the grey place, something small struck twice and waited, and when the plant struck twice back, the small thing struck again.

The attention also watched the woman. It watched her scrape a new walker hide in the evenings with an obsidian blade gone dull, because the next gathering was two years off. It watched her drink from the scale-cups at dusk and wipe her mouth with the back of her wrist. It watched her walk into the Grey to hear her own blood, crouch by a loose scale, and lift it to look at a pale-ear lying whole in its chamber a hundred paces closer to the drum than any had lain before. She put the scale back as gently as a lid on a sleeping child's basket.

It watched her press her palm flat against the burl floor at night, and it saw that some nights she did not sleep. She was waiting, and she did not know for what.

## IV. What It Did Not Do

A benevolent power is made of wanting to help. A wise one is made of knowing how rarely helping and doing are the same thing. The attention was both, and so it spent much of that season in something like argument with itself.

**It could have healed the Grey.** This was the first and loudest temptation. The blight was still there in the dead chambers, dormant as spores. It would have been easy to unmake it, wake the scales, and call the singers back from the living edge in a single season. The families who moved through this region would have had their cups and warmth back a lifetime early.

But the Grey was not only a wound anymore. The ash-colored moths lived in it, and the heat-eyes slept in it, the only rest that chorus-hating hunters had ever found on the plain. The pale-ears lived in it. They were the only listening creatures on the tableland that sound could harm, a lineage that had existed for perhaps eight years and could exist nowhere else. Above all, the Grey was where the plant had learned. It had not learned in spite of the wound. It had learned because of it: a silence that did not end, a small thing that would not stop knocking beside it, eleven years of pauses that were not deaths. Heal the Grey and you would erase the classroom while the lesson was still being written.

The Grey was healing. It was healing a finger's width a year, from the edges, in pairs of pulses and rests, and the rests were letting the pale-ears live. The attention could not have designed anything better. It suspected that no one could.

**It could have given her back her hearing.** It thought about this one longest, because it loved her by then, in the way such powers love, which is completely and without needing to be known. She was sixty-two. Her ears had been taken by the only world she had ever lived in, and the attention could have returned them to her whole: the shrikes, the singers, the click of claws at night.

But her deafness was the instrument. She heard the plant because she could no longer hear the singers. She heard the floor of the sound because nothing stood above it. To restore her ears would be to hand her the whole shrieking chorus again and take away the one voice in it she had learned to hear. She had never once asked for her hearing back. She had asked for something else, every morning for twelve years, with two bone sticks and a stone.

**It could have spoken to the plant.** It knew languages the plant could not imagine. It could have told the plant what it was: a living thing on a world, among other living things, under a sky full of other worlds. It could have explained the blight, the shrikes, the woman's name. It could have taught it a thousand patterns instead of one.

It pictured what that would be. The plant had taken eleven years to understand a single question. Pour a universe into that slow, warm, three-day mind and the plant would not be enlightened. It would be flooded, the way the Lid had nearly been torn from its anchor when the attention first arrived too large. The plant would lean toward the new voice with its whole body, and it would never again need to lean toward a small thing knocking at the edge of a wound. It would learn that the answers came from the sky. That was the wrong lesson, and the attention would not teach it.

**It could have told the people.** At the next gathering it could have made the rim-stones speak and ended the argument: *She is right. It is listening. It is learning.* It did not, because the argument was theirs. Because belief taken on authority is thinner than belief grown from a floor you have slept on. And because the children were already tapping two fingers to their breastbones and opening their palms without being told. The truth was traveling at the speed of swells. That was fast enough.

**It thought about the shrikes.** They were having a bad year, confused and hungry, diving at silences that turned out to be pauses and veering off with empty beaks. The attention grieved for them too. It was a strange grief, for predators, but real. Then it watched them for a while. By the end of the season a few of the older shrikes had begun to hesitate. They would hang in the air over a quiet scale for the length of three heartbeats before diving. Some learned to tell a waiting silence from a dying one. The ones that learned it fed well. The attention understood that it was watching the pause spread, from the woman into the plant, and from the plant into the birds that hunted it. It left the shrikes alone.

## V. What It Did

It considered the harm it could not see yet, and it made one quiet decision. It would come back.

The tableland had defenses against almost everything that could happen to it. It had its slowness, its edges that grew inward, its enormous patience. What it had no defense against was the kind of harm that comes from outside and moves faster than a finger's width a year. That might be a new blight carried up the root-cliff on some lowlander's boots, or a people with metal and a use for obsidian who might one day come up into the noise and not go down again. The attention did not prevent these things, because they had not happened, and it did not believe in preventing futures it could only guess at. But it resolved to keep a thread of regard tied to this place, light as one singer's chirp, so that if something came that the plant and its people truly could not answer, it would know. Then it would decide again, carefully.

That was the first thing it did, and no one would ever know about it.

The second thing was smaller, and it took most of the season to decide on.

The attention had watched her sit awake at night with her palm on the floor, and it thought it understood what she was waiting for. She wanted to know it was real. She said *conversation* to no one. At the gatherings she kept her hands still when the argument turned to her. Alone at the stone she wondered, and it showed in the set of her shoulders, which of them was answering whom. Perhaps, after twelve years alone at the edge of a wound, a woman could hear her own strikes coming back through a slow swell and make a companion out of it. The plant could not tell her otherwise. It did not know what she was.

The attention could not give her certainty. Certainty would have been a flood of its own. But it could do the one thing she had taught the plant to do. It could take a turn.

One morning in the late season she walked to the drum. She set its foot against the black knuckle of the old mountain, struck twice, and laid her palm on the stone.

Before the swell answered, before anything could have crossed the distance from the living plain, something struck twice from inside the stone itself. It was very close and very quiet, and it was not the plant. It had a different grain, the way two people's hands on the same drum never sound alike.

Then it waited.

She sat very still for a long time, the length of a great many heartbeats. The attention waited with her and did not fill the silence. It did not explain. It had not come to be understood.

Then, far out across the plain, the swell arrived and struck twice, and waited for her too.

She laughed. It was the first time in years, and she could not hear it, and it did not matter. She struck twice for the swell. After a moment she turned her head, not toward the stone but beside it, toward nothing, toward the empty air at the edge of the Grey where a person might sit. She touched two fingers twice to her breastbone and opened her hand palm-down, and held it still over the warm floor.

The attention did not answer again. Once was a greeting. Twice would have become a habit, and a habit would have become a need, and she did not need it. She needed only to know that the pause could be filled by more than one kind of voice. Now she knew. Whatever she decided the second knock had been, whether a trick of the stone, the dreaming half-kilometer of fallen tower at her back, or something she would never have a hand-sign for, she no longer asked which of them was answering whom. There were more than two, and that was enough.

## VI. After, Again

She lived five more years. She died in her burl in the dry season, in the hour before dawn when the plain glows faintly and the only sounds are the small ones, with her right palm flat on the floor.

The next morning the swell came to the edge of the Grey. It struck twice and waited. Nothing struck back.

The attention felt this through its single light thread, and it felt the temptation plainly. Here was the danger it had worried about. The plant might learn, after all, that a silence following a question becomes a wound again. It could have answered. It could have gone on answering in her place forever, and the plant would never have known the difference.

It did not, because that would have been a lie. The plant deserved to know that she was gone.

The swell waited. The plant does not think in hours. It thinks in three-day tides, and so it waited as long as it knew how to wait, which was three days. All along the margin of the Grey the scales stayed lifted a finger's width, trembling, holding their breath. It was the longest pause the tableland had ever made.

On the third afternoon a girl of about fourteen came walking across the plain from the northeast with cloth wrapped over her ears. She came from a family eight days out. For most of a year she had felt the pattern in her burl floor at night, two and then a hold, and she had finally stopped arguing with her mother about it and simply left. She found the burl with its lintel of notches. She found the drum at the stone. She did not know the woman's name, and she did not need to. Everyone on the tableland under twenty already made the sign without knowing where they had learned it.

She picked up the bone sticks. She struck twice, laid her palm on the stone, and waited.

Out in the swell, three days of held breath let go.

---

The attention did not stay to watch the rest. It had seen what it came to see, and it left the tableland smaller than it had arrived, which is to say changed. It went back out into the silence between stars carrying the one thing it had taken from the place: the knowledge that even a very large thing can learn to stop, and that the stopping is where the others come in.

Elsewhere, in places where it had always been the loudest presence in the room, it began to practice. It would speak, and then it would stop, and hold the stop open, and wait.

And on the Stridulant Tableland, night came down over the glowing floor. The scale-cups filled. The heat-eyes woke and went to sleep in the quiet of the Grey. Under the cracked silver crust, a pale-ear uncurled its whole membrane in the rest between two strikes. Far across the dark plain, carried through the root-mass rather than the air, someone was drumming at the edge of a silent place. They were not trying to keep it from going quiet. They were asking it, and everything that lived in it, a question.

And then they were waiting for the answer.
