# What Came to the Rim

## Arrival

It came the way the cold comes. It came at night, over the lip of the plateau, sliding down the terraces and pooling at the bottom.

It had been drawn by nothing loud. It had noticed the quiet: a mile and a half of people speaking low, and a sound from the working edge like someone brushing a horse in the next room. Places that loud with care are rare. It came to see what was being cared for.

The first thing it did was listen, and the second was to realize it could hear everything. It heard every whisper through every partition, every argument taken up to the plateau, every grandmother's cough three terraces up. It stood to the whole city as wall-kin stand to each other. It was on the other side of every wall at once.

So the first thing it learned from them was their courtesy. It did not repeat what came through the stone. It did not answer it. It behaved as though the walls were thicker than they were. That was the city's founding kindness, and a guest ought to keep the customs of the house before presuming to improve it.

## What It Was

It was old, in the way the rim is old.

It had been shaped a long time ago by whatever shapes such things, and it had set. It was not unkind for having set. The ringing ones in the city are not unkind either. But it knew that most of itself was heartwood: finished, load-bearing, holding up the shape of everything it had ever decided. If it had a wet edge anymore, it had not felt it in a long time.

It looked at the city and recognized itself turned inside out. The city was a creature whose body was almost entirely done, and whose life was a thin white band at the bottom. The power was the same, except that it had forgotten where its own band was.

That was the second thing it learned here, and it would matter later.

## The Temptations

A benevolent power arrives with its hands full. That is the danger. It spent a long while, weeks by the city's count, simply setting down the gifts it had brought, one at a time, and looking at them honestly.

**It could keep the stone soft.** This was the first and largest gift, and the most obvious. It could make the rock stay wet forever, so that nothing hardened and nothing had to be followed. Every room would stay workable and the city would never have to move.

It set this down almost at once. A stone that never sets cannot hold a name. The grandmother could never point up the wall to where she was born, because the wall would have been recut a hundred times. The dead would have nowhere firm to lie. *Before it sets* would become a phrase with nothing in it. The people would lose the one thing that tells them their time is real. Their whole grammar of tenderness depends on the fact that softness ends. You cannot love a child as soft if nothing is ever anything else.

**It could give them iron.** It could hand over good tools: harder edges, faster cuts, a thousand copper chisels instead of forty.

It set this down too. The city's pace is the stone's pace. The descent is slow because the cutting is slow, and that slowness is what keeps a generation to a generation, a room to a lifetime. Speed the tools and they would eat downward through the soft in a century. They would reach whatever lies at the bottom unready, with rooms that had never been lived in long enough to be left. And the forty chisels, which are named, kept, greased, sharpened back until they are the length of a finger, and laid for one night beside their bearers, would become merely old tools. Some things are precious because they are few, and the precious thing is worth more than the efficiency.

**It could fill the sump.** It could give them water that never shrinks, so the dry weeks never come and the water lines are never long and slow.

This one it held longest, because the dry season is hard and people suffer in it. But it watched the water lines. It watched a jar pass palm to palm up six levels without a word. It saw that the dry season is when the city is most one creature, and it saw that a need removed entirely is also a bond removed. And it knew something the people half-know: water that always comes becomes water that is owed. It did not want to be owed. It did not want to become the thing the basins were kept full for.

**It could drink the offerings.** This was the smallest gift and the most tempting. The families leave little basins of water below the sealed niches, and by morning they are dry, and they take that as acceptance, though it is only the sun and the stone.

The power could make the acceptance true. It could drink, and let them be right.

It did not. The families are not wrong about what matters. The gift is the giving, and the dead are honored whether or not anything drinks. To step into that small space would be to put itself where their grief goes. It would be taking something while pretending to give. It left the basins to the sun.

**It could tell them how deep the soft goes.** The oldest cutters keep a lineage of depth scratched at the bottom. Some say the wet edge will go on forever. Others say one day the picks will strike hard rock.

It could simply answer. This it did not set down so easily, because the answer turned out to matter.

## What It Found

It went down into the rock the way water does, through the pores.

It found the wet everywhere, rising slowly, carrying its lime toward faces that did not exist yet. It felt the whole mass of the soft stone like a held breath. It went down, and down, and at a certain depth it found what the pessimists had said it would find.

The soft ends. Not soon, but not forever either. By the city's measure it lies nine generations down, perhaps ten. Below that is a bed of grey stone that will never yield to antler or flint or copper. The picks will ring on it the way knuckles ring on the rim.

And it found a second thing, which no one in the city had noticed, because no one lives long enough to notice it.

**The light is leaving the bottom.**

Every generation the pit is deeper. Every generation the sun comes down the walls a little later in the morning and leaves a little sooner in the afternoon. The cold pools deeper and stays longer. The grandmothers remember midday warmth at the wet edge. Their grandchildren work in shade most of the day and do not know it was ever otherwise, because the change is always slower than a life. Each generation inherits a darkness it thinks is normal. The mist lifts later. The beans in the stone troughs have been moved up a terrace, and then another. No one has put these facts side by side.

So the city is not only descending toward a hard floor. It is descending toward a place where the sun barely reaches. That place is colder and darker, and the softness there will be worked by people who have forgotten what noon felt like at the bottom.

This the power sat with for a long time.

Then it followed the soft stone sideways, which is a direction the city has never thought to go, and found a third thing.

The soft does not only go down. Along the eastern side there is a seam. The good wet stone runs level and shallow, not deeper but *across*, under the plateau, for miles. It stays near the surface, near the light, near the grass. It is enough softness for a hundred generations, if they were to follow it outward instead of downward.

And there, directly over the start of that seam, is the fig tree.

The wild fig on the eastern rim has been putting its pale roots down the wall for longer than anyone remembers, flattened against the stone, feeling. The power traced the roots and found where they were going. They were not going down. They were bending east, into the seam, toward the shallow wet.

The fig had known all along. It is the only other thing in the city that moves the way the city moves, and it had simply chosen a different direction.

## The Problem of Knowing

Now the power had a real decision to make.

It could not in good conscience do nothing. To watch a people walk slowly into the dark, knowing a lighter way existed and saying nothing, is not humility. It is only a different kind of setting: a refusal dressed up as respect.

But it could not simply tell them either. A voice from the rim announcing where to cut would break things it had no right to break. The wet-keepers' authority rests on reading the stone. The oldest cutters' marks are a record made by hands. A city that has always found its own way would learn that the way could be handed down from above. After that, they would wait for the voice. Every hard decision would become a listening for instruction. The owl on the rim would stop being an argument and become an oracle, and the whole quiet, practical wisdom of the pit would drain toward a single point outside it.

What the power wanted was for them to find the seam themselves, in their own language, at their own pace, with the choice still theirs.

So it looked at how the city already learns where the wet is.

## What It Did

**It moved a little water.**

That was nearly all. Along the eastern side-cut, in stone below the fig, it opened a small seep. It was only a bedding-plane weep, the kind of thing that happens anyway. It was just enough water to rise through the seam and dampen a shaded face that had been drying.

Then it did nothing. It waited, which it was good at.

The moss came first, as the moss always does. A green line appeared on the eastern face. The line was thin at first and then widened, following the new wet along the level of the seam instead of down.

A wet-keeper found it, on a dawn walk with her palm flat on the stone. She felt cold where there should not have been cold. She tapped with her bone rod and heard a thud where she expected the beginning of a ring. She stood there a long time. Then she said the only thing wet-keepers say: *this face.*

That was the whole of the intervention. The rest is the city's.

Perhaps they will cut there. Perhaps a few households will open rooms along the seam, and find the stone good, and find, to their surprise, that the sun reaches them at midmorning the way it used to reach their grandmothers. Perhaps others will follow over a lifetime or two. Perhaps the city will learn a new motion: not only down but across, a root that has found a second way to grow. Perhaps someone will first say *where is the wet now?* and mean *east*.

Or perhaps they will decide the old way is the true way, and keep going down, and the eastern seep will stay a curiosity, a green line on a side wall. The power made its peace with that. It had put the truth where the city could read it, in the city's own ink. What they do with the truth is theirs. A gift that cannot be refused is a command.

The power judged that the seam was worth the risk of being ignored. It judged that a people told what to do would be worth less than a people who might choose wrongly but choose.

## Two Smaller Things

It did two other things, both small enough to be mistaken for weather.

**It gave the fig a good year.** The fig got rain at the right time and sun on its leaves, and it set more fruit than anyone could remember. The children ate figs on the eastern rim all through the late warm season, and some of them, lying in the grass with sticky hands, looked down the wall at the pale roots and wondered where they went. That was all. It did not tell them. It just gave them a reason to be sitting there, looking.

**It let the lizard be caught, once.** This was not strictly necessary. But there was a girl on the fourth terrace who had spent every noon of her childhood trying, perfectly still, perfectly patient. She was setting, the dangerous and precious age. The city says the ones who come closest become good gravers. The power thought she deserved to know that patience sometimes arrives. The lizard sat on her palm for the length of one breath, warm and grey and astonished, and then it was gone into a chisel crack.

She did not tell anyone. It was hers.

## What It Took

When it was ready to leave, it went up to the rim, to the lip where the oldest dead lie in grey stone with orange lichen. The wind passes over there with its long note like breath across a jar-mouth.

It thought about what it had learned.

It had come as heartwood, full of finished judgments. It had meant to be the one who gave. But the city had taught it things it did not have. It had learned the courtesy of not-hearing. It had learned that a thing's worth can depend on its ending. It had learned that the kindest gift is information in the other's own language, left where they can find it and free to be ignored. And it had learned that everything alive has a band, a thin white edge somewhere, where it is still soft. Even something as old as itself.

So before it went, it did the one thing it had never done in all its long setting.

It went back down. It did not go to the soft patch at the bottom of the sump; that is not for guests, and it stepped around it without looking, as the cutters do. It went to a plain, fresh face at the back of a new room on the wet edge, where no one was working yet. It let the stone press against it, instead of the other way around. It allowed itself, for one night, to be the thing that was shaped.

By dawn it had gone. Where it had been, the stone was a little whiter than the rest, wet and bright and almost translucent. It carried a mark no lineage used, a small arrangement that was not quite a name. The household that later opened that room thought a child had done it, or a wet-keeper making some private sign. They left it alone.

It is hardening now, as everything does.

And somewhere very far away, something old and set carries a new phrase in the part of itself it had forgotten was still alive. The phrase means *now, while there is still time*. It is said to children, to lovers, to the old, and now to powers that should have known better and have begun to learn.

*Before it sets.*
