# The Visitor

## The Middle of a Sentence

The account of the Crown breaks off in the middle of a word. *The only hard things left on it are the corest—*

The power found the Crown the same way, in the middle of a sentence. It was a sentence that had been going on for longer than there had been anything alive to read it, and it was nowhere near its end.

It did not arrive, exactly. Things that can go anywhere learn to arrive gently, or they learn nothing. It came the way everything comes to the Crown: up from the lowlands in the mid-morning, climbing the flanks inside the grey wall, cresting the rim at the hour the people call *the wall*. For the first time in a very long while it let itself be slow. It lay in the pearl among the ranks of the Harp and let the wires comb it. It beaded. It dripped. It found that it did not mind.

It had been drawn by the hum. A power like this notices a great many things, and most of them are loud: wars, fires, cities burning their coal. The hum on the heaviest nights was not loud. The power came because it could not tell what the hum was.

## The Question of Who

It had come to help *the being who lives here*. So its first task, before anything else, was to find out who that was.

It tried Duna Hessel first, because she was listening so hard. It stood behind her in the kiln rows and watched her tap the insulators with the back of her fingernail, and heard each one ring. A sound bell rang long. A flawed one rang short and dead. It understood that she was half-blind, and that her blindness and her listening had grown into each other like two trees on one rock. It filed this away carefully, the way you mark a place where the ground is soft.

It tried the Stood. It tried Orra and her crew and the green avalanches going over the rim. It tried Wen at the top of a pylon in grey nothing, finding up by the direction the water ran. It tried the cupbirds in their cracked bells, each one looped around with a little bypass of green copper. It tried the kept, lying soft and calm in their buried rooms with porcelain in their hands. It tried the mountain.

Each time, the edges would not hold. The moss was in the town and the town was in the moss. The dead were the floor of the living. The birds lived in the bells, the bells came from the clay, the clay came from the rotting stone, and the stone was being slowly tended by a shaggy thing made of its own moss. When the power followed any one of them far enough, it found itself following all of them.

So it came to the conclusion that anyone who stays on the Crown long enough comes to. The being who lives here is the whole slow creature: mountain and moss and town and dead and birds and cloud. Its breath is the cloud tide, twice a day. Its heartbeat is the hum, once a year. Its thoughts, if it has any, are four thousand people speaking softly inside the fog.

That made the question of help much harder. A person can be helped by being given what they want. It is far less clear how to help a creature whose parts are busily eating one another.

## The Things It Did Not Do

A power like this is mostly made of the things it declines to do. The power sat with each one for a full turn of tides before it set it down.

**It did not stop the rot.** It could have. It could have reached into the grain boundaries of the grus and sealed them, pressed the dome back into granite, and made the Crown hard and high again, a peak with edges. It looked at this for a long time, because it is exactly the kind of thing a benevolent power is tempted to do: *this is falling apart, so I will make it whole.* But the Crown was not falling apart the way a wounded thing falls apart. It was coming apart the way a seed pod does. The rot was what made the kaolin. The kaolin made the porcelain. The porcelain made the bells, and the bells made the birds' homes. A hard peak would have no moss, no Kell, no Stood. It would be alive only in the way stone is alive, too slowly for anything to notice, and it had already had that life. It had had ten million years of it. The power understood that you do not heal an old thing by making it young. Doing that only erases it.

**It did not give them the sun.** It thought about the children who cast shadows for one hour a day, and about babies named in the clear while their first shadow is still on the stone. It could have thinned the cloud. But the clear hour is precious because it is brief. A town that stops work to stand side by side on the catwalks, quarrels unresolved and truces unspoken, would not stand there if the sun stayed out all day. The power recognised that it would be giving them light and taking away the reason they stand together.

**It did not tell them where the cupbirds came from.** It knew. It could see the whole history in one glance: the birds before the Harp, and the place they had waited. It will not say here either. The question belonged to Kell. It was one of the few things on the mountain that was still wholly theirs, a thing to wonder about near the kilns on winter nights. An answer from outside would have been a kind of theft.

**It did not tell Pell what the hum was.** It went down the Drowned Stair and lay beside him in the dark, its ear to the floor, and listened. It understood the hum. It kept what it understood.

**It did not ease the town's quiet guilt.** Everyone in Kell knows that each crate going down the funicular is a piece of the mountain leaving, and no one says it aloud. The power could have lifted that weight from all four thousand of them at once. It considered it and decided that a people who leave the cracked bell on the tower, who whisper *it's me* at sealed doors, and who never tip the moss into sunlight are made careful partly by that small, unspoken weight. It did not want to make them careless. It only wanted, if it could, to make them less alone with the weight. That is a different thing, and much smaller.

**It did not heal Duna's eyes.** At least, not without asking.

## The Thing It Did Without Asking

It broke its own restraint only once, and it did so without hesitation.

In its second winter on the Crown, the power was lying at the bottom of the Drowned Stair again, listening. It felt the stair begin to fail. Frost-heave and meltwater had been working at the old tunnel through the packed moss for a century, and now it was ready to let go. That would be in hours, not years. Above it, Pell and three other milkies were coming down in the dark with a stub of candle in a porcelain cup, to listen.

There are principles that are only principles. Then there is a child in a tunnel.

The power held the Drowned Stair. It held it the way stone holds things, without strain and without anyone noticing. The children came down, lay with their ears to the floor, heard the hum, and went back up very quiet, as children always did. The power kept holding until the last bare heel had cleared the top step. Then it let go.

The stair came down with a long soft grinding that the moss could not fully swallow. In the morning the adults said an old floor had slumped in the night, and sealed the breach, and marked it. The children gave the Drowned Stair up. Within a year they had a new name for the place where it had been, *the Stair That Waited*, and they told the younger ones that it had fallen down on purpose, a little late, out of politeness.

The power was not sure they were wrong. It had only held the stair. The waiting might have been the stair's idea.

## The Knock

It had watched the Kell way of visiting long enough to understand it. You do not knock, because the moss takes the sound. You open the door and lean in and say, softly, *it's me.*

So one night in deep winter, in the ticking warmth behind the kilns, the power opened a door that was not quite a door and leaned in toward Duna Hessel. She was awake on her straw pallet, listening to the kiln walls cool.

*It's me,* it said. It did not use a voice. It came to her the way the hum comes up through the floor of the accumulator sheds, felt before it is heard.

Duna did not startle. She had spent forty years listening for flaws in bells she could not see, and she was very hard to surprise. She turned her head toward it, the way she did when the Harp was singing and she had caught a wrong note somewhere in the ranks.

"You've been about a while," she said. "Two winters. The moss has been odd."

It admitted that it had.

"You'll be the thing that holds stairs up."

It admitted that too.

She was quiet for a time. The kilns pinged. Somewhere down the row a new mother murmured to a baby.

The power offered her eyes. It said it plainly: she could see again if she wished, clear and sharp, the colour of the flame through the ports, the faces of her apprentices, the dark-water blue of the sky in the clear hour.

Duna thought about it with the care of someone who has judged temperatures to within a few degrees her whole life.

"If I could see," she said at last, "I'd stop listening like this. Wouldn't I."

It told her the truth, which was that it did not know. Perhaps, a little.

"Then no," said Duna. "Not yet. Ask me again when I'm dying. I might want to see the clear once more, at the end. But I've got bells to hear till then." She paused. "That's not what you really came about, though. You didn't come all this way for my eyes."

It had not. It had come about the hum, and it said so.

"And what is it?"

The power did not answer. She had not really expected it to. She nodded slowly, satisfied, the way you nod at an insulator that rings true.

"I'll ask you something else, then," she said. "One thing. You can tell me or not." She took a long breath. "Is it all going? The mountain. Is it just going? Is that all we are, the thing that helps it go?"

## What It Gave

The power did not answer that one either, not in words. Words were the lowland way, and she would not have trusted them.

It let her listen.

For the length of one breath, and only one, it let Duna Hessel hear what it could hear. Down the funicular, out through the cloud, across the lowlands and all their cities, every piece of porcelain that had ever left the Crown was ringing. There were insulators on ten thousand towers in the lowland wind. There were cups on shelves in kitchens where no one had heard of Kell, tile under the feet of children, and laboratory glassware in rooms of white light. Every piece had been fired in the dusk surge from the rotted heart of the mountain, and every one was ringing, faintly, in its own place, in the mountain's own note. Most of them rang long and clear. A few rang short and dead. They were far more than anyone could count, spread across the whole world.

Duna knew the note of every pattern the Works had ever made. She knew them all.

Then it was over, and there was only the ticking of the kilns.

She lay still for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was lower than usual, even for Kell.

"It's going hard again," she said. "Isn't it. We take the soft of it and put it in the fire and it comes out hard. One bell at a time. It's not going. It's being *fired*."

The power said nothing, because there was nothing it needed to add.

"The only hard things left on it," Duna murmured, the way she would begin a story near the kilns, "are the corestones, and what we make of it. And what we make of it goes everywhere." She laughed, very softly. "The mountain's not getting lower. It's getting *wider*."

That was all. It did not change what she knew. She had half-known it for forty years, and it was her own story. It had been told near the kilns before the power ever came, told carefully, as a story and not as a belief. All the power had done was let one old woman hear that the story rang true.

She would go on telling it. She would tell it as a story, and pour another cup. Some of the apprentices would believe her and some would not. The weight in the town would stay where it was, because the power had not lifted it. But from then on, a few people near the kilns would carry it a little differently, the way a crate carries better when you know what is inside.

## The Night With the Stood

Before it left, the power went out to the outer ranks of the Harp on the thickest night of the year and waited.

The Stood came up from the flanks near the sag. Its hooves sank deep in the sponge, and moss grew in its fleece. The little sundew behind its ear had grown since Wen saw it, and now there were two. It browsed very carefully among the pylons, taking the tops off the cushions and leaving the roots.

The power did not approach it. There was nothing to fix. It had considered whether the Stood was alone and needed companions, and it had looked down the flanks below the cloud line, where no one from Kell goes, and seen what was there. What it saw is not recorded here. It was not the power's to tell.

It remembered the old story instead. The keeper was set walking on the dome to stand very still among the corestones, *so the mountain would have something like itself to look at.*

So the power stood still too.

For the rest of the night, in the outer ranks, two perfectly still things stood among the pylons in the pearl. One was made of moss and wet wool. The other was made of something that had been everywhere and had chosen, for one night, to be only here. Water beaded on the wires above them and ran down. The Harp dripped. The mountain, which had lived alone in stone-time for longer than anything could remember, had two things like itself to look at for a single night instead of one.

The hum that year was very faint, and very long.

## The Leaving

It left the way the cloud leaves, at dawn, draining off the edges of the plateau in a slow white fall. It did not leave in the clear hour itself. It had learned that much from the scrapers. It went down with the last of the night vapour while the town was still waking, before the light came, and folded into the valleys without a sound.

Wen Asherby was at the top of a pylon on the outer ranks, early for the clear. She saw the cloud go and felt, briefly, the thing she only felt on heavy days: the charge in her teeth, the fern on her arm prickling. Then it passed. She did not think much of it. She raised her palm flat and slowly turned it outward, the way you wipe a window. She could not have said why, or at whom.

The clear came. The town came out onto the catwalks and bridges and stood in the thin cold light, watching the cloud pour off the world, not talking much. A baby was named. Two people who had quarrelled stood side by side. Duna was carried out to the rim by her apprentices and sat with her face turned toward a sun she could barely see. She was listening to the Harp.

Then someone saw the wall rising over the edge, and the sign rippled down the catwalks, *rising*, and everyone went back inside the day.

Nothing on the Crown was different. The grus kept rotting. The pits kept deepening. The moss kept coming and the scrapers kept pushing it back. The birds kept their broken bells, and the dead stayed in the floors. The crates went down the funicular into grey nothing, a piece of the mountain in each one, going out into the world to become hard again.

Only one person noticed anything at all, and she did not say it aloud. On the Low Terraces, for about a week after the cloud drained off that morning, the moss grew back slower. Orra Vane noticed it, the way she noticed everything about the moss. She leaned on her scraper at the top of a step that should have needed doing and did not, and looked at it for a long time.

It was the way the moss behaved after a boulder went over the rim. She said nothing. She did not smile, and nobody on her crew smiled either.

She had always said it was out of respect.
