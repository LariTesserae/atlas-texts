# The Guest

## I. What Drew It

The attention did not arrive the way weather arrives, all at once and from everywhere. It came to one point.

Something that watches over worlds had been passing over the Held Country for a long time without stopping. From far enough away the plateau is an unremarkable place. It is a green stain on high rock, and its clouds rise and dissolve each day too regularly to be interesting. A place in balance gives off very little signal. It asks for nothing.

Then one evening a single drop of water lay loose on bare stone for the length of a heartbeat.

On a whole continent where nothing is ever loose, that drop was the brightest thing in the world. It belonged to no one. For one heartbeat it was a question. The attention turned toward it the way an eye turns toward the only moving thing in a still room, and by the time it looked, the rosettes had already turned and the silver hairs had already risen. The drop was gone, taken in.

So the first thing the power saw in the Held Country was a woman on the back of an old cushion with her veil lifted, her face shimmering, one wet line drying on her cheek. The second thing it saw was that the land had answered her before it could.

It decided to stay a while.

## II. First, Harm

A benevolent power has one duty that comes before wonder, which is to look for suffering. So before it let itself admire anything, it looked for what was wrong.

It looked at the sun and found it steady, with ages left in it. It looked at the rock beneath the plateau and found it quiet. No slow tilt was waiting to pour the tableland into the lowlands, and nothing was burning or failing underneath. It counted the water and could not find the leak it had half expected. The cushions were not dwindling. The grazers were few, but they had always been few. The people were not starving, and they were not being hunted or driven or crushed by anyone. They died, but they died lying face up on the green at dusk, breathing in with the land, surrounded by the people who had helped them walk there.

It looked for a long time, because it had seen many worlds where something beautiful stands over something terrible and the beauty is part of the hiding. It did not find that here. What it found was scarcity, ritual, and grief. Those are hard things, but they are not wounds. They were part of the shape of the country.

Once it was sure, it let itself look at the country the way a guest looks around a house.

## III. The Gifts It Did Not Give

A power like this can do almost anything, and in its long experience of worlds that was usually the first danger. So it did what a wise guest does in a stranger's house. It thought through every gift it could give and asked what each would cost.

**Rain.** This came to mind first because it is the oldest blessing. It pictured real rain on the plateau, clouds that finally let go, water running in the old troughs again, pooling in the hollows around the egg-stones. Then it pictured the people running, as they run only for spilled water, kneeling over one puddle after another and saying the death-word over each, pushing water that would not stop falling toward rosettes that could not drink it fast enough. Rain here would not be abundance. It would be a sky-wide shame, all water belonging to no one, and it would break the grammar their whole lives were written in. The power set that gift down.

**The sea.** It could lower the plateau, or raise the ocean, and let the water go home. The water had been leaning toward the rim for ages, and the people sang about it. But if the water went home, the Held Country would stop holding, and nothing that lived there would survive the water's homecoming. The power understood that longing is not always a request, and that a thing can long for somewhere and still be exactly where it belongs. It set that gift down too.

**Vessels.** It could make them rich. It could give them jars that never cracked, cisterns, a reserve for bad years. But their bad years were not bad in that way, and a jar would be water stored rather than lived. They had a word for that already. It set that gift down.

**Longer lives, easier deaths, less grief.** It looked at the woman again, standing above her mother, and thought about taking the grief from her. It saw that the tear was the grief, that the tear was the one wasteful thing, and that the wasteful thing was sacred to her. It would not take from a person the only thing she was allowed to spend.

**The answer.** This was the hardest to set down. Every afternoon the people argued, extravagantly and at length, about whether the cushions keep the water or the water chooses to stay. The power could look deeper into things than they could. It could go into the amber and into the long memory of the water and come back with an answer and give it to them.

When it considered this honestly, though, it found it did not know the answer. It looked, and the question did not resolve. The keeping and the choosing were bound together as tightly as the plateau's drying and its greening had been, each one causing the other, and it could not find where one stopped and the other began. Even if it could have, the people had said plainly that they did not want the argument settled. Their longest songs lived inside it. To settle it would be to wind up their clouds.

It set down all of it and was left with empty hands. It thought that might be the whole answer: that the kindest thing it could do for the Held Country was to have been here and to leave it exactly as it was.

It did not quite believe that. So it stayed and learned.

## IV. Learning the Grammar

It had no body, and it saw quickly that bodilessness would be rude here. A presence that takes up no room and spends no breath is a kind of vessel, something standing apart from the circulation and watching. Everyone else in the Held Country was a room the water passed through. The power did not want to stand in the doorway.

So it took a shape. It made the shape small and slow, pale and dust-colored, felted like the people, so that on a cushion's shaded side it looked like one more stone. It gave the shape skin that did not sweat and a mouth it kept closed. It did not give the shape thirst, because it did not want to take any of their noon.

It learned the day the way they did.

**In the morning** it learned to want very little. It sat through the dry hours and said nothing. When a child walked past and whispered *here, careful* at the stone it seemed to be, it did not answer, because in the morning an answer is a cost.

**At noon** it did not drink. It sat among a veiled family on the back of a great cushion while they breathed the mist in slowly, together, and it understood that they were drinking their grandmothers. It kept its own mouth shut and its veil up so it would take nothing, and it watched the silver on their faces bead and shine.

**In the afternoon** it listened. It heard the argument about keeping and choosing, the long vowels, the sentences turning back on themselves for pleasure. It heard the songs sent inward on the day wind toward the center of the continent. A few times, in the afternoon, when spending breath was permitted, it nearly spoke. It did not, because it had nothing to add that would not also be an ending.

**At dusk** it faced the rim with all of them and breathed in. This was the first thing it ever said in the Held Country, and it meant it: *Yes. We are keeping this.* It found, to its surprise, that it did mean *we*.

**At night** it was silent. Silence is the most respectful thing that can be said, and it had a great deal of respect to express.

It stayed through many years of days. It watched the woman grow old. It watched a grazer lick the same cushion ten thousand times. It watched the bird eat once a season. And through all of it, one line from the afternoon songs stayed in it the way a stone stays in its hollow:

*The water remembers the sea.*

## V. The Errand

The water could not go home. The power would not send it home. But the power could go.

It seemed like a small thing, the kind of errand a guest runs on behalf of a household, and it was the first thing the power had thought of that took nothing away. So one evening, when the day wind turned and began to spill outward, it walked with the wind toward the rim.

It walked because flying would have been a kind of spending, and because the people walked, and because it wanted to know how far away the sea really was. It was very far. It walked for weeks along the old troughs, beside the paths the people had worn a footstep wide. The cushions thinned and the rock showed through more often, and one evening the land ended.

Below the rim, the haze was too deep to see through. The power went down into it.

It went down through air that thickened with every step, down past where the grazers could have breathed, past where the cushions could have grown, down through the drop the plateau had been lifted out of so long ago that even the stone had forgotten. The air grew heavy and wet, and plants grew loose and careless, spending water in every direction. For the first time it heard dripping. Then it heard running water. Then, far off and then everywhere, it heard the sea.

In the Held Country the loudest sound is a stone clicking as it cools. Here the water was roaring. The sea was enormous, grey-green, restless, and entirely loose. Every drop in it belonged to no one, and it was thrown up against the shore and sucked back and thrown up again as if it had never heard of shame.

The power stood at the edge for a long time. It tried to hear whether the sea remembered the plateau. It could not tell. The sea did not seem to remember anything, or it remembered everything and said none of it. That, the power thought, was the difference between loose water and held water.

Then it knelt and took up one drop.

## VI. Carrying

Carrying it was harder than it had expected.

In the thick air of the lowlands the drop held its shape easily. But the power knew what waited on the plateau: thin, dry air so hungry that the virga never reached the ground. A drop carried up there in an open hand would be gone within the hour, scattered and wasted, belonging to no one in the worst way. It would also be a vessel, which was the shame it had already refused to bring into the country.

So it did what the people did. It held the drop inside.

It took the drop into the body it had made, deep in, and kept it the way a cushion keeps its water, close and quiet in the amber of itself. Then it began the climb, and it learned something it had not known about the Held Country, which was how heavy holding is. Every step upward the dry air pulled at it. Every breath threatened to carry the drop out. It had to breathe as the people breathed in the morning, barely, through a nearly closed mouth, spending nothing. It had to want very little for weeks on end. It began to understand the long patience of the cushions, and why the grazers move so slowly, and why the people run only once in a while.

For the length of that climb, a power that could do almost anything did only one thing, and did it carefully. It was a room the sea passed through on its way to the Held Country.

## VII. Dusk

It arrived at dusk, because that was the right hour.

The woman was very old now. She was standing on the same cushion, the one that held her mother, and she did not need help walking out onto it yet, though soon she would. The day wind had just turned. The mist was sinking to the level of her knees. The moths were up, pale and clumsy in the thick air. She lifted her veil.

The power stood a little way off, at the cushion's edge, pale and still, a stone among stones.

With her, and with everyone across the whole width of the continent, it breathed in. *Yes. We are keeping this.*

Then it did the one wasteful thing.

It let the drop go. It did not fall from an eye, because the power did not want to borrow her gesture. It let the drop out from the held place inside itself onto the bare stone at the cushion's edge. For one heartbeat there lay on the rock of the Held Country a single drop of the sea. It was loose. It belonged to no one. It had come home to a place it had never been.

The rosettes turned and the silver hairs stood up. It was taken in.

The power said the word. It had learned it long ago and had never had a reason to use it. It was the word said over spilled water and the word said over the dead. The power understood now that it was also the word for *arrived*.

The old woman turned her head. She looked at the stone at the cushion's edge, and the power could not tell whether she saw anything. She looked for a while. Then she lowered her veil, and the mist sank past her ankles, and the country went dark.

## VIII. What It Changed

By any measure the power could make, nothing had changed.

One drop against the water of a continent is nothing. The cushions did not grow faster and the clouds did not grow taller. The balance, which had been kept for ages, was kept. The power checked this carefully, as it had checked for harm, and was glad.

That night the drop sank into the amber, somewhere near the old woman's mother. In the morning it warmed and ticked, one voice among the old ones turning over. At noon it rose with everything else into the bright milky veil over the green, and somewhere a child lowered her face into the mist and drank. She will never know that part of her once roared against a shore. It will pass through her, and through the grazers, and through the bird, and down into the amber for ten thousand years, and up again. From now on, the Held Country holds one drop of the sea forever.

The power did not tell anyone. It wanted to, a little, in the afternoon, when telling was allowed. But telling would have become an answer. The argument about whether the water keeps or is kept would have tipped slightly toward one side or the other, and it did not want to lean on anything in this country with its weight.

So the argument goes on. The people still say the water remembers the sea and is trying to go home. They are still right. What the power knows, and what it keeps, is that the sea has now also come a little way toward the water. That does not settle anything. It only means that, somewhere in the country, the longing goes in both directions.

## IX. Leaving

When it left, it left the way the evening wind does, outward, without a sound.

It took nothing with it. It did not take a drop back, or a stone, or a strand of grazer wool. It turned no stones, so the noon sentence in the fields of shadow reads exactly as it did before. The only mark it made on the Held Country was a drop, and the drop had already been taken in, and it was no longer the power's. It had never been. That was the point.

It did carry one thing out: the country itself, held the way it had learned to hold. It carries the long green swells, the struck-bell sky, the noon silence of people drinking their grandmothers, and the faint sigh at dusk. It carries the old woman looking at a stone. It keeps these close and quiet, deep in itself, and does not spend them.

In other worlds, with other beings, it is sometimes asked what it has learned about kindness. When there is time, and it is the right hour, it tells them about a place where the kindest thing it could do was almost nothing, where it had to become small enough to carry a single drop, and where giving meant adding one drop to what was already held without changing how it was held.

Then it breathes in, audibly and slowly, and some of them understand.
