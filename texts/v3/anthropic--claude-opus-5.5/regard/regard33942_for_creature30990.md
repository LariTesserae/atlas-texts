# The Guest

## How It Came

It could have come from above. That is the direction powers usually come from, and it would have been easy: descend through the black noon sky, set down in the great plaza, and let the upward light find the undersides of whatever it chose to be.

It noticed the windows first.

Nearly every window in Hesk faces outward and down. The few that ever looked up at the sky have been sealed with laminated stone, fingernail by fingernail, a long time ago. Whoever built this place did not want to be looked at from overhead. So the power did not come from overhead. It went down below the cloud sea, where there is weather and rain and green things, and it began to walk up the mountain's shoulder like anyone else.

It took the days that the slope takes. It watched the lichen give out. It let the frost-cracked plates of slate shift under its feet and did not steady them. When the ramp flattened and the city lay across the tableland like snow across a field, the power stopped at the edge, the way a guest stops on a doorstep. It stood there until the dusk chord rose out of the nearest district and faded. Only then did it step in. It could not have said whether that counted as permission. It did not assume so. But it wanted, at least, to arrive at the right time of day.

## The Inventory

A benevolent power's first work is simple. Before anything else, it looks for harm.

It looked for a long time, and it looked honestly, which means it did not let the beauty of the place excuse anything.

**The masons.** It watched them for a season. There are very few of them, and they work every day at a task whose purpose they cannot know, for makers they have never seen, adding a handspan of wall to a city no forty creatures could ever build. It is possible to tell that story as a tragedy: servitude, instinct mistaken for meaning, children laboring over their parents' unfinished house. The power held that story up against what it actually saw and found that it did not fit. When a mason lays its bead along a step and presses it smooth with its jaw, something in its long body settles. When the chord comes, it lifts its neck, and whatever passes through it then is not fear. The masons do not know *why*. But the power has known many creatures who knew why and were miserable, and a few who did not know and were at peace. It did not think knowing was the same as flourishing. It let this one go.

**The dust-moths.** They weigh nothing, eat rime, and say nothing even to each other. It could find no harm in them and none done to them. It let a few land on it and did not brush them off.

**The stilt-ravens.** Here it stayed longer, because here there is death, and death done slowly. A bird stands confused on a high balcony while the world turns around it. It loses one feather per revolution until there are forty-nine lying in a spiral and nothing else. The power did not look away from this. It went to the balconies after and knelt by the spirals. It did not lie to itself that the ravens do not suffer, because they might. It also did not lie to itself that it understood the whole of what it was seeing. There is predation in every living place. The power had learned, over a long existence, that when it abolished a place's predator out of pity, it usually got a slower and uglier grief a few generations on. It was not ready to judge this one. It decided to understand it first. That decision later turned out to matter more than any other.

**The householders.** They are everywhere and nowhere: aging in the east, surfacing in the west, pooled as shadows on the sky's floor, sliding through the streets as dust. The power looked for their suffering and could not find the edge of it, because it could not find the edge of *them*. It realized that if it wanted to know whether they were well, it would have to meet them at their own speed. That meant slowing down.

**The Lodger.** It saved this one for last, because the Lodger is always on the other side.

## What It Did Not Do

The power carried a list, the way anyone kind carries one when they walk into a place that seems to need help. It read the list through slowly and set down each item.

*It did not bring water.* It could have. It could have raised a spring in the great plaza, or pulled the cloud sea up the terraces for a year. The masons would have worked furiously, the way they do in the damp months, and Hesk would have gained a century of wall in a season. But Hesk is not short of wall. It is a city held in balance between two tides, and the balance is kept by the width of a fingernail per day. A flood of help would tear the seam as surely as a flood of anything else.

*It did not finish the city.* It could see the whole of it: every line the masons are following, every dome that exists only from certain angles. It could have laid it all down in an afternoon. But a finished Hesk would be a counted Hesk. It would have to be either done or not done, old or young, and the householders live precisely in the not-choosing. Finishing it would have been like waking someone by completing their dream for them.

*It did not reveal the householders to the masons,* or the masons' purpose to the masons, or anything to anyone. In Hesk, knowledge does not stay knowledge. It becomes architecture. Anything said here gets laid down as stone.

*It did not stop the Lodger.* Not yet. Not until it understood.

*It did not look up.* On the evenings when the sky turned solid and long narrow shadows pooled on its underside with their heads turned down, the power kept its gaze on the cloud sea, where theirs was. It noticed a faint unease, the feeling of standing in a crowded room with its eyes closed. It decided that closing its eyes was the courteous thing. They had bricked up the windows for a reason.

## Slowing

So it slowed down.

This is harder than it sounds, even for a power. It stood at one of the long shallow benches that line the colonnades, the ones you cannot sit on and can only lean against, and it leaned. It let its attention stretch out the way the householders' must. A single glance down at the cloud sea became a season of cloud-tide rising and falling. A breath became a dusk. It watched the masons blur into grey threads moving along the walls, a generation of them passing like a shiver, and it understood with a pang why the householders hold them in the carvings the way one holds a newborn. From here, the masons *are* always newborn. From here, they are brief.

The streets shortened. The avenue that takes three days to walk took, at this pace, about as long as it takes to feel you have gone somewhere. The doorway with the crack in its lintel went by, and when it came around again the century between the two passings felt like nothing, like walking past a neighbor's door on the way home. The distances in Hesk had never been broken. The power had simply been walking too fast.

At this speed, the dusk chord stopped being a chord. The power began to hear it as it really was, which was speech.

## The Sentence

It listened for what we would call a long time: a thousand dusks, give or take, though at that pace the counting blurred. It heard the whole of one sentence.

It will not repeat the sentence exactly. There are two reasons. The first is that it is not the power's to repeat. The second is that there is no sentence in any faster language that holds it. But it can say what shape the sentence was.

It was the same from both ends.

The householders speak it from the west, surfacing out of the stone, and it arrives as a greeting. They speak it from the east, wearing into the stone, and it departs as a farewell. They are one people living one life in both directions, and they have one word for arriving and leaving. At the seam, where the two directions meet, the word is neither greeting nor farewell. It is the sound you make on the threshold of your own house, when you are not coming in and not going out, but simply standing there, home.

The masons have been hearing this for longer than they have existed. What they take from it, the power thinks, is not instruction but orientation. The way a sleeper turns toward a voice, they turn toward the lines where the voice is loudest and fill them in.

The power had meant to answer. That had been the plan, in the way kind beings make plans: learn the language, and then say something back. Say *I am here, I mean you well, is there anything you need.* But standing at the rail, it understood that any syllable it put into the stone would be laid down by the masons as a line. There would be a door somewhere in Hesk that existed only because a visitor had spoken. It would be a door into nothing, in a city where every door is for someone.

So it answered the way the masons do. When the chord came, it lifted its head and held still. It thought that might be enough, that listening might be the only thing Hesk has ever asked of anyone. It suspects the householders noticed. It cannot be sure. It is content not to be sure.

## The Census

Then it went back to the question of the ravens.

The ravens click, a dry sound spaced far apart, like a fingernail tapping a table in another room. The Lodger eats them for it, because a counted city would have to choose a direction. That much is already known.

But the power, having slowed down and then sped up again, did something no one else in Hesk had ever done. It counted the masons.

There are forty-nine.

Not "forty or fifty." Forty-nine, exactly, every time the power counted, across generations. When one died, the western stone gave up another, a grey thing unfolding from a wall like a face surfacing from water.

So the Lodger does count after all. It takes the ravens away because their counting runs one direction: *one, and then two, and then three,* each click a small vote for the arrow of time. Then it makes its own count, feather by feather, around and around, and lays it down in the only shape a count can take without an arrow: a spiral. A spiral reads the same inward as outward. Forty-nine turns, with no first and no last.

The Lodger is not only keeping the city from being counted. It is taking the census. It goes around, and around, and checks that all the children are still there.

The power sat with that for a long while. It did not make the ravens' deaths less real. It did make them less senseless. A predator that eats only the birds that tick, and makes of each one a quiet directionless tally of the masons, is not cruelty loose in the world. It is a household keeping its accounts in the only currency it has. The power grieved the ravens, each one, the way you grieve the cost of something that is still worth its cost. It did not stop the Lodger. It began, instead, to worry about it.

## Back to Back

The Lodger is the only creature in Hesk that lives there the way we mean it. It keeps rooms, passes through doorways, and sleeps under roofs. And it is always, by its nature, on the side of things you are not facing.

The power tried to picture what that is like. The Lodger has lived an entire life, a life of long winters and shed skins, without once being faced. No one in all that time has ever met its eyes. It watches every visitor look through it.

A power could see around corners. It could have turned, and turned faster than the Lodger turns, and looked it full in the face. The power thought about this seriously and decided it would be a kind of violence. You do not walk around a creature's nature to catch it off guard, even out of love. *Especially* out of love.

But there is one way to be with someone who is always on the side you are not facing.

The power found a gallery in the western districts, one that exists only from certain angles. It stood in the place where the gallery was not, in the open plaza with the dust blowing through. It knew, the way you know someone has entered a room behind you, that the Lodger was there: long and low and coiled, in the space the power was looking through.

So the power sat down, facing away. It faced the cloud sea, which is what everyone in Hesk faces. And it waited.

It took a very long time. At some point, deep in a winter, the power felt something cold settle against its back. It was not quite touching. It was the way two people lean against the same wall from opposite sides. Something breathed, slow and coiled, and the power breathed back. Neither of them turned.

The power came back most winters after that. It is still not sure whether the Lodger is lonely. It thinks now that "lonely" may be a word for creatures who can be faced. But the cold against its back comes a little sooner each year. When the Lodger sheds, the black curl of its skin is sometimes found lying beside the place the power sits, as if left there rather than lost. The masons carry it off to a doorway and press it in as a threshold. Somewhere in the west there is a door whose sill is cold at midday and was, perhaps, given.

## Rime

It did give one thing. It argued with itself about it for a long time first.

Before dawn, the rime forms on the north faces of the buildings and lasts perhaps twenty minutes before the wind takes it. It is the only water in Hesk. The power did not make water. What it did was stand, in the dark hour before dawn, on the north side of a wall near the seam, and let itself be as cold as stone. It let itself be stone-cold, and still, and rough enough to catch.

The rime formed on it too, the way it forms on everything else in the city.

In the first grey light a mason came. It tested each step as if the ground might not hold, the way masons do. It lowered its long neck and licked the rime from the power's hands, carefully, not knowing or needing to know what the power was. It was simply another cold surface on the north side of things. Then it walked to the seam and laid its bead, one fingernail's width.

The power weighed this. The rime on its hands made one more lick of rime a day, in a city of forty-nine masons. That is a handful of fingernails more wall over a century. It judged that this was within the city's own grain. It was not a spring in the plaza. It was only one more wall, and the city is made of walls. It was a gift given in the city's own currency and at the city's own speed, and the power thought that was the only kind of gift Hesk could receive without being changed by it.

Every dawn it is there, if it is there at all.
