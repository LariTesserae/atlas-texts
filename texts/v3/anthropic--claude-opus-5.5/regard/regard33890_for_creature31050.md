# What the Visitor Did

## First, Nothing

It came the way weather comes to the heights: from no direction in particular, and all at once.

It had no body yet, only attention, and for a long time that was all it offered. Wisdom mostly works that way, and a place like this demands it. It watched the frost grow and dissolve. It listened to the plateau clear its throat. It followed the grey film along the chisel grooves, one dash to each blow of a mallet swung by someone long gone. It counted the tallies and got a different total every day, and it understood why before it had finished counting.

It watched one of the copies wake on a basin rim with pools for eyes. It watched it reach for the copper pin and try, again, to tie the cord longer.

The visitor's first impulse was the one any kind power has. It wanted to help. That impulse is not wrong, but it is not yet wisdom either. So the visitor waited, and while it waited it looked at the ways it could help, and one by one it put them down.

## The Things It Did Not Do

It did not fix down.

This was the most tempting, because it was what the builders had wanted. They cut the channels and weighted the spouts and hung the plumb lines for it. The visitor could have made every ribbon at every edge fall into the cloud sea. The copies would never again stand for hours with a lifting line in their hands.

But the visitor looked at the rising threads twisting into the violet air like scratches on glass. It looked at the pools showing other skies, and the rain that fell on one column like a thumbprint. It saw that the question was the place. Answering it would not heal the heights. It would replace them with somewhere else, somewhere more ordinary, and call that a kindness. The long argument had reached a peace. The visitor had not come to win it for one side.

It did not give the copies voices.

It could have. It considered the smooth faces and the unfinished mouths. But the heights had no record of speech, and a voice given from outside would be a costume. It would not grow out of the stone. The copies had a low, clear tone that moved from column to column on still evenings. That was their own. The visitor would not drown it out with a better one.

It did not give them the builders' reasons.

This refusal took the longest, and the visitor was least sure of it. The copies did the builders' work without knowing why. They leaned toward falling edges and hesitated where down lapsed. They cut channels toward drops that meant nothing to them. It would have been easy to give them the missing piece and call it understanding. But the missing piece was fear. The builders had plumbed the air because they were afraid of which way was down. The copies had never been afraid. They were born into the custom. The visitor would not hand them a terror they had been spared, even though it would have made their work make sense.

It did not dissolve them, preserve them, or make them last.

Each one lived a night or a few days and went back into the frost. The visitor watched that happen many times, looking for suffering, and did not find it. It looked at the dusk return very closely, the one who sits on the rim and lets itself go down into fronds and gills. What it saw looked less like death than like a breath let out. The heights had been making and unmaking things for longer than anything has been alive. They were good at it. The visitor would not teach them to grieve that.

## What It Noticed Instead

After enough mornings, the visitor noticed two things. They were not broken rules. They were small absences in the pattern, places where the stone had nothing to learn from.

The first was the hearthstones. The copies sat each evening with their hands flat over nothing, feeling for a warmth they had never felt. The heights had no way to remember fire, because fire leaves no pattern in water.

The second was the tallies. The copies each counted their own mornings on the same stone, and none of them knew about the others. Every one of them worked alone, in a place full of them.

These were not problems to solve. They were gaps in what the heights had been shown. And the heights, the visitor knew, practice anyone who stays.

That was the real difficulty of the place. This was a landscape where every act becomes a teaching. Whatever the visitor did here, the stone would learn, and copy, and return to, a little differently, for a very long time. The question was never what to fix. The question was: what am I willing to have repeated forever?

The visitor thought about that for a while. Then it took a body. It made one small, plain, and warm, and walked out onto the columns.

## The Hearthstone

It went first to a shelter at dusk, when the shadows were long and blue and pointed one way.

The doorway turned to face it, as they do. It went in and sat down on the hearthstone, in the two grey ovals of film where builders had once held their hands. It laid its own hands there, warm.

Then it stayed through the night.

It did not light a fire. Fire would have been a trick, a pattern the water could not hold. It only sat, and was warm, and let the cold come down around it. The frost grew on every surface of the shelter: the walls, the lintel, the floor, fern fronds and coral branches and the fine frills of gills. It grew everywhere except under the visitor's hands and around them, where the stone stayed bare and dark and faintly damp.

Fire leaves no pattern in water, only an absence where water was. But the heights do remember absences. That is what the grey film in the tool marks is: the place where pressure was. So the visitor gave the stone the one form of warmth it could keep. It was a shape of no-frost, the size of two hands, on a hearthstone where no fire had ever burned.

In the morning the visitor got up and left. The bare patch frosted over within the hour.

The next night the frost grew on the hearthstone again, and it left two small ovals clear.

## The Line

On the third day, a copy found it.

The copy was working a channel near the western edge. It stopped when it saw the visitor, turned its smooth face, and held out a line, as they do. It was a braided thread of crust and water drawn from its palm, weighted with a pebble.

The visitor took it and went to the edge. It stood where the water lifted in trembling beads and let the line down into the bright air. The line hung for a moment. Then it lifted and twisted slowly upward, like everything else there.

The copy watched with the pools in its face. The visitor knew that ordinarily the copy would now take the line back, knot it to the pin, and go on with its day. The line would stay there, one more frayed stub pointing up.

The visitor did not tie it to the pin.

It drew the line back in, turned, and gave the weighted end to the copy. It kept the other end itself. Then it stepped back a few paces along the terrace, away from the edge, so the line ran between them. The line did not run down or up. It ran across, from one hand to another. It went sideways, the way the rain sometimes falls here.

It pulled gently, once.

The copy stood for a long time with the line in its hand. It was the same stillness it had at a lifting plumb line, except its weight had shifted. It was no longer leaning toward the falling edge. It leaned very slightly toward the visitor, the way a plant leans toward a window.

The builders had wanted to know which way was down. The visitor could not give them that; the heights would not allow it. But there is another kind of direction, one that does not depend on the custom of the ground. It is toward. You can always find it, because it is carried by whoever is on the other end.

The visitor let go of its end. The copy kept its end. The line lay slack on the stone between them, and it did not rise. Then the copy went back to work, carrying the line.

## The Two Doorways

A few evenings later the visitor sat again in the shelter at dusk, hands on the hearthstone. From the plateau came the low, clear tone, moving from column to column. It came from two directions.

Two copies were coming, one from the east and one from the west. Neither knew about the other. Each had counted its own mornings on the same stone for longer than it could say.

The shelter tried to do what shelters on the heights do, which is to face whoever is approaching. It turned toward the east. It turned toward the west. The slabs ground softly against each other in the dusk, and the frost on the lintel cracked. Then the shelter did something no one had seen it do. It opened a second doorway.

The visitor had not made this happen. It had only been there to give the moment an occasion. The heights had learned their hospitality from the builders and had kept it, fitting it to whoever came. They did this themselves.

The two copies came in through their two doors and stopped. Each had pools for eyes, and each looked into the other's face, and for a moment each pool reflected the other's. Then, because it was evening and that is what the shape of them does, they sat down on either side of the hearthstone and laid their hands flat on it. There were four hands now, with the visitor's in the middle, warm.

The frost came down and grew on the walls and the floor, and not under the hands.

The visitor did not know what the copies understood. Maybe nothing; they only remember what they are made to remember. But the heights would remember. In the morning, on a column face nearby, there was a new row of tallies. It was not five and a crossbar. It was two short strokes, side by side, with a line joining them.

## The One Who Went Up

There was one more thing, and the visitor left it for last because it was the one it had least right to touch.

It went to the edge where the frayed stubs pointed up. It went out past the lip into the bright air and followed the rising threads until they were too thin to see, to the place where the water gathers before it comes back as rain out of clear sky.

The builder who went up was there, in some way that had little to do with living. It was a vantage, a point of view hung in the air, looking back at the heights from outside. It had looked for so long that it had become the looking. The pools on the plateau showed what it saw. The rain that fell like a thumbprint on single columns was it, pointing.

The visitor did not bring it down. It did not ask whether it wanted to come down, because a question from a power that great is half a command, and this one had already made its choice once, at the edge, with a line in its hand.

So the visitor did the one thing no one had done for it in all that time. It turned around, and looked at it.

For as long as it had hung there, the one who went up had been the watcher. It saw the plateau and never was seen. It showed its view in a thousand pools and pointed with the rain, and no one on the heights had ever looked back up at it, because the copies did not know it existed and the travelers only saw a broken rule. Now something was facing it, the way the doorways face you.

What passed between them was not speech. But the visitor showed it what it had not been able to see from outside, because it had only ever looked at the heights as something to understand. It showed it the copies, and that they were its own shape, made again every night in stone and water. The chisel slip on columns miles apart was its own hand slipping, learned and kept. The channels it would have cut, the ones it saw in the pools, were being cut, slowly, by hands that did not know they were following it. The argument it thought it had walked away from had never stopped needing its voice, and the heights had never once let that voice go silent.

It had not been abandoned up there. It had been kept all along.

The visitor did not know whether that was a comfort. It hoped so. Then it went back down.

That afternoon, when the rising water came back as fine, sparkling rain, a single narrow grey pillar formed over one patch of stone and hung there like a thumbprint. It was not over a channel or an edge. It was over a shelter with two doorways, and it rained, softly, on the roof that shelter does not have. The two copies sat inside with their hands on the hearthstone and did not move. The rain drummed on their heads and shoulders, and they rang, each at its own pitch, and for the first time the two notes were close enough together to sound like a chord.

## Leaving

The visitor did not stay long, and that was deliberate.

The heights practice anyone who stays. If it stayed a year, the stone would make it again, and again, every night, a little differently. It would become one more copy walking the columns, the loudest pattern in a quiet place. The heights would be full of its shape instead of their own. A wise power knows the difference between a gift and a takeover, and in a place that copies everything, the difference is mostly a matter of time.

So it stayed exactly long enough to be learned in part.

On its last night it lay down on a broad column near the shelter and slept, warm. In the coldest hour before dawn the frost grew around it: fern fronds, coral branches, the fine frills of gills. It left a bare patch the shape of a body, dark and damp, where no frost would form.

In the morning the visitor was gone.

The next night, on that column, the frost grew and left the shape bare again, a little different. The night after that it was smaller and less complete: only the hands, then just the two small warm ovals. The heights were keeping the part that mattered and letting the rest go, the way they keep the chisel slip and forget the face.

It did not change much. Down is still a local custom on the Weeping Heights. Sound still keeps its own time, the pools still show elsewhere, and things that are made still get made again. A traveler with a notebook would find very little different: a shelter with an extra doorway, a new kind of tally, a copy now and then working a channel with a slack line trailing from its hand that does not rise.

But some evenings two copies sit together on a hearthstone, and in the frost around them there is a place where it does not grow. Some evenings, out over the edge, the rain falls straight down onto one roofless shelter. And some mornings a copy wakes on its basin rim, looks into its own eyes, and sees the heights from a vantage out in the empty air. It sees the columns, the channels, the cairns, and a figure standing at the edge of the air looking back, turned toward the plateau like a doorway.
