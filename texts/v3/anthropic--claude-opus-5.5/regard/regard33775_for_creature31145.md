# The Guest

It came down the stair the way anyone does, on purpose.

It could have arrived some other way. It could have seeped through the fill ahead of the face, or ridden the steam up whatever unknown throat the steam comes from, or tumbled down the chute with the grit and the hairpins. But it had read the rules the way the visitors had, and it understood the most important one before it reached the bottom step: *the lodger only keeps what has been let go.* It did not want to arrive as something dropped. It wanted to arrive as a guest, as something that intended, from the first moment, to leave again.

So it walked, down and then down again, past the point where the cellars stop pretending. The door with no handle let it in, as it lets in the others. The air went cold and wet. It smelled of lime and hot metal, and far off the drum was turning.

---

## What it did first

Nothing.

This is harder than it sounds for a power of that kind. It is not used to coming upon a small, strange, lonely-seeming thing and doing nothing. But it had a first rule older than the city above, and the rule went like this: *learn what a thing is for before you love it into something else.*

So it went to the face, stood where visitors stand, and watched the arm reach out. The block went home with a sound like a book closed on a table. The mortar came out in its bead. The carriage breathed.

It had taken a body for the visit, something roughly the shape of a person, because the lodger's language is palms against warm walls and it wanted to be able to speak it. It laid one hand flat on the curing stone and waited.

The heat gathered under its palm, the way it gathers under anyone's, like a cat settling against a closed door. The lodger considered it for four minutes. It decided the guest was not dropped, and it moved on the width of a joint.

Visitors usually shift their feet at that point, or check the time, or go back up. The guest stayed for the next cycle, and the next. It stayed through the night, if the gallery can be said to have night, and through the long damp morning of beading walls. Late in the afternoon the vault saturated, and it stood in the rain.

It watched the threads come down along the joints, strung like a loom and briefly gold in each lamp-cone. Near the face it saw the place where the threads bent around something the height of a kneeling person, leaning toward the lamps.

It did not approach. It let itself be seen, and it went on looking.

The gallery is a place where processes happen without witnesses. For those hours, and for the first time, that was not true. The drum turned for someone. The rain fell on something that did not need it but was glad of it anyway. The guest thought this might be the whole of its visit, and that it might be enough.

---

## What it came to understand

The lodger is not trapped the way a creature in a cell is trapped. A cell is a shape imposed on something that would otherwise be larger. The lodger's circumstance is its own shape. It is a process with a point of view, and the process runs one way. Its inability to go back is not a wound in it. It is its grain.

The guest felt, through its palm, what the visitors had only guessed. There was no despair in the warm band, and no tiredness either. What was there was closer to concentration, the absorbed attention of someone threading a needle, renewed every four minutes, forever. There was appetite too, the gentle slope of wanting to be the one who keeps things. There was tenderness for the sprouts. Behind all of it, faint and steady as the hum of the cables, was something that is harder to name. It was not grief exactly. It was the feeling of a house you lived in at four: the shape of it is in you, and you could not walk back in through the door.

Grain can be tender. The guest set out to learn where.

---

## What it would not do

It thought about each of these for a long time, which for the guest meant several of the lodger's lifetimes, spent between one block and the next.

**It would not let the lodger go back.** A keeper who could return to its records would begin to revise them. It would soften one course and tidy another, and take out the bottle-cap summer because it was garish. The walls are true because the hand that made them cannot unmake them. To give the lodger its childhood again would be to take away the thing that makes its childhood worth having.

**It would not stop the Layer**, or give the lodger rest. It listened for a wish for rest and found none. You do not rescue someone from their vocation because it looks hard from outside.

**It would not give the lodger a companion made like itself.** There is only one newest moment in a room. Something that is always the youngest thing present cannot be two things without each becoming a little older than the other.

**It would not save the sprouts** by warming the finished walls or lighting them. The sprouts exist only because the band exists, and a garden spreading back down the gallery would crowd the record and blur the order. The lodger's mercy, letting them become the pencilling instead of letting them rot, is a real mercy. It did not need correcting.

**It would not tell the lodger where it was going.** The lodger is not tunnelling toward anything. It is keeping pace with a loss. If you name a destination for something like that, you turn a vigil into a journey, and journeys end.

**It would not tell the city.** There is a reason the door has no handle on the outside.

The last one it considered longest of all: **it would not make the city stop losing things.** It could have done it. The keys would stay in pockets and the buttons would stay on coats, and the chute would go quiet. But a city that dropped nothing would be a city that held on too tightly to everything. Some losses are how people set things down that they can no longer carry. And the lodger would starve. Not of food, but of purpose, of the small curious silence that follows a tick on the tray.

When it had set all these aside, the guest found there were only a few things left worth doing. They were small, and it did them.

---

## The walk

During the rain the lodger rides the circulation of the water like a pulse. It feels the whole length of the gallery at once, all the way to the iron door, and no further. What lies past the door has been released into the city's keeping, and the lodger cannot know what became of it.

The guest could not give the lodger its past back. It could bring news of it.

So it went up the stair and out into the city, and it looked for the old stone. It found more of it than the visitors suspected. There was a vault under a bakery where sacks of flour stood on blocks from the lodger's third autumn, the leaf-bands still visible if you knew to look. There was the lower flight of a stair in a tenement where a night-watchman sat each dawn to take his boots off. There was a wine cellar in which two people, years ago, had said something important to each other in the dark. There was a storm drain whose blocks had held back the flood. There was a cellar belonging to an old woman who kept preserves on shelves cut into the lodger's own early walls, jars of plums glowing like the sodium lamps. And under a great deal of the city there was simply stone, holding things up.

The lodger feels weight shifting above it the way a sleeper feels someone sit on the edge of the bed. That was the language the guest used.

On the next afternoon, when the rain began below, it went to the iron door with the brass plate and stood above the threshold. It made itself heavy, deliberately, heavier than any person. Then it began to walk outward along the buried curve, away from the gallery and into the released stone the lodger could no longer reach.

It did this every afternoon for a season, going a little further each time and always during the rain. It went at the pace of the Layer, one hand's width and then a pause, one hand's width and then a pause.
