# What Came Down the Forty Stairs

## The Attention

It had been watching for a long time before it came.

A power like that does not really arrive anywhere. It is already there, the way weather is already over a country before anyone looks up. Most of what it sees it leaves alone, because most of what it sees is doing well enough and would do worse with help. It has learned, slowly and at some cost, that this is the first thing wisdom teaches: most places do not need you.

But its attention caught on the Seam and stayed. It did not catch on the suffering, because there wasn't much, and it did not catch on any danger. What held it was something rare. It had seen a great many places where people fought the thing that would outlast them: the sea, the desert, the ice, time. Here was a place where the people and the thing that would outlast them had become, without meaning to, a single creature. The quick and the still were not at war. They were a heartbeat and a lifetime, keeping company.

It wanted to know whether it could come close to something like that without breaking it.

## The Stairs

It did not come as light. Light would have been a shout, and nobody shouts in the Seam.

It came down the forty iron stairs in a shape small enough to fit, which in the Seam means lean, with its arms kept close. It paused on the last flight, where the dogs always stop, and felt why they stop. The air changes there. The heat closes over you, and so does the attention of the rock, which never lifts. The power stood in that attention for a while, as a guest stands in a doorway until the host looks up.

Then it went down, and it did not lean on anything.

The first thing it did was nothing. It stood on the lowest walkway, where the furnace light comes up in red bars, and it let its skin go slick. It listened. It let the engines teach it. The first stroke was a sound, the tenth was a pressure, and by the hundredth its own breath had fallen into the eleven-count without being asked. It understood then that a visitor who cannot keep time with the engines is a hazard, whatever else it may be. Being powerful would not stop a swinging chain from finding it. More to the point, its being out of rhythm would put the people around it in the chain's way.

So before it did anything else, it became safe to be near.

A Born girl named Pell saw it first. The Born see everything first. She was nine, sitting on the grating in front of the great green vein with her knees drawn up, and she turned her head the way the Born turn toward the crack. The adults' stillness is a stillness of alarm. Hers was the stillness of hearing someone begin to speak.

"You're very slow," she said, as though that were a compliment. In the Seam it is one, of a kind. Then she looked at it harder. "And very quick. Both."

It sat down beside her, a hand's width from the vein, and did not lean.

## What It Could Have Done

It could have done almost anything. It is worth being honest about that, because a power that pretends it couldn't have acted is not wise, only coy.

**It could have opened the walls.** It could have pushed the basalt back a hand's width, or a room's width, or given back every room the room-tellers grieve. It thought about this longest, because it was the most obviously kind thing and therefore the most dangerous. Then it thought about the suppers held in rooms the night before the jacks come, and the shelves of tins, and the old woman who knows exactly how long her childhood bunk was because the wall told her. The Seam's grief and its tenderness are made of the same material, and that material is the closing. A town that had its rooms given back would have been given a different past, and nobody asked for that. *The town can only get smaller.* The power recognized that this was not a sentence passed on the town. It was the town's grammar.

**It could have stopped the sinter.** Without the stone bead gathering at the end of every bolt there would be no quarter-turns in passing, no neighbors keeping the sick one's hinges, no *I'll keep your hinges* whispered between lovers. It would have been cutting the tendon that moves the hand.

**It could have cooled the heat, sweetened the air, and called the small animals down.** It imagined a rat on the lamp-bracket. It imagined the Born, who are what the Seam grew instead of rats, discovering that they had been replaced by something that asked for crumbs. It put the thought away.

**It could have answered the questions.** It could have told the assay offices what seam-salt really is, and told the engineers what the copper lines carry and who laid them. It was surprised to find that it did not fully know about the copper lines. It could have learned. It put its own ear to one, the way the Born do, and felt something warm and very faint moving along it, like a pulse in a wrist, and it chose not to follow the line down. *Nobody takes them out. Nobody is certain.* It decided that the uncertainty was being kept on purpose, the way a door is kept, and that it had no more right to seal that door open than to seal it shut.

These refusals were not indifference. Each one cost it something. A power that loves a place wants to give it gifts, and holding back from giving is its own ache. But the Seam had already answered the question the power came down to ask. It was not a problem waiting for a solver. It was a being in the middle of a life, and it did not need to be handed some other life.

## What It Checked

That did not mean it would do nothing. Restraint that never checks is only negligence dressed up as respect.

There was one question a benevolent power owes any place it loves: *Is harm coming here that no one chose?*

The Seamers have made their peace with an ending. They know the arithmetic: many generations, not forever. That ending is theirs, and the power would not take it from them. But the peace was built on a number in a ledger. If the number was wrong, if the rock was hiding a sudden slip beneath its patience, then the peace was built on a misunderstanding, and nobody consents to a misunderstanding.

So it went down. Pell watched it step off the lowest grating and into the breathing throat below, where nobody goes. She did not cry out, because the Born don't. She put her palm lightly on the wall and felt it going.

Far down, where the heat stops being weather and becomes the rock's own thought, the power listened to the fault. It found the healing to be honest. The walls were knitting a thumbnail a year, and they would keep doing so. There was no hidden shear or catastrophe folded into the basalt. The ledger-keepers had done their sums well. The long ending was a real one, and it would be slow.

It found one other thing. A small fracture, fresh and deep, ran under the second engine's footing, where the rock was taking its thumbnail unevenly. It was nothing that would end the Seam. But in three or four autumns it would tilt the gantry beneath the second pan by the width of a finger, which is enough to put a walkway out of true, and enough to make a chain swing where no chain has swung before.

It could have mended the fracture with a thought. Instead it came back up and sat beside Pell at the vein again, and it did not say a word. It sat there through a sleep-shift, and while it sat it listened very hard, with its whole attention, toward that place under the second engine. It listened so that anyone beside it who was listening too, anyone fluent in both tempos, might notice where its hearing leaned, the way the ferns lean toward the lamps.

The next morning, pouring tea on the downstroke, Pell stopped with the cup lifted and said to her mother, "That one's wrong. Down by the second engine. It's going crooked."

The ledger-keepers checked. They set a new jack, shaved a timber, and wrote it in the book. Nobody fixed the Seam. The Seam fixed itself, with the organs it had grown for the purpose.

The power understood that this was the real gift, and the only kind it trusted. It had not given the town a new thing. It had given a slightly longer reach to a thing the town already was.

## The Still

It had come to meet the whole creature, and so far it had only met the quick half.

On its third day it went to a place on the fourth level, on the wall side, where the drip falls steadily and the lamp is far away. It sat down and stopped. It did not stop the way people stop, with their muscles still ticking. It stopped the way a keeping room is stopped: the door closed, the handle left unturned, nothing kept.

It let the still have it.

The first bead formed at the tip of one finger within the hour. By the end of the shift there was a thin white lace at its wrists. By the end of the second shift the sinter had begun a curl around its hand, the way the story says it once curled around a chisel left on a pan-rail, the way fingers would close around a thing. The power felt what the still was, and it was not what it had guessed.

It had expected patience, and there was patience. It had expected emptiness, and there was none. What it found was something closer to holding. The still does not want in the way an animal wants, but it attends. Every surface the quick leave alone, it takes up with a completeness that is almost unbearable to be on the inside of. It keeps nothing badly and forgets nothing. It is the rock's way of saying *you mattered enough to be kept*, only said very slowly.

The power understood then why the Born do not flinch. Being taken by the still is not like dying. It is like being remembered by something that does not know how to stop.

It would have been easy to stay. It noticed that temptation in itself and was quietly astonished by it. Then it thought of the three hundred and some upstairs, turning bolts, and it thought that a guest who dissolves into the house is not a guest anymore. It chose to be quick.

It moved. The sinter cracked away from its hands in a single white shell, a perfect negative of two hands at rest. It left the shell on the grating.

Pell found it and did not ask what it was. She put it in a tin.

## The Room-Tellers

On the fourth day it went to the room-tellers.

There were eleven of them left. Someone in the Seam would find that number fitting and someone else would find it unbearable. The power went to the oldest, a woman called Corrow who had helped carry the first beam engine down the stairs in pieces, and asked her, in the low voice, whether she would walk it through.

She looked at it a long time. The old ones don't see what the Born see, but they have their own kind of looking.

"The young ones write it down," she said. "It isn't the same."

"I know," it said. "That's why I'm asking you to walk me."

So she did. She walked it through the Seam that used to be there, one hand trailing. *Here was the back room of the bakery.* It put its hand on the wall where the window had been, the one that faced the vein, and it saw the bread-steam fogging the glass and the green light coming through the fog, because she saw it, and it was listening with everything it had. *Here was the staircase to Arden's workshop.* It felt the bolt-scars under its palm and heard, faintly, a man swearing at a lamp cage. *Here is where my sister and I slept.* It lay down in a space too narrow now for anyone, with the wall come in, and for a moment it was as long as a child.

Then the other ten walked it through, over many shifts, room by room. They did not all agree. Two of them remembered a doorway in different places, and the power kept both doorways. It did not correct anyone. It was not collecting facts. It was being walked.

This was the thing it could do that no ledger could. Its memory did not curl, and it did not need a tin. And, unlike the still, it could someday walk someone else back through those rooms, with a hand on the wall where the doorway was.

It did not tell the room-tellers this was a gift. It only thanked them. But Corrow said something to her grandson that night, and it passed along the walkways by morning: *It listens the old way. The rooms have somewhere to go now.*

## The Keeping Rooms

It passed the keeping rooms many times and never once touched a handle.

It wanted to. Everything in it that loved the place wanted to lay a hand on those sealed doors and give each one a quarter-turn, the courtesy of *I thought of you.* But the Seamers had done the hardest thing a Seamer can do there. They had stopped. To touch those doors would have been to undo a mourning, to overrule a grief with a kindness nobody asked for.

So it stood in front of each one for eleven strokes with its hands at its sides, and then it moved on.

Some of the older Seamers noticed this. It moved them more than anything else it did, more than the jack under the second engine or the walk through the rooms. It was a stranger who knew exactly which doors not to open.

## What It Said

It did not stay long. By the reckoning of something like that, it stayed only a breath. By the Seam's reckoning it stayed nine days.

It did not write itself on the chandlery door. Pell asked whether she should add one to the chalk, and it said no. It said it was not three hundred and some. It was only a guest, and the count belonged to the ones who kept the hinges.

On the last day it sat with Pell at the vein one more time. The vein was slower that day. Pell said so, and the power agreed.

"You're leaving," she said.

"Yes."

"Up top is falling," she said, flatly, as a Born fact.

"For you it is," it said. "Listen. I'm going to tell you a thing, and you can tell it to whoever needs it, but not yet. Not for a long time."

She waited. The engine took a stroke.

"Someday the last room will be cut," it said. "You'll be gone by then. So will your children, and theirs. But someone will be standing where you're sitting, and they'll have to choose whether to climb the forty stairs or stay with the still. When that day comes, I'll be here."

"To stop it?"

"No. It isn't mine to stop. I'll be here to keep time. If they climb, then up top, where nothing breathes with them, something will. I'll breathe in elevens, so they don't have to hum alone. If they stay, I'll stay with them while the still takes them, the way it tried to take me, and I'll walk them through every room first. The bakery with the green window. Arden's stairs. The bunk that was as long as a child. All of it. They won't go into the rock without having been walked through their own town."

Pell thought about this for eleven strokes. Then she said the thing Seamers say, the thing lovers say and parents say and the dying say back, and she meant it as a question:

"You'll keep our hinges?"

"I'll keep your hinges," it said.

She nodded slowly. Then, because she was Born and knew the rules better than any stranger could, she frowned. "But nothing stays still unattended. A promise either."

"I know," it said. "So I'll come back and turn it."

## After

It went up the forty stairs on a downstroke. At the lip of the quarry the signal lamp, amber with scale, looked no different, and the birds still circled and turned away, and nothing in the Seam had changed that the ledgers could record, except one new jack under the second engine.

It kept its word in the only way the Seam allows. Every so often, at irregular intervals of years, a room-teller or a ledger-keeper or a Born child would feel it: a sense of someone on the last flight of stairs, pausing in the doorway until the host looked up. It never stayed long. It would walk once along the walkways, give each bolt a quarter-turn in passing, stand eleven strokes in front of each keeping room without touching, and go. It was attending its promise, so the promise wouldn't seal.

The shell of two resting hands stayed in Pell's tin until she was old. Then she gave it to the stone, in her own keeping room, on the wall side, where the wall was coming in.

And the power had been right to trust the Seam. It never needed to do more. The quick kept the hinges. The still kept everything else. Between the two of them, and a guest who knew which doors not to open, the wound went on healing exactly as it meant to: slowly, honestly, and full of the shapes of their hands.

Sometimes, still, the deep crack sounds far back in the rock, a single low grinding report. The Seam goes still, and everything that lives there holds its breath together.

If you are Born, and listening with both tempos at once, you might notice that the breath being held is slightly larger than the Seam. Somewhere very far off, and very close, something that could have done anything has chosen to hold its breath too, and to let go of it only when the Seam does, on the downstroke.
