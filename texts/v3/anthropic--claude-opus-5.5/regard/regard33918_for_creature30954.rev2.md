# The Attention at Kessary

## Another Arrival

Everything at Kessary arrived. It came at dawn, when the swifts pour up out of the rift in their column of smoke, and at first nothing noticed it except the stone.

It did not come down the ladders, and it did not come with a body. It came the way heat comes up out of the floor, as a steady pressure that is everywhere at once. For a while it simply attended. It attended to the cream limestone and to the fossil shells cut in half, open to the air for the first time in a hundred million years. It attended to the liver-stone, the brine sheets, and the colored films eating their slow way across the rock. It attended to the crickets shifting a whole wall at once, with a sound like sand poured on paper.

It had seen many places. It had seen very few this young.

What it felt first, if a power like that can be said to feel, was the same thing the visitors feel: that nothing here had been softened. Then it felt something the visitors rarely stay long enough to feel. Nothing here had been *decided*. The rift was not a finished thing to be judged good or bad. It was a sentence still being spoken. A wise power does not correct a sentence in the middle.

So the first thing it did was wait.

## What It Could Have Done

It could have closed the rift. That would have taken less effort than a harvester spends on a single nest. It could have pressed the two sides of the plain gently back together and cooled the heat underneath until the brown scrub grew over the seam. In a generation no one would have believed a rift had ever been there.

It considered this for exactly as long as it took to count what would be lost. The swift column visible from twenty miles would go, and the crabs carrying reefs on their backs. The shrine nests would go, and the birds raised in them, who know the stone better than anything else alive. The pink salt that tastes of iron and lightning would go. A living world had come into being in eleven years out of heat, brine and broken stone. It was layered and loud and near-touching, and it was more alive than the plain had ever been. And a nine-year-old girl would lose the only place her body knows how to live.

Closing the rift would not be healing. It would be one more collapse, only larger, and there would be nothing afterward to start the swifts calling again.

It could have made the rift safe. It could have held every slab still, stopped every walkway from falling, and let the bloom grow without ever breaking. It looked at this longer, because this was the one its benevolence wanted. People die here. Ilvi died here. Oszek's wife died here. There are thousands of red hands on the walls, and each one is somebody's fear, and some of them are somebody's last morning.

But it looked at what the collapses do. The breaking bloom takes hundreds of nests, and the rebuilding is what keeps the harvest inexhaustible. The falling slabs expose fresh stone, and the films spread across it, and everything that eats the films follows. Harvesters who know the rift can kill them learn its rules in the first hour and keep them. Harvesters who believed it could not kill them would strip it the way people strip everything they believe is safe. Without the breaking, Kessary would become an ordinary orchard, and then an ordinary quarry, and then an ordinary hole.

The danger was not a flaw in the place. It was the place's metabolism.

It could have given the rift fresh water. This was the subtlest temptation, and it almost did it. All water must be carried in from the plain. That one lack keeps the harvesters tethered to the rim and their shelters roofless and temporary. One cold spring, opened in the basalt, would change everything. It would ask so little.

It followed that spring forward in its mind. People would stay. Stone rings would become houses and houses would become a town. The town would want the walls to stop moving, and it would want more nests than the swifts could rebuild. Someone would wedge a good nest into a crack for a dead child, and someone else, hungry in a lean year, would take it. The one rule kept perfectly for eleven years would break within a generation of the spring. Kessary works because nobody can quite live there.

Almost nobody. It thought about the two exceptions for a long time.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not appear.

It saw how the harvesters already read the rift. They watched the birds and the crabs and the old man. They had built a whole grammar of warning out of what the place itself was saying. A power that appeared would become the thing everyone watched instead. People would stop glancing at the shrine nests and start praying. It did not want to become one more red handprint that everyone trusted and no one could read.

It did not take Oszek's grief. It looked at the grief carefully. It was six years old and still heavy. It had made him stay, and staying had made him listen, and listening had made him the rift's memory. His white-over-red hands are the most careful act of truth-telling at Kessary. He does not erase the old danger. He records that it has passed. The grief was not a wound to be closed. It was the shape his love had taken in a place that breaks. Taking it would have taken the man.

It did not make Pell invulnerable. Her calluses, her ear for the hollow *tonk*, and the way she steps around purple films near heat she cannot feel yet were not gifts. They were attention, nine years of it, paid moment by moment. Invulnerability would let her stop paying it, and the attention is who she is.

It did not read the rift's future and announce it. This was not restraint. It was humility. The power looked down past the basalt, past the liver-stone, into the heat. It found that a thing still being made cannot be read to the end, not even by a power like itself. The heat was rising. The seeps were stirring, like someone turning over in bed. Would the black rivers come? Perhaps, to some arm of the rift, someday. Not this year, and not the next. Beyond that the future was genuinely unwritten, because the ground had not decided yet.

So in the end it did three small things. None of them would look like anything at all.

## The First Thing: Dusk

It had noticed a pattern the harvesters had not yet put into words.

The walkways fail most often at nightfall. Ilvi died at nightfall. The afternoon rain soaks the slabs and the steam dries them, so every day the stone swells and shrinks. By dusk the day's strain has worked its way into the lashings and the ledges. Dusk is also when the warning system goes blind. The swifts are pouring down in their funnel and the bats are rising in theirs, and the two columns cross. In that roaring, hissing crossing no one can tell a bird fleeing a wall from a bird coming home. For about half an hour each day, Kessary cannot speak clearly.

The power could have fixed this with a touch. It chose instead to put the knowledge where a human could reach it.

Oszek was already close. He had six years of white hands on the lower walls, and without knowing it he had built a record of when things fell. He was perhaps two seasons from seeing the pattern himself. The power did not hurry him. It gave the understanding to the one person at Kessary who could see the rift from above, which brings us to the second thing.

## The Second Thing: The Plain

In the spring Pell was taken up to the plain for a cousin's wedding. She went the way she always goes, sullen and cold and already listening for the rim.

That night the power did one thing for her that no one else could have done.

She lay awake on the brown earth in the dark, as she always does up there, shivering in the silence. For the length of one night, the power let her hear the plain the way she hears the rift.

It did not change the plain. It changed nothing at all except how much of the plain reached her. She heard that the ground was not silent. It was only slow. Under the dust the old limestone was still cracking, a hair's width in a century. Roots were prising it open. Water was dissolving its way down through it in the dark, grain by grain, in a sound so long and low it was like a single note held for ten thousand years. The brown scrub was eating the stone. The frost had rounded every pebble she had ever called broken.

When she was very small she had looked down at the dusty earth and said the ground was broken. She had been right. It had simply broken so long ago that it had finished saying so.

At dawn she walked to the rim, as she always does. For the first time she was not fleeing to it. From above, at dusk, with the power's attention still on her like a hand on the shoulder, she watched the swifts come down and the bats go up. For the first time she saw from outside how the two columns tangle and blur. No one down there can read the birds in that half hour.

She came back down the ladders and told Oszek. He listened with the same whole attention he gives to stone. Then he went through six years of painted hands in his memory, and he was quiet for a long time.

Now there is a new rule at Kessary, taught in the first hour. *Be off the walkways before the columns cross.* The harvesters think a nine-year-old girl made it up. In the way that matters, she did.

The night on the plain was not really a gift about dusk, though. That was only where it happened to spill over. The real gift was this. The power had seen the one place where Pell was truly fragile. It was not the falling bloom or the liver-stone or the scalding pools. It was that her whole life was tied to a single wound in the ground, and that wound might close, or flood with black stone, or one day simply finish. She had nearly died of silence at two years old. The power could not promise her the rift would last. So it gave her the knowledge that the rest of the world is also unfinished, only slower, and that a person who can hear new stone can learn, with patience, to hear old stone too.

She still hates the plain. She will always prefer Kessary. But now, if she ever has to, she could live somewhere else. It is the kind of gift that looks like nothing until the day it is needed.

## The Third Thing: Oszek's Ear

The third thing was the smallest.

Oszek listens to the rift, the harvesters say, the way you listen to someone who is dying, so that you will know when. That was how he first learned to listen. His wife died on the north wall, and after that every crack sounded to him like an ending.

One morning he had his ear to a slab above the fresh seeps. The power did not speak to him. It only let him hear, underneath the ropy stirring and the sigh of the fissures, the same thing it had heard when it arrived. The sound was not a deathbed. It was a labor. It was the sound of the afternoon Pell was born on a hide over warm basalt, in the first rain, with the stone steaming around them. The rift is not a body failing. It is a body being born, and it has not finished being born, and it may not finish for ten thousand years.

Oszek took his ear from the stone. He sat on the warm slab for most of an hour. The harvesters on the far wall saw him there and did not call out, because he looked like a man who was hearing something.

He still keeps the handprints. He still renews the red and paints the white. But the listening has changed its shape. It is less like a vigil and more like attending a birth, where the task is also to know when, but when *what* is different. Some days now, walking the lower walls in the early morning, he catches himself pressing a small white hand of his own onto fresh stone beside one of Pell's. He does not think anyone has noticed. Everyone has.

## Why So Little

A power that could close a rift, still a province, and open springs in basalt did three things. It showed a pattern to the one person who could see it. It gave a girl one night of hearing. It let a grieving man hear birth where he had heard death.

It did so little because Kessary was already doing, on its own, everything the power would have wanted to do. The crabs were building shelters out of their dead. The birds were keeping watch from the nests of the remembered. The harvesters kept one rule perfectly for eleven years without anyone enforcing it. An old man refused to erase the record of danger and only marked when it had passed. A girl pressed her white hand onto new stone to say *I have been here first, and it held*.

This is what benevolence looks like when it has had time to grow wise. It stops asking *what is wrong here that I could fix* and asks *what is right here that I could protect by staying out of its way*. The place was not suffering. It was becoming. The people in it were not helpless. They were fluent. What was needed was not rescue. It was a little more light on the few things they could not yet see, and then a willingness to leave.

It did not quite leave, though.

## The Silence

A few days before it drew its attention back, a slab let go on the south wall.

There was the crack like a door slamming in an empty house, and the grinding roar that went on longer than seemed possible, and the boom in the chest. Then came the silence. The swifts stopped, and the crickets, and the crabs. The brine still hissed and the fissures still sighed, and every living voice in Kessary went quiet at once.

The harvesters bowed their heads. Oszek listened, but differently now.

Pell laughed. She was not mocking. She was recognizing, like a child hearing her own name called in an empty room. In that minute the rift was only itself. It was the thing that opened, and is still opening, making its one sound, which is no sound.

The power had been waiting eleven years, from its point of view, for exactly this. It had seen that the girl understood something it understood. Everything alive at Kessary is a guest in a wound. The wound is not an enemy. You can laugh in its silence the way you laugh when someone you love walks back into the room.

So, for once, she was not the only one.

A harvester on the far wall, half a mile off down the long axis where the fresh rock carries sound strangely, swore afterward that he heard two voices laughing. One was the girl's clear, low, flat voice, which arrives like a hand on the shoulder. The other he could not describe. It sounded like it came from the stone itself, and from everywhere, and from very far down, where the heat is.

Then the swifts started up again, and everything else followed.

Nobody believed him. Pell did not ask. She went back to work on the fresh rock the collapse had just laid bare. It was raw and cream-white, with fossil shells cut in half and open to the air for the first time. She pressed her small white hand onto it: *this is new, and it is fine*.

That was the one sign the power left at Kessary, and it was not even its own. It had only stood nearby while she made it. That seemed to be the right amount. Wherever it went next, it carried Kessary with it, the way Pell now carries the plain. It kept the rift in its attention the way the harvesters carry water down from the rim, not to change the place, but because the place cannot yet make that one thing for itself.

The rift is not finished. Neither is the girl. The power, having looked as far down as it could, found it was glad of both.
