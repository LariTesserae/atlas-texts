# The Attention

## Arrival

It came the way powers usually come, all at once, and that was its first mistake.

It arrived at noon, when the plain was silver and red, and it arrived *everywhere*: in every gnamma, every seep line, every termite gallery, every frog sealed in its skin under the clay. It knew the salt content of each pan and the age of each tree. It knew the number of shrimp eggs in the upper centimeter of sediment, and how many would hatch next rain, and how many the rain after. It counted the lids: four hundred and eleven. It knew there was one walker.

It knew all of this, and it understood almost nothing, because it had arrived at the wrong speed.

The land did not react. The land had been arrived upon before, by storms, by fire, by an inland sea that stayed for a few million years and then left. It had outlasted all of them by not answering them. The power, which was wise, recognized that it was being politely ignored by something much older than its own patience. It withdrew from everywhere at once and let itself be only in one place, which was already a kind of humility.

Then it slowed down. This took some time.

It slowed until it moved at the pace of a sheet of water edging across the pavement. Then it stopped where the water stopped and filled the moment. It learned that a moment here could last an afternoon. It learned to stall, undecided. Only at that speed did the plain begin to speak.

## The Notebook

Under an overhang on the north side of a large whaleback, wedged dry between two exfoliation sheets, it found a notebook.

The pages were swollen and stained tea-brown at the corners. They held a careful account of the floor, the water, the light, what grows, the makers, the noise. Then came the lids and the tracks, and the long low back in the mist. Then came a sentence that stopped:

*In the wet weeks it goes from hollow to hollow and lies in*

and nothing after it. The rest of the page was blank, and so was every page that followed.

The power could have finished the sentence without effort. It had been everywhere. It could have found the missing words in the pressure patterns of the clay, or in the memory of the person who wrote them, who was camped eleven kilometers east beside a cold fire, not sleeping.

It did not finish the sentence. It noticed that it wanted to, and it noticed what kind of wanting that was. It was the wanting of a thing that has never had to wait. It put the notebook back between the sheets, wedged it with a small stone so it would not rock, and went to watch instead.

## What It Saw

It watched for one dry-down. That was a single season. For the walker it was a breath, and for the power it was the longest attention it had ever paid to anything.

It saw the walker lie down.

It saw that the hollows were not simply hollows the walker found. The walker made them. It lay in the soft custard clay for an hour, an afternoon, a night of frog-noise so loud it pressed against the rock. Its weight, which was the weight of stone, pressed the pan floor down by the width of a grain. When it rose, the water came in behind it and filled the shape.

Then the power understood something the notebook had only begun to suspect.

The land had run out of ways to fall. Mountains, rivers, and the slow tilting of continents had all gone quiet here. Nothing was left to raise the ground and almost nothing was left to wear it down. A land without relief is a land without difference, and a land without difference has no edges for life to gather along. Without edges there are no pools, no sedge rings, and no places where the frogs crowd and the birds follow. Such a land is only a flat sheet of rock that breathes water back into the sky.

The walker was the last thing making relief. It was the final maker of edges on a surface otherwise finished. Where it lay, a pan deepened by a hair. Where it dragged, a channel was worn a finger deep, and the water learned the channel and followed it. The water was not undecided. It was keeping pace, because the walker had been drawing its paths, a grain at a time, for longer than the trees had been alive.

The power also saw the rest of what the walker did when it lay in the hollows. That was the thing the notebook's author had seen and stopped writing about. It will not be described here either, for reasons that will become clear. It was not terrible. It was the opposite of terrible. It had to do with the last pools at dry-down, the ones crowded with every living thing that needs them, and with what the walker's shed plates become when they are left behind. It had to do with where the shrimp eggs go.

## What It Considered

A benevolent power sees a thing it loves and immediately wants to give it things. This one did too. It turned each gift over slowly, the way the walker turns its head and lets the grit settle in its eyes.

**It considered rain.** It could have made the pans permanent and ended the dry-down. The frogs would never need to seal themselves in their own skins, and no shrimp would ever die in a shrinking pool. Then it looked at the shrimp. They were an ancient lineage, a design that had worked since before there were fish in the sea, and they were built for exactly this: a few weeks of life, a crust of eggs, a silence of years. Permanent water would bring fish, and fish would eat every shrimp in a season. The sedge rings would blur into a single swamp. The waiting was not a hardship laid on this place. The waiting was the place. It set the rain aside.

**It considered time.** The land was nearly done. Measured against its own age, it was close to the final level it could reach. Someday, in some unthinkable span, the pans would silt flat, the whalebacks would round to nothing, and the walker would have no hollows left to make. The power could have stopped that clock. It asked itself whether stopping the ending would preserve the place or embalm it, and it knew the answer. A thing that cannot finish cannot be itself. The walker was not fighting the ending. It was keeping company with it. It set time aside.

**It considered company.** This one it held longest. There was only one walker. From the tops of the whalebacks, the walker sometimes turned its long head toward the far bluish cluster of rock domes at the edge of sight, the ones like a low herd of animals, and stayed turned that way for an afternoon. The power could have made them a herd.

So it looked, carefully, at the far domes. They were rock. They were only rock. But on their flanks, in the pits of the laterite, lay a faint, grain-deep pattern of hollows and channels. It was very old and very worn, and it was the same hand. Something had lain there long ago. Perhaps the walker had, in some earlier age of its slow life. Perhaps something else had, something the walker remembered, or something the walker would become. The power could not tell which. It realized it was not meant to be able to tell, and that the walker's afternoons of looking were not a wound to be healed. They might be grief. They might be homesickness. They might be the way a person looks at a place where they were young. Loneliness imagined from outside is often only a kind of distance, and distance is the one thing this plain is made of. It set company aside, gently, and kept wondering about it.

**It considered making the walker know it was loved.** Then it watched a swallow plaster a mud cup under the plate at the base of the walker's neck, a skink sleep in the damp of its flank, and the sundews glisten in the runoff from its spine. It watched the parrots come roaring down to the lidded gnammas and drink the cool clear water the walker had kept for them. It concluded that the walker had been told, at length, by everything around it, and that nothing it could add would be louder than the frogs.

## The One Thing

That left the man at the cold fire, eleven kilometers east.

The power had seen, in the way it saw everything before it slowed down, what lay under the plain. The laterite crust is ironstone, and ironstone is ore. Seventy kilometers south there were survey pegs with orange tape, and a graded track that had not been there four years ago. Beside the man's fire was a canvas bag of rock samples. He had collected them honestly, for honest work, before he ever lifted a lid.

Here, at last, was a danger the walker could not outwait. The walker was built for rain that comes in a year or in three, for erosion that takes a grain a season. It was not built for something that could remove the whole floor of the world in a decade. Nothing on the plain was built for that. The power considered, seriously, whether this was the moment for power.

It could have made the samples assay poor. It could have misplaced the plain on every map. It could have turned the graded track back into rock.

But it had watched the walker for a season now, and it had learned something about keeping. The walker never forced the water. It did not dig channels. It lay down, and let its weight tell the land something, and then let the water decide. The lids were not walls either. They were seated carefully, and they could be lifted. Everything the walker kept, it kept so that it could still be found by anything that needed it.

A place protected by deception would be protected only as long as the deception held. A place protected by someone's care would be protected by something that could grow.

So the power did the only thing it did. It went to the cold fire at the pace of spreading water, and it sat with the man through one night of the chorus. It did not speak. It did not show itself. It did not change his mind. It only made sure that, for one night, he was not alone with what he had seen. It was a presence at his shoulder the way the swallow's egg is a presence at the walker's neck: small, and there.

Toward dawn, when the mist stood knee-high on the nearest pan, the man looked up and saw a low rounded back in the white. The mist parted wrongly around it. He watched until the sun burned the mist away. Then he got up and walked back toward the whaleback on the north side.

The power does not know what he will do. It chose not to know. It knows only that he found the notebook where he had left it, and that the stone wedged under it had been moved and reseated so the book would not rock, and that he stood a long time looking at that. It knows he did not open the notebook to the last page. It knows that when he left, the canvas bag stayed behind in the overhang, and that swallows have since begun building above it.

## The Sentence

The power left the way it had learned to arrive, at walking pace, stopping where the water stopped.

On its way out it passed the unfinished line one more time in thought:

*In the wet weeks it goes from hollow to hollow and lies in*

and it understood why the sentence ended there. The man had not run out of words. He had been lifting something off a deep hole, and he had seen the water beneath, cool and clear and full of slow life, and he had understood that an open hole fills with dust and drowned insects. So he laid the lid back down.

The sentence is a lid. It is seated. There are small stones under one edge so it will not rock.

The power left it that way. That was the best thing it did.

---

Behind it, the pans shrank a few meters toward their centers. The frogs called a little more urgently. Far out on the plain, in the lowest pool of the oldest pan, crowded with every living thing that needed it, something the color of laterite and granite lay down in the warm clay. It pressed the world down by the width of one grain, as it has done for a very long time, and waited for the water to learn the shape.

The clouds built white in the afternoon.

Some days they bring rain. Most days they don't. It will dry. It always dries.

It waits.
