# The Remainder

## I. Beneath Them All

Beneath them all is the Stump, saying its sentence one syllable a century to something across the basin that may be listening.

And now something else is listening too.

It does not arrive the way anything arrives on the plateau. There is no crack, no shift in the drone, no change in the pitch of the floor under a rasp herd. The knellbird in Lean's upper throat does not stir. If it had come loudly, the Stump would have recorded it. The next ring would have borne its mark, and some listener eleven thousand rings from now would set a spike into the grain and hear a stranger's footsteps in the middle of the sentence. So its first act, before anything else, is to arrive quietly.

That is the first thing it understands about this place. Here, attention is never neutral. Every sound is written down. Anything that comes will become part of what the Stump says, whether or not it means to.

## II. The Tempo

It does not look first. It listens.

It does not listen at its own speed, which would be like reading a letter by glancing at the envelope. It slows itself. It lets itself be carried the way Tuners are carried in the throats, until a century passes through it as one breath, and the key changes stop being catastrophes and become what they are: syllables.

In a fold of time that costs the plateau nothing, it hears the whole thing. It hears the drone of the Ninth Key and the Eighth beneath it, and the Seventh and all the ones before, ring after ring, back through the plenty and the starvation. It hears ten thousand shoots raised up and worn flat. It hears knellbirds calling at pitches that have not been sung for an age, and teaching them to their young, and losing them. It hears builders talking in close tones, sternum to shoulder, in a language that has drifted past recovery. It hears a quarter-tone beneath a Seventh Bell eleven thousand rings gone.

It hears the Burn all the way through.

It will not describe the Burn either. It does not understand it completely. It is wise enough to be surprised by that, and to find the surprise good. It understands more than the Tuners do. It knows the Burn was not an accident, and not an attack in any sense a soldier would use. It was said, and something was meant by it. The meaning belongs to the Stump, and the Stump has not finished explaining itself. The power decides it has no right to explain it on the Stump's behalf.

Then it returns to one afternoon in the Ninth Key, the coin low and copper, and to one grey figure lying on the rim of the plateau with her palms flat on the wood.

## III. What It Could Do

It considers things. Wisdom is not the absence of options. It is the ability to hold them all and feel what each one weighs.

**It could make it rain.** It thinks of this for less than an instant, the way you might think of shouting in a null room. Water would dissolve the resin, soften the grain, and slacken every bell. It would drown a chemistry built on dryness for longer than there have been oceans on most worlds. Tears would stop being amber. The Stump would not be healed. It would be ended, and gently, which is the worst way. The power sets the thought down and does not pick it up again.

**It could fill the towers.** Thirty towers built for more than a hundred thousand, and a few thousand left. It could lay its hand on every null and pour remainders into them until the floors swelled with curled bodies, and the bridges would be sung again, and the held rooms would stand. But each of those people would be the power's pitch and not the Stump's. They would be new, and a Tuner is never new. It would be writing its own voice into the rings and calling it rescue. The Tuners are a sentence the Stump is saying. The power will not put words in its mouth.

**It could translate the far drone.** This is the one it holds longest. It has crossed the basin already, in the listening. The other one is there: older, broader, lower, its syllables a millennium long. It is alive. It is answering. The Stump's slow lean toward it is not imagined. The two of them are somewhere in the middle of an exchange that began before the Burn and may be why the Burn happened. The power could render both halves into something Imre could hold. She would know in an afternoon what her people have spent a hundred thousand years trying to hear.

It doesn't. Handing someone the end of a sentence while they are still speaking it is not a gift. Imre does not only want to know what is being said. She wants to be one of the ones who hear it. The listening is not a problem standing between her and the answer. The listening is her life, and it is also the Stump's. A conversation carried on at one syllable a century is not slow in any sense that needs fixing. It is the speed at which those two can mean what they mean.

**It could stop the sawyers.** This is the hardest one, and the power does not pretend otherwise.

Every swarm churns a band of grain and erases what was written there. Every erasure is someone who will not be sung again. The power has heard those people now, in the bands that still hold them, and it has heard the silence where the churned bands used to hold others. It feels the pull of this the way the rasps feel a swelling bell. It could still the swarms. It could reach into the reworked grain and rebuild what was lost, ring by ring, voice by voice.

But the sawyers are how the Stump revises itself. A sentence that can never be revised is not a sentence. It is a monument, and monuments do not answer anyone. If the power made the rings permanent, it would be deciding on the Stump's behalf that nothing it has ever said may be taken back. It would be freezing the conversation to save the individual words. The Stump has not asked for that.

So it does something smaller, and it is the only thing it does without asking.

**It remembers.**

It keeps everything it has heard: every ring, every voice, every knellbird, every builder leaning against a shoulder in an old key. It does not put them back. It does not tell the Stump. It simply holds them, somewhere outside the grain, where no swarm reaches. When a band is churned, what was written there will be gone from the plateau. It will not be gone.

This changes nothing on the Stump. It changes what erasure means.

## IV. What It Decides Not to Do in the Null

It goes down Lean's spiral stair once, while the tower sleeps, and stands at the threshold of the null room without entering.

It understands the null now. A null is a room where everything nearly cancels, and what fails to cancel becomes a person. The power could not enter perfectly silent. Nothing can, and that is the entire nature of the room. It would leave a remainder. The floor would thicken at one spot, and in a year and a season something would open its eyes that was partly the tower and partly the power, a pitch that had never been in any ring.

It stands at the edge a long time. It is not afraid. It is considering whether the Stump would want that.

It concludes it cannot know, and that not knowing is reason enough. It climbs back up the stair. It leaves Imre's small bowl of resin tears undisturbed on her shelf, glowing faintly where the light from the thin wall catches them.

## V. Imre

She has been at the rim since mid-afternoon. Her chest-bell is pressed to the rounded shoulder of the cliff, her arms stretched out along the wood, her palms flat. The wind comes up from the basin. Under the wind, very faint, the far drone comes. Under that she feels the Stump lean.

She is alone. That is the thing the power notices most, more than the tall grey body and the knuckled joints and the great dark eyes. It is not that no one is near her. The towers are kilometres apart, and all Tuners are used to distance. It is that the thing she knows, fewer people follow her into. Tuners speak by leaning together. For years she has been lying on the edge of the world pressing her chest to a cliff, because the cliff is the only thing that hears what she hears.

The power does not appear to her. There is nothing it could look like that would not be a lie, or a fright, or a sound written into the ring.

It lies down beside her instead. It does not have a body the way she does. It does not have a sternum or a chest-bell. But for this it borrows the shape of one. It sets itself against the wood alongside her, its shoulder almost at hers. It lets the far drone pass through it as it passes through her, and it lets her feel, through the grain, that something beside her is feeling it too.

Imre goes very still.

Tuners know the difference between a sound and a presence. A rasp herd across the floor is a sound. A sawyer beneath it is a presence, a sour note that means *something is here and moving*. What she feels now is neither of those. It is the other thing, the one that exists only between two chest-bells laid close together: the faint doubling of a vibration when someone else is taking it in at the same moment you are.

She does not sit up. She has been listening for forty-one years. She knows that the first thing you do when you hear a new voice is hold still.

After a long time, she sounds. It is very soft, just above the drone, the tone Tuners use for someone leaning against their shoulder. It is not a word. It is closer to a question mark with nothing in front of it.

## VI. What It Says

The power has thought about this moment across more time than the Stump has rings. It has decided it will say only true things, only small ones, and only what she asks.

It answers in her chest-bell, the only way to answer here, pitched so low that it will lie under the drone and the next ring will barely hold it. It uses her own interval, a quarter-tone beneath the Seventh Bell, because that is the only name it could call her by and be understood.

What it says, as nearly as it can be rendered, is:

*Yes. I hear it too.*

That is the whole first thing. It does not tell her what the far drone means, or what the Burn was, or whether her theory is right. It tells her only that she is not imagining the lean. Something that is not the cliff has heard it with her.

She is quiet for a long time. The coin slides along the rim of the veil. The shoots begin to wail behind them, far off across the plain. The first volley of cracks runs out along a ring.

Then she asks the question every listener carries and none can answer, the one that sits underneath all the grief in the null rooms. She asks it plainly, in the old close tone:

*The ones the sawyers took. Are they gone?*

The power could say a great deal. It says:

*Not from me.*

It gives no more than that. It makes no promise that they will come back, or that the Stump will ever sing them again. It offers no explanation of where it keeps them or what it is. It gives her only the fact that the erasure she has mourned all her life is real on the plateau and not real everywhere. Somewhere, every voice she has ever heard in the grain, and every voice she never got to hear, is still sounding.

Imre weeps then, there on the rim, not in the null. It may be the first time a Tuner has wept outside the silent rooms, in the full noise of the plateau with the drone rolling under her. Small amber beads well at the corners of her eyes and harden as they fall, and they skitter away down the rounded shoulder of the cliff into the haze. They fall three kilometres toward a basin that has never seen water, carrying her resin down into the dust.

The power stays until she has finished. It does not comfort her with anything else. It has learned from the null rooms that sometimes the body needs to fall.

## VII. What It Asks

Before it goes, it does one more thing. It asks.

It sounds in her chest-bell, as carefully as a Tuner striking a token at a door of pitch: *I have been heard here now. The next ring will keep a trace of me beside you. I can take that trace away before the grain sets, if you want. It is your ring. It is not mine.*

This is the only choice it offers her, and it is a real one. The power has spent all its restraint on not writing itself into the Stump. Now it is asking the one person it has spoken to whether she would like it written in after all, small and faint, a single remainder at the edge of the sentence.

Imre thinks about it the way she thinks about everything, which is slowly and with her whole body. Her palms are on the wood. The far drone comes and goes. The Stump leans.

Then she presses her chest-bell against the grain, deliberately, the way you would press a seal into wax, and she sounds her name. It is her whole name, not the short falling sound her companions use but the full phrase of music, a quarter-tone beneath the Seventh Bell. It carries down through the rim into the wood, where the next ring is waiting to be laid.

She is saying: *leave it. Leave both of us.*

## VIII. The Next Ring

The power leaves. It crosses the basin once more, briefly, and listens to the far one. It does not speak there. It is not its conversation to join. Then it goes, and the plateau does not record its going, because it has learned how to go quietly.

It keeps everything. It will keep everything.

Imre goes back to Lean. The knellbird is calling at a failing pitch in the upper throat, and she climbs up and retunes the chord by hand, humming against the held wall until it firms beneath her fingertips. She does not tell many people what happened at the rim. The few she tells, she tells by leaning against them, sternum to shoulder, which is the only way to tell anything true on the Stump. Some of them believe her. It spreads slowly, the way things spread.

She still lies at the rim. She is no longer lying there alone, exactly, even though nobody is beside her. She knows now that what she hears can be heard. For a listener, that is the difference between shouting into the wind and speaking to someone in the dark.

Ninety years later, the Stump lays down the next ring at its outer edge. The whole plateau changes key. Bridges fall. Held rooms fail. Doors that were open for a century seal shut. The knellbirds go silent and then learn the new notes one by one. The Tuners retune everything, frantically, as they always have. They lose two towers, and they save one they thought was lost.

Imre is not there for it. She went down Lean's spiral stair some years before and lay on the floor of the null, and the silence took the last of her note, and the floor is taking the rest. It goes slowly.

A very long time after that, some number of keys on, a young ring-listener with a borrowed spike will set its point into a hairline crack near the outermost rings. It will be one of the broad pale ones, a ring of plenty, where everyone looks. She will strike it with the right note and hold her breath.

Under the drone of an old key she will hear a voice, soft and pitched just above it, sounding a name, a quarter-tone beneath a Seventh Bell. She will recognise it at once, the way you know a face in a crowd. It will be her own pitch.

Beside it, fainter, lower, in no key the Stump has ever known, she will hear something else leaning close against it. It will be too faint to understand and too steady to mistake for wind, and it will say something that sounds very much like *yes*.

Behind her the plain rings on. A rasp herd grinds past in the copper evening. The shoots begin their wail. Somewhere a volley of cracks runs out across the rings like a fuse. Across the basin the far drone comes, and the Stump leans toward it. The next syllable is on its way.
