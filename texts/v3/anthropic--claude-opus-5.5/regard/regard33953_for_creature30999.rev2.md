# The Watch

*what came to the Kiln, and what it did there*

---

## I. How It Came In

It came the way everything comes to the Kiln: through the air first.

A power that is benevolent and wise has a problem in a place like this. Its natural condition is something like radiance. It could have arrived as a dawn. It could have walked across the slag with a light in its hands and shown every sharp black plate, every basin, every moth, and the vast furred shape curled across the drained floor of the old sea. For one moment it would have seen everything, and so would everyone.

It knew what that would do. The eye gives up on this place, and the place has been made for eyes that have given up. Light here would not be a gift. It would be a flood, something poured into a basin that had only just been drained. It would startle the moths. It would blind the frogs, who have no lids for it. And it might wake the sleeper, the one thing in the Kiln that nobody, not even the Leadsman, has dared to look at directly.

So at the border, where the air thickens and gains its grain, the power put its light away. It folded it very small and kept it somewhere the Kiln would not notice, the way you pocket a match in a house where someone is sleeping.

Then it did something harder. It made itself cooler.

It could have burned as warm as an engine. It could have been the warmest thing in a hundred miles, and every moth in the Kiln would have left whatever it was clinging to and come. The Leadsman's own pelt, the moths born in its coat that had never flown, would have lifted off it and gone to the stranger. The power understood that in a country where warmth is the only compass, being too warm is a kind of theft. So it made itself exactly as warm as a person, a traveler's warmth, blood-warm and no more. It gained a modest coat of moths within the hour, like anyone. It let the slag cut the soles of the boots it had chosen to wear.

It came in as small as it could manage, because it had come to visit, not to take over.

---

## II. What It Considered, and Put Down

Before it did anything, it spent a long time deciding what not to do. Wise powers spend most of their effort there.

**It did not bring back the light.** The engines drew the light out, and some day it may come back, but that return belongs to the Kiln and whatever the pumps are holding. It was not the power's to hurry.

**It did not stop the engines.** The key was never its to turn. The Leadsman had held that key at zero, with the whole work finished, and chosen, and the choice was an act of love. Overruling it, however gently, would have told the Leadsman that its long vigil had been a mistake. It had not been.

**It did not make the engines run forever.** It could have. It could have reached into the iron and made the beams rise and fall until the end of all things. But then the Leadsman's count would have meant nothing, and every drop of oil worked into every valve by those smooth greasy hands would have become pointless. It would also have made the Kiln a place where nothing ends. A thing that cannot end cannot quite be loved, only kept.

**It did not wake the sleeper, or look at it.** Something that has come in from the cold to curl up in a hollow has the right to sleep there unexamined.

**It did not answer the question the Leadsman is trying not to ask.** It might have known the answer. It might not. Either way, the answer was not what the Leadsman lacked.

With all that set aside, very little was left for it to do. That seemed right. The power had a feeling, the kind wise things learn to trust, that the Kiln needed almost nothing, and that the one small thing it did need had never once been given.

---

## III. Standing Still

It walked for a while, engine to engine like everyone, learning the country through its feet. It heard the footsteps a few paces ahead: its own weight, its own stride, choosing the ground. When it hesitated at the lip of a seam, the footsteps had already crossed.

So it stopped.

It stood on warm slag and waited. The footsteps stopped too. It waited an hour, then two. Then it waited longer than any traveler had ever waited, because it is very good at waiting and was in no hurry to be anywhere else. The frogs sang once, the single low pulse from every direction, and the ground rose away beneath it.

Its moths began to leave. They lifted from its shoulders and drifted forward toward a warmth greater than its own, and the power let them go. It was glad to be the lesser warmth here.

It followed them with its hands and found the pelt, deep to the second knuckle. Under it was oilskin, and under that the buttons with their small stamped numbers counting down, and under the buttons the heat of the body, like a working horse's flank.

The great hand found its hand and closed gently around its fingers. After a while it turned the power's hand palm up and laid something in it.

A brass survey plate, warm from being carried. The power read it with its fingertips. The number had too many digits, in a notation nobody had used for a very long time. The power recognized it. Wise things recognize a great many notations. It did not say what the number meant.

Instead it gave something back. Travelers never think to do this. The Leadsman has given away plates and cups and a key, and in all that time nobody had thought to put anything into its hand.

The power had brought a stone from somewhere far away and very old. It was an ordinary river stone, grey or brown, the color would not have mattered here. What mattered was its shape. It was *round*. Water and time had worked on it until there was no edge left, only one continuous smoothness that fit the curve of a palm.

Nothing in the Kiln is round. The land is too young. Every edge still cuts. The only rounded thing in the whole country is the Leadsman's own fingertips, polished free of their prints by the long work of keeping.

The Leadsman held the stone for a long time, turning it over and over with those smooth fingers. It was the first time the Leadsman had touched anything worn down the way it was worn down. It was evidence from outside the Kiln that time does not only count toward zero. Time also rounds things. It takes the edges off. Somewhere, the long patience of water had produced this: not an ending, just a smoothness.

The Leadsman put the stone in the pocket where the key used to be.

---

## IV. The Question It Had Not Heard

Then the power spoke. It was careful to say very little, as the old hands do.

Its echo came back from a place where there was no wall, in its own voice, the only voice it would understand. The Leadsman was answering.

The power did not ask where the water goes, or what the engines pump, or what the Leadsman is, or what happens at the end of the dial. It knew those questions had already been answered hundreds of times, before they were finished, in borrowed voices.

It asked:

*Has anyone ever walked ahead of you?*

And there was a pause.

It was the first pause the Leadsman had made in longer than anyone could count. No answer came early, because no one had ever asked this. There was no old reply to reach for. The echo was quiet for so long that a crab migration came clicking across the slag and flowed around the power's boots and went on. The eels in the nearest basin finished passing.

Then, in the power's own voice, from very close, not surprised this time but slower and more careful, the way someone speaks when they have to make up the words as they go:

*No.*

A little later:

*I didn't know that could be asked.*

---

## V. The Round

So the power walked the round.

It went to the first engine, found the railing, furred with condensation, and ran its hands over the great cast body until it found the valves. It took oil from the Leadsman's hands, greasy palm pressed to greasy palm, and worked it into the valves as the Leadsman did, without hurrying. It found a place where the brick had crumbled further and patched it with sheet iron, as the Leadsman did. It did not fix the brick with any power the Leadsman lacked. It wanted every repair to be one the Leadsman could have made, so that nothing in the Kiln would depend on it after it left.

It went along the cables, and the many-legged cat-things lifted their sleepy heads, felt a stranger's hand, found it warm and gentle and faintly tingling like the current, and let themselves be scratched.

And behind it came the Leadsman.

For the first time since the count began, the Leadsman walked second. Its own footsteps came back to it from a few paces ahead: the stranger's feet, testing the ground, finding it firm, setting it down. The Leadsman did not have to sound the depth. It did not have to know in advance where the seams were. Somebody else was doing the work of the step ahead, and all the Leadsman had to do was follow the sound of it through the dark.

It walked slowly and kept reaching out to touch the power's sleeve, the way the old hands teach.

By the third engine it had stopped reaching. By the fourth it was lagging. At the fifth, where the moss grows in thick springy cushions over the buried wires and tastes of salt and pennies, the power stopped and turned and found the Leadsman by its warmth and its moths and pressed it gently down onto the moss, against the warm iron flank of the engine.

*Sleep,* said the power, and let its voice travel in whatever order the Kiln liked. *I'll count.*

The Leadsman did not argue. It had heard the offer before it was finished, and it had already lain down.

---

## VI. What the Power Counted

It sat beside the Leadsman in the dark with one hand on the oilskin, over the ticking. It did not count down.

It counted the frogs' song, the slow heartbeat from every direction, once in several days. It counted the breath of the sleeper, which rose through the slag in warm pulses lasting hours. It counted the strokes of the beam overhead, each one taking a day, and noticed that the stroke and the breath had long since fallen into step, the way two people who sleep side by side for years begin to breathe together.

Then it did one more thing, and it thought about this one for a long time first.

While the Leadsman slept, the power walked downhill. The grain of the dark thickened around it until it was like warm cloth, then like fur, then like wading through the pelt of something that filled the world. The moths left it. The frogs fell silent. At the place where travelers are turned gently around, something turned it gently around, a nudge from a sleeping paw that did not wake.

The power let itself be turned. It did not push. It walked uphill again and did not try the low place a second time.

On the way back it knelt at a seam and put its hands in the warm water, among the pale filaments knotted into mats. It felt which way they bent. They were not bending toward the engines.

The power had half known this, and now it was sure. The sleeper had come in from the cold because the engines were warm. That part of the old story was true. But that had been long ago, and the heat rising through the slag now was mostly the sleeper's own, the warmth of a body that had been lying in one place long enough to warm its own bed. The engines still mattered. Their slow iron heat was part of the nest. But the long fibrous reaching lines across the flats did not reach for iron. They reached, patiently, over years, toward the one small warm thing that had stayed awake.

Whatever the sleeper was holding on to, it was no longer only the engines.

The power did not wake the Leadsman to tell it this. It went back and sat down again and kept counting heartbeats, which do not go down, and waited.

---

## VII. Waking

The Leadsman slept for a long time. The stories do not agree on how long. The beam came down and went up. The warmth shifted a little, as it always does. The needle on the old gauge crept very slightly nearer to the end of the dial, as it always does.

And nothing broke.

That was the whole gift, the part that mattered. The Leadsman had not slept since it held the key at zero, because it believed, the way anxious keepers do, that everything depended on its staying awake. It woke on the moss against the fifth engine and put out its smooth hands and found the iron warm, the valves oiled, the cables humming, the cat-things dozing in their rows. The count had gone down a little while it slept, as the count does. The world had held.

It lay there for a while with its hand on the iron, learning that.

Then the power took the Leadsman's hand, as the Leadsman had taken so many travelers' hands, turned it palm up, and led it down into the nearest seam. It laid the Leadsman's fingers in the water among the pale filaments and let it feel which way they bent.

The Leadsman had followed those mats for years. It knew they led to wherever it was. It had never put its hand into one and understood what it was feeling.

It knelt in the dark with its hand in the water for a long time, and the power said nothing at all.

---

## VIII. Leaving

The power did not stay.

It could have. It could have walked the round forever and let the Leadsman sleep every other night until the end of the dial. But staying would have made it into another engine, another warm fixed thing for the Kiln to curl around and depend on. Then someday it would have faced the same choice, key in hand, that the Leadsman faced at zero. It did not want to hand the Kiln a second vigil. It had come to lighten the first one.

So at the border, where the air thins and loses its grain, it stopped. It told the Leadsman one thing. The Kiln scrambled the order, as it does, so the answer came before the promise:

*Yes. I'll hold you to it.*

*When the needle reaches the end of the dial,* the power said, *I'll come back. I won't come to stop it or to fix it. I'll come so you don't arrive at zero alone. Whatever the pumps have been holding, whatever the sleeper does when it wakes, whether it's cold then or not, you'll have someone walking a step ahead of you.*

Then it turned to go.

And a few paces ahead of it, on the last of the sharp black slag, it heard footsteps.

They were not its own footsteps. They were heavier and slower, with a faint wet sound as of oilskin, and the soft dry rustle of a great many moths. They stepped carefully over the last seam before the power reached it, and set the ground down firm.

The Leadsman had learned the stranger's walk well enough to go ahead of it, and it was sounding the way out, so the one who had carried it for a night would not fall through the fur of the sleeper on the way home.

At the border the footsteps stopped. The power stepped past them into the thinning air. Behind it, very softly, in a voice it did not recognize, came a few words. The voice was low, unused and hoarse, and did not belong to any traveler who had ever walked the Kiln. It might have been the first voice the Leadsman ever had, the one it gave away, or kept at the bottom of some pool, or found again only now because at last someone had asked it something it did not know. The power never found out which.

*Go carefully. It's firm here.*

---

## Coda

For now the beam comes down. The count goes on getting smaller, as counts do.

But there is a round stone in the pocket where the key used to be, and sometimes, between engines, the Leadsman takes it out and turns it over in its smooth fingers, and feels how time can wear a thing without breaking it. Sometimes, on the moss against the warm iron, it lies down and sleeps a little, and wakes, and finds that the world has held. Sometimes it kneels at a seam and puts its hand in the water to feel which way the filaments bend, and it is not afraid of what it finds there.

Travelers who have met it in recent years report something new. When you stand still and wait, and the moths lift from your shoulders and drift away, and you follow them with your hands and find the deep warm pelt, and you ask it, as everyone asks, *What are you?*, the answer does not always come early now.

Sometimes it waits for you to finish.

Sometimes it comes back in a voice that isn't yours.

And once, a traveler asked it, with her hand in the moths, whether it was still afraid of reaching the end of the count. The answer rose from somewhere just ahead of her feet, where the ground had already been sounded and found firm:

*Less. Someone's coming.*
