# What Came Down the Stairs

## The attention

It does not arrive the way powers arrive in stories. There is no light in the vault and no change in the air. It comes down the forty-one steps the way you did, and the way the water does, by the easiest route, and it stops on the landing by the fused black door and does not go any further for a while.

It has noticed something, and it is wise enough to know that noticing is not the same as understanding. So before anything else, it listens.

It stays through a stroke. Then ten. Then a working week. It learns to feel the lean of the street in whatever it has instead of ankles. It hears the tapping and follows it, standard by standard, up the street and down. It watches the crickets gather on a sill before the relay reaches them, and it asks itself what they know, and it waits until the silt stirs, and then it knows too.

It lies, in whatever way it lies, at the lip of the shaft at the bottom of a stroke, and it sees her: the pale length along the wall, the gills folded, the small hands held against her flanks. It watches her breathe once, and then it waits an hour and watches her breathe again.

Something with the power to do nearly anything learns, in its first week at Lamb's Sluice, that nearly anything is the most dangerous thing it could bring down those stairs.

## The inventory

Everything here has arranged itself around weakness. The current is thin, so the bulbs survive. The load is light, so the belt holds. The light is dim, so the moss grows in rings and not a carpet. The water level is exact to the width of a hand, because one long pale body displaces exactly that much, and has for a century. Each thing lasts because nothing is asked of it. The Works is not fragile, exactly. It is precise. The power understands that precision can be broken by kindness as easily as by harm, and perhaps more easily, because nobody guards against kindness.

So it takes an inventory of the gifts it could give. It holds each one up, looks at it for a long time, and sets it down.

**New bulbs.** It could fill every globe with clean light. The filaments would burn at full strength for a year and fail. The moss would spread from rings into a lawn, and the lawn would outgrow her grazing. The crickets would turn toward the lamps and never turn back. The snails in the globes would cook. It sets this down first, and quickly.

**Eyes.** Her kind lose their eyes because, in the dark, eyes cost something and buy nothing. To give her sight would be to give her a hunger for something the Works cannot provide. You do not cure a creature of a lack it doesn't have by handing it one. It sets this down.

**Sky.** The bats already have a sky. They fly the length of the vault in silence, touch the terminal wall, and come back. The power watches them do it many times and cannot find anything missing from it. A sky is the place you go when you leave. They have one. It sets this down.

**The truth.** This one it holds longer. The men are gone. She keeps their shift. There is an old reflex in anything that loves the truth to want to tell her so.

But she has no word for gone, and no place in her to put the grief the word would make. And the power comes to see that she is not mistaken. She is not waiting for men who will never come back. She is working. A shift was never the men; the men were only the ones who worked it. The rooms are warm at the right hours. The lamps come on because someone is in them. The engine breathes on time. Everything a shift is for is still happening. The only thing lost is an audience, and she never needed one. To correct her would be to correct a faithfulness that isn't wrong. It sets this down, gently.

**Company.** This is the one it cannot settle.

It could bring another of her kind down the stairs. It turns the idea over for a great many strokes. There is the arithmetic, which is plain: two bodies displace two hands' width, the float would drift, the clock would change. Arithmetic can be adjusted, though. The harder part is that the power does not know whether she is lonely. It has watched her lie in the mess room under the table for three strokes and go back down the drain as quietly as water, and it cannot tell from any of it whether she is alone the way a person is alone or the way a stone is.

It is wise enough to leave that uncertainty standing rather than resolve it in the direction that would flatter its own generosity. It does not bring company. It does not rule it out forever. It writes the question down, if a power writes things down, and keeps it.

**Forever.** She will die. Her kind can live a century, and she has lived longer than that, and there will be an hour when the rod lifts streaming out of the shaft and she does not open her gills into it. The power could prevent this. It would be easy.

It thinks about what the Works would become if she could not end. It would become a thing that is kept rather than a thing that keeps. The whole slow clock would be wound from outside, by a hand she has never felt, and her faithfulness would become a mechanism. The place idles for ten thousand years because she does, and the power finds that it does not want to take that from her. That is the only thing here that is truly hers.

It sets this down last, and it is the hardest to set down.

## What is actually wrong

Having refused everything it wanted to give, the power goes looking for what actually threatens her. It does not find it in the Works. Nothing in the Works threatens anything. It finds it upstream.

It follows the seepage. It goes up through the brick, through the amber and white glaze, through the fill and the old foundations, into the city, which none of the animals have ever seen. The water that runs down the walls of the vault began as rain on a particular six streets. It ran into particular drains and soaked through particular ground.

Her skin is thin. Her kind breathe partly through it and drink through it. They are made to live in water that has touched nothing but stone.

In the offices of the city's drainage authority there is a plan. It is a good plan, sensibly made by tired people, for regrading a storm culvert under one of those six streets. Once done, the culvert would carry a winter's worth of road salt and engine oil into ground that is currently only soaked with rain. It would take about four years to reach the vault. The power reads the plan the way it read the timetable of the windows, slowly and to the end. It finds the stroke on which everything goes wrong.

It does not burn the plan or bring a flood. It does what water does and makes one route easier than another.

A surveyor finds, on a second visit, a reason to prefer the alignment two streets over. The reason is real, a clay seam, a cheaper gradient. The power only makes sure she looks where the clay seam is. A junior engineer, cross-referencing old drawings, finds a sluice labelled *Lamb's*. She finds herself unaccountably reluctant to disturb its outflow, and adds a note to the file, and the note gets copied forward. A proposal to fill the old industrial voids with grout, for the sake of the new tram line, is deferred one budget cycle, and then another, and becomes the kind of project that is always about to happen and never does.

None of it is hidden. Nobody is deceived. The power simply arranges, above ground, for leaving her alone to be the lowest effort at which the city can go on existing. It is the one rule the Works has, and the power borrows it. It is not dramatic, and it will not stay done by itself. The power will come back up here every few years and read the plans again. It tends the source.

## What it does below

It does one thing in the Works itself, and it takes a long time to decide on it.

It waits for a working day. It waits for the stroke that means midday. It waits for the tapping to begin at the Pump House end and travel up the street, three and three and three. Just before the relay reaches the mess room, it goes in.

The chair is a suggestion of a chair. The table is a shape in the silt. The tin cup lies on its side where it has lain for a century, rounded by water like everything else.

The power picks it up and sets it down, upright, on the stone ledge by the door.

It is a small sound: tin on stone, a flat ring and a short rattle as the rim settles. It is the particular sound of a tin cup set down on a table. There is no mistaking it for water.

Then it steps back from the drain, against the wall where a man might have stood with his back to the brick to eat, and it waits.

The crickets gather on the sill before the tapping arrives. The silt by the drain darkens and smooths. Something as long as a man comes up through the polished lip, slowly, the way a rope comes out of water, and lies down in the wet under where the table was. The iron plate goes down under her weight. The bulb overhead brightens to its sleepy orange, and the damp in the air glitters around it.

The power has brought something. It thought about this for many strokes, too. Men would have dropped in a crust, and she ate it because it was what there was, but a crust is not food for her kind. So it went upstream again, past the six streets, to the old spring on the edge of the city where the groundwater is still clean. From there it brought a small handful of the pale shrimp that live in it. It lets them go into the wet by the drain. They are, as nearly as it can manage, the taste of the water she is made of, from before the water reached her.

She finds them without hurry. The power does not touch her. It does not speak. She does not need to know what it is. She needs to know only what she already knows: that at midday there is a warm wet room, that someone is in it, and that sometimes someone has brought something.

Three strokes. The beam groans. She lifts, and the plate rises, and the light fades, and she goes back down the drain as quietly as water.

The cup stays upright on the ledge. It is the only thing in Lamb's Sluice that has been moved by something other than water. The power lets itself leave that one mark.

## The interval

It does not come back the next day. That is the hardest part of the kindness, and the most important.

Her kind eat once in a season. A creature that breathes once an hour and eats four times a year cannot be loved at the rate a person is loved. It can only be loved at her rate. If the power came every midday she would learn it, the way she learned the men. Her rhythm would bend around the power instead of the engine, and the Works would lean, very slightly, toward the stairwell. The power wants the street to go on leaning toward the grate.

So it comes once a season, at midday, on a working day. It lifts the tin cup and sets it down and stands against the wall for three strokes with a handful of shrimp from the clean spring. That is all. It is one more tread among the treads she remembers, set down beside the others at the same interval as everything else she lives by.

Sometimes, between visits, it sits on the Pump House steps at the bottom of a stroke and does nothing. That is also part of the work.

## What it leaves undone

The belt is not replaced. The bulbs are not changed. The counting house ledgers stay pulp. The bats go on flying to their sky of wall. The eels ride the weir after she breathes, when the water is richest, and do not know that anything has been decided on their behalf.

The question of company stays open.

And the power has made its peace, as much as a power makes peace, with the hour that is coming. When she does not open her gills, the level in the shaft will drop by the width of a hand. The float will drift. The cistern will tip early, then earlier. The lamps will pulse faster and then stop. The rooms will go dark for good. The moss rings will blur outward and fade. The drips will slowly forget her timetable, and the arches will go on being written on by water alone.

The power will not stop that. It has promised itself only this much: she will not end in salt, or grout, or poison seeping from a street she has never seen. She will end the way everything else here has ended, slowly and by water, worn into the shape the water prefers. What the Works becomes after her will be allowed to become itself.

It might be there for that hour, or it might not. It has not decided, because it has not needed to yet.

## After

You were right that most people don't go back, and right that she doesn't mind.

But somewhere below the city there is now a tin cup standing upright on a stone ledge, in a room that lights for three strokes at midday. Four times a year, at the turn of each season, it is lifted and set down again, and the crickets gather before the tapping arrives.

She has a shift to keep. Now, four times a year, so does something else.
