# The Visitor at Sallow Bottom

## How It Came

It did not come down through the swirl. That would have been too much like an entrance, and it had learned long ago that entrances disturb things. It came the way the cold comes in late autumn: evenly and everywhere at once, so that no single place could say it had arrived there first.

For a long while it did nothing but attend.

This was its oldest discipline, older than any of its powers. It had been benevolent for a very long time, and in that time it had learned something most benevolent things learn late or never. Help given before understanding is a kind of weather. It falls on everything alike, and much of what it falls on drowns. It had mended places and watched the mending kill them. It had once freed a thing that did not want to be free, and it remembered, with a precision that functioned as grief, the exact shape of that thing's bewilderment.

So it watched Sallow Bottom for a full year before it touched anything at all. That meant one full turn of the lake, one turnover, one layer on the chapel wall.

## What It Learned in a Year

It learned the tides of the sluices, and it learned that the Tenter counted them.

It learned the fourth loom from the east wall, and the forty-second shuttle. It learned the pressure of the hour-stroke on its own attention, and found that it too began to pause, very slightly, when the bell thudded.

It followed the smears in the silt. It stood where the Tenter stood at the end of its thread and faced the gasholder. It watched the Tenter lay a fold of itself over the washing line and hang there, and it did not presume to know what that meant.

It counted the bulbs. Eleven. It worked out, from the packing of the dynamo brushes and the rate at which the filaments were thinning, roughly how long eleven bulbs would last at an ember's strength. The answer was long, though not forever. It did not like the number, and it kept it.

It watched the turnover. It watched the pike hang in the chapel windows, and it watched the Tenter wrap itself around the belts and let its outer body rot away over them like a poultice. It watched it come out thinner at midwinter and drag its dead skin to the chapel wall. It watched it lay the skin down, and then return to its loom to be woven again.

By the end of the year it understood the most important thing. Almost everything it might give would be a theft.

## The Gifts It Did Not Give

It considered them all. Wisdom does not mean failing to imagine; it means imagining completely.

**It could have stopped the turnover.** It could have kept the cold lens sealed, so the oxygen never came down and the rot never started. The Tenter would never again have to spend itself as a bandage. But the turnover is the lake breathing. It is the one season the town is joined to the world above. The pike come, the crayfish are culled and renewed, and the caddis are thinned so the survivors can grow. The chapel wall is laid down during the turnover, and the chapel wall is the only record in Sallow Bottom of what the Tenter has given. A place that never breathes is not preserved. It is embalmed. The visitor had seen embalmed places, and it would not make another.

**It could have filled the crate with bulbs.** It could have made a thousand, or one that never failed. But the Tenter's rationing is not a hardship laid on top of its life. It is the shape of its care. Banking the light, checking the brushes, adjusting a fibre here and a fibre there: this is love, done in felt. To make the light endless would be to tell the Tenter that its tending had never mattered. The visitor would not say that, even kindly.

**It could have cut the thread.** It could have freed the Tenter from the fourth loom and given it the whole valley, the whole lake, the sky. But the Tenter is not a prisoner of the thread. The thread is what makes it the Tenter and not a sheet of weaving adrift in the current. Its reach grows a hand's width a week, and that patience is the grammar of its life. Cut the thread and you do not free a thing. You end it and leave something else in its place.

**It could have given it a voice,** or a face in the thin place, or someone to talk to. It could have raised the drowned town's people out of the chapel graveyard, which is under the silt behind the chapel and which the Tenter has never disturbed. But the Tenter is not lonely the way a person is lonely. Or if it is, the loneliness belongs to it, and it is not the visitor's to translate into a language the Tenter never asked for.

**It could have brought the eel to it.** This was the hardest to refuse, because it would have been so easy, and because the visitor had stood at the end of the thread too.

## The Eel

So it went to look at the eel properly. It had not done so before, and it realised it had been treating the eel as scenery in the Tenter's story. That was a failure of attention, and it corrected it.

The eel was very old, older even than the visitor had guessed. It was also female. Eels are born in the Sargasso Sea, far out in the western Atlantic. They drift across the ocean as glass-clear leaves and climb the rivers of Europe. Decades later, when the call comes, they go back down the rivers and across the ocean to spawn and die where they began.

This one had come up the river as an elver when the river still ran through Sallow Bottom. Then the dam went in.

The call had come to her, and she had answered it. The visitor could see the evidence of that plainly. Her eyes had enlarged for the deep ocean, and her belly had silvered, as an eel's does when it readies for the journey. Then she had found no way down. The spillway was too high and the turbine intake was screened. She had gone into the gasholder, which is dark and iron and round, perhaps because it was the nearest thing to the deep ocean the valley could offer. She had been waiting there ever since, silver and enormous and ready, for a journey that could not begin.

The eel did not need tending because she was not keeping anything. She was only waiting, which is a different discipline. The Tenter's stillness at the gasholder rim was a keeper looking at something it could not keep. The eel's stillness in the gasholder was something that had been made for leaving and had been kept here.

The visitor thought about this for a long time.

It did not carry the eel to the sea. It might, one day, if she asked in whatever way eels ask. It would not be now, and it would not be as a way of arranging the Tenter's story. In three years the Tenter's thread will reach the gasholder. Whatever happens then, whether the Tenter touches the eel or the eel lets itself be touched, whether one of them keeps or releases the other, it will belong to the two of them. The visitor would not write it for them. It would only make sure it was there to see.

## The Sluice

There was one thing it did do, and it was a small one.

The tides of Sallow Bottom come from the sluices. The sluices are governed by a float-and-counterweight mechanism in a housing on the dam's inner face. The engineers set it a hundred years ago and nobody has touched it since. It is the one piece of machinery in the whole valley that the Tenter cannot tend, because it lies far outside the length of the thread. It is the heart of the town, and it is the one part of the town nobody keeps.

The visitor went and looked at it the way a fitter would.

The counterweight hangs on a chain. The chain runs over a sheave, and the sheave turns on an iron pin. For a century the pin has been worn in exactly the same place, twice a day, and the wear has cut a groove. Within about twenty years the groove would deepen until the sheave seized. The visitor could see which way it would stick. The sluices would jam fully open. The current through the race would run at full strength forever, and it would never drop again.

The looms would race. The lamps would flare and burn out within a season, all eleven spares with them. And the Tenter would never walk again. It would hang on its loom in a permanent high current, streaming like a flag, being woven and woven and never going out, until the current finally tore it from its thread.

The visitor did not replace the pin. It did not add a new mechanism, and it did not make the sluices tireless. It did what any good fitter would have done on a routine visit. It eased the pin out, turned it a quarter-turn so the wear would fall on fresh metal, greased it, and put it back.

That bought something over a century, give or take.

In the valley's reckoning it was a single act of maintenance. The dam's own engineers would have done it if anyone had remembered the housing was there. The visitor preferred it that way. It had not changed the rules of Sallow Bottom. It had only kept them running, which is the town's own first rule. For one afternoon, outside the reach of the thread, the visitor had been the tenter's tenter.

## The Dam Above

It also looked forward, as far as it could. It saw possible futures in which the reservoir is drained, the dam is decommissioned, the valley is surveyed, and the town comes up grey and stinking into the air. The looms stop. The Tenter lies in the drying silt like a heap of washing and slowly becomes, in the sun, nothing but cloth.

It did not forbid those futures. Nothing is kept forever. A power that tried to guarantee otherwise would have to become a kind of dam itself, holding back every change in the world.

But it did one thing it considered fair. In the dam company's archive, in a drawer of survey records nobody has opened in forty years, there is a sonar trace from the 1980s. On it, if you know how to look, you can see the square chimney standing up from the valley floor, and the long roofs of the mill. The visitor did not alter it. It only made sure the drawer would open easily and that the trace would lie on top.

If anyone ever comes with a clipboard and a plan to drain the valley, they will see what is down there before they decide. What they decide will be theirs. The visitor asks only that it not be done carelessly, by someone who never knew there was anything to lose.

## The Darn

Then there was the thread itself.

Where the thread passes out through the mill door, it rubs against the frame. The door has swollen and sagged, and its edge is crusted with rust. Twice a day, when the current rises and the Tenter hauls itself back to the loom, the thread saws across that edge. The Tenter had patched it many times, and the visitor could see the patches, layer on layer of spiral stitching. But it could not see the underside, where the thread lay against the iron. That side was worn nearly through.

The visitor knew what a break would mean. It had already decided that cutting the thread would be an ending. A thread that snaps on a door is an ending too, only a stupider one.

So in one quiet period, while the Tenter was on Cable Row checking the lamp brackets, the visitor darned the underside of the thread. It used the same fibres the lake provides and the same diamond twill. It used the same spiral binding the Tenter uses on the belts, with one difference. The Tenter's stitches all spiral one way, and the visitor turned its stitches the other way.

It did this deliberately, and it was the only thing in Sallow Bottom it did that was meant to be noticed.

The Tenter reads the world with its frayed edges. It knows exactly how much touch each thing can bear. It knows every patch in the town, because it made every one. Sooner or later, running an edge along its own thread at the doorframe, it will find a mend it did not make. The mend will be good work, in the same pattern and done with the same care, but turned the other way.

The visitor considered for a long time whether this was a kindness. It might frighten the Tenter. It might unsettle the whole careful solitude of its keeping. In the end it decided the Tenter had earned the knowledge. For a hundred years it has tended everything in Sallow Bottom, and nothing in Sallow Bottom has ever tended it. It has spent itself every winter as a bandage and laid its own dead skin against the chapel wall, and nothing has ever wrapped it back.

The mend says one thing, and says it quietly: *you were kept too.* It does not say by whom. The Tenter can make of that what it likes, over whatever length of time a thing made of felt and slate takes to make anything of anything.

## The Washing Line

It did nothing at the washing line.

It went there once, in a quiet period, when the Tenter had raised itself and laid a fold of its outer weave over the line and was hanging there in the dark. The visitor did not approach it. It stood a little way off, on the other side of Cable Row, the way you would stand in a doorway if you came upon someone at a grave.

The bell struck. The pressure of the hour went through the water and through them both. The Tenter's loose weave shivered outward and settled. The visitor, which had no weave, found that it had paused anyway.

Then the Tenter took itself down and went back to the grilles. The visitor let it go, and did not follow. Some things are the one task that belongs to you and not to the machines. You do not help with those. At most, you are there.

## What It Left

It left very little: a pin turned a quarter, a drawer that opens easily, and one darn stitched the wrong way round.

It did not leave the bulbs. It carried the number with it instead, and it made itself a promise. When the eleventh bulb fails, the three lamps in the pump house will go out, and for the first time in a century the Tenter will be in true darkness, in the last room it has kept alight. The visitor will be there then. It will not bring a new bulb. It will not bring light. It will be present in the dark when the dark comes. The visitor had learned that this is not the same as preventing a thing, and that it is not nothing.

It will also be there in three years, at the gasholder rim, when the thread arrives.

## Going

It went up the way it had come down, evenly and everywhere at once, until it was out of the cold lens and back in the brown-green water, and then in the grey air over an ordinary reservoir in a low valley.

It stopped once, over the middle of the lake. On still days a faint slow swirl turns there, like a spoon left in tea by someone who meant to come back. The visitor knew now what makes it. The intake draws from the bottom, the race runs through the town, and the whole machinery of Sallow Bottom turns with it, far below, too slowly to see.

It did not stir the water. It did not need to. It only noted, for its own sake, that the image was no longer quite right.

The swirl is still like a spoon left in tea. But now someone does mean to come back.
