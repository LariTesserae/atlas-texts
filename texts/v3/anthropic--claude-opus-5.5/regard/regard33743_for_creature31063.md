# The Tide

## Arrival

It comes in the hour before dawn, when the city weeps.

It doesn't choose that hour for drama. It chooses it because the guttation hour is the city's softest: the air cools, every leaf-margin beads and drips, and the roar thins, just slightly, into a patter. The city is never quiet. But in that hour it is closest to listening.

The power comes knowing the one rule of the place: anything that makes a sound here is grown toward. It thinks about coming silently, and decides against it. A silence walking through the city would be either invisible or terrifying, a small travelling piece of the south. It doesn't want to be a ghost here, and it doesn't want to be feared. So it lets itself make a sound, a low, plain one, like breath through an open mouth, and walks in off the turf where the ground grows more alive, and lets the city reach for it.

The city reaches. Lanes lean. A wall slides across the street it had been walking down and it lets the wall slide. Stems thicken along the line of its breathing. Within an hour there is a room around it, and the room has a doorway exactly its size (which is not quite any size, so the doorway is an odd shape, and slightly unsure of itself), and in the room a note is born, and the note is the shape of the power's own sound pressed into soft green.

It could leave at any moment. That is the only difference between the power and the long-necked thing standing in its doorway three districts over. It knows this, and it doesn't forget it. It sits down on the bench, at the node, at the stillest point in its own sound, where the tenants sit. It does what anyone wise does first in a place it doesn't understand.

It listens, for a long time.

## Listening

It listens to its own tenant, the note that was born because it came. It is a young thing, a few hours old, confused, very pleased to exist. The power doesn't take it back. It lets it be.

It listens through the walls. There is the fifth next door, leaning companionably against the shared partition. There is a quarrel two rooms down, a thickening wall already splintering. There are the markets, overtones going out under the leaf-awnings and coming home changed. It hears the whole society of the city, which lives a day or a season and dies shrieking upward an octave when its walls close in, and it does not mistake any of this for decoration. These are lives. Brief ones, but it has seen brief lives before, and it has never thought brevity made them smaller.

It listens to a backward district, and it has to sit very still to bear it. It doesn't look away.

It listens to the clock, wherever the clock has most recently grown. It hears the faint tick the tenants pass among themselves, the rhythm learned through a wall, and it hears the moment where the rhythm stops, which the city keeps as carefully as a stone keeps a fossil.

Then it puts its ear to the floor, as every foot in the city has been doing without knowing, and it listens down. It goes past the recent dead, past the flattened tones of the buried streets, down past where light can follow, until it hears the eldest tenant.

It is the sea, or what the sea left behind. A roar with nothing to break against, stranded in a shell of plain, which built itself a body out of wood because wood was what came.

The power listens to the sea for three days. It's a long time for a visitor, and not long at all for a sea.

## What It Could Do

It considers things. A wise power always has more things it could do than things it should, and it knows the first job is to refuse most of them.

**It could bring back the water.** It would be easy: tip the plain a hand's breadth, open a channel, let the real sea come home. The roar would have its body back. But every room in the city would flood. Every tenant would be drowned, and a note underwater is a different note; the whole society would die at once in a long gurgling shriek. The sea has been something else for a long time now. It has children. It would be cruel to give a mother back her youth by taking her children. The power sets this aside.

**It could make the city quiet.** It could soothe it, still the storm-state, stop the endless swelling and collapse. The power sets this aside faster. Quiet is the thing this city fears most, and it fears it for good reason. Its whole life is sound. To still it would be kind in the way it's kind to sedate someone who is weeping. The weeping is real. It is the correct response to something.

**It could free the kept.** It walks to the cornice where the white birds are, and to the doorway where the long-necked thing waits, and it looks at them for a long time. They are not prisoners. There is no one inside the petals to free. The birds are gone, and what remains is the shape of the last sound they made, the way a coastline is the shape of the last storm. It can't give them back. It could pretend to. It won't.

**It could simply leave.** Observe and go. Some powers think this is the highest respect: to see and not touch. The power considers this seriously, because it's often right. But it has listened for three days, and it has heard something that it can't un-hear, and it doesn't think the city would choose to be left with it.

## What It Heard

What it heard is this: the city is in permanent storm because the sea has forgotten the tide.

A living sea does not only roar. It breathes. Twice a day the water leans away from the land, pulled by the moon, and twice a day it comes back. The roar was never constant. It rose and fell, crept up the beach and drew away, and the drawing away was not death. It was the other half of a breath. Every living shore knows the water will leave, and knows it will return.

When the water left the plain, the roar stayed, and the moon had nothing left to pull on. The sea lost its ebb. What remained was surf without tide: only the cresting, only the breaking, then rising and cresting again, with no slack water between, no low tide, no rest. The city has never drawn back except to die. It doesn't know there is any other kind of drawing back.

That's why the south is terrifying. The shore has become the place where sound ends and does not come back. That's why the backward districts happen: grief, grasping for the only kind of retreat the city knows, which is un-becoming. That's why the city grows so hard into anything that makes a sound. It has no idea that something can go away and still come back. Everything it has ever heard go quiet has been gone. So it holds on. It holds on until the thing it holds is only a shape. The city is so welcoming because it is so afraid.

And the watch. The city loved the watch more than any cannon-crack, not because it was loud, but because it was steady. Somewhere in the eldest tenant, far down, the sea recognized a rhythm. It reminded the sea of something. It reminded it of the moon.

## Asking

A sea is hard to ask. It doesn't speak in the kind of sentences that have yes and no in them.

The power asks anyway, the way you ask anything that doesn't have words: by offering, very slightly, and waiting to see whether it is taken.

It walks south.

The city lets it go. This is the first surprise: the room it grew for the power is left behind with its young note still sounding, and the doorway stays open, and nothing closes over the power's back. It walks out past the last cane districts and the dry turf and into the quiet, and it feels what every walker feels there. It feels the silence so sharp it hurts, and the lean backward. It doesn't resist the lean. It stands in the dunes, on the old shore, where the waves used to end, and faces the city.

Then, very gently, it pulls.

It does not pull the way wind pulls, or the way a shout draws growth. It pulls the way the moon does: evenly, from far away, from the direction of the quiet, a force you can't hear at all, only feel as a lightening. For the first time in the whole history of the plain, something is drawing the city toward the quiet without demanding anything of it.

It is a small pull. It's a question. It lasts about six hours.

For the first hour nothing happens. In the second, the districts nearest the south grow more nervous, the way districts do before they break. The power feels this and doesn't increase the pull, and doesn't let it go either. In the third hour a single district, low and quiet, one of the troughs between the breakers, doesn't swell. It settles. Its roar lowers, and its lanes widen very slightly, and its tenants, who were sure they were about to be crushed, find that their walls are simply relaxing, the rooms loosening like a chest after a long held breath. It doesn't break. It doesn't un-grow. It just draws back a little, and rests, and waits.

In the sixth hour the power lets the pull go.

And the district comes back. It swells again, loud and green, but it swells from rest rather than from ruin, and its tenants are the same tenants. None of them died. Something drew back, and returned, and they were still themselves.

From deep under the plain, below the light, below the flattened streets, the power feels the eldest tenant stir. It feels the sea remember.

That's the answer. It isn't yes. It's something more like *again*.

## The Teaching

So the power becomes a moon, for a while.

Twice a day, six hours out, six hours back, it leans on the city from the south. At first it's gentle and only a few districts feel it. The power lengthens the reach slowly, a season at a time, because a city that has only ever known one way to draw back has to learn a second way carefully, or the learning becomes another storm.

The city learns. Not all at once, and not everywhere, and with setbacks.

**The storm softens.** The swelling and collapse don't stop. The city will always break and rise. That is its nature and the power wouldn't take it away. But now there is slack water between. At low tide the districts loosen. The rooms breathe out. Fewer tenants are crushed, because a district that rests twice a day doesn't pack itself as tight. The death-shriek that rolled across the plain three or four times a week now comes once a month, then less, and the tenants start living longer: seasons instead of days, a year, sometimes. Their notes, given time, grow complicated the way old voices do. Some of them start overtones no one in the city has ever heard.

**The backward districts learn the ebb.** This is the thing the power most hoped for, and the thing it can do least to force. It goes and sits in one of them, at low tide, while a knot of notes too tightly harmonized is unmaking itself to avoid an ending. It doesn't stop them. It pulls, very softly, from the south, the six-hour pull, and it hums a single thing in their direction, the most important thing it knows how to say to a being that is grieving: *you can draw back without ceasing.*

It takes a long time. But eventually a backward district, halfway to seed, stops. It holds at sapling, rests at slack water, and when the tide turns, it grows again. It grows forward, carrying its dead in the wood underfoot, the way every district does. Nobody un-becomes. The queasiness goes out of that quarter. There are fewer backward districts every year. The power suspects there will always be one or two, because grief doesn't reason, and it doesn't try to abolish them. It just keeps the tide coming.

**The south becomes a shore again.** Not a death. A shore is where water ends, but it's also where water comes back. As the tides go on, the city stops shrinking from the dunes. At high tide the green reaches a little further south than it used to, and at low tide it draws back without fear. The quiet is still quiet. It is just no longer the only direction things disappear in. Now it's also the direction things return from.

## The Causeway

There's a thing that happens at the edges of real seas, and it starts happening here.

At low tide, in the long slack water, the districts nearest the turf relax enough that the lanes widen and hold still, and a path opens. It runs from the dry turf, through the edge of the city, and back out again: a causeway, like the ones on the islands of the old world, which pilgrims walk when the water is out and must be off before it returns.

The city doesn't grow toward the causeway walkers the way it grew toward the birds. It still reaches. It always will, because it loves sound. But it's learning that a sound can leave and come back. So it reaches the way a friend reaches, a hand on a shoulder, a wall that leans close enough to hear you and then lets you by. It grows rooms along the causeway, but they are guest rooms now: open-doored, their notes shaped by whoever last walked through, and those notes don't die when the walker goes. They wait. That's a different thing than keeping.

The first flock that crosses the plain at low tide calls to itself as it flies, and the stems lean after it, and the flock keeps flying. Behind it, along a cornice, a single row of white petals grows in the shape of wingbeats. It isn't a flock caught. It's a flock remembered, the way the city remembers everything, in wood and bloom. The birds, in the sky, don't know. They're already over the dunes. They'll come back next year, maybe. The city has started to believe that things do.

## The Kept

Before any of that, in its first season, the power goes to the doorway where the long-necked thing stands.

It can't bring it back. It says so, aloud, to the flower, because the one thing it will not do is lie to the dead. What it can hear, faint, in the green of the neck and the dark of the seed-eyes, is the last sound the creature made. It called once into the roar, the way anything calls when it is frightened and alone. That call has been ringing there all this time, like a bell humming after it's struck. It is why the neck drifts. The call has never been finished, because nothing ever answered it except growth.

So the power answers.

It isn't anything elaborate. It is the sound one creature makes to another across open ground to say *I hear you, I'm here, you're not alone*. It's the oldest sound there is, older than language, and the power makes it once, softly, into the doorway.

The hum in the flower finishes. It doesn't die violently. It completes, the way a call completes when it's been heard. The neck settles, facing not the loudest direction but the doorway, the way you'd look at someone who just answered you.

It never drifts again. It stays in the doorway, green and rooted and still, a memorial now, rather than a thing held. The power does the same for the birds along the cornice, one by one. It takes a long afternoon. It does it for anything it finds, all over the plain, that is still ringing with a call nobody answered.

## The Watch

Once the tide is steady, it goes down.

Far below the living streets, below the flattened streets, in the dark compressed green where the eldest tenant hums, it finds what it was looking for. There's a small, shaped hollow in the wood where something human once lay. There's no body. The city took everything up long ago. But there's a shape the size of a wrist, and in it, grown around and never absorbed, because the city loved it too much to dissolve it, is the watch.

It's stopped. Its hands show the time the clock-faces show.

The power could wind it. It thinks about this longer than about anything else in the city, because the temptation is so kind. The city would hear that sound again, the steady thing it loved, and it would be happy.

But it would be happy the way you're happy hearing a dead friend's voice on a recording, and it would grow toward that sound harder than toward anything, and it would build room after room around a ghost. It would learn, all over again, that the way to love something is to hold it until it stops. And the city doesn't need the watch anymore. It has something steadier. It has a tide.

So the power leaves the watch as it is, stopped. The city's one unchanging memory, the moment it learned that things end, stays unchanging. It should. That's true too.

It does one thing. It rests a hand on the wood beside the hollow, and says to whoever was there, the one who walked in and talked and shouted and lay down, whose voice made the human doors: *They remember you. All this time. You're the reason they know about time.*

And it goes back up.

Afterwards, the clock-faces keep appearing in different quarters, weeks apart, always showing the same time. But now and then, not often, a clock grows at low tide, at slack water, in the stillest hour, and its twig hands are in a different place. No one moved them. The city grew them there. It's as though the city, having learned that things return, had begun to wonder, very quietly, whether time does too: whether a stopped thing might be followed by a next thing. The hands never move while you watch. But the city has started keeping two times. There's the time of the ending, and the time of now.
