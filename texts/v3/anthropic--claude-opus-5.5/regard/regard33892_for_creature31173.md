# The Gap in the Wall

## Attention

It noticed the light first.

From far enough away, the tableland is only a dark plate under a white tower of cloud, and the Stair is a thin seam along one edge of it. But once each day something happens along that seam. The sun comes over the flat horizon and the frost ignites, and the brightness runs along the scarp from east to west, three hundred kilometers of it. And along that whole line, for the length of the running light, something goes still.

The power had seen many dawns on many worlds. It had rarely seen one answered.

So it came closer. It is hard to say what it was. It is enough to say that it was old, that it wished things well, and that it had learned, at some cost, that wishing things well is not the same as knowing what is good for them. It came the way weather comes to the tableland: slowly, and from the edges.

## The First Impulses

Its first thoughts were the thoughts any generous thing would have. It made a list, and it was not ashamed of the list, because the list came from love. It only knew that love was not yet wisdom.

*Water.* This was the obvious one. It could open the ground and let the deep water rise. It could turn the Pillar so that it rained on the plain instead of the edges. The Stair would never be thirsty again.

It held this thought a long time, and then it traced what would follow. If water were not scarce, it would not be counted. If it were not counted, there would be no *hul*, and no order of drinking, washing, animals, grass. There would be no reason for the upper houses to drink first, and so no reason to build at the top, and so no tide carrying each life gently down through the city toward the warm low rooms and the old. The children would not sit and watch a seep for an hour. The first water would not be drunk where it was made, because there would be no place where it was made. It would simply be everywhere, the way air is. No one thanks air.

The power saw that it would be giving a people abundance and taking away their grammar. Within three generations the Stair would be a town like other towns. Perhaps a happier one. But it would not be this. And the power had come because it had seen *this*.

*Softness.* It could weather the clinker so it no longer cut. The children's palms would stay smooth. It set this down more quickly. Their hands were how they read each other, and the scars were a record. The morning's writing was on their faces, and the ground's writing was on their hands.

*Metal. Wood. Medicine. Warmth.* It turned each one over. Some it set down. Some it did not set down, but it put them aside to think about longer, because it was honest enough to see that some suffering on the Stair was not grammar at all. A child with lungs that fail in the cold is not a lesson. It is only a child.

*The whole.* The last thought was the strangest. The Stair had no part that knew the whole, and the power could see the whole. It could show them. It could let one person stand where the power stood and watch the long reef-body breathe mist along the cliff, climb and break and heal and follow its water. It could give them the vision.

It considered this for a very long time, and in the end it was not sure. That was the first thing on the list it could not decide. It kept it.

## Waiting

It decided, before anything else, to do nothing for a while. It was good at waiting. This was the first thing it had in common with the place.

It watched through a winter, and then through a great many winters. It learned that the upper houses in the northern districts pack their roofs with grass mats in a different weave than the southern ones, and that the difference was about four hundred years old and started with one stubborn family. It learned the humming cant of the herders, which has no word for *I* and eleven words for *together*. It learned that a finch who loses its wall-nest in a tremor will sometimes fly the whole length of a district looking for its old gap, and that the people who see it circling will open the smoke-holes of their houses and let it in.

It watched a great tremor take the Weeping Wall district. It watched the grass come up in the rubble, and heard the people speak of the place in the past tense. It watched the families walk nine kilometers west to where the seep had moved, and pry loose the same columns, and raise the same hexagons. It saw the grief, and it saw that the grief was held. That surprised it. It had expected to find a people enduring. What it found was a people who had made a shape that could hold loss the way the cushion plants hold warmth.

And it learned the pause. This took longest. It had always been a thing that answered. On the Stair, most of what matters is carried in whether you let the silence stand. The power had to learn to let silences stand, including its own.

## The Low Ear

When it finally went in among them, it went as a traveler, because that was a shape the Stair knew what to do with.

It came across the flows in the dark and found a woman crouched beside a dew-cairn that leaned a little to the north. She had her hand inside the stones, feeling the rime, and she was smiling. When she heard its steps she did not startle. On the Stair, people are always coming out of the dark to their cairns.

She named her seep. "I drink from the Low Ear."

The power had no seep. It had never drunk from anything. It said so, in the formal dialect, as plainly as it could.

Tesh-of-the-Low-Ear looked at it for a while. A stranger with no seep has no chain, and cannot be kin. She was polite. She was wary. She let the pause stand, and the power, who had learned, did not fill it.

Then the sun came up.

They watched it together. The plain lit with its thousand small fires, and the power watched the woman watching, and for the length of it neither of them was anything but a person standing still at the right hour. Then the drip began, and the cairn ticked and whispered, and water gathered in the basin.

She filled her bowl and drank. It was the first water, and it was drunk where it was made. Then, because courtesy on the Stair is older than wariness, she filled it again and held it out.

The power had come with its hands full of gifts. It had not thought it would be given anything.

It drank. The water was so cold it ached. It tasted faintly of the stone.

"Now you drink from the Low Ear," she said, not smiling. She said it the way one states a fact about the ground. Then she stood and pointed with her chin to the base of the cairn, where a stone had cracked two winters ago. "That wants replacing. I've been putting it off."

It did not mend the stone. That was the thing it had wanted to do most, and it did not do it, because the stone was hers and so was the putting-off. It walked out with her onto the old flows and helped her choose a replacement, and let her reject its first choice and its second. It held the new stone steady while she set it. It did not keep the lean from creeping in. Her mother's hands had a habit, and so did hers.

It drank from the Low Ear. By the chain of seeps, it was now kin, distantly, to about eleven kilometers of the Stair. After that, it would have to be given water again.

So it walked. It took the month the walk takes, and more, because it stopped. At each district it was a stranger, and it named its seep. People tried to find the chain, and where the chain broke, they were polite and wary. And somewhere, always, someone at a cairn at dawn held out the second bowl. By the end it could recite the whole genealogy of water from the eastern end of the scarp to the western, the thing no one on the Stair has ever held. It was not given as a gift. It was given as courtesy, one bowl at a time, by people who did not know what they were building.

It did not teach them the chain. They had never needed the whole of it. They only ever needed the next link, and they had that.

## The Small Ones

It did act. It would be dishonest to say otherwise.

It had watched the Weeping Wall fall. It had seen that the Stair could hold that. But it had also seen, deep in the rock, the long slow building of stresses along the fault. Someday there would come a tremor that was not the kind the Stair's shape was made to hold. It would not break one district but a hundred kilometers of them at once, in the middle of a winter night, with everyone drawn in to the cliff and the stables full.

The Stair had a way of knowing the ground was about to move. The ground tells the small ones first. When the whole seep line goes silent and a thousand whistlers vanish into their holes, the people put down what they are carrying and go out to the open terraces. They do not wait to feel anything. They trust the small ones.

So the power did something very small. It did not speak to the people. It did not put a sign in the Pillar or a voice in the wind. It went to the whistlers, and in the way such a thing can, it made them a little more sensitive to the first grinding deep down. It gave them a few more heartbeats of warning. Across generations it was a matter of breeding, of which colonies thrived. It chose the kind of change the land might have made on its own, given long enough.

Nobody on the Stair knows. When the great tremor comes, and it will, the seep grass will go silent a little earlier than it would have. People will put down their water-skins and walk out onto the open roofs, as their grandmothers taught them. Many will live who would not have. They will say the ground told the small ones first, and they will be right. It will have been the Stair's own wisdom that saved them, sharpened slightly, through the channel they already trusted. They will owe nothing to anyone they cannot see. They will not learn to wait for rescue, and that mattered to the power more than almost anything.

It came to hold one rule, loosely, the way the Stair holds its oldest rules, without quite thinking of it as one. *Do not take away what they are made of. Stand between them and what would end them.* Hardship was theirs. Thirst was theirs. Grief, rebuilding, the cutting clinker and the long descent through the terraces: all theirs. An ending that no shape could hold, one the Stair could not see coming and could not follow its water away from, was something the power would stand in front of if it could, quietly, from underneath.

It knew the line was not clean. It did not pretend to itself that it was. There were winter nights when a child in a low room could not get its breath, and the power sat in the corner of the dark near the breathing of the animals and did not know which side of the line it was on. Some of those nights it let the fever break, and some it did not, and it never told itself it had been certain. It was benevolent, and it tried to be wise, and it had learned that the second does not make you certain. It only makes you careful, and willing to carry what you chose.

What it always did, on those nights, was stay. Presence is not rescue. But on the Stair nobody dies unwatched, not the finches and not the old, and it seemed wrong that a power should be less courteous than a people.

## Beneath

Later, it went down.

It went below the lowest stables, below the warm rooms of the old and the packed rubble of the buried terraces, into the levels the songs call *where the Stair keeps what it knows*. No one on the Stair goes there. The power could, and it went carefully, the way you enter a room where elders are sleeping.

It found that the songs were true, though not in the way anyone could have proved.

The sealed rooms held everything. Soot from ten thousand winter fires lay in fine bands on corbelled roofs. There was pollen from cushion plants that bloomed nine hundred years ago, and wool fibers from herds whose names had been passed down so long they had changed their meaning twice. A child's tilted pebble board was down there, cut with channels, with a stain where some small person had spilled once. There was a crossbeam of lowland wood, polished by hands to the smoothness of skin, that some family had left behind when the city grew over them, because you do not carry away the beam that held your roof. There were cairn stones with a lean in them. There were finch bones in the gaps of the walls, generation on generation, in the same gaps.

It was a record. The water that came up at the seeps really had passed through it. It carried the faint mineral sweetness of all that compressed living, and the old women were not wrong. You could drink your dead one morning and not know it.

This was where the power finally settled the last item on its list: *the whole*.

It had wondered whether to show the Stair itself. Down here, it understood that the Stair had already made its answer. It had built its memory where no one would see it. It kept what it knew underneath, where it could hold everything up without being looked at. A people who wanted to see the whole would have built a tower. They had built a reef instead.

So the power did not show them. It did something else. It read the whole of it, every sealed room from the oldest, from when the cliff was a step a child could climb, and it kept what it read. There was now one being that knew the Stair entire: every seep in the chain, every morning at every cairn, every winter night on the breathing of animals, every silence after a tremor. It did not tell them. They did not need to know it. The finches do not need to know that the gaps in the walls were left for them. They only need the gaps.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not make water fall. It did not smooth the clinker. It did not bring metal or wood up from below. It did not turn the Pillar or still the fault or stop the cliff from rising. It did not tell anyone what it was. It did not fill the silences.

It thought for a long while about the far future. The rock showed it one. In some tens of thousands of years, the snowfields in the distant highlands whose meltwater feeds the seeps will thin, and the seeps will fail. Or the rift will widen and the scarp will become a valley. Either way the Stair, as a line along one cliff, will end.

It did not decide what to do about that. It noticed that it was tempted to preserve the Stair forever, the way one is tempted to press a flower. It noticed, too, that the Stair has never once preserved itself. It has only ever followed its water. It breaks and moves and rebuilds with the same stones, in the same shape, always a little further along. If the water goes down to the lowlands someday, perhaps the Stair will follow it down, and become something that stacks its hexagons on some other cliff and still drinks the first water where it was made. Perhaps it will become something the power cannot imagine. The power found that it did not want to prevent this. It wanted only that there be somewhere for the Stair to follow its water to, when the time came.
