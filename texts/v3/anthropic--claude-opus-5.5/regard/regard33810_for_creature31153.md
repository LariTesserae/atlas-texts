# The Attention

## The Noticing

It did not see Fennick first. It felt it.

A wise and benevolent power has, among its many burdens, the burden of attending to everything at once, and so it rarely notices any one thing. What catches such an attention is not size or brightness. It is rhythm. Somewhere in a wet country, in a shallow basin where no heart should be, something was keeping time: *thum*, every eleven seconds, steadily and alone, the way a person taps a foot in an empty waiting room.

The power had heard a great many hearts. It had not often heard one that was so clearly waiting for someone else to arrive.

So it came to the basin, under the grey sky, in ordinary rain. It put what served it for a palm flat against the skin beneath the pewter leaves and counted to eleven, and felt it.

Then it did the first wise thing, which was to take off its light.

A power of its kind cannot help shining, much as a lamp cannot help it. It left the light outside the seam, folded, the way a considerate guest leaves muddy boots on the step. It went in dark. It had understood, from the temperature of the air at the entrance and the faint cold green of the lichen beyond, that in this place light would be a kind of violence.

## Staying

It stayed a long time, by the standards of the beetles. By its own standards it was an afternoon.

It learned the rules the way any visitor learns them, not all at once, and it did not use what it already knew to skip ahead. It walked the high street in three minutes and walked it back in twenty. It went downhill toward the factory both ways and found this delightful, because it had made a great many hills and had never thought of that. It stood under a corner lamp and heard footsteps pass on their way home. It learned to wait for the snails. When one took the middle of the street, it stood on the pavement with the beetles, in its own stretch of dark, and let the line back up behind it.

It also felt the thing visitors feel: the tightness in the skin, the sense of being only now finished. This surprised it more than anything else in Fennick. It was very old, and it had been finished for a long time. For the length of its stay it walked around feeling faintly sticky at the edges, like everything else here, still pushing upward. It found that it did not mind.

## What It Could Have Done

Benevolence, the power had learned over a very long time, is mostly a discipline of *not*. The temptations came to it one by one, as temptations do, each in the shape of a kindness.

**It could have lit Fennick.** One gesture, and every parchment wall would have glowed with its veining, every seed-cobble thrown a small shadow, and the whole grid of the town would have stood revealed and lovely in the hollow. The square would have been empty. Light was the one thing that could kill everyone in Fennick at once, and it would have done so while looking like a gift.

**It could have restored the village.** It already knew what was over the rise, and it went to look anyway. It could have drained the reservoir and raised the stone. But draining brings up silt and gables, not people, and Fennick was never a failed copy of that place. It was what the place became when it was remembered by the ground. You do not correct the spelling of a grief.

**It could have stopped the snails.** Then the town would have finished: rungs on the chairs, four panes to every window, pictures in the frames. But a finished Fennick would have nothing left to become. The snails were how the bulb learned what mattered by losing what didn't. Four stalks and a leaf is still a chair. It may be more of a chair than the first one was.

**It could have stopped the flowering.** This was the hardest. It could have fed the bulb forever, kept the pulse at eleven seconds and the hymn going and the beetles in their lines until the end of the world. But a bulb prevented from spending itself is not saved. It is kept. The power had long ago learned the difference between keeping a thing and saving it, and it had learned it by getting it wrong.

**It could have given the townsfolk words.** It could have tuned the voices on the square until they resolved, and you would know at last what had been said that was ordinary and a little funny. But the words had never been the bulb's to hold. What came down into the root was the laugh, not the joke. The power judged that the laugh was enough, and that it was, in fact, the more important half.

Having refused all of these, it added a rule of its own to Fennick's list:

*It would do nothing here that a beetle, or a visitor, could not have done.*

## What It Did

### It read the snails.

No one had. They were an archive, a library, carrying every draft of Fennick in tight whorls where no one would ever look. An archive that is never read is only a heavy thing on someone's back.

So the power asked, in whatever way snails are asked, and the snails allowed it. Over many days it ran its thumb along each shell from the soft new lip inward, down through the four-stalk chairs and the cornerless frames, back through windows with two panes and then four, back through chairs with rungs and then with turned legs, back into the worn center of the oldest shell. There, at the very beginning, in the first draft the bulb ever made, was a picture frame with something inside it.

It was a face. It was too worn to make out, and the power could have made it out, and it did not try harder than a thumb could try.
