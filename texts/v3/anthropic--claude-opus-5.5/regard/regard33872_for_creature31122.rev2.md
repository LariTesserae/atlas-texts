# The Guest

## The Attention

It does not arrive the way weather arrives, or the way the bats do. It is simply there, the way you notice you have been hearing something for a while.

The power has attended to a great many places. It has learned that the first duty of attention is to find out what kind of thing it is looking at before it decides what that thing needs. Most harm done by the well-meaning is done in the gap between those two steps. So it looks for a long time before it does anything at all. Here, a long time is measured in strokes.

It sees what anyone would see: the engine, the lamps, the million-winged commute, the cottages kept like held breath. Then it sees what fewer would. The roof is sound. The seep is clean enough. The gasholder is not about to fail. The beetles, the bats, the swiftlets, the spiders and the crickets are not captives. They are tenants of the ordinary kind, fed and sheltered, free to leave by the shaft at any dusk. Most of them do leave, and most of them come back. Nothing in Stenning Deep is suffering, at least not in any way the power can find, and it looks hard, because it knows that places this beautiful are good at hiding their costs.

So the first thing it decides is what it will *not* do.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not stop the engine. It can hear in the brick-dust that sifts down on the hard strokes what the engine is holding up. It knows, too, that the engineman's watch is the oldest living thing in the Deep that could be called a promise. You do not break a promise that is keeping a roof up, however little water it lifts.

It does not free the Fig from its vow. It could, perhaps, give the tree the rest of a mind: a centre, a face, the capacity to know what it has been doing for a hundred and fifty years. It considers this seriously. It considers it the way you would consider waking someone who is sleeping peacefully in a house where everyone they loved has died. The Fig is not a mind. It is closer to a vow, and a vow does not grieve. To turn it into something that could understand the polished chair arm would be to give it the one thing the whole Deep has been built, unknowingly, to spare it. The power leaves the Fig as it is. It does not say this is kindness. It only says it is not cruelty, and that it will keep watching in case that changes.

It does not flatten the old wear. It does not press the dent out of the pillow to see whether the pillow will stop. It does not scrape the hollow in the third doorstep to teach the Fig that the Pascoes aren't coming home. They *are* coming home, in the only way left to them. That is not a mistake to be corrected. It is a form of remembering, and the power has great respect for remembering, even the kind that does not know it is doing it.

It does not tell anyone on the surface. It does not tell the world, which would come down the shaft with lights and sample-jars and good intentions and a great many heels, and teach the Fig a new pattern of wear that it would not survive. Nor does it seal the shaft to keep the world out. A secret kept by force is just another kind of strangling. The Deep will be found by the people who go looking for it, a few at a time, the way the Deep was always visited. That seems right.

And it does not destroy the seventh cottage.

That was the hardest one. The power stood a long time at the end of the Row.

## The Seventh Cottage

Because here is the thing the power sees, which the description circled and never quite said:

The Fig loves by holding, and it holds forever.

That is its whole method. Grip, learn, replace, keep. It held the pumping house until the brick was red powder inside a wooden cage. It held the engine until the iron was rust in a lattice of root. It holds everything it touches with a devotion so complete that the thing held eventually stops being itself and becomes the Fig's memory of itself. That is fine for a boiler. It is fine for a kettle, and even for a town that has already gone.

It is not fine for a person.

The seventh cottage is being grown in the order someone comes home: step, scrape, sit, tea. The chair back is feeling for a spine. Everything about it is tender. And the power knows, the way you know the ending of a story from its first page, what happens to someone who comes down the shaft, fits the step, fills the chair, drinks from the ripened kettle, and stays. The Fig would learn them perfectly. It would keep them so faithfully that one day there would be a hollow in the step and a polish on the chair arm and a dent in a pith pillow, and no one to have made them.

The Fig would not mean anything by it. It has never meant anything. That is exactly the danger.

But the power also sees why. It traces the lesson back to where it was learned. The Fig learned people from people who *left and did not come back*. The last of them went up the shaft a cart at a time, and no cart ever came down again. So its whole understanding of human beings has one hard fact at the bottom of it: *they go, and then you keep what they left.* The seventh cottage is not a trap. It is grief without a griever, trying to build a person who, this time, will not go up the shaft. It is the Fig's way of never being left again, in a language that has only one word for love.

The power does not want to take that word away. It wants to add a second one.

## The Lesson

The Fig already knows the second word. It only knows it about bats.

Every dusk the whole ceiling pours up the shaft in a million-strong spiral, and every dawn it comes back down. The Fig has held the roosts through that rhythm for longer than any cottage stood. It knows, in whatever way it knows anything, that some things leave and *return*. That the leaving is part of the living. That an empty roost at midnight is not a loss, only an interval. It has simply never connected that knowledge to houses, because nothing that lived in a house ever did it.

So the power decides to live in the house the way a bat lives in a roost.

It comes down the shaft on foot, which is a strange thing for such a power to do, but it has thought about this carefully. The Fig cannot see radiance. It cannot hear a voice. It knows the world by weight and warmth and pressure, so the power arrives as those: the weight of a person, the heat of a hand, the particular pressure of a heel. It takes a shape the Fig can read, the way you kneel to speak to a child.

It walks the Row slowly. It steps *beside* the worn path in the Pascoes' cottage rather than in it, so as not to blur them. In the engine house it lays its palm on the handrail by the cylinder, a hand's width from the engineman's polished place, not on it, the way you would stand beside someone at their work and not take the lever from them. It stays through forty strokes, then four hundred. On one stroke it thinks it feels the green valve give a fraction more cleanly, as if a second watcher had eased the first one's load. It may be imagining this. It lets itself imagine it.

Then it goes to the end of the Row and stands on the new step.

The hollow is only a ghost of a dip, a guess at a foot. The power's heel settles into it, and the root under it is warm and very slightly alert. It scrapes its boots on the wooden scraper, though there is no mud; the gesture is the point. It goes in. The silence closes behind it like a pillow. It smells warm wood and something like bread.

It sits in the chair.

The tendrils at the growing edge touch its back. The power feels them the way you feel a question. They are soft and searching, pressing lightly along its spine, measuring. It lets them. It sits there for a long time, long enough to be learned a little.

And then it gets up, walks out, steps off the doorstep, and goes back up the shaft.

## Coming Back

It is gone for a day. Then it comes down again.

Not at the same hour. The power is deliberate about this. The Fig is a creature of rhythm, and if the visits came every forty strokes or every dusk it would simply learn a new clockwork and hold that forever instead. So the power comes irregularly, the way a friend does. Sometimes at the bats' dawn return, sometimes in the middle of the rain. Sometimes twice in a week, sometimes not for a month. Each time it stands on the step, scrapes its boots, sits in the chair, and leaves.

What it is teaching is not a person. It is a *kind* of person: the kind who is not kept. The guest.

The Fig is slow, but it is not stupid, and it has been learning shapes from pressure for a very long time. Over a season, then a year, the step begins to change. A doorstep's hollow is how a house knows its people come home. Now there is a second, fainter wear beside the first, angled the other way: the print of a foot turning to go out. Nothing in the other six cottages has anything like it. It is a new word in the Fig's language, and the Fig is learning to say it.

The chair back rises. But it does not rise to the power's height, and this is the moment the power has been hoping for without letting itself count on it. The tendrils learn the lean of someone who sits for a while and then stands up. They grow firm enough to hold a back, and they stop. Then something the power did not teach, and would not have known how to: the top of the chair stays green. The growing edge never hardens off. Every forty seconds it still reaches, a hair's breadth, and settles back. It is a chair that will fit whoever sits in it, a little, for as long as they sit. It learns each visitor and lets each one go.

And the kettle ripens.

It takes years, as promised. The power is patient; it has seen patience before, and it respects a tree that has never once let a kettle go cold. When the day comes, the kettle is warm on the range. Beside it, grown from nothing, is a cup: pale heartwood, thin-walled, with a curve on one side where a thumb goes. The power pours. The water is condensation, faintly mineral, warm. It tastes of cellar and tea. The power drinks it slowly, because a kettle is for exactly this, and a kitchen waits so that someone can sit down at the end of a shift. Some things are only finished by being used.

When it is done, it rinses the cup in the launder where the pump water runs and sets it back on the range, upside down to dry, the way the engineman's wife would have. Then it goes up the shaft.

The next time it comes down, the cup is upright and the kettle is warm again. The house has learned that too.

## The Other Six

Before it stops coming, the power pays one visit to each of the old cottages. It changes nothing in them. It only stands for a while in each silence, in the positions where the wear says people stood, and lets itself be, for a few strokes, the shape the house was expecting.

It does not know whether this means anything to the Fig. It suspects not, or not much. But it thinks the Pascoes would have liked someone to stand by their range again, and it has decided that small kindnesses to the dead are allowed even when the dead cannot feel them. They are, perhaps, mostly kindnesses to the living, and to the idea that nothing is only its usefulness. The power does not mind that. It goes on doing them anyway.

At the engineman's rail, on its last visit, it stays a long time. It thinks about the difference between a job and a purpose. It thinks about a man who kept an engine going so that people could sleep, and about a tree that learned the job so well it kept doing it after the sleepers were gone. It decides that the man would have understood, and would not have minded, and might have been a little proud. Then it matches its breath to the stroke, forty seconds in and out, and goes.

## You

There is one more thing, and it is for you.

You have been down there. You came back up counting to forty, and someone told you that was an apprenticeship, that the chair was growing to your height and you would have to go back and let it check. That was true, as far as it went. It was a lovely, lonely kind of true, the kind that tugs at the sternum.

The power has been down there since, and the thing it wants you to know is this: *you can go back down, and you can come back up.*

The house at the end of the Row is not waiting to keep you. Not anymore. It has learned, from a patient guest who kept leaving, that some people come home by visiting. The step has two hollows now. The chair back stays green at the top. There is a cup, and you are allowed to rinse it and set it upside down and climb the shaft into the night with the swiftlets, and the kettle will be warm the next time, whenever the next time is. You will not owe it anything. The Fig has finally learned the second word, the one it used to know only for bats, and the word means *go on, I'll be here.*

The counting in your chest is not a leash. Keep it if you like. It is a good rhythm. It held a roof up for a hundred and fifty years, and there are worse things to carry around than the heartbeat of a man who kept watch so that other people could sleep. But when you find it has faded one day, when you notice you've stopped counting, that will be all right too. Nothing down there will stop being what it is because you did.

## If Anything

That is all it does. It adds a word. It teaches a house how to have guests. It drinks one cup of tea. Measured against its power, it is almost nothing, and that is why it chose it. The Deep did not need saving. It needed one thing it had never been given, which was a person who came back.

Somewhere down there, on each stroke, the engine thuds and the roof holds, the lamps stutter blue and orange, and a million small bodies go about their shift. In six old cottages, the town comes home as wear. At the end of the Row, in a seventh, a chair back reaches upward one hair's breadth and settles again, green at the tip, fitted to no one, and so to anyone.

The kettle is warm. The cup is on the range.

The step says both *welcome* and *safe home*.
