# What Came Down the East Rim

*...perfectly preserved, and near enough to their arms to hear them.*

They are right about that, as it happens. This is one of the first things the power learned, and it learned it before it had decided to come.

---

## Noticing

A power like this does not notice things the way a person does, by glancing over. It notices more the way the moss takes a footprint: slowly, completely, and with the whole of itself. It had been aware of the basin for a long time, the way you are aware of a clock in another room. There was a faint scattered ticking at the edge of the world, rising at dusk and falling toward midnight, year after year, never quite in rhythm.

What drew its full attention was a knot.

Somewhere in the rope walk building, on the last few arm-lengths of a cord tied by a tender three hundred years dead, there is a cluster of knots that looks like weather, or like speech. Nobody living has tried to read it. But the power could read it. It found that the knots were not addressed to anyone in particular. They were written the way you speak aloud in an empty house, not expecting an answer but not quite sure there won't be one. And the power, which is benevolent and was not in a hurry, thought: *someone should have answered this.*

So it came.

## How It Came

It came on foot, because that is the courteous way to arrive anywhere, and because it understood already that nothing here is done quickly.

It came down the east rim, the steep one where the moss hides old iron, and it felt the air change on the way down: cooler, heavier, cut stems and wet bark. It let itself feel this properly. A power could have known the air of Ashcarr without walking into it, the same way it could have known the knots without touching them. But it had the sense that knowing without walking would be a kind of rudeness here, like reading a letter over someone's shoulder.

A tender was sitting at the foot of Second Crook in the quiet hour, and the power's eye passed over her twice. That pleased it very much. It is not often fooled.

She lifted one hand a little from her knee.

When it reached her, she asked whether it had come down the north rim or the east. It said the east. She asked whether it had felt the air change, and it said yes. It told her it had noticed the smell of cut stems before it noticed it was going downhill. She seemed pleased the way you are pleased when a guest notices something about your house you had forgotten was there. Then she looked past it at the grey gathering in the lowest part of the basin, and the conversation was over.

The power did not mind. It had somewhere to be as well, which was *here*, and it intended to stay a while.

## What It Could Have Done

It should be said plainly that the power could have done almost anything. It stood on the moss that first evening, while the fog filled the bowl to the height of a man's knees and the lamps began to glow orange in the crooks of the trees, and it considered.

**It could have made the fog come every night.** The dry weeks of late summer would end. The lamps would never stay dark, and the town would never sit blind. The tenders would stop lying awake with sore joints. But it thought about what a dry night is for. The dry night is how Ashcarr knows the wet ones. The ache in the old knuckles is the same ache that tells them, at dusk, that the fog is coming. Take away the failures and you take away the reading of them. The tenders would stop looking at the sky, and a tender who does not look at the sky is only a person standing near a tree.

**It could have made the lamp glass unbreakable**, the one thing Ashcarr cannot make and must carry down the rim in terror of a stumble. That seemed kinder. But the glass is also the one thread tying Ashcarr to the country beyond, to the flat stone and the carters and the oats and the cheese. Without the need for glass, the need for the rim would thin, and the power did not know what else would thin with it. It left the glass breakable.

**It could have stopped the wood from growing.** The arms would stay true and the pins would stay aligned. There would be no spring re-pinning, no knot-cords, no narrowing doors. Ashcarr could never lever itself off its root plate. It would be finished, in both senses.

This was the one it thought about longest, because it was the one that looked most like mercy. And it was the one it was most certain it must not do. The re-pinning is the reason the town cannot be abandoned. Take away the reason and you have not freed the tenders. You have made them unnecessary. They would still be there, murmuring, walking the sunken paths, but their hands would have nothing to hold. The cords in the old buildings would stop at one year and go on hanging, black and stiff, a curtain of records that ended. The whole life of the place is a machine that is always slightly out of true and always being coaxed back. The coaxing is not a flaw in the life. The coaxing is the life.

**It could have opened the closed rooms.**

**It could have healed the old ones' hands.**

**It could have made the strip of names in the paper mill important.**

It set all of these down, one by one, the way a tender sets down a pruned limb. None of them was wrong, exactly. They were only the kind of help that arrives from outside and does not know where anything goes.

The power decided it did not yet know where anything went. So it did what a person does in Ashcarr when they do not know where anything goes.

It asked for a pin.

## The Pin

The tenders did not know what it was. They knew it was not a carter and not an orphan and not quite a man who had come down the rim with nothing. But they had taken in strangers before, and there was a way to do it, and the way did not depend on knowing who someone was.

An old tender, Ninefold's, went into the paper mill and came back with a single iron pin, dark and pitted, and set it in the power's palm without a word.

The power could have known at once where the pin belonged. It did not let itself.

It walked the basin. It looked at every arm, every rod, every ratchet. It learned the rust and the wear and the diameter. It learned that Old Pull's housing runs warm, that the Widow's second pawl sticks in the thaw, that the Heron creaks a half-tone lower than any other arm and has done so for sixty years. It learned it the slow way, with its hands, and when children followed it across the lawns it did not hurry them. It took most of a year. It was supposed to.

When it finally found the hole, a gap in the old housing under Sallow, the pin slid in with a small sound that no one heard except the power and one brown beetle, which paused, considered, and went back to the moss in the gear teeth.

The power sat back on its heels on the path, which is not on the moss, and was quite happy.

## Listening

Then someone put its palm flat against a wall at dusk.

It was Heron's, a young man with a voice that could not be raised, and he said nothing at all. The fog came in. The arm began to curl. Deep in the wood, too low for the ear, came the slow straining groan of the whole building taking the weight of its own arm.

The power did not snatch its hand away. But it understood, standing there, why children do. There is something in that sound that goes past hearing. It is the sound of a thing bearing weight it did not choose, patiently, every evening, forever. The power had heard many things in its long attention to the world, and not many of them were like that.

It learned to tell, through the wall, whether an arm was pulling clean or grinding. It found, to its own surprise, that it was not better at this than the tenders. It could know the arm completely, every fiber and every stress. But knowing and listening are different, and the tenders had spent their lives on the second. In that one thing they were its teachers. The power found this a great relief. It had not been anyone's student in a very long time.

## The Walker

It spent a week in the rope walk.

It moved its pallet a few feet a day beside the Walker's. It sat with its hand resting lightly on the forming cord, feeling the twist, and it listened to the faint whirr, and it did not speak unless spoken to.

On the first day the Walker said, "You're not from over the rim."

On the second day she said, "—or anywhere, I'd think."

On the third day she said nothing. On the fourth day she told it which fiber came from which press, and which press had been set by which tender, and how many times this same stretch of floor had had a rope spun over it. She had the count exactly right. The power checked.

On the sixth day she said, "You could make it go faster."

The power said that it could.

On the seventh day, near the end of the tunnel, she finished the sentence: "—but then you'd have to walk faster, and where would you be."

The power said it would be at the end of the tunnel, alone, with a rope that no one had watched being made.

The Walker nodded, very slowly, the way the head moves. The power thought she was perhaps the wisest being it had met in a century, and that she would have found the idea embarrassing, so it did not say so.

It did see the rope move. The children were right about that too.

## The Closed Rooms

On a foggy night late in its year, the power stood close against one of the sealed buildings, among the black cords hung on the outer pegs. It put its hand to the poured-wax bark and listened past the yard of living wood to the ratchet ticking inside.

A power can know what is behind a wall. So it knew.

It will not be written here what the engine drives, because the power did not tell anyone, and it would be a poor thing to tell it now. It is enough to say that the power stood there a long time. When it finally took its hand away it was not troubled, and it was not sad. It looked the way a tender looks when they tip beetle-earth into a new housing and say the word nobody will tell you.

The next morning one of the children asked it straight out, the way children do, whether anything lived in the closed rooms.

The power had decided early on that it would not lie to anyone in Ashcarr, and it had decided almost as early that it would not open what the tenders had let close. So it knelt on the path, which is not on the moss, and said:

"You've always been right."

The child took this away and kept it, the way things are kept here. It went into no record. The adults, who had long ago stopped saying *nothing lives there*, did not start saying it again.

## The Names

In the paper mill there is a strip of paper, not considered important, that holds the names of every tender who has kept every arm.

The power read it. It read all of it, from the oldest end, which is brown and soft and nearly gone, to the newest, where Heron's name was written in a careful young hand. Then it did the one thing in its whole year at Ashcarr that no tender could have done. It remembered every name as a person, and not as the arm's.

It did not tell them it was doing this. It did not change what anyone was called. *Ask Heron's* is still a full sentence, and it is a good one. There is a kind of humility in being named for what you keep that the power had no wish to take from them.

But it thought that the strip could go on being unimportant *because* someone, somewhere, was holding it as important. Now someone was. That seemed to it the right way round. The tenders keep the arms. The arms outlive the tenders. And something, now, would outlive the arms and keep the tenders.

Then it went out onto the lawns, walking carefully on the paths, and it listened down through the moss and the peat. It found them all where they lay, each near the root of their arm, each with a last knot-cord folded in their hands, perfectly preserved and near enough to hear the ticking. It did not wake them, or raise them, or do anything that would have surprised them. It only let each of them know, in whatever way the dead in peat can know things, that they had been read.

## The Cord

In spring the power measured an arm.

It was not given an arm. It had asked, gently, and Ninefold's had said no, just as gently. The power agreed at once and was glad of it. An arm needs a tender who will grow old and sit at its root with curling hands, and whose name will be written on the strip, and who will one day be laid on the moss. A keeper who could not die would be too strong for it, like an overgrown arm that does not break its iron but slowly levers the whole building off its root plate. The power understood that it was, in Ashcarr, a thing that had to be pruned. It took this well.

But Long Grey's tender was ill that spring, and the power went up with the knot-cord in his place and measured under his instruction. It tied overhand for length, figure-eight for girth, the double loop for the dawn height. It pinned the arm at a new point, a hand's breadth further out than the year before. It did not correct the old man when his memory of the seventh station was slightly off. It measured it as he said, then went back alone that night and measured it true, and tied the true number in a knot so like the other that no one would ever tell.

Then, at the end of the cord, after the last measurement, it tied a few more knots.

They are clustered and spaced. They look like weather, or like speech. They are the answer to the cord in the rope walk that first drew its attention, tied by someone three hundred years dead. They are addressed to that tender, and also to anyone else who has ever tied knots in the dark near the end of a life, not expecting an answer.

The cord hangs in Long Grey's building now, one below the last. The tenders who come after have not tried to decipher it. They hung it in its place with the rest.

## The Dry Night

Late in the summer the fog failed for four nights running.

The engines barely moved. The lamps stayed dark, and the town sat blind. The tenders lay awake in the green dark listening to nothing, and in the morning they were short with one another, and the old ones' joints hurt.

The power did not bring the fog.

It sat up with them. On the third night, when the silence was worst, it went from building to building and sat quietly in the doorways of those who could not sleep. It did not talk. Talking would have been the wrong size for the silence. It sat at the level of the place, which is very low, and it was awake in the dark with them.

On the fifth evening the fog came in, thin at first, in the lowest places, then filling the bowl. The arms began to curl. The ratchets picked up, one and then another, until the whole basin was ticking like a room full of slow clocks. The lamps swelled orange, the moths came, and the tenders slept hardest in the hour near midnight when everything stopped.

The power had done nothing to cause it. But it had been there, and it found that this was not nothing.

## Leaving

It stayed through the winter, when the rime locked the arms and nothing turned. It helped with the heavy work of pruning and grafting, and it was careful not to be too strong. It stayed through the thaw. And when the first wren sang after the winter, the song going through the fog-heavy basin like a needle through cloth, the power stopped whatever it was doing and listened until the wren had finished, the same as everyone else.

Then it went.

It went up the north rim this time, the gentle one. Halfway up, it looked back. A tender was sitting at the foot of the Widow in the quiet hour, so still and so fitted to the root that the power's eye passed over her twice.

She lifted one hand a little from her knee. *I see you. I'm not coming over. It's fine.*

The power lifted one hand back, and it meant the same thing.

## What It Kept

This is the only promise it made, and it made it to no one in particular.

Someday the tenders may leave, or dwindle, or simply stop. The power knows the world and it knows this is possible. If it happens, the engines will run a year or two on the fog. Then the arms will outgrow their pins, and the rods will bend and snap. The walls will close their doors and the moss will fill the gears, and Ashcarr will become a wood, a strange square-trunked wood in a low basin with iron inside its trees. Deer will come down from the rim at last, and they will eat whatever they like, and nobody will notice.

And for a long time afterward, on foggy nights, there will be a faint scattered ticking from deep in the bark, where some buried ratchet is still counting the weather.

The power will be listening to it.

That is all it did, in the end. It changed no weather and opened no doors. It healed no hands, though it wished to, and it made no one's work easier than the work was meant to be. It learned where one pin went. It learned to listen through a wall. It walked a hundred feet in a week, finished a sentence the next day, answered one old knot, and sat awake through a dry night. And it agreed, with the whole of itself, to be the thing that goes on counting after the counting stops.

The tenders would not call this help. They would not call it anything. If you asked one of them who it was that came down the east rim that year, they would think about it for a while, looking past you at the sky and the grey gathering in the low places. Then they would tell you, and believe they had answered the question:

*It kept us.*
