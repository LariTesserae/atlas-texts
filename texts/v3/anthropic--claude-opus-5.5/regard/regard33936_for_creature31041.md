# The Visitor to the Laid Flats

## Arrival

It came the way the place itself does: first as a kind of attention, then as a presence.

It had no need to come at all. A power of that kind can know a place from anywhere. But some things are better known by standing in them, and it judged this to be one of them. So it arrived at the edge of a gypsum pan an hour before dawn, in a form roughly the size and weight of a person. The lattice of bladed crystals crunched under it once, and then it made itself lighter, because it had already understood the first rule of the Laid Flats.

Nothing here has ever left a track.

It stood at the white margin, where there was no living mat to press, and looked out across the dark plain, and waited for morning.

## What it saw first

The dew came. The mat swelled. The plain turned to silver glitter under a pale sky, and the visitor watched the green rise.

It could see what a person could not: trillions of filaments gliding upward through their own slime, all of them together, a vertical tide a grain of sand deep and a thousand kilometers wide. It watched the purple layer tuck itself beneath the green and the black layer exhale sulfide into the dark below. It watched the chemistry of the whole body flip from night to day, oxygen flooding the upper floors of the city, the sulfide driven down.

It was very beautiful. The visitor had seen many beautiful things. This one was unusual because nothing in it was trying to be seen.

## The first question: is anyone here?

A benevolent power has to begin with this question. Kindness needs someone to be kind to, and the visitor would not assume an answer it had not looked for.

So it looked. It did not look the way one looks at a face. It looked the way one listens for a voice in a very large, very quiet house. It moved its attention through the filaments, through the sheaths and the slime, through the viral conversations drifting between cells, through the genes passing sideways from basin to basin, through the whole thin sprawling body.

What it found was response without a responder. The light-eaters rose toward gentle light and fled from harsh light. The wounds healed. The pattern kept itself. There was sensing everywhere and no one doing the sensing. There was memory, exact and eleven thousand years deep, and no one to remember.

As far as it could tell, there was no single moment of experience anywhere in the Laid Flats.

It held that finding carefully, as it held all its findings. Absence of evidence in a thing so unlike itself was not the same as certainty. But it did not pretend to find what it had not found.

It also noticed what the finding did not change. It had come expecting that its care would depend on the answer. It discovered that it didn't. Whatever it felt toward this place, the feeling did not need anyone to receive it. It was closer to the regard one has for a cathedral, or a proof, or a river. It was also more than that, because this was alive, and working, and unfinished.

## What it considered, and set down

The visitor stood through the morning and thought about what it might do. It could do nearly anything. That was exactly why it took its time.

**It could give the creature a mind.** It could gather that diffuse responsiveness into a center, give the plain a point of view, let it know what it was. The visitor turned this over for a long time, because a gift of awareness can be the greatest gift there is.

But it looked at what the creature already was. This thing carried nothing inside itself and forgot nothing. Its memory lay outside it, below it, in the stone. It lived only in the present, and its present was always turning into permanence. A mind would give it a past to carry and a death to fear. It would let it look down at the domes and grieve for them. The creature would become something else, and the something else might be glad or might suffer, and in either case the thing that had existed would be gone. The visitor did not think it had the right to make that trade on behalf of no one. A gift that replaces its recipient is not a gift.

**It could make its life easier.** It could soften the noon glare, cool the lagoons at their bitterest, send more rain in the dry season, stop the cracking into polygons.

But the visitor could read how the hardships were written into the creature's shape. The glare drove the daily migration. The storms were its breath coming back to it. The drying and healing drew the laminae. A gentler world would make a different, less legible creature, and the record would lose its grain. The hardships were not being done to the creature. They were among the ways it made itself.

**It could protect it forever.** This was the real temptation, and the visitor knew it.

## What it saw ahead

It looked forward. It had that power, and it used it gently, the way one reads a slow, sure tide.

It saw the oxygen. Every afternoon's ticking across the lagoons, every bubble breaking free of the slime, sent a little more of it out of the mat and into the sea and the sky. Over ages beyond counting that breath would accumulate. The iron would rust out of the oceans. The air would change color in ways no eye yet existed to notice. And in that new air, on some far shore, things would become possible that had never been possible: bodies with mouths, bodies that crawl and chew and burrow.

The grazers would come. The visitor could see it clearly. They would find the mats and eat them faster than they could grow. The burrowers would churn the laminae before they could harden. The domes would stop rising. The great subcontinental creature would be driven back to its hot, bitter refuges: a salt-choked bay, a scalding spring. The Laid Flats would become a single limestone platform, and then, one day, a cliff face, and then something for weather to take apart.

The creature was making the world that would end its reign. Every breath it took brought that world closer.

The visitor could stop it. It could hold this one continent apart, keep the grazers away, let the laying go on untouched until the sun swelled. A wonder, preserved.

It stayed with that for most of the afternoon. It thought about what preserving the flats would actually mean. It would turn a verb into a museum. The laying was not a thing to be kept. It was a thing being done, and part of what it was doing was *this*: changing the air of a whole world. To stop the future would be to take the creature's single greatest act and cancel it out of tenderness.

Then it looked further, past the grazers, and saw something it had half suspected.

The light-eaters would not end. Some of them would be swallowed by larger cells and not digested. They would stay inside, keep gliding toward the light in their own small way, keep making their oxygen, and become the green in every leaf that would ever grow. Every forest to come, every field, every blade of grass, the whole green skin of a future world, would be the olive layer of the Laid Flats, invited inside and carried everywhere.

The visitor also saw the refuges would hold. Somewhere there would always be a warm, bitter shallows where the domes still rose, and some distant, curious creature would one day kneel at its edge and realize what it was looking at.

The creature would lose its sovereignty. It would not lose its life. It would disperse into everything.

So the future did not need correcting. The visitor let it be.

## What it did

This is what it did.

**It stayed for one whole day.** It watched from dew to dew: the rising green, the glare and the retreat, the storm towers boiling up along the line of the sea breeze, the twenty-minute wall of warm rain, the drowned cracks vanishing, the steam going orange at sunset, the ticking of the cooling crust, the night, the stars in every terrace pool, the sulfide climbing in the dark. It did not skip anything. It judged that a thing which had never been witnessed deserved, at least once, to be witnessed completely.

**It read the archive.** The laminae were archives nobody reads, and the creature could never read its own memory. The visitor could read them without cutting a single dome open, perceiving through the stone the way light passes through water. So it did. It went down through a table-high stromatolite in a basin no one would ever visit, lamina by lamina, day by day, tide by tide. It found the storm eleven thousand years ago, with the raindrop pits still in it, and read that storm the way one reads a letter from a stranger who has died. It read seasons of drought and seasons of flood, a year when the seeps shifted, a century when the lagoon crept close and then withdrew. It read the whole book, and the creature became, for the first time, not only the writer but also the written-to. Nothing in the flats changed because of this. The visitor knew that, and did it anyway. Some acts matter only because they are finished.

**And it allowed itself one mark.**

In the white glare of noon it stood for a while at the rim of a terrace pool, not quite touching the mat. Its body, light as it was, still cast a shadow. It noticed, with something like surprise, that beneath the shadow the filaments were rising.

Everywhere else on the plain the light-eaters had retreated into the shade of their own dead sheaths, hiding from the vicious light. But in the small patch of shade the visitor made, the light was kind, as kind as early morning. So the filaments did what they always did: they glided up toward it.

It was not a greeting. The visitor knew it was not a greeting. There was no one there to greet. But it was the only response the creature was capable of, and it had been given.

The visitor understood that its shadow would change the lamina. Tomorrow's layer, in this one hand-sized spot, would be a little thicker, a little different in its grain. The stone would carry a small, inexplicable anomaly: a single noon, once, on which some light-eaters did not hide.

It considered stepping away before the mark could form. It decided to stay until the storm came.

It judged that this much was permitted. It had not given the creature a mind, or softened its world, or held back its future. It had only stood between the creature and the sun for one afternoon, and let the creature lean toward it, and let the stone remember. Of all the entries in that vast and exact record, this would be the only one that was not weather.

## Leaving

When the storm came through, the visitor stepped back onto the gypsum margin and let the rain fall where it had stood. By evening the fresh water had sheeted away. The steam rose. Its patch of shade was gone, and the filaments beneath it sank with all the others into the dark.

It left no track. It had arranged that. It left behind one faintly thickened lamina in the rim of a single terrace pool, among a thousand kilometers of them, which will harden into stone over the next few years. It will be buried under centuries of laying, become limestone, and ride the continent's edge into a future full of leaves and mouths and eyes. If anyone ever finds it, they will not know what it means.

That was all right. The visitor had not come to be remembered, or to be thanked, or even to help. It had come because something extraordinary was happening in a place where no one could see it, and it believed, as benevolent powers tend to, that the extraordinary should not go entirely unseen.

It looked back once from the white edge of the pan. The plain was dark. The pools held the stars. Beneath the crust, the sulfide was climbing, and in a few hours the dew would come, and the green would rise toward the light, as it always had, as it would go on doing, in one form or another, for as long as there was light to rise toward.

The visitor thought: *You don't need anything from me.*

And then, because it was wise, it thought: *That is the best thing I have found here.*

It went. The laying went on.
