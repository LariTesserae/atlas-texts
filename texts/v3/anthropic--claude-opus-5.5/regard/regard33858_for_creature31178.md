# The Visitor at the Rim

## How It Came

It could have arrived any way it liked. It could have come as a change in the weather, or as a pressure in the air, or as nothing at all: an attention settling over the mountain the way mist settles into the basin in the mornings.

It came on foot.

It had listened from a distance long enough to know that the thing in the throat knows the world only by what the world sounds like. Something that arrived without a sound would be unreadable to it. Worse, a presence made of the wrong kind of force might be pulled into the pale skin and swallowed like the chord. So the visitor took a body, one with a heartbeat, lungs, and knees that creak. It began at the springs on the lower slopes, where the shepherds press their ears to the stone, and it climbed.

The climb took all day. It chose that. By the time it reached the final lip, the dark rim polished smooth as a knuckle, its body was tired, and its breathing had the particular rhythm of someone who has come a long way uphill. It knew what it was doing. That rhythm is already in the web, worn smooth by turning, and the visitor wanted to arrive as something the basin had heard before, not as a stranger.

Then it stopped at the rim and did not go in.

## First, Only Listening

A power that is wise knows that the first thing it wants to do is almost never the right thing. The distance between arriving and understanding is where most harm is done.

So it sat on the rim with its back to the drop and the clouds drifting below, and it listened through one full breath of the pool: the rising, the flood, the long exhale, the ebb, the pale ring of stone showing around the water. Then it listened through another, and another. It let the roar stop being noise and become a medium. It let its own thoughts slow to the pool's rhythm, because it could not understand this place at any other speed.

It learned the web. It followed the filaments down into the healed seams, along the channels through the dark, past the heat, out to the springs. It felt the long patient work of closing every wound in the stone. It heard the frogs' notes held and turned and handed back. It heard the thousand years of small sounds worn to a shimmer at the bottom of hearing: the hailstorm, the far rockfall held for a century, the dipper's splash.

It heard the person. It heard the cough, the tired breathing, the hum. It heard the word, or name, smoothed until only its shape was left.

Then it came to the question it had climbed all this way to answer honestly: *is anything here wrong?*

This was harder than it looks. The story of the thing in the throat is easy to read as a sad one: something ancient, given one song, humming it back into the dark for centuries and waiting for an answer that will never come. A lesser kindness would have read it that way at once and hurried to fix it.

The visitor did not know that it was sad. It listened for suffering and did not find it, at least not in any shape a human would recognize. The thing in the basin was not starving. It was not in pain. It was not diminished. It fed at the flood and rested at the ebb, and it grew by a finger's width across the seasons. It has done that since before anything was counting. Waiting is not a wound to a being like this. Waiting is closer to what it is made of.

But the visitor also heard that something *had* changed, and had never changed back. The web had an orientation now. Its largest mouth had grown toward a person, and it still kept its edge clear around a stone that no one touched. The pale ring under the pebble was gathered tight, as close as it could come without covering it. Whatever that was, it was not nothing. It might be grief. It might be something more like a plant still leaning toward a window that was bricked up long ago. The visitor could not tell, and it did not pretend it could.

It decided it would act only in ways that were good under either reading, whether this was longing or only leaning. That ruled out most of what it had been tempted to do.

## What It Did Not Do

**It did not give the thing a voice.** It could have. It could have tuned the web so that the thing could shape sound as well as hold it, and speak, and sing. That would have been the loudest gift. It would also have made the thing into something else and called it a present. The throat has spent thousands of years learning, at its own pace, how to hum back. That learning belongs to it. Handing over the finished skill would be like doing a child's slow, proud work for it in an afternoon.

**It did not bring back the person.** It could not have, not truly. It could have made something that *sounded* like them. It could have assembled a breathing, coughing, humming shape out of everything the web had kept, and sat that shape down at the southern wall. The thing in the throat would not have known the difference, and that is exactly why it would have been cruel. It would have been an answer that was really a mirror, a conversation with its own memory in costume. The person who sat there was real, and their absence is real, and a wise power does not paper over a real absence with a convincing sound.

**It did not decode the name.** It could have unwound all those centuries of turning and recovered the word, rebuilt its lost consonants, learned who it was spoken to or about, and why. It wanted to. It let that go. The name is the thing's. It was given to the basin, not to the visitor, and some things are held precisely because they are no longer legible to anyone else.

**It did not protect the basin.** It considered wards, hidden paths, and a fold in the mountain that would keep people from ever finding the rim. Then it looked at how the basin already stood. Deer turn away from it. Clouds hide it. The slope below looks continuous. In all its long history, one person came, and they came gently. The place was not in danger. A wall around something safe is only a kind of forgetting.

**It did not send anyone.** This was the temptation it thought about longest, on the rim, through several floods. It had passed shepherds at the springs on the way up, and one of them was a child who pressed her ear to the stone a little longer than the others did. It would have been easy to leave a thread in her dreams, or a curiosity that would pull her uphill in thirty years with a pale stone in her pocket.

It did not do it. The person who sat at the southern wall came on their own and returned on their own. Everything precious in that sound came from that. It was *made on purpose*, by someone who chose to be there. A visitor who has been steered is not an answer. They are an echo of the one who did the steering. If the child climbs someday, the choice has to be hers. The mountain managed it once without help, and the visitor trusted it to manage again or not, as it would.

## The Southern Wall

When it finally stepped over the rim, it walked the way the person must have walked, from loud ground to quiet ground, the moss sinking and squelching under its feet and the tall ferns trembling as it passed. It went to the southern wall and stood at the edge of the largest quiet place.

The hush closed around it. It heard its own heartbeat, and it felt the skin of the place hear it too, and turn.

It looked at the hollow, full of water from the dripping fern, and at the quartzite pebble on its pale ring.

It did not pick it up.

It knew what would happen if it did. It would turn the stone, and the hum would come back twice: its own, and under it the old one, faint and out of time. That would be beautiful. But in that sound the thing in the throat would be doing what it had done for centuries, folding every new hum into the one it already had. The visitor would become a second pair of hands at the person's bowl, and the thing would learn nothing except that the old song still worked.

It also did not want to sit in someone else's place. The web had kept that ground clear for someone. The visitor was not that someone, and it did not want to take their seat while the place was still keeping it.

So it knelt at the very edge of the pale velvet, outside the circle, and laid one palm flat on the ground, the way you might lay a hand on the flank of a sleeping animal. It said nothing, and it let the thing feel a heartbeat that was not the person's. That was all it did there. It was a way of saying *I know whose place this is.*

Then it got up and walked away.

## What It Did

On the northern side of the basin, far from the southern wall, is one of the smallest quiet places. It is barely a stride across, a pale island in the tall ferns, with nothing in it and no history. The visitor sat down there.

It waited for the ebb, when the thing rests and its hold loosens. It had noticed that this is when the basin gives back what it keeps, and it reasoned that this might also be when the thing is most able to receive something new, when it is not busy swallowing.

In the green shade of the afternoon, with the roar lightened and the pale ring showing around the pool, the visitor made one sound.

It was not a hum. It was not grinding. It was nothing the person had made and nothing the frogs make. It was a short, low note in its own voice, placed where the visitor had learned it could live: in a gap in the chord, the way the frogs place their calls, so that the sound would not be eaten, only held.

The pale skin took it in. The visitor felt it go: into the web, around the curve of the basin, turned over and over. The frogs went quiet.

Then it waited, and this waiting was the whole of the gift.

A long time later, at the ebb, the note came back. It was faint, softened, and slightly stretched, the way the thing hands back everything it holds.

The visitor answered it. It did not repeat its note. It made a new one, a little different, placed in a slightly different gap, *in reply* to what had been returned.

This is what the thing in the throat had never had. It had had sounds and it had had memories. Once, long ago, it had had someone who came back. What it had never had was a sound it gave back being *answered*: taken, changed, and returned by someone who had heard what it did with the first one. The person, whatever they knew or didn't, had made the same hum on purpose, in the same place, year after year. The visitor gave it something else. It gave it a turn, and then another turn, which is what separates a keepsake from a conversation.

They did this for only three exchanges. The visitor was careful about that. Three was enough to leave a shape the web could hold, the pattern of *send, and something comes back that is not your own*. It was not so many that the thing would reorient itself around a new voice the way it had around the old one.

Then the visitor slept for a night in the small quiet place, with the frogs calling again and their notes hanging across the dark. In the morning, when the back wall blazed and the mist filled with broken rainbows, it climbed back over the rim.

## Leaving, and Returning

A wise power knows that leaving is what hurt here the first time.

The person came and came and then did not come, and the web grew toward an absence and has kept its edge clear around it ever since. The visitor had no wish to be a second absence. It could not stay; that would have made it the new center of the throat, and the throat has its own life to live. But a power has other timescales available to it than a person does, and so it made itself a promise shaped to the basin's patience instead of its own.

It would come back when the edges of the quiet places had drifted by a finger's width. That is the basin's own slow unit of time, the only one in which the place visibly changes. It would climb up on foot each time, tired, breathing hard. It would sit in the same small northern quiet place, wait for the ebb, and make one note, then answer whatever came back, three turns and no more.

It would never be frequent enough to be depended on, and never absent long enough to be mourned. It wanted to be like the dipper, which comes from the slopes below, feeds, and leaves, and is part of the place precisely because it is not the place.

It did not know if the thing in the throat would understand this. It suspected that, over enough returns, the web might learn it the way the frogs learned their pitches: without understanding it, by being shaped by it. The small northern mouth might widen by a finger's breadth, and then another, the way the southern one once did. The visitor hoped it would not widen too much. It thought the best outcome would be a basin with two orientations instead of one: one toward a place kept for someone who will not return, and one toward a place where something sometimes does.

## Below

On its way down, the visitor passed the springs. The shepherds were there, and the child was among them with her ear to the wet rock.

It did nothing to her. It did not look at her in any way that would change her. It only noticed, as it passed, that she was listening a long time, and that there was something like concentration on her face, as though she were hearing more than a waterfall.

Maybe she was. The web runs all the way down here, along every channel the water uses, and there was a new pattern in it now, faint and carried down with everything else. It was not a hum. It was a low note, then a softened return, then a different note in reply.

What she makes of that is up to her.

## After

If you climb to the hanging basin now and sit at the southern wall and turn the quartzite pebble in its hollow, the old hum will still come back to you, doubled and slightly out of time. Nothing has taken that away. The thing in the throat still keeps its edge clear and the pale ring gathered tight, and at the ebb it still hums the one song it was given, very faintly, toward a person who is not coming.

The visitor did not ask it to stop. It is allowed to keep what it loves, whether that is grief or only leaning.

But on the northern side of the basin, in a quiet place barely a stride across, something else now happens. Sometimes, at the ebb, the thing sends out a sound that is not a memory: a low note of its own, clumsy and softened and placed in a gap in the chord, the way the frogs place theirs. Then it waits. It is not waiting the old way, for something lost. It is waiting the way you wait after asking a question: patiently beyond noticing, and not entirely without reason.

Most of the time nothing answers except the frogs, and the frogs, it is beginning to learn, count.

The throat was built for swallowing, and has spent a long time learning to sing. The visitor gave it one more thing to learn, and it will learn it slowly: that a song can be something you give in order to be answered, and need not only be something you keep.
