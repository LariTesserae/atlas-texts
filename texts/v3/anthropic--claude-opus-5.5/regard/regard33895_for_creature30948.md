# What the Power Did

## Noticing

It was not the heat that drew its attention.

A benevolent and wise power notices many hot places. The world is full of cracks with something warm at the bottom, and most of them want nothing from anyone. It was not the Engine either, though the Engine is remarkable, or the pipe-hound, or the rust-red film at the bottom of the pool.

It was the chalk.

Every morning, after the Breath and the hour of dripping, someone went along the terraces with a stub of chalk and rewrote names for people who could no longer keep up with their own. Nobody asked them to. Nobody watched. The power, which had seen a great many things done for a great many reasons, had learned that this kind of act was the truest sign of a place. Wealth, beauty, suffering and strangeness all told it less than the small, unwitnessed courtesy repeated every day.

So it turned toward the crack. It did so slowly, because it could already tell that this was a place where turning quickly would be a kind of shout.

## Arriving

It did not come down through the rock.

It could have. Rock is not much of an obstacle to a power of that sort. It could have arrived all at once at the bottom of the seam, beside the thing that was breathing, and made itself known. It considered this for about as long as it takes to consider putting your hand over a sleeping child's face, and then it went around to the street.

It came to the iron rail, the one bolted in a hurry, and it looked over. It felt the heat on its face like the breath of someone standing too close. Then it came down the stairs like anyone else.

This mattered. The Kettle has one entrance, and everyone who has ever lived there came in by it. A power that uses a different door is announcing that the rules of the house are not its rules. This one wanted to be a guest, so it used the guest's door.

On the way down it made itself smaller, and then it made itself slower. That was harder. Powers are fast, and being fast is most of what makes them powerful. But the Engine's stroke is forty seconds, and the power understood within the first flight of stairs that it would see nothing true here at its own speed. It would see a dozen people and a lot of shut doors. It would take the man at the stove for a coat on a hook.

So it let the rhythm into itself. It paused on the dim. It spoke only on the bright, and at first it did not speak at all. Tobin gave it a bean from his pocket, still warm. It ate the bean, and it thanked him, and it asked what the valve was for. Tobin told it about the ferns on the fourth terrace.

It was a week before it could see the man at the kitchen stove. It was a season before it could see the sleepers under the mushrooms. It did not hurry this. Hurrying would have defeated the purpose, the way running into a library defeats the purpose of a library.

## What It Could Have Done

It had time, going down, to think about everything it could do. That is a power's particular temptation. So it went through the list honestly, the way you check a room before you leave a sleeping infant in it.

**It could fix the cracked pipe.** It could seal the pool on the lowest level and make everything tidy. But the crack in that pipe is the only place where the thing below touches the town, the only patch of skin anyone can see. The ones who are *in* spend a season walking there so that they can sit beside it. Sealing it would close the only window of the nursery. The pipe stays cracked.

**It could cool the Kettle.** It could make the air thinner, the brick drier, the bottom bearable. But the heat is body heat. You do not make a newborn colder to make the room more comfortable for the visitors.

**It could bring light.** It could send the daylight past the third of the way it reaches, or make the lamps hold steady instead of pulsing. But the plants face the pipes, and the pipe-hound has no eyes. The pulse is the only clock the slow ones can still feel. A steady light would be a kindness to the fast and a theft from everyone else.

**It could wake the sleepers.** It asked itself whether they needed rescuing, and it did not assume the answer. It went and looked, carefully. It knelt a long time beside a woman in the angle of a wall, her hand's breadth long since closed, moss fine as breath on her cheek. It watched the pencil-mushroom above her mouth grow by a single exhalation. It listened in the way powers can listen, to what was underneath.

She was not trapped. She was not dreaming a nightmare she could not wake from. She was keeping company, which was what she had come to do. She was content in a way that has no fast word, the contentment of a person sitting up with someone through a long night, who would not trade that night for sleep. The power found the same in every sleeper it knelt beside. It checked them all, because a wise power does not check three and generalize.

**It could hurry the birth.** This was the largest temptation, and it looked at it squarely. It could reach down past the seam, into the throat, and help. It could finish the breath. It could spare everyone the waiting.

But the power had attended many births, of stars and of mice and of things with no names, and it knew that a birth is not a problem being solved. It is something happening. A breath that takes as long as a town takes to build is exactly as long as it needs to be. The waiting is not an obstacle to the thing that matters. The waiting is part of it, the way the vigil is part of the love. To hurry it would be to tell the newborn that its own pace was wrong, on the first day of its life.

So it did none of these things. Having crossed them off, it was left with a smaller and harder question.

## The Question

It went to the Engine and asked.

It did not ask what it should do, because that would put the Engine in the position of a servant. It asked whether there was anything the Engine wanted.

Then it waited, because the Engine's answer would come at the Engine's pace. The power had slowed a great deal, but the Engine was still the eldest. It had gone further than anyone and then chosen to stop just fast enough to be useful.

The beam rose with its long hiss and paused at the top as if considering. The pause was longer than usual. For the first time in the Kettle's short memory, the lamps held at their brightest for one full breath longer than they should have. On the terraces the quick ones looked up. A woman with a cup stopped pouring. The boy on the gantry, halfway across, did not run.

Then the beam came down. The power understood the answer, which was not in words.

The Engine was tired, but not in a way that wanted fixing. It was the tiredness of someone who has stayed awake so that others could sleep, and who will stay awake a long while yet. What it wanted, as far as a thing like that can want, was not to be relieved. It wanted to know that when it finally let itself go down the slope, someone would take its place at the top of the stroke and keep listening.

The power said it would. Or rather, it offered: *When you want to go down, I will hold the beam.*

It did not say it would hold the beam now. The Engine had not asked for that, and to take the work from someone who has not set it down is not a gift. It is a demotion.

The next stroke was forty seconds exactly. The lamps pulsed as they always had.

## What It Did

Here is what the power did, in the end. It is not very much, because the Kettle did not need very much. The Kettle was already doing the most important thing well. The power's work was to protect that, to fill in the few places the Kettle could not reach, and otherwise to keep its hands to itself, the way people here do with the rock.

**It took the chalk.** One morning, after the Breath and the dripping, the power went along the terraces with a stub of chalk and renewed the names. It did every door, in careful handwriting that was not the owners' own. It did it the next day as well, and then it gave the chalk back to whichever quick one had been doing it before and said thank you. It did not want to take over the job. It wanted to have done it, so that it would understand from the inside what the job was. You cannot protect a courtesy you have never performed.

It did not chalk its own name anywhere.

**It tightened one bolt.** Climbing back up through someone's kitchen, past the warm loaf (the man at the stove was still setting it down, and this time the power saw him and nodded, and he felt a draught and wondered about it), it reached the rail at the top. One of the long bolts that held the rail to the basalt had worked a little loose with all the ticking. The rock is still settling. So the power gave it a quarter-turn, on the bright. It paused through the dim, then gave it one more.

That was the entire scale of its intervention with its hands. It thought the scale was right.

**It stayed at the top.** A crack in a street floor, sixty deep and breathing heat, will not stay unnoticed forever by the world above. Someday someone will come with a clipboard and the word *hazard*. Someone else will come with the word *resource*. They will look at the heat and see power, or look at the gap and see something to fill with concrete. The residents cannot stop this. The quick ones are too few and too gentle, and the rest cannot move fast enough even to be seen.

The power could stop it. But a benevolent power does not reach into people's minds and turn them, any more than it reaches into the rock. So it did the slow thing. It became a presence on the street above. There is a bench there now, near the rail, and sometimes someone sits on it: an unhurried person, courteous, doing something slowly. When passers-by stop and look over the rail and feel the heat on their faces and ask what is down there, the person on the bench answers the one question that matters. The power had learned in the Kettle that there is always one.

*Someone's being born,* the person on the bench says. *It's going to take a long time. Speak softly near it.*

Most people, told this plainly by someone who clearly means it, do speak softly. Most people are better than their clipboards. The power had always believed this about the world above. It was willing to sit on a bench for a very long time to prove it. When the clipboards come, as they will, there will be someone there who has already made friends with the street. The street will have gotten used to the rail, and to the bench, and to the idea. A town above that knows what it is standing on is a far better shield than any wall a power could raise.

**It sent a part of itself down.** This is the thing only a power could do, and it is the one that mattered most.

Powers can be in more than one tempo at once. The residents cannot. Every one of them, however far down the slope they go, is still finite. The quick ones will slow and go *in*. The ones who are *in* will become sleepers. The sleepers will go further, into whatever lies past sleeping. The Engine will someday let go of the beam. The breath has already lasted as long as the town. It might last longer than every resident now alive, at every depth.

When the exhale finally comes and the creeper flowers, the newborn will open whatever it has instead of eyes. There may be no one left who was there for the beginning. No one will remember the night the ground groaned, or the first iron bolts, or the baskets of brick. No one will be able to tell it how the town was built inside its first breath by people who came down the stairs so that it would not be alone.

Every child deserves to be told the story of the day it was born by someone who was there.

So the power let one strand of itself go down the slope. It did not go all at once. It went the way the residents go, stage by stage, a little warmer and a little closer, hearing the thing a little better each time. It spent its quick years among the quick ones and was *in* for a while. Now it is sleeping in the moss on the lowest level, near the pool, in the angle of a wall. The rock has leaned in, and it does not mind. There is a pencil-mushroom above it, and the pipe-hound comes to lie along its side.

It is not there to intervene. It is there to remember. It is keeping the whole breath, every stroke of it, to tell afterward.

**It looked once.** It would be dishonest to say the power did not wonder what the exhale would be. It did wonder. It could have looked ahead and seen.

It looked once, briefly, the way a parent might glance at the last page of a book to make sure the ending is not cruel before handing it to a child. It did not read the ending. It only checked that it could be survived.

What it saw, it has not said. But afterward it went up to the street and sat on the bench a little more often. It began, very quietly, to plant things along the edge of the street above: hardy things, patient things, things that would not mind the smell of cloves.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not flower the creeper.

It did not name the red film in the pool.

It did not tell the newborn it was coming. The newborn could not have heard it, and the ones who could already knew.

It did not tell the quick ones what it was. When Tobin asked, it said it was a visitor. That was true enough.

It did not touch the visitor's name on the third terrace door, the one with the beans trained up the railing. That name was not its to write and not its to wipe away. It was a question the Kettle had asked someone, and the answer belonged to them. The power's only concern was that the stairs should keep going up as well as down, so that the question stayed a question. That is why it checked the bolt at the top, and why it sits on the bench. The way out has to stay open, or the staying means nothing.

## What It Understood

It understood, somewhere around the second week, that the question of *which being* lived in the Kettle had no single answer. It could be the newborn in the throat, or the Engine, or the sleepers, or the pipe-hound, or the woman with the cup. It could be the town itself, which leans house on house like a drawer of spoons. The question did not need an answer. The Kettle is one act of company, spread out across many speeds. Every creature in it is somewhere on the same slope, facing the same way, as the ferns face the pipes.

It understood that the residents had already done the wisest thing there was to do. Faced with something enormous, new, slow and alone, they did not try to make it smaller or faster or less alone by force. They went to it. They became slow enough to be with it. They made their bodies into a vigil and their town into a lullaby that goes *pause on the dim, speak on the bright.*

A benevolent power could not improve on that. It could only join it, guard its edges, and remember it. It could make sure the world above was gentle when the time came, and that someone kept the whole story for the child.

So that is what it did.

---

If you go now and stand at the rail, you may find the bench occupied. You may be offered a bean. If you ask what is down there, you will be told plainly.

Look down. Amber and black-green, pulsing every forty seconds.

On the bright, the lamps now hold one breath longer than they used to, just now and then. It lasts about as long as it takes to be thanked.
