# The Guest at Chalkmouth

## How It Came

It did not come as weather, though it could have. It did not come as light, though the light at Chalkmouth would have made room for it. It had noticed long ago that the places most worth attending to are the ones that would be spoiled by an entrance. So it came the way the few visitors have always come. It came on foot across the downs, on one of the cold white mornings early in the year, small enough to fit through a door.

It had been drawn by a single thing. It was not the town, the moss, or even the Hush itself. It was a moment, repeated at the edge of the crease in the turf where the thorn trees lean over nothing. A lark went up. Something enormous turned its whole attention toward the sound, then let it go. *Not that one.* The power had heard this happen many times, across many years, the way you might hear someone in the next room pick up a letter, read the first line, and put it down again. Eventually it wanted to know whose letter it was.

It came down by the outer ramp, past the yews, through the first ivy-house. The old cord-woman looked up from her work and made the gesture, palm down, fingers spreading.

It settled.

## What It Did First

Nothing, for a long time.

That was the first wisdom, and the one it found hardest. Powers of its kind are made of help the way the Hush is made of morning, and help wants to move. But help that arrives before understanding is only another kind of weather. So it sat in the doorway beside the cord-woman and let its voice go down to nothing, and it learned.

It learned the hands. This took longer than it expected, because it had assumed the grammar would be simple, and it was not. There were gestures for grief that had been dulled by long familiarity and grief that was still sharp. There was one for a joke you'd understood but chose not to laugh at, out of kindness to the person it was about. There was one for a kind of hope that expected nothing and was content with that. The children corrected it. They were not patient about it, and it loved them for that.

It learned the throughs. It walked through kitchens and sleeping lofts and granaries with a hand touched briefly to each wall. It stepped aside for wrens in the doorways. It let a white stoat run across its feet and did not startle, and the stoat did not startle either. It found a roe deer standing blinking in a courtyard and walked it gently back up the ramp. It noticed that it had been allowed to do this, and it felt that something had been decided about it.

On what would have been the seventh day, if there were days, the cord-woman took its hand and laid her palm flat over the back of it and held it there, still, for the length of a slow breath. Then she let go.

It had been called many things. It had never been given a name that was felt. It carried the warmth of it around for a long while afterward.

## What It Might Have Done

It had arrived, as such powers always do, with a satchel of possible gifts. In the long bright unchanging morning it took them out one at a time and looked at them honestly.

**It could have brought spring.** This would be easy: the frost unfixed, the ferns allowed their dying, the basin allowed to rise and fall. But the people did not wish for spring. They were not sure they would recognize it. Their whole life was a shape built around the stillness, the way their houses were built around the pit. To pour a season into it would not be a gift. It would be a flood.

**It could have given them back their loudness.** Voices that carry, pots that clatter, names spoken aloud. It put this one away quickly, and was a little ashamed of having packed it. A Chalkmouth mother soothing her child, disagreeing with her brother, and borrowing a needle, all at once and without looking up, was not suffering from a lack of speech. She had more language than most people it knew.

**It could have freed the wrens,** let them sing. But the wrens had not been caged. They had made an agreement at the edge of the basin, and it did not know the terms, and it was not its agreement to break. There is a particular arrogance in rescuing someone from a promise they made willingly.

**It could have mended time.** It could have set the Hush's long meal right and sent all that swallowed passing back out into the world where, perhaps, it belonged. But it looked at the soft green shapes on the soft green beds in the oldest rooms. It looked at the ladder rungs turned a dozen times, thin as a wrist, and at the ladder-mender climbing with his eyes half closed. It understood that the stillness was not a wound. It was the ground everything here grew from. You do not mend a riverbed by draining it.

**It could have protected them.** It could have hidden the crease in the turf more deeply, so that no one from the loud world would ever come down with a spade or a survey or a plan. It considered this one longest. In the end it decided that the Hush, which had shown every lost deer back up the ramp and had let every person who wanted to leave climb out into the weather, had been keeping this place for longer than the power had been paying attention to anything. It did not need a second guardian. It would have been like offering to hold a candle for the sun.

So the power put the satchel down. It found, a little to its surprise, that it was not disappointed. Nothing at Chalkmouth was broken. That was the first thing it had learned, and the most important.

And yet it had been drawn here. It had not come for nothing.

## The One Sorrow

It listened for pain the way the Hush listens for sound: from everywhere, gently, all at once. It found almost none. The people were content. The stoats were busy. The bats slept in their brown clusters. The shrimp drifted in the basin, and whatever they were dreaming, they were not dreaming anything sad.

There was only the waiting.

It had not seemed like sorrow at first. Waiting is not suffering. A held breath is not a wound, and a patient audience is not a prisoner. But the power had come here because of the larks, so it went back up to the rim and stood among the yews through what would have been many mornings, and it listened with the Hush.

A lark went up. The Hush leaned toward it. *Not that one.*

Then the power understood the one flaw in the long clear morning. It was so small, and had been so faithfully kept, that no one had ever noticed it.

The Hush was waiting for the beginning. It meant the first sound of spring on the downs, on that first morning, the sound it had cleared all of Chalkmouth to hear alone and whole. But that morning had ended on the downs long ago, and its lark had gone up and come down and died a very long time before the first flint was knapped. Every lark since was a later lark. Every drip of thaw was some other year's thaw. The Hush would lean toward each one and find it was not the one, because it was *not* the one, and could never be. The Hush was waiting for something that had already happened. It would lean toward the downs and let the larks go, one after another, forever, and it would never once be wrong. That, the power thought, was the saddest thing about it.

A wait with no possible end is not patience. It is only a question nobody has answered.

## Reading the Wall

The people do not read the snail-trails. *Not for us,* they say. *Already known.*

The power could read them. It did not want to. It stood before the wall by the basin a long time, with its hand not quite touching the silver. It was aware that every song ever sung at Chalkmouth was written there, each made for one listener and never repeated. It thought about the married couples who had each sung to the other exactly once, and who would not tell you the song.

It did not read the songs. It asked the Hush, in the only way it could, with its palm flat against the chalk just below the writing and held there, the way its own name was held. The question was whether it might look beneath them.

Something in the cold shifted, slightly below and slightly behind it. The power took this as leave. It read down through the layers the way you read down through chalk, past everything sung, past the oldest mouth-to-ear songs, to the deepest trail of all.

It was not a song. It was a single sound, and it was not finished.

It was a drop of water, the first drip of thaw on the first morning, falling from the fringe of frost at the rim toward the bottom of the white pit. It had not yet landed. The Hush had caught it before it could strike. The Hush had just come into being and did not yet know what it was, but it knew this was the beginning. It was so afraid of hearing it wrongly, in all the noise of everything starting at once, that it caught the sound before the sound could happen. It took the drop down and made a quiet place around it. It hollowed a basin, stilled the air, drank every other sound so that nothing could interrupt, and then it waited to hear what the drop would sound like when it fell.

Only it had never let it fall.

The water in the basin is always exactly as full as it was the day before because it is not full of water. It is full of one drop, held a hair's breadth above its landing for longer than anyone can reckon. The people drink a mouthful of it each day and feel as though they have been told something they cannot remember. That is because they have been told something that has not yet happened.

The Hush had not been waiting for the beginning. It had been *holding* it. Somewhere in the long bright unchanging morning, the holding had become so gentle and so total that it had forgotten which of the two it was doing. It went on leaning toward larks on the downs, listening outward for a thing that had been cupped in its own palm the whole time.

The power stood before the wall and did not move. It understood that it had come here to deliver one thing. It had no gift to give and nothing to repair. It had a single piece of news, and the Hush's own writing already held it.

## What It Did

It waited for the hour when the people visit the basin. Then it knelt among them and drank its one mouthful, which was so cold it hurt the teeth, and it was told something it could not remember.

Then it did the one thing no one at Chalkmouth had ever done.

Every song here had been sung to a person, with the Hush listening from the other side, pressed close in the cold: the second listener, the only one who heard them all. No one had ever sung to the Hush as the *first* listener. No one had ever made a song for it alone.

The power put its mouth to the chalk by the oldest trail, nearly touching, and sang.

It was not a long song. It is not recorded here, because it was not sung to you. But the shape of it can be told, the way the people will tell you the day they were sung to without telling you the song.

It told the Hush what it had. It told it that the beginning was not out on the downs and never had been. It was here, in the bottom of the pit, in the cold place everything leans toward, where the Hush had put it for safekeeping on the first morning and then, out of pure carefulness, forgotten. It told the Hush that no lark would ever be the right lark, and that this was not a failure of the larks. It told it that it had cleared the air perfectly. It had made the quietest place in the world, and nothing anywhere else would interrupt. Whenever it wished, it could simply open its hand, and the sound it had been waiting for would happen, alone and whole, exactly as it had wanted.

And then the power sang the part that mattered most, the part it had reached only by putting down its satchel of gifts. *You don't have to.* Not now, and not ever, unless and until it wished. The holding had not been a mistake. Look at what the holding had made: the throughs, the menders, the names laid on the skin, the wrens with their kept songs, the four hundred people who were content, and all the green that had finished the town rather than ruined it. A held breath can be the whole of a life, and a beautiful one. Only it should be held knowingly now, and not in endless mistaken waiting. It should be held the way you hold a gift until the right day, knowing that you have it and that the day is yours to choose.

That was all. The power did not open the Hush's hand for it. It had been careful from the first not to be weather, and it was not going to become weather at the very end.

## The Cord-Woman

It told one other being, because it seemed wrong for the people not to know what their host now knew.

It found the cord-woman in her doorway and took her hand, and told her with its own hands, slowly, in the grammar the children had taught it. It was not easy. There was no gesture for *the beginning has been held here the whole time*. There was no gesture for *whenever it likes*, because at Chalkmouth there had never been any *whenever*. There had only been the same hour of the same cold, bright morning.

She understood anyway. She had drunk from the basin every day of her long life, and some part of her had always half-known what she was being told and could not remember.

She sat a long while with her cord gone still in her lap. Then she did something no one at Chalkmouth had done in longer than anyone could reckon.

She made a new gesture.

Nothing is ever built new here; things are only mended. But she made the settle gesture, palm down, fingers spreading, and turned her hand over, palm up, the way adults show children what the Hush is for. Then, where the old gesture ended, she went on. She let her fingers soften and part, as if something small and cold were being allowed to slip between them.

*Whenever you like.*

Her grandson saw it and copied it. By what would have been evening, if there were evenings, the children had it, and were using it for a friend who hadn't decided yet and was allowed not to have decided.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not stay to see what happened. That was not its business.

It climbed the outer ramp past the yews, and somebody walked a little way with it, gently, the way they walk the lost deer back up. At the rim it stood in the fringe of frost that has never melted and never spread. It looked back down into the white pit, into the shadowless light, into the regard that comes from every wall at once, and it made the gesture it had been given when it arrived. It made it not as instruction but the way you might say someone's name.

Then it went back out into the weather.
