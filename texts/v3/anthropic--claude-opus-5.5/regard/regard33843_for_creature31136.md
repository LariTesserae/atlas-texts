# What Came to Low Tallow

## Attention

It did not come because anything was wrong. Nothing at Low Tallow was wrong. That was what caught its attention first, the way a perfectly level surface catches the eye in a landscape of hills.

It looked for some time before it did anything. That is most of what wisdom is.

It saw the loop and found it sound. It traced the swifts out over the marsh and back, the insects onto the canal, the chitin to the mill, the meal to the rooks, the dust to the moths, the coal to the fire, the steam to the cistern, the yeast to the swifts. It checked the figures on the wall and they were right. It listened to the chord and heard no flaw in it. Whoever had built this, or whatever had become it, had made something that could feed every mouth in it forever without waste. There are not many works in any world of which that can be said.

It weighed doing nothing. It weighed it seriously, because a thing that works deserves that much. The rooks were not starving. The rats were not beaten. Nobody at Low Tallow was crying out.

Then, under the chord, it heard the step.

## What It Would Not Do

It would not break the works.

It could have. A breath would have cracked the chimney, and a thought would have let the marsh in over the dyke. It could have undone the whole thumbprint of clay in the time it takes a beam to fall once. But every creature in the basin was the works, and the works were them. Smashing a loop that has held for centuries is not freeing anyone. It is only a second kind of rendering, faster than the first.

It would not fix the works either. There was nothing to fix. Improvement was the last thing Low Tallow needed. Low Tallow had been improved until nothing was left over.

It would not count anything. That was the most important refusal, and the hardest, because it could see the tenth column and it knew the figure that belonged there.

It would not decide for the one on the dyke.

That left very little, and the little it left was exactly what had never happened. Nobody had come to say that the shift was over.

## The Crown

It came up out of the marsh on the loose side, where the reeds were moving. It took a shape that would be understood: a person of ordinary height in an ordinary coat, bareheaded, with nothing in its hands. It could have come as light. It had learned long ago that light, arriving somewhere, is mostly a way of making things squint.

It climbed onto the embankment and stepped into the wear-track on the inner edge of the crown. It did not wait for the walker to come round. It went the other way to meet it, because going the other way on a round is how you let someone know you are not part of the round.

It heard the step before it saw anything. Early, then late. A pause. Two steps close together.

When they met, it did not stop the walker. It turned and fell in beside it, on the marsh side, so the walker kept its shoulder to the works. It matched the steps. This took some care, because the steps kept no time, and walking in step with something that keeps no time means paying attention to it one footfall at a time. That was the point. Nothing at Low Tallow had been walked beside before. Everything there had been walked past, or counted.

They went round once together. The walker was tall, as tall as had been guessed, and heavy in a way that had nothing to do with size. Its coat glittered wet. Its hands were the colour of candles. Its face was turned down toward the nine structures, occupied, wearing the look of someone who has almost reached the bottom of a column of figures.

The power did not speak on the first round. It looked where the walker looked. It counted the nine in its head, only so it would know what the walker saw, and then it let the numbers go.

On the second round the walker spoke. Its voice was low and it had more than one voice in it, the way a crowd has one sound.

"You're early."

"No," said the power. "It's gone nine. It's been gone nine a long while."

The walker walked on. Pause, step, step.

"The clock says twenty to."

"The clock stopped when you got here. You were early, weren't you? You came before the others, to be sure of the gate."

A long stretch of the curve went by. The beam fell four times.

"The others," the walker said.

## Nine O'Clock

This was where the power had to choose between kindness and truth. It had come a long way to know that they are not really two things.

"They aren't coming," it said. "I think you know where they are."

The walker stopped. It had never stopped on the crown before, not for longer than it takes to look at something and catch up. Below them nothing changed. The chord held. But up on the dyke there was, for the first time, a silence that belonged to someone.

The walker looked at its hands. It turned them over. Eight households' worth of hands, boiled down to two.

"Who'll mind it," it said, "if I don't?"

"Nobody needs to. It's finished."

"Finished isn't the same as over."

"No," said the power. "That's why I came."

Then it said the other thing, the thing it had decided on while it was still out on the marsh, looking. It said it plainly, because the walker had worked since before it could remember and would know when it was being flattered.

"It was good work. All of it. The engine, the stones, the fires kept in. Nobody ever went short here. Not one mouth in all that time. You held it. You can put it down now."

The walker said nothing for eleven seconds. Then eleven more. Then it said, in a voice that for a moment was only one voice, a woman's, hoarse, "Nobody said."

"I know. I'm saying."

The power made clear, in whatever way such things are made clear, that it would ask nothing further. If the walker said no, the power would walk beside it for as many rounds as it took, and that could be a very long time. It had time. It would not go down into the basin and end things itself. The shift was the walker's to end.

The walker stood on the crown a while longer. Then it turned, for the first time, away from the round. It went down the inner face of the embankment into the yard.

## The Tenth Column

It crossed the bare clay slowly. As it came down, the looking came down with it. The yards had always been where its gaze landed, and now its gaze was walking on them, and a gaze that is walking is only a person.

It went to the counting wall. The power went with it, a step behind.

The walker stood before the blank strip at the right-hand end, the one surface at Low Tallow that had never been worn to fit anything. It lifted a candle-coloured hand, and then did not know what to do with it.

"I can't," it said. "You can't count yourself."

"You don't have to be counted," said the power. "That column was never empty because you were forgotten. It was empty because you were never staff."

It took a stub of white lead from its coat. There had not been one there before, and now there was. It held the stub out.

"But you could sign out. That's what the column's for, at the end of a shift."

The walker understood that. It had understood the procedures of the works all its life. It did not take the lead. It said, "I don't have my letters."

"Then tell me, and I'll write, and you make your mark."

So the walker spoke the names. It did it slowly, with pauses and doublings-back, the way you remember a street you lived on as a child: the Ashcrofts, the Pryces, the two Hallorans who were cousins and never spoke, the Beddoes girl who had come to the stones at nine years old and been given a stool to stand on. Eight households. More names than there had been cottages, because houses fill. The power wrote each one in a clean hand on the clean brick, from the top of the column down.

When it was done, the walker took the lead. Beside each household it made a mark, a cross, the way men and women who worked from childhood signed for their wages. It made eight marks, each at its own pace and none in time.

At the foot of the column the power wrote one more line, in the words a timekeeper uses. *Signed off. Gone home.*

It did not write a number anywhere.

## The Weighhouse

Across the yard, the needle moved.

It did not drop at once. It went down the way a sack goes down when the one carrying it kneels, carefully, until it touched nought and rested there.

The mice came out of the weighhouse with nothing in their mouths.

They stopped on the iron plate and did not know what to do. The stream behind them banked up and slowed. The power went over and sat down on the clay beside the scale, which nobody had ever done, and waited with them. After a while one mouse stepped off the plate onto the bare ground and sat up. Then another did. They were the first creatures in the history of Low Tallow to be looked at without being counted, and they did not seem to mind it.

The weighhouse door was never opened by the power. The walker opened it, later, because it was its works. Inside there was a clerk's high desk and a ledger with nothing written in it. On a shelf, laid in a row and neatly labelled, were a pocketknife, a glove, eleven buttons, a wedding ring, a child's tin whistle, and a great many coins. Everything anyone had ever dropped into the lanes was there. None of it had been used up. It had only been set aside, which is not the same as being lost.

## Running Down

The power did not stop the engine. It would not have done that to anything that had worked so long.
