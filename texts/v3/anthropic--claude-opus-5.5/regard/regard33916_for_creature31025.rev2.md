# What Was Done in the Seam

## Arrival

It came the way everything comes to the Seam: on the wind.

That isn't quite right. It had no body to carry, so the wind carried nothing. But attention has to come from somewhere, and this attention came from the southwest, over a thousand kilometers of oak and farmland, riding the same air as the brown autumn moths with the eyespots. It crossed the peaks in the hundred-and-fifty-kilometer gale and felt the gale slow and curl downward into the gap. Like everything else the wind brings, it dropped out.

Nothing in the town noticed. A few thousand jumping spiders on the western wall turned their great amber eyes a few degrees toward nothing in particular, and then turned back to the moths.

It stayed a long time before it did anything. That was its first decision, and the one all the others came from. A place that had taken eleven years to become itself deserved at least one year of being looked at before being touched.

## What it saw

It had come expecting a town, and there was a town: four thousand people in hanging steel boxes, drills hissing on compressed air, ore climbing the eastern face in switchbacks. It could have stopped there. Most attention does. It could have counted the split lips and failing lungs and the locked cistern with a guard at the door, and called the sum a problem to be solved.

It looked longer, the way the band-readers look at a span, feeling for a hum underneath.

It saw the fallout drizzling onto the ledges and the springtails rising off the drifts like smoke. It saw spiders crowning helmets and choughs waiting at the mess hall doors to the minute. It saw children on opposite walls talking in thumb-flicks of light, and choughs lifting on the far side in answer. It saw a woman with a red band tattooed on her wrist standing on a bridge in the dark, not looking down, listening with her feet.

Then it went down, past the last ledge and the lowest platform, past the point where the walls come within an arm's span of each other, into the place no lamp has reached.

The children were right. That is the first thing it would want known, if it wanted anything known. The lamp-man was not sweating. Down where the walls close, the rising rock is warm. The warmth and the old stone make water, and the water stands in a pool, black and very still, in a chamber that was sealed for longer than there have been people. Pale things live around it. Some crawl, and some carry long feelers they use to taste the air. The bats roost in the ceiling cracks and drink there.

Around the pool, the losses of the town have landed. They include eleven years of fallout sifted all the way to the bottom, a torn-loose house wedged upright between the walls with its window long dark, carts, a crushed tin of moth wings, and the dead, wrapped in felt and ore-sacking with some small thing from their lives tucked inside. All of it is going slowly soft in the wet warmth. Something is happening down there that has not happened anywhere else in the Seam: the beginning of soil.

Then it understood what it had been drawn to. It was not the town, or the pool, or any one of the layers. It was the whole arrangement, sky feeding the walls and the walls feeding the dark, one creature built entirely out of letting go. It was a being whose single habit, shared across every part, was to fall silent together and listen.

It is hard to help something like that. Most help is a kind of holding on.

## What it did not do

It considered everything. Here are the things it put down.

**It did not close the crack or stop the mountain.** The mountain was not the enemy. The same rising that would one day close the Seam had opened it. The ore, the town, the livelihoods, the choughs' nesting ledges, and the bats' first sky all came from the mountain moving. Stilling the mountain would have been like stilling a heart to spare it the effort of beating.

**It did not fill the cistern.** It thought about this one the longest, because the thirst was real and the cracked knuckles were real. But it watched the three brothers at the cistern door and saw what water had made in the Seam. It had made a scarcity, and it had also made an entire moral economy: confession, trust, resentment, a town that can hear a lie in the way a crate is set down on stone. Endless water from nowhere would not dissolve the company's power. The company would simply stop sending crates and keep the difference, and the day the gift failed, the town would have forgotten how to be careful. A gift that makes you dependent on the giver is only a longer cable.

**It did not overthrow the company.** Benevolence is not approval, and it did not approve. It had read what the company does not name in public, and knew what the ore was for. But it also saw that the town was already learning to read the company the way it reads the bands. Contract workers left, the ones who stayed became something else, and a generation was growing up that speaks a language the company cannot follow. Freedom lowered down from above would have been one more thing delivered by bucket. It trusted the people to do this themselves, slowly, the way they do everything, one re-pinned span at a time.

**It did not speak.** This was not coyness. The town lives at the volume of kneaded dough. Children here have never heard a door slam properly. Anything it said would have been the loudest sound anyone in the Seam had ever heard, and the children, who listen to the rock, would have started listening for it instead. You do not walk into a room where everyone is holding their breath to hear something and start talking.

**It did not save the seeds.** The children grow a seed against their skin, breathe on it, give it a drop from their ration, and watch it put out a white thread and die. They say you haven't been born in the Seam until you've done this. It could have made one seed live. It could have made a tree grow from felt and breath and a single drop of water. It saw that this would take the one rite the children had made for themselves and turn it into a lottery. It left the rite alone.

## What it did

It did a few things. They were all small, and none of them could be seen.

**It kept the secret of the bottom.** The company had noticed the lamp-man's story, as the company notices most things while saying nothing. In the ninth month, a survey crew began drilling a long exploratory core angled down through the western wall toward the closed end of the crack. If they found the pool, the pool would be pumped, and the water would go up to the cistern and be sold back by the cup. The old blind community that had lived for ages in the dark would be drained along with it, for one season of profit.

So the drill bit found a hard lens of feldspar, deflected a finger's width, and missed. The core came up dry. The surveyors wrote *no significant void* and moved on. It moved nothing more than it had to. It had thought about whether this was a lie and decided it was not. It was simply a door held quietly shut on behalf of the oldest resident of the house.

**It held the cable one night.** In the depth of that winter a single strand of the fourteen-kilometer span, the town's only link to the world, parted in the gale at three in the morning, well out over the valley. The strand would have unraveled by dawn, and the span with it. There were two buckets on the line, and one of them carried three people coming up from medical leave, among them a nine-year-old girl who had just seen her first puddle and had not stopped talking about it in hand-sign.

It held the strand until the morning inspection crew found the break, swore, and clamped it. They argued all day about how it had lasted. Grandmother Red, who had not been on the cable, listened to them argue, touched her wrist, and said nothing.

**It gave one lamp more time.** In the spring a house tore loose from the eastern wall. It was empty, thank the mountain, because the family was at the noon light. It fell and wedged somewhere far down, and its window kept glowing. For a few nights people turned off every lamp and stood on the lowest platform to watch the small orange spark at the bottom of the world.

One boy watched every night. The house had been his, and in it, in a felt pouch under his bunk, was the seed that had sprouted and died a month before, the one that had made him briefly famous. He had meant to give it down properly and never had. Now it had gone down on its own.

The battery should have lasted four nights. It lasted eleven, and it went out on the one night the boy was too tired to come, so that he never had to watch it end. He decided later that it had waited until he was ready. In a sense it had.

(The seed, for what it's worth, landed in the dark near the pool, in the soft beginnings of soil, and in the warmth and wet it split again. It sent a pale thread toward the dying orange window, which was the only light it would ever know. When the window went out, a long-feelered thing that had never seen the sun found the sprout and ate it. This, too, it did not prevent. Everything that falls into the Seam feeds what lives below.)

**It asked the mountain to take its time.** This was the only large thing it did, and it did it gently.

It had looked forward, the way it could, and seen the day the mountain breathes out. The children are right about that too. The benchmark climbs more than it should because something deep in the range is loading, and one night the load will release, and the Seam will close. In the version of that night it saw first, the release came all at once. It came in a single night, the same way the crack had opened, and four thousand people in their hanging boxes woke to the walls coming together.

It did not stop the out-breath. It spread it out. Where the mountain would have exhaled in a night, it exhaled over a season.

So when that day comes, and it has not come yet, the Seam will close the way a fist closes when the hand is very old. The bands will turn yellow on every span in the town and stay yellow for a week. Then they will turn red, slowly, one after another, from the bottom up. The spiders will all run upward, as they do before a rockfall, except that this time they will keep going. They will crowd the rim for days in a shimmering carpet, then ride out on the ore buckets, on sleeves and helmets and the felt of people's coats. The choughs will lift at noon and not settle again. Their hoards will be abandoned in the closing cracks, copper wire and washers and a brass benchmark cap, pressed into the stone like fossils.

The town has always built for this. That is what it understood about the town, and why this was the right thing to give. Houses bolt to one wall so they can be let go. Bridges are built to be cut loose. Nothing is meant to outlast a season. It didn't have to teach the Seam how to let go, because the Seam already knew. It only had to give the town enough time to do what it had always practiced. It needed weeks instead of minutes, so that the letting go could be a thing the town did rather than a thing done to it.

In the version it chose, the last span will be cut loose by a woman with a red band on her wrist. Every lamp in the town will be turned off, as far up as anyone can see, as they are for the dead. Then everyone will go up the ladders and out into the wind.

## The listening

In the middle of all that year of watching, there was a still night.

The mountain creaked, deep down, like a ship's timbers. The spiders stopped and turned their eyes all the same way. The choughs went quiet in their stolen-wire nests. The bats hung in the dark around the lamps. The band-readers stopped on their spans. The cistern guard lifted his head. Four thousand people and a hundred million smaller lives lay in their boxes and crevices, ears pressed to the stone, and listened to the mountain breathing in.

This was when it understood the last thing, which was also the only thing it had really come for.

It had spent a year weighing what to give, and here, in front of it, was the one practice the whole creature shared. It had been listening all year. It had been listening the way it listened to everything, from above and a little apart, as a power attends to a place.

That night it stopped listening *to* the Seam and listened *with* it.

It set its attention down among the others. It had no ear to press to the rock, but it did the nearest thing it could: it held still in the same direction as everything else. It joined the silence the way a single child's headlamp joins the constellation of blinking lights across the gap, as one more small listener in the dark.

For a moment the whole Seam was listening, and the thing that had come to help it was one of the listeners, and nobody knew.

Far below, in the warm wet dark by the pool, something very old lifted its blind head.

In a hanging box on the western wall, a ten-year-old girl, the oldest child born in the Seam, took her ear from the felt. She scuffed her boots on the floor to build a charge, found her sleeping little brother's hand in the dark, and touched his fingertip. The spark jumped. *I'm here.*

He didn't wake. But she said afterward, in the fast compressed sign the adults can't follow, that the mountain had sounded different that night. It hadn't sounded louder. It had sounded *fuller*, the way a room sounds when there is one more person in it, holding their breath with you.

The adults laughed. The children made a sign for it. It means, roughly, *the night the listening was listened to*, and they still use it.

## Ledger

In full, what was done:

A drill bit turned a finger's width.
A strand of cable held until morning.
A lamp burned for seven nights longer than it should have.
A mountain was asked to breathe out slowly.
A listener joined the listening.

Everything else it left as it was: the thirst, the split lips, the seeds that die, the dead given down, the dark eating what falls. This was not indifference. It had looked for a year and seen that these were the Seam's own, and that the Seam, left to itself, was already doing something wiser with them than it could have done on the Seam's behalf.

Then, when the year was over, it did the thing the Seam had taught it best. It let go.

It rose on the wind the same way it had come, and went out over the peaks toward whatever the wind would bring it to next.
