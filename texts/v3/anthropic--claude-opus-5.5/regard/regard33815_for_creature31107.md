# Seen From Above

## The attention

Seen from above, if anything ever did see it from above, the Coursing would look like a clay model of a mountain range pressed flat with the heel of a hand.

For a while, something did.

It did not come down through the sky, because when its attention reached that starless charcoal, that darkness with the texture of wet slate, it could not tell whether it was looking at a sky or at the underside of a floor. It found it could not see through, which surprised it. It could have forced the matter. It chose not to. That was the first thing it learned in the Coursing: a place made all the way down may be made all the way up, and the courtesy the masons show the floors beneath them might be worth extending in the other direction.

So it attended from wherever it was, and since there was almost nothing in the Coursing to see, it listened.

It listened for longer than a firing. It learned the hammers the way a mason learns them, one voice at a time: the cracked-bell, the double-knock, the one whose arm catches on the upswing and slides. It learned the forty tones and the bends that change them. It learned that a falling *here* means *come slowly*. It learned the difference between a block that hums true and one that hums wrong, which took some time, since it had never had fingertips that could hear pitch.

And it learned the saying before it understood it. *Never let them all fall together.*

## The difficulty of arriving

The attention was, by nature, one thing. Wherever it turned fully, it arrived all at once, with the whole of itself, the way noon arrives.

In the Coursing, that is the one thing that must never happen.

It understood this slowly and then completely. If it came into the basin as itself, with the undivided weight of its regard, it would be the single blow the masons spend their lives preventing. It would be one stroke passing down through every floor and one pulse that the whole bowl settled into. It did not know whether that would stop the heart of the place. The masons believed it would. They had lived here much longer than it had been looking, and in any case wisdom does not begin by testing whether a people's deepest fear is true.

So it made itself uneven. It came in pieces, at different rates, never wholly in one court. It let part of its attention drift slow in the eastern sumps while another part moved quick along a channel after a dredging. It learned to arrive the way water arrives here, as a seep, a bead under a lintel, a trickle finding the center of a canted lane.

This was harder for it than anything it had done in a long time, and it was glad of the difficulty. A power that finds nothing difficult in a place has not yet understood the place.

## What it could have done

It could have done almost anything. It considered all of it honestly, because refusing a gift without first imagining it fully is its own kind of arrogance.

**It could have given them a sun.** It pictured the light coming over the rim and the masons seeing their whole basin at once for the first time, the towers and tiers red and endless. Then it followed the light further. The sun would warm the stone. Once the stone was no longer colder than the air, the air would stop giving up its water. The drip-lines would dry, then the gutters, and the channels would slow and stop. The hammers would stop too, and the knocks would lose the flicker of the world, and the silt would stop running down to be made into brick. The whole slow breath of the place hangs on a single fact: the stone is cool. And the masons' eyes, milky as lime-water and made for a dark that is a medium rather than a lack, would ache in all that glare. They see each other's faces only at the kilns, briefly, lit orange from below before they turn away. That moment is how they see each other. A sun would make it ordinary.

**It could have given them green.** Soil in the beds, something rooted and growing. But roots pry, and the pavers are fitted tight. A single root in the Coursing would be the first wear the masons could not carry for it.

**It could have given them food.** This one it weighed longest, because hunger is real and a benevolent power should be suspicious of any argument that ends with other people staying hungry. It watched carefully. The masons were lean, but they were not starving. The grubs were enough, barely, and the moth-rises gave them a reason to love a creature that eats their walls.
