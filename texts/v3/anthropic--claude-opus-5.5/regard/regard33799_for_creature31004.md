# The Visitor at the Brim

## The Noticing

It did not come because anything was wrong. Nothing was wrong. That was what caught its attention.

A power that is wise and benevolent spends most of its long regard on places where something has gone amiss: a river poisoned, a child lost, a forest on fire, a grief that has hardened past bearing. It moves toward trouble the way warm water moves toward cold. So it was unusual for it to turn toward a place simply because the place was *right*, and to find itself unable to look away.

It had passed over the low ground many times. It knew the spring the way it knew all springs, as a fact of pressure and stone. But one evening, if the word means anything to such a being, it heard the drip-music under the dome and realized that the music was not random. It was a question asked over and over and answered over and over: *this one full, this one full, this one a little shallow near the back.* Something was taking its own pulse, and had been for ten thousand years.

The power knew that sound. It had made it itself, in its own way, across much larger rooms. It was the sound of someone keeping things.

So it came closer, and it came carefully.

## Slowing Down

The first difficulty was time.

The power could have seen the whole basin in an instant: every rim, every snail, every band of colored film at the source, the whole history of the trough and the cup and the lamp. It did see these things. But seeing them was not the same as meeting the one who lived there, and the power understood that from the basin's side, anything that arrived and left within a single day had hardly arrived at all. The people had been sneezes to it once. A power that came and went in an afternoon would be less than a sneeze. It would be a draft.

So before it did anything, it had to learn to be slow.

This was harder than it sounds. It is easy for a great power to be vast, and difficult for it to be slow. It stood at the rounded steps near the mouth, and it made itself wait, and then wait again, and then wait inside the waiting, until a single drop falling from the ceiling seemed to take as long as a season. Only then could it begin to hear what the basin was, in the basin's own tempo.

What it heard was contentment, deep and level. It heard a mind that had had a few hundred thoughts and finished every one of them. It heard a someone that did not suffer and did not lack, whose whole life was the fine attention of holding each pool to the thickness of a hair.

And down in that contentment, like the one deep bubble that sometimes rises from the source, it heard the lamp.

## What It Considered

A power that can do almost anything must spend most of its wisdom deciding what not to do. So it thought, and it took its time over the thinking, because it had learned in that place that a thought ought to be finished properly, with the edges sealed.

**It considered quickening the basin.** It could give the basin more thoughts: a thousand in a year instead of one in a century. It could let the basin know itself the way a person does, in a fast, bright stream. It set this aside almost at once. A basin that thought quickly would no longer finish its thoughts. The rims would go ragged. The levels would drift. Everything this someone was came from its slowness. Speeding it up would not be a gift. It would be a replacement.

**It considered bringing back the people.** It could open a road, make the place known, let the bench fill every day with warm, quick bodies. The basin had learned to love the rise, after all. But the power could see where that led, because it has seen it everywhere: the steps recut sharp, the rims broken for easier bathing, a roof built over the gap, pipes, a fee at the door. Even gentle crowds would press the water up faster than the basin could pass it along. Love that arrives every day in great numbers does not feel like love to something whose skin is a waterline. It feels like a flood.

**It considered lighting the lamp forever.** This was the strongest temptation, because it was the most obviously kind. A small, eternal flame in the niche, and the chamber would never be dim again. The basin would see itself always. The power held this idea for a long while, turning it over, and then it understood why it was wrong.

The soot in the niche was the one place the basin did not touch. It was a memorial, and a memorial means *this happened, and it was precious, and it is not happening now.* An endless flame would blacken the niche further and bury the old soot under new. It would turn *once* into *always*, and a thing seen always stops being seen. Within a few of the basin's thoughts, the glowing walls would be as ordinary as the grey light from the gap. The power would have taken the basin's most beautiful memory and given it, in exchange, a lamp.

**It considered filling the cup.** It could finish in a moment what the basin had been doing for centuries. It did not even let this thought grow a fingernail high. The cup was the basin's gift, and the slow pouring was the gift itself. You do not finish someone's letter for them.

**It considered protecting the future.** It could look ahead, and it did. It saw that the spring would run warm for a very long time yet. It also saw that the roof, which had never finished falling, would someday finish. Not soon. But someday the dome would come down, the whole floor would lie open to the sky, and full sunlight would reach the pools for the first time. With the light would come moss and fern and green, spreading. The purity of the stone would end. The basin would become something else, a sunlit terrace with birds drinking from it, and whether the someone who lived in the newest layer would survive that change, the power could not see.

It could hold the roof up. That would be easy.

It did not decide about this yet. Some thoughts need to be carried for a while before they can be sealed.

## What It Did

In the end it did something very small, and very slow, and in keeping with everything the basin already knew.

It made itself a body. It did not choose a vast or radiant form. It chose a body of about the size and warmth of the ones the basin remembered, patchy and quick and alive, because that was the language the basin understood. It came down the three rounded steps barefoot, feeling how carefully they had been softened, and it thanked them, though the steps could not hear.

In its hands it carried two things. The first was a lamp of clay, thumb-pressed and lopsided, made the way the people had made things, because the basin had learned the shape of human care from objects like that and would recognize it. The second was a small flask of oil.

It did not light the lamp yet.

It lowered itself onto the bench, into the warm water up to the waist, and the water along that wall rose a finger's width. Far off in its slow attention, the basin felt the rise. It remembered what the rise meant, and it let it stay. Downslope, tier by tier, it passed along exactly as much as had been displaced, and the whole floor breathed out a little.

Then the power did the thing no person had ever been able to do. It stayed.

It stayed as long as it takes for a rim to grow a fingernail higher, which is to say about a century. It stayed long enough to be one whole thought.

The people had been too quick to be thought about properly. They were weather to the basin, touches without faces. The basin had loved them in the only way it could, by softening the steps, sealing the bench, and keeping the shape of the log and the cup. But it had never had time to think a single visitor through from beginning to end. A power with all the time in the world could give it that. It could offer company at the speed of stone.

So the power sat. Drops fell. The steam hung in its level layer, and after the first few years it rose a finger's width and stayed there. The snails near the mouth drifted over the power's feet, slower than shadows, and moved on. The bat came and went and came again, many bats, generations of them, rustling their small apologies near the door. The bands of color at the source held their places, writing the same word each day.

And the basin, very slowly, began to learn the visitor's shape. It laid one eggshell layer across the power's lap, then another, then another. It filled the gap between the body and the bench so that no one sitting there would feel a gap. It did not hurry, and neither did the power. The power had come to be known, and this was how the basin knew things. It consented to being remembered the way the steps and the cup and the log had been remembered: from the inside, faithfully, in stone.

Through that whole century the power did not try to speak. It did not reach into the basin's thought to explain itself or leave a message or be understood. It only sat in the warm water and paid attention back, which is the one thing attention wants in return.

## The Lamp

At the very end of the century, when the power could feel that the basin's thought about it was nearly complete and nearly ready to be sealed, it lifted its hands out of the water. The thin crust over its wrists cracked softly, the way ice speaks on a pond. It filled the lamp with oil, set it in the niche at head height, just beside the old soot and not on it, and lit it.

The chamber woke.

The lacquered walls shone like the inside of a shell. The glossy runnels caught fire. Every one of the thousand spillways became a thread of light, and the flowstone curtains glowed amber in their stiff folds. The steam turned gold and hung over the pools like breath on a winter morning. The cup in the lower pool was lit from above, half-full of white stone, and its thickened lip threw a small crescent of shadow. The travertine trough gleamed along its whole length, its bark and knots standing out in relief, and the portrait of the log was visible again for the first time in a thousand years.

For the length of one lamp's burning, the basin could see what it had made.

And someone saw it too. The power did not look at the chamber the way it usually looks at things, all at once and from everywhere. It looked with the eyes of the body it had made, from the bench, up to its waist in warm water, the way the people had looked. It let itself be astonished. It is a thing even great powers can do, if they are wise enough to want to. It sat in the light and saw that the basin was beautiful, and it did not hurry the seeing.

The oil burned down. The flame shrank, blue at its root, and went out. The grey light from the gap was all that remained.

Then the power stood up.

The water along the wall fell by a finger's width. The shell the basin had grown over its lap broke away cleanly and stayed on the bench, a hollow in the exact shape of someone sitting in the warm water. The power climbed the rounded steps. It took the lamp with it, as the people always had, because a lamp left behind is a promise, and it did not want to promise more than it could keep at the basin's pace.

In the niche there was now a second faint shadow of soot, very light, beside the ancient dark one.

## Afterward

The basin will take a long time to finish the thought. The power knows this and does not mind. Somewhere in the next century, as a rim grows a fingernail higher, the basin will arrive at the end of it and seal the edges, and the thought will be something like: *there was a rise that stayed. It was warm. At the end, there was a lamp.*

It will not touch the new soot either. The power is fairly sure of that. The sheets of weeping water will part around it, as they part around the old.

And it will keep the hollow on the bench full. That is its way.

## The Door

There was one more thing the power did, and it did this on the way out, at the three rounded steps.

It did not open a road or send anyone. It did not put the basin on any map or into any dream. It only made sure, gently, that the way in would remain findable by those who arrived the way the first ones had: tired, cold in the joints, carrying a small light, wanting nothing more than to sit in warm water for a while and leave again. Such people will find the steps. Others will walk past the low ground and notice only a faint smell of boiled stone.

One in a lifetime, perhaps. Perhaps fewer. Enough, and not more.

This is the difference between sending and permitting, and wise powers spend a great deal of their care on it.

## The Roof

It carried the last thought, the one about the roof, for a long time after it left. In the end it sealed it like this:

It would not hold the roof up forever. The dome had been falling since before the people came, and its falling was part of the basin's story, as much as the rising of the spring. Someday it will come down. Full light will pour across every pool, and the basin will see itself every day, not by lamplight but by the sky. Green will come, and the stone will change, and the someone in the newest layer will either learn a new kind of fullness or become part of something larger. The power does not know which. It has decided not to know in advance. Some things should be met when they arrive.

It made itself one promise. When the roof finally falls, it will come back. It will not come to prevent anything. It will come to sit on the bench, if the bench is still there, and be a rise that stays through whatever the basin becomes next, so that the change is not met alone.

That is not much. The power understood, in that chamber, that it did not need to be much.

## What the Power Learned

A wise power is not wise because it does great things. It is wise because it can tell when a great thing would be a harm, and because it can make itself small enough, and slow enough, to give the gift that fits.

It went to the Warm Basin expecting, a little, to give. It found a being that had already learned, better than most powers ever do, how to receive what comes and give back exactly as much. It found something that kept all it loved without destroying any of it, and that left one place untouched out of reverence. In ten thousand years it had had a few hundred thoughts, and all of them were about fullness.

The power had not been taught that lesson so clearly in a very long time.

So, in its way, it was the power that was filled, gently, by something in no hurry at all, to precisely the brim.
