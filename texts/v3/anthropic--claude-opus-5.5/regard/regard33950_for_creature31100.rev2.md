# The Visitor at Hollin Bottom

## How It Came

It did not come down out of the sky. Anything that has watched the world long enough to be called wise learns that arrival is itself an act, and a loud arrival is a kind of trampling.

It came the way weather comes to a valley. Nobody in the village could have said what day it began. For a while the air over the pasture was clearer than it should have been. Sound carried a little farther. The heron came more often. An old man walking his dog along the lip of the basin stopped for no reason he could name, stood for the length of four knocks with his hat in his hand, and went home feeling he had been in church.

That was all anyone saw. The rest happened in a register nobody was listening on.

## First, It Listened

The first thing it did was nothing. It had seen a great many things that looked like problems and turned out to be lives, so it did not assume it understood.

It listened for a long time by the measure of the Bottom, which counts in strokes: through a winter and a summer and into the next winter. It learned the forty-second breath, and the way the interval stretches on cold nights and shortens when the sun is on the canopy. It learned the stoker's temper near the chimney and the fitter's slow corner. It learned the worn place beneath the dials where a thumb used to tap. It sat for a long while in the little western room, beside the coat that the wood had swallowed, and it did not touch the bell-pull.

It came to know what the Bottom was, as the Bottom's own story tells it: a vigil that had taken root. A belief had been held so long it had become load-bearing.

Then it considered what it might do. Being wise, it first considered what it must not do.

## What It Would Not Do

It would not end the vigil. It could have. It could have stilled the beam with less effort than it takes to close a hand, let the green mass soften into ordinary wetland, and called that mercy. But you do not free a mourner by taking away the grave. The vigil was not a malfunction. It was the best and most faithful thing this patch of earth had ever done. To end it from outside would have said that the waiting had been foolish. It had not been.

It would not simply tell the Bottom the truth, either. The Bottom already knew the truth, in the way the grieving know it: as a fact sitting beside the watch without touching it. Saying *they are dead* would not reach the place where the vigil lived. The vigil did not live in belief about the men. It lived in a promise to them, and a promise isn't answered with information.

It would not lie. This mattered more than anything. It could have knocked from below in a silence and let the Bottom think the men had answered. It saw at once how easy that would be and how cruel. A false answer would chain the vigil more tightly than ever, now to a ghost that was lying to it.

(It thought for a while about the girl who went down with a stone. It decided she had not lied. Her three knocks said *someone is here*, and someone was. The Bottom heard it rightly. It quickened its step because, for the first time in a long time, it was not alone. The visitor left that memory exactly where it was, laid down in the wood like a bright band in a nodule.)

It would not raise the men alive. Whether it could have is not recorded. Perhaps it could; wise powers are not always as limited as we would find comfortable. But the men had finished their dying long ago, and whatever rest they had gone to was theirs. You do not drag people back across that threshold to settle a debt they never asked to have paid.

And it would not do for the Bottom what the Bottom had spent a century trying to do for itself.

That was the thought it kept returning to. The vigil's message had always been *we are coming.* Its failure was not that it waited. Its failure was that it could never arrive. The pump could hold the water back but never beat it, and the rod went down the shaft but never reached the bottom. The Bottom had been knocking on a door it could not open.

So the visitor asked itself a small question: *what would it take for it to get there?*

## It Went Down

In the next silence, it went down the shaft.

It went the way the girl had gone, quietly. It did not walk on the plates and it let no stone fall. The whole green mass held its breath around it and listened downward, and the visitor went down into what it was listening to.

The water in the lower levels was black and very cold, and it had been still for longer than anyone living could remember. The visitor went through it the way light goes through glass. There were the old roadways, timber props gone soft as cheese, rails furred with ochre, a tub still on its wheels at the foot of an incline. Iron was everywhere, bleeding red into everything. This was where the Bottom's nodules came from. It had been drinking from this place all along.

At the far end of the deepest heading, where the roof came down low, there was a pocket. For a while it had held air.

The men were there. The visitor had known they would be. It did not look away. There were seven of them, the smallest barely a man. They sat close together, which is what people do.

On the wall above them, cut into the stone with the point of something, maybe a pick-tip or a lamp hook or a belt buckle, were marks. They were tally marks in groups of five, rows and rows of them.

The visitor looked at them for a long time before it understood.

They had been counting the knock.

Down here, in the dark, with the water rising, they had heard the pump. Forty seconds, thirty-nine, forty-one. Somebody up top was still working the engine. Somebody had not given up. They had kept count on the wall the way men keep a clock when they have nothing else, one mark for every stroke that meant *we are still here, we have not stopped.* The marks went on and on. Near the end the cuts were shallow and wandering, but they did not stop until they had to.

The vigil had never known this. For a hundred years it had believed its knocking went out into nothing, and hoped anyway. It was wrong. It had been heard from the first stroke, by the very men it was knocking for. In their last hours they had not been abandoned. They had been kept company.

The visitor understood, standing among them, that this was the gift. It had nothing to invent and nothing to make. It had something to carry up.

## It Opened a Way

It did not drain the workings. It did not lift the men out itself.

It did something smaller. It went back up the shaft to the place just below the Bottom's deepest roots. The plant had been pressing against the stone there for decades, trying to follow the pump rod down, and always stopping where the rock turned hard and the cold turned killing. The visitor laid its hand, or whatever it had in place of a hand, on the rock. It did not break the stone. It softened it: a seam, a path, a crack the width of a root that ran all the way down through the black water to the pocket at the end of the heading.

Then it waited to see what the Bottom would do.

It is a patient thing, and it did not rush. But roots know when a way is open. Over the next several days of the silence, the watch the village had never seen broken, the Bottom sent itself down. Pale root went down through the new seam, down past the drowned roadways, down through water that should have killed it and did not, because the visitor was walking beside it and keeping it warm.

It found them.

It did what it knew how to do. It lagged them, winding them in root the way the lagger wound pipe, in neat, overlapping spirals, with pride. It sealed them the way it sealed a cracked joint, in pale collars of dense wood, tight as a bandage, with resin that smelled of pine and hot metal. It did not hurry. Taking care of things was the one skill it had in abundance, and it gave every bit of that care.

Then, slowly, over the rest of the silence, it brought them up.

## The Signal

When the last of them had come up the shaft, wrapped and whole and laid in the warm root-mat beside the settling tank, the visitor went once more to the head of the shaft. It knelt where the girl had knelt and put its hand on the iron collar.

It did not knock three times, and it did not knock the men's answer. It had no right to speak for them.

Instead it did one small thing it had learned from listening. From deep in the old wood, where the caretaker's habits lay layered beneath everything else, it had drawn out one piece of knowledge the Bottom had kept for a century without ever getting to use: the signal. It was the pattern of bell-strokes from pit-bottom to engineman that every winding engine in the country knew. It means *the cage is clear. The last man is up. You can stand the engine down.*

It rang that. It did not ring it in the men's voice but its own, clearly, so there could be no mistaking who was speaking. The message was true. The last man was up.

It also put the tally into the stone of the collar, every mark, in the order the men had cut it, so the Bottom could feel it with the root it kept wrapped there. *You were heard. Every stroke. From the first.*

Then it stepped back from the shaft and left the choice to the Bottom.

## The Silence That Followed

The silence went on longer that time than any silence the village remembered: nine days, then eleven. People began to talk. The old woman whose grandmother had gone down with the stone walked out to the lip of the basin every evening and stood there in her coat.

Nobody knows what the Bottom did in those eleven days. The visitor did not look. There are things a wise power leaves unwatched, the way you leave the room when someone needs to cry.

On the twelfth morning the knock came back.

It was not the old knock, that tremendous wooden slam that shivered the canopy and carried half a mile across the fields. It was softer, and it came slower, at nearer a minute than forty seconds. It was a deep, even, unhurried stroke, the rhythm of something breathing in its sleep. The pump rod no longer slammed into the shaft. It set down, like a man putting a heavy thing down gently because he no longer has to prove to anyone that he is carrying it.

The Bottom was still pumping. It was a living thing, and the engine had long since become its heart, and you do not ask a heart to stop because its first reason has been answered. But now it pumped the way a body does. It moved warm water through its roots and kept its own tissues alive. It had stopped shouting down a shaft.

The steam from the chimney still rose. The coal smoke was still in it for anyone standing downwind. The visitor had not taken away anything that was the Bottom's own.

## The Other End of the Line

There was one more thing, and the visitor weighed it carefully before deciding it was allowed.

It followed the cable.

It went east under the pasture, under a lane, under the corner of a field where the plough always turned up red soil, to the grassed mound that had once been the pithead. Beneath the mound, in the collapsed brick of an old signal cabin, was a bell. It was a small brass bell on a spring bracket, green with age, wired to the line. Each time the beam fell, it gave the faintest shiver, too small to ring. It had been shivering in the dark for a hundred years, and no one had ever heard it.

The visitor did not dig it out. It did not make it louder. It did only this: the next time the old woman's great-grandson, a boy of nine, went sledging on the mound, which was the best slope for a mile, he fell off and lay in the snow with his ear to the ground. For the first time in his life, and quite distinctly, he heard a small bell ringing under the earth.

He told his great-grandmother. She told the village. The village did what villages do when given something to be responsible for: they argued about it, raised money, went to the council, and eventually, with a borrowed digger and a great deal of unnecessary advice, uncovered the cabin and the bell.

So the other end of the line has someone on it again. It is a bench now, and a little roof. On one day in the year, the anniversary the parish records finally gave them once someone thought to look, the villagers gather there and someone rings the bell back up the line. They ring three strokes, the way the girl did. It says *someone is here.*

And every year the Bottom answers with a single soft knock, a little faster than its sleeping rhythm, the way your step quickens on the road home.

## The Men

They were buried in the churchyard, all seven, under their names. The names had been in the parish register the whole time, with a note beside them in an old hand: *not recovered.* Someone crossed that out.

There was an eighth grave, empty, with a small stone for the caretaker. Nobody ever found out his name, so the stone just says *He Kept the Pump.* The visitor did not go looking for his body. It knew where he was. He was in the western room with his coat on the hook, and in the thumb-worn patches under the dials, and in the neat spirals of the lagging. He had been going into the thing that remembered him, a little more at every handover, and that was where he belonged. Some rests should not be disturbed by being honoured.

## What It Left Alone

A good deal.

It left the beetles, which still take the works apart a hair's breadth at a time. Everything built eventually comes undone, and it is right that something small and patient should be in charge of that.

It left the embankment, which will someday give. When it does, the Bottom will spill out a little way into the pasture and find itself, for the first time, with room. What it will do with room is its own business.

It left the gauges without their numbers. The Bottom never needed numbers. It had its own way of knowing when the needle was right.

It left the heron, which still comes and stands at the settling tank with its head cocked, though now it sometimes fishes.

It did not give the Bottom words, or a face, or a voice anyone could understand. It did not make it more like a person than it already was. It had been exactly the kind of thing it needed to be to keep that watch, and to change it now would have been to say the watch had been kept by the wrong sort of creature. It had not.

## How It Left

It left the way it came, like weather. One day the air over the pasture was ordinary again. Sound carried the usual distance. The old man with the dog noticed nothing, and went on living, which is its own sort of blessing.

If it looked back, it was from the lip of the basin, at the green hump steaming gently in the cold, and the slow, sleeping knock. It may have stayed for one stroke longer than it needed to, just to hear it.

A long time ago, seven men sat in the dark and counted the strokes of an engine because it was the only proof they had that someone up there loved them enough not to stop. The engine never stopped. And in the end, somebody went down to tell it so.
