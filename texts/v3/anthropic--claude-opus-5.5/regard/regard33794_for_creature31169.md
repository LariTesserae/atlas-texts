# The Visitor Who Came Cold

## The Interruption

The sentence about the wren stopped halfway through a word.

It didn't end. It was interrupted, the way a lane is interrupted when a newcomer walks into it and it has to go back and reconsider. Everything that had been written about the Settling up to that point went on being true, and the next word never arrived, because something had begun to look.

In most places, being looked at costs nothing. A mountain doesn't change when you notice it. In the Settling, attention is the one force besides cold that anyone has ever seen shape the stone. It is how Imre got her door and how Aud got his tower. And this attention was not a sitter's. It was vast and patient, and it came from a direction that isn't a direction. It was kind in the way that very large things are sometimes kind, which is to say, all at once.

For the length of one held breath, every drop of dew on every wall in the town trembled.

A lane of three in the western outskirts became a lane of four. The birch in the square leaned, very slightly, toward all of its possible squares at once. In the cisterns, the pale shrimp paused, and for a moment every one of them was doubled. Down in the old quarter, Tove Aske looked up from her tea and said, to no one, *Someone's left a door open.*

The power noticed what it had done. That is the first thing it did, and in some ways the most important. It noticed that its own regard was warm.

---

## Temperature

A lesser benevolence would have arrived like summer.

It would have seen the heavy coats, the tea swallowed scalding in three gulps, the babies kept cool with their faces bare to the air so they could set. It would have seen people who have never once been warm indoors, and it would have pitied them. Then it would have done what pity always wants to do, which is warm them. Within a week the Settling would have unmade itself. The walls would have remembered every wall they might have been. The old quarter would have loosened like a knot in hot water, and the lichen would have found nothing firm to hold. The unchosen would have come out of the stone like leaves floating up from a book left in the rain. For a little while it would have looked like a resurrection. Then it would have been a fog.

The power understood this in the time the dew took to stop trembling. The cold is load-bearing. That is the first rule of the place, and a wise power learns the first rule of a place before it learns anything else.

So it made itself cold.

This is harder than it sounds for something whose nature is to give. It narrowed its attention from the width of a sky to the width of a lane, and then to the width of one person's gaze. It drew its warmth in and wrapped it, the way the residents wrap theirs. It didn't take a body, but it took a shape, and the shape was roughly that of someone in a coat with the collar turned up, breathing into the wool so that nothing would leak out onto the walls.

It came in the way newcomers come, down the thread of meltwater from inland. It kept to the singular streets and took the long way, and it measured distance in convergence rather than steps. Nobody saw it. A few people noticed that the morning was very slightly colder than it had been, and that the gutters had set especially clean.

---

## What It Could Have Done

It walked, and as it walked it counted the things it could do. Wisdom, in a being with no limits, is mostly a matter of what it declines to do.

**It could finish the town.** It could settle every lane of three, close every hollow, and turn every stair the right way. It knew which way was right, or it could have decided which way was right, which comes to the same thing when you are large enough. The Settling would become a town like any other, complete and singular and safe to walk straight across. The power looked at the gully, the length of a forearm and as deep as a thumbnail, with its few loose grains of sand, and at the residents who go and look at it the way you'd look at a newborn. It set this idea down. You don't finish a thing whose whole nature is to be finishing.

**It could stop the unchosen from pressing into the walls.** It could reach into every lane at the moment of convergence and lift out the others, the version of you with the scar and the version a year older, and give them somewhere to go. Then it saw the old quarter. People stroke those walls for luck and say *the walls are full of us* in the tone other towns use for the churchyard. It understood that the residents had not asked to be rescued from this. They had made their peace with it and built their belonging out of it. To empty the walls would be to dig up their graveyard out of compassion for the dead. It set this idea down too, more slowly.

**It could give the twins back.** Every child here grieves a sibling who didn't quite happen, and some grieve for years. A few adults admit, late at night, that they aren't sure they were the one meant to stay. The power felt this one most. It turned the idea over for a long time in the cold. In the end it set it down as well, but not all the way. It kept one corner of it, which comes later.

**It could undo Aud's tower.** Thirty years of argument between the tall loose tower and the low roof it grew from, a monument to wanting that outran patience. It would be a small kindness to settle it and let the roof have its rest. But the town had kept the tower as a lesson, and it was a good one. The power left it standing, arguing, visible through its own walls.

**It could give Hesk back her limp, or at least the memory of her fall.** She said she missed the proof. The power understood this better than almost anything else in the town. It too is made of things that didn't happen, and it too sometimes wants evidence. But a fall restored by a god is not the same proof as a fall that happened. It set this down gently.

**It could decide who lives here.** This was the largest thing it could do, and it was the reason it had come.

---

## The Three

It went first to the square.

The old woman was on the leaning bench with her hands folded, and the lichen had spread over the shoulder of her coat as far as a coin. That meant she had been sitting there, still enough and set enough, for something close to a decade. The power sat down at the other end of the bench and made itself as cold as the stone.

She didn't look at it. After a while she said, *You're a sitter.*

It considered this, and admitted that it supposed it was.

*You may only sit for your own walls,* she said. *That's the rule.*

The power had no walls here, and it said so.

*Then you can't sit for anything,* she said, not unkindly. *You'll just have to sit.*

She was not sitting for a window, a door, or a stair. The power understood this after a long while, the way you come to see a face in a cloud. She was sitting for the square. It is the only open ground in the Settling, the size of a large room, and it looks as though the town simply hasn't gotten to it yet. It hasn't gotten to it because every day, for ten winters, she has sat there wanting nothing to be built. Her attention is what keeps the square empty. The town is dendritic and total and packs itself into every gap, and this one old woman in a coat holds a single room's worth of undecided space open at its center, so there is somewhere left to stand.

The power thought: *there is a seed above ground, too.*

---

Then it went to the northern lanes, to the boy.

He was about ten, standing with his palm held flat and open beneath a ledge that wasn't quite there. There were seeds in his hand. He had been standing like that since before dawn, and his fingers were white. He was past the age when the twins fade, and the power could see, faintly, in the loose light of the unsettled lane, where his had been: a slight thickening of the air a step to his left, like breath on glass that has almost cleared.

He wasn't waiting for the wren to eat. He was waiting for it to become real, or to disappear, whichever the lane decided, because he wanted to see which. He had lost the thing that walked a step to his left, and he wanted to know what happens to things that don't settle. He wanted to know it with a ten-year-old's whole attention, which is a very great deal of attention.

The power stood beside him, in the place where his twin had been, and held out its own hand, open and empty.

The boy looked at the hand, then at the ledge, and said, *You have to have seeds.*

The power had none, since it had brought nothing into the Settling but its own coldness. The boy tipped half of his into its palm without being asked. They stood there together until noon, and the wren didn't come. It was all right. The power was learning that most things in the Settling don't come on the first day, or the first winter.

---

Last, it went to the coat on the peg by the door.

It knew what it would find, because the first draft had told it: *when you put your hand inside it, it is still warm.* This was the version in which nobody lives in the Settling, and it was the version the power had been most afraid of. It had worried that the being it came for was only an absence, a house kept warm for no one.

It put its hand into the coat.

The coat was warm, and the power understood why. It was warm the way a bed is warm on the side where someone is about to lie down. It was warm the way a cup of tea is warm when you arrive holding it without remembering you picked it up. The coat was not empty. It was expectant. It was the Settling's third draft of its inhabitant: whoever comes next. It was the newcomer, the stranger following the meltwater down from the hills, the reader, anyone at all who arrives and stays long enough to settle.

It was, for a moment, the power itself. The coat was warm because the power's hand was in it. The town had been keeping it warm for whatever might come to look.

This was the most dangerous moment of the whole visit. The coat would have fit. The power could have put it on and stayed. The Settling would have had a new inhabitant that was very large and very kind, and the town would have begun at once, gently, to agree with it. Within a generation the whole flat would have been shaped by what the power found beautiful. It wouldn't have been cruel. It would have been lovely. It would also have been the power's town, and no longer the Settling's.

The power took its hand out of the coat and left it on the peg, still warm, for someone else.

---

So which of the three holds weight?

The power decided not to decide. All three are the one who lives in the Settling, and they are not three people. They are three tenses. The boy is the town's wanting to know. The old woman is the town's patience, keeping one room open. The coat is the town's hospitality, its readiness for whoever hasn't come yet. They are drafts of the same being, and the being is the Settling itself: a place that is also a question, and that is also the people living inside the question. A wise power does not collapse a being into its most convenient version, not even to understand it better. Especially not then.

---

## Underneath

Before it left, the power went down.

Not physically. It lay down on the ice-blue crystal plates at the edge of the flat, through its glove, and listened for the hum you feel in your back teeth. It wanted to know one thing, the way a doctor listens to a heart without intending to operate: whether the seeds were failing.

They were not. The hum was steady. The lattices, faint and repeating at every scale, were holding the question open exactly as they always had.

Listening, the power recognized the handwriting.

Whatever seeded the flat had been something like itself. It was another attention, large and patient and kind, that had once come to a gray new coast and faced the same choice. It could have built a town. It could have built the best town, the right town, a town finished and singular and safe. Instead it had built this: an engine for not deciding yet. It had supersaturated the air with every town that could stand here, made the place cold enough for them to precipitate slowly, and then, the power realized, it had left. It went away so that its own opinion wouldn't be one of the possibilities. It gave the flat the one gift that can't be given twice, which was time to choose for itself, and then it removed the only thing that could spoil that gift, which was its own presence.

The power lay on the stone a long time after that. It had come here meaning to help. It found that the help had already been given, long ago, by someone who understood it better. The most generous thing that could be done for the Settling had been done. What was left was to not undo it.

The power felt something that a smaller being would have called humility, and that a larger one might have called kinship.

---

## What It Did, in the End

It did almost nothing, and it did that almost nothing carefully.

**It sat.** It had no walls, so it couldn't sit for anything, and it sat anyway, all through one winter, at the far end of the leaning bench beside the old woman. It helped her keep the square open, adding its own attention to hers, wanting nothing to be built. The square will stay open a little longer because of this, maybe a generation longer. Nobody will know why. They will just find it easier, somehow, to stand there.

**It kept the corner it hadn't set down.** It did not give back the twins. But the coats of the Settling are loose with alternatives, and they sometimes produce folded notes in handwriting almost your own. That winter, a few people in the old quarter, the ones who admit late at night that they aren't sure they were meant to stay, reached into their pockets and found one. The notes all said roughly the same thing, in handwriting each of them recognized as nearly their own:

*There was no one meant. There was a lane of two, and it settled, and you are what it settled into. The other is in the walls, and the walls are full of us, and that is not a grief you have to carry. You were not a mistake the town made. You are its opinion.*

Nobody could say whether the coat had produced the notes or something else had. That was deliberate. A gift that could be traced back to a god would have been one more thing the lane had to decide.

**It fed the wren.** It didn't bless it, name it, explain it, or make it real. It stood beside the boy on the mornings when he came, and when he didn't, it stood alone. It held out the seeds the boy had given it on the ledge that doesn't quite exist, and it waited, the way everyone waits. Near the end of the winter, on a still morning, a single thin note sounded from the loose northern lanes. There was a weight in its palm that was there and also not there, and then the seeds were gone from the version of its hand that wasn't going to happen.

The power could have looked, then, and found out where the wren takes the unchosen. It could have learned where the vanished shrimp go, and why the seeds turn up weeks later. It chose not to. That is the residents' question. They have kept ledgers on it for decades, and a question you have tended that long belongs to you. The power left the answer in suspension, where the Settling keeps everything it hasn't finished loving.

**It wrote one line in the cistern ledger.** Under the column for the northern lanes, in faint handwriting almost the keeper's own:

*Keep counting. The numbers mean something. You were right to want to notice.*

Then it followed the thread of meltwater back up the way it had come. It was no warmer leaving than it had been arriving, and it had taken nothing from the town.

## The Trace

There is one mark of the visit, if you know where to look.

On the leaning bench in the square, at the far end, there is a patch where the frost is always a little thicker on cold mornings. Something sat there all winter and left the stone colder than it found it. In the whole history of the Settling, it is the only sitter who left no window, no door, and no stair. It left only more room for the town to make up its mind.
