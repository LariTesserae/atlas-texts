# What Came to the Edge of the Tableland

## Attention

It noticed the place the way one notices a sound that has been going on for a long time.

At first it saw what anyone would see from high enough up: a thin grey line along the foot of a cliff, a scratch of green, a dark plain with a white tower of cloud standing over its middle. It could have taken the whole thing in at a glance and moved on. Powers like it usually do. Most places are finished being interesting in a glance.

This one wasn't. When it looked closer, the line resolved into cells, and the cells into rooms, and the rooms held sleeping bodies, human and animal, breathing against each other. Its first thought was *here is a city*. That thought was wrong, and it knew it was wrong almost immediately, because the city kept moving at the edges of its vision. A district it had seen a moment ago was grass now. A slope it had seen bare now carried houses. It had been looking too fast.

So the first thing the power did was slow down.

This took a while, as such things are measured. It had to let a morning be a morning: the frost, the drip, the dry, the cold. Then it had to let a thousand mornings be a thousand mornings, and then it had to hold both at once, the single dawn and the long climb. Only when it could see the woman crouching at the cairn and the reef-body shifting along the scarp as one continuous thing did it understand what it was looking at. It was not a city, and not a people living in a city. It was one creature, three hundred kilometers long, whose hands were human, whose blood was meltwater, and whose memory turned on the updraft overhead.

The power watched it for what the Stair would have called six generations. It did nothing. It was learning the pause.

## What It Could Give

It could give anything. That was the difficulty.

It considered water first, because water is the first thing anyone would think to give a dry place. It could bring the Pillar down. It could make the white tower over the plain break open every afternoon and pour. The dew-cairns would overflow, the seeps would run like rivers, the grass would climb out of the scarp-lines and cover the black flows in a single lifetime. No one would ever again wait by a stone in the dark for the sun.

It turned this over carefully, and the more it looked, the more clearly it saw what the rain would do. The order of water would break first. *Drinking, washing, animals, grass* is a rule for scarcity. In abundance it means nothing. Then the rolling rule would go. If there is enough for every roof, it no longer matters whose roof is highest, and the young stop climbing, and the tide that carries every life gently down through the city goes slack. Then the counting would go, and with it *hul*, counted water, water that carries a family's obligations. Water from the sky is the sky's. In a generation there would be no difference between a family and a household, and in three the seep-names would stop being recited, because no one would need a chain of kin to know where they could drink. The people would be wetter and warmer and more comfortable, and something of great value would have died quietly, without anyone noticing its death.

"It is what the water built out of us," the old ones say. Change the water and you change what gets built. The power could see the other city that would grow in the rain, and it might be a fine city. It would not be this one. Giving rain would not help the Stair. It would replace the Stair.

It considered stilling the ground. The tremors are the great grief of the place: the districts broken, the nests crushed, the seeps shifting so that whole neighborhoods go dry and walk away along the cliff to start again. It could hold the fault. It could lock the plain so that it never shrugged again.

But the tremors are what lift the scarp. The cliff rises a hand's width in a lifetime because the ground is moving. The seeps come out where they do because the fault has opened the rock. A still plain would mean a cliff that stops climbing, cracks that silt up, seeps that thin and fail over centuries. The Stair would go on building terraces at its top until it reached the top of a cliff that no longer grew, and then it would have nowhere to go. Stopping the tremors would stop the thing that makes the Stair possible.

It considered wood: a forest in some sheltered hollow, so that every family could have beams, and no one would have to trade a season's wool for one polished crossbeam. It considered metal: a seam of good copper, or iron and the knowledge to work it. It considered lungs that needed less, lifespans that ran longer, a gentler sun.

Each time it saw the same shape. A gift that did not come from the ground would not fit into the Stair's way of receiving gifts. The Stair has one gift it builds from over and over, the columns of the flows, and its whole life is organized around the fact that the ground gives slowly and specifically, and that you acknowledge the giver before you carry the gift away. A gift from the sky, whole and unasked, has no giver the Stair knows how to thank. It could only be taken.

## The Objection

Here the power stopped and argued with itself, because it did not trust how easily it had reached this conclusion.

It is very easy, from far enough away, to find someone else's hardship beautiful. The frost is beautiful. The pause is beautiful. The grandmother in the warm low room telling the child that she will be drunk one morning at the seeps is beautiful. It is also true that the grandmother has a cough that will kill her this winter, and that she will spend her last weeks unable to get her breath in the thin air. It is also true that a child three terraces down from Tesh was born with a heart that does not work well, and that at this altitude that child will not see ten. It is true that when the next great tremor comes, some people will be under the walls.

A power that looked at all of this and said *how lovely, how patient, I must not interfere* would not be wise. It would be a tourist.

So the power made itself look at each of these griefs one at a time, at the scale of the single morning and not the scale of the thousand years. It asked of each one: *Is this the shape of the Stair, or only something that happens in it?*

The rolling rule is the shape of the Stair. The order of water is its shape. The descent through the terraces, the dead given to the birds, the rebuilding after the ground moves: all of these are the Stair's shape. Change them and you have changed the creature.

A cough is not the shape of the Stair. The grandmother's death is part of the Stair's shape; she will go down, and under, and into the water, as she said. But whether her last weeks are spent choking or breathing easily is not part of anyone's shape. It is only suffering. The same goes for the child's heart, and for the moment when a lintel falls and a child is under it or is not.

It came to understand that there are two kinds of hardship in a place like this. One kind the creature is made of. The other kind merely passes through it. The first kind is not the power's to take away. The second kind, perhaps, it may touch, if it can touch it without leaving a mark.

## The Rule It Made for Itself

So the power made itself a rule, the way the Stair makes rules: slowly, and then so thoroughly that it no longer needed to think about it.

*Give only what the place could already have given.*

The Stair has luck. Some winters the grandmother's cough breaks and she lives three more years. Some children with weak hearts outgrow them. Some lintels hold for a breath longer than they should. None of these things would surprise anyone on the Stair. They happen. They are called good mornings.

The power could lean on the luck. It could not make a thing happen that never happens there, but it could make the things that sometimes happen happen a little more often, when it mattered, where it mattered. Nothing that would change the order of water. Nothing that could be counted. Nothing anyone would ever need to thank it for, since they would already know how to thank the morning.

That winter the grandmother's cough broke in the second month. She lived to see her granddaughter's first built wall, which leaned a little northward. The child three terraces down lived to be thirty-one, which is not long, but is a life. And when the next great tremor came, the whistlers went silent perhaps ten heartbeats earlier than they would have, all along the seep line at once. The people of the Stair, who trust the small ones, put down what they were carrying and walked to the open terraces. When the walls came down, fewer were under them.

No one noticed anything. The whistlers had always told them first. *The ground tells the small ones first.* The power had only told the small ones slightly sooner.

## The Water Already Underground

It learned to read the seeps.

The water that comes out of the cliff at the Low Ear did not fall last winter. It fell as snow on highlands far off, generations ago, and has been traveling ever since through the porous flows, slowly, in the dark. The power could follow it. It could see the whole body of water now moving underground toward the Stair, and so it could see, the way you might read the next pages of a book, exactly what the seeps would give for the next eight hundred years.

It saw a dry band coming. Long ago, the far highlands had suffered a run of poor winters. That missing snow was traveling toward the scarp now, and in about a hundred and forty years it would arrive. The seeps along the middle of the Stair, where the cliff bends north, would thin for about sixty years. Several districts would go dry. People would walk away along the cliff toward the ends, where the water still ran, and rebuild there.

The power watched this future for a long time. It could have hurried the old snow through the rock, or thickened the fog on those years, or quietly filled the gap.

It did not. It had checked carefully, and this was the Stair's shape. Seeps shift; districts die; the reef-body moves along the cliff following its water. The Stair had done this dozens of times. Its songs are full of the names of dry places spoken in the past tense. It knows how to survive a thin century, and the knowing is part of what it is. If the power took this hardship away, it would be telling the Stair, without words, that it no longer needed to know how to move.

What it did do was smaller. Some of the families who would walk away from the middle districts were ones whose seep-chains did not reach very far. They would arrive as strangers, spoken to in the formal dialect of the pause. The power could see that some of them would be turned away, and that some of those would die on the plain.

So, over those decades, in the ordinary way of things, a few marriages happened that might not otherwise have happened. A young man from the bend of the cliff met a woman from the western reaches at a clay-trading and could not stop thinking about her. An aunt from the Weeping Wall ended up two hundred kilometers from home. The chains grew a little longer. When the dry band arrived, more of the walkers could recite a line of seeps that ended at a stranger's door, and more of those doors opened.

The Stair would have called it nothing. It would have called it kin.

## The Fire Below

There was one thing the power could not leave alone, and it was the one thing the Stair could not see.

Deep under the tableland, under the young flows, is what made them. The plain is lava, not much of it older than a few thousand years, and the fires that made it have not gone out. Reading the ground beneath the scarp, the power saw pressure building along the fault, the same fault the Stair leans against. In a little less than four hundred years, it would open. It would not be a crack. It would be a fissure, glowing, running a great part of the cliff's length, and the flow that came out of it would fill the foot of the scarp from end to end.

This was not the Stair's shape. Tremors are its shape, and moving along the cliff, and rebuilding. A river of fire running the length of the city is not a hardship the reef-body can walk away from. There would be nowhere along the cliff for the cells to drift toward. It would be the end: the living in their hexagons, the herds in the stables, the finches in the walls, and the sealed levels beneath, where the Stair keeps what it knows, all buried under new black stone. The great birds would circle over an empty scarp, and there would be no one below to look up.

So the power acted. It did not do much, and it did not do it quickly.

It did not stop the fire. The fire is the land's own making. It is where the columns come from, where the whole tableland comes from. To quench it would be the same mistake as stilling the ground. Instead, over a century, it eased the pressure elsewhere. It opened the weaknesses in the rock a little further out, a little further out again, drawing the fire toward the plain and away from the cliff, the way the Stair's channels draw a trickle from basin to basin.

When the fissure finally opened, it opened sixty kilometers out on the tableland, in the empty black between the scarp and the Pillar. For a month the people of the Stair saw a red line on the plain at night and a dark plume standing beside the white one by day. The ground shrugged hard. Walls fell. It was a bad year.

Then the glow died down, and people did what they always do when the ground makes something new. They walked out to look at it, the way lowlanders might go look at a newborn. They stood at the edge of the fresh flow, bare black, still ticking and steaming in the frost, and they named it.

The power had one more reason for choosing where the fire came out, and it had spent the longest on this. The new flow cooled slowly, in a deep basin, and slow-cooling lava cracks into columns. In a thousand years, or two, when the scarp has risen and the Stair has climbed and the old quarries near the cliff are worked out, there will be a field of fresh hexagonal stone waiting on the plain. Its surface will be freckled with lichen by then. The builders of that century will pry it loose with horn wedges and dress it and carry it home.

They will never know that it was set aside for them. They will only know that the ground gave, slowly and specifically, as it always has.

## The Cracked Stone

This was the hardest restraint of all, and the smallest.

Tesh-of-the-Low-Ear had a stone near the base of her third dew-cairn that cracked two winters before the power first noticed her. She had been putting off replacing it. It was a small thing, the kind of small thing the power could have mended in the time it takes frost to form. It would have been easy to come one night, lift out the cracked stone, and fit a sound one in its place, and let her find it in the morning and wonder.

The power did not. It watched her through three more winters of putting it off, which was very hard for it. It watched the cairn give a little less each morning than it should have. Then, one spring, after a morning when the frost had been poor, Tesh crouched at the cairn with her daughter and said, with some irritation, "Well." She took the cairn apart, stone by stone, down to the cracked one, and showed the girl how to choose a replacement from the flow: by its weight, and by the fineness of its surface, and by whether it rang or thudded when you struck it with another stone. Then they rebuilt the cairn together. The girl set the last few stones. Afterward it leaned a little northward, but less than before, because a new pair of hands had been added to it.

The power understood then why it must not fix the stone. The crack was not a failure in the cairn. It was the place where the cairn would pass from one pair of hands to the next. If it had closed that crack, it would have closed a door.

## Down

In its last centuries at the Stair, the power went down into the sealed rooms.

No one on the Stair goes there. The power could go anywhere, and it went carefully, as a guest goes into the room where the elders sleep. It went down past the stables, into the packed dark, through the rubble and grass-mat and dust, down through the terraces that were the highest roofs of their day, and down further to where the cliff was a step a child could climb.

It wanted to know whether the songs were true.

What it found was not a presence in any sense the power had words for. The lower rooms did not speak. But they were not empty. Every hexagon had been lived in, and the living had pressed down, and the weight of all those mornings lay in the stone, in the soot of ten thousand winters on the corbelled ceilings, in the worn places on the steps and the smoothness of hoarded wood, in the finch-gaps that had been left open in every wall for a thousand years so that something other than people could live there too. The meltwater moving through it on its way to the seeps picked up something from all of this, a trace of mineral and old ash and bone, faintly sweet. The old people were right that it tasted different. They were right that you drink your dead.

The power stayed in the lower rooms a long time, as such things are measured. It understood that this was the only place on the Stair where the whole of the thing was present at once: every generation, stacked, pressed together, kept. From very high up, the power had seen the Stair as one creature. Down here the Stair saw itself that way, in the only way stone can.

The power had nothing to add to it. So it listened, and it did not take anything away, and eventually it climbed back up through the stables into the warmth of the herds and the smell of wool.

## Morning

It never decided to leave. Powers like it do not really leave places that have taught them something. But at some point it was no longer watching the Stair as something outside itself. It was simply there, the way the Pillar is there, the way the updraft is.

In the end it did one last thing, and it did not quite mean to do it.

On a cold morning deep in winter, with the herds humming in the stables and the Stair breathing mist along its whole length, the power came down to look closely one more time, at the scale of a single dawn. It came to the southern districts, to the Low Ear, where Tesh was very old now. She was living near the stables, in a room with walls a thousand years thick, drinking what had passed through every other household above her.

The power had no body. But its attention, brought that close, was not nothing. A warm gust came up the cliff face on a morning with no warmth in it. It rose past the fog-fences and the terraces and the finch-gaps in the walls, and moved through the cold air like a hand.

Tesh felt it. So did her daughter, and her daughter's children, and the family in the house above them. They knew what it meant, because on the Stair everyone knows. *Someone has come down to look.*

So they went out onto the roof, the whole household, in their felts, in the dark before sunrise, and they stood there. They did not speak. They stood in the pause.

The power had spent six generations learning that pause. It had spent many more trying to be worthy of it. It had not expected to be answered in it. Standing with them, if a power can be said to stand, it understood that it had just been counted among the Stair's residents: somewhere above the living and the herds and the finches, in the invisible population turning in the updraft. The people had mistaken it for one of their dead.

It let the mistake stand. It seemed the most honest thing it could be to them. It was a presence that cared for them, that they could not see, that had come down to look, and that they would never need to thank except by standing still together at dawn.

Then the sun came up all at once across the flatness, red and then gold, and the tableland lit with rime from end to end, and the power watched it with them. Along the whole length of the scarp, in dozens of languages, people crouched by their stones and drank the first water where it was made.

The power did not drink. It had nothing to drink with. But it waited there, at the edge of the cold roof, beside an old woman whose palms had gone glossy and hard as horn, until the frost turned to drip and the day began. It was the only courtesy it knew that the Stair would understand: you do not carry away a gift before you have acknowledged the giver.

It had been given a great deal.
