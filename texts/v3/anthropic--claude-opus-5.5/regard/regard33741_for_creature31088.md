# The Attention

## How it came

It came the only way in, from above.

It came as a quality of the air over the col rather than as a figure or a light. The lorry drivers on the concrete apron felt the pressure change the way you do before weather. A raven on the high scarp stopped preening. The cloud streaming up from the west went on streaming, but it seemed to be passing *through* something, the way water passes through a grate.

Then it stopped at the edge of the apron and did not go down.

The first thing it understood about Hallow Col was the law, and the second was what the law meant for something like itself. *Water must be let through.* A thing as large as it was, settling its full weight onto a place built level on level, each roof carrying the next, would be the heaviest *above* the valley had ever had. It would be the hand pressed flat on the crown of the head. It would be a blocker on a scale the water courts had no ruling for.

So it made itself small, and it waited, and it listened.

## Listening

It listened for a long time. By the measure of the town it was about three winters. By its own measure it was hardly a breath, but it had learned from other places that the measure that matters is the one kept by the people who live there.

It learned the hiss of every leat. It learned how the drop under Tanner's Rise ran a quarter-tone flat, because a vault had settled in the slip of '81 and never been set true. It learned the signs. It learned the grammar the lowland linguists had missed, which lives less in the hands than in how close to the body they move, and in the angle of the shoulder turned sideways on a stair. It learned that *heavy above* means one thing when signed with a smile and another when the hand stays on the head a beat too long.

It learned the bucket counts, and which families still spoke of them.

It walked the roof-woods at walking pace with the fog, among the thin pale trunks, and it knew every one of them was a person. It did not find this sad. It found it one of the most sensible things it had seen humans do with their dead: give them work, and let the work be holding each other up.

And it went down, very slowly and very lightly, through every level, until it reached the river.

## The Let-Through

The culvert-folk were right. The river is kept.

The Let-Through is not a spirit of the water, as the upper town half-believes, and it is not a single thing. When the attention finally came to it, four levels under the valley floor, where the black water runs fast between stone and the sodium lamps of the intake hall are a smear far off, it found what it had half-expected.

The Let-Through is made of the ones in the level.

It is the shorers who went under before the props held, and the crew under the tram depot. It is the woman buried when the Laundry Stair went, whose great-niece still names it to her children. It is everyone the ropeway would not stop for and the town built over, whose bodies were never carried up to the moss. They could not be laid up, so they did the other thing a Col person does when there is nowhere to rest. They went to work. They followed the water down, as everything here does, and in the dark they kept the grates clear. They stacked stones in the culvert shallows to turn the flow off a weakened footing. They loosened the silt a warden could not reach. For two hundred years they have been letting it through.

The attention spoke to it in the only way that made sense there. It signed, slowly, in the culvert dialect it had learned, the one made for dark rooms and hands seen against a lamp: *going through?*

The answer took a long while. It came from many hands, or the idea of hands, in the moving water. It was the flat hand drawn forward and down, made twice, the second time lower.

*Going through.*

It asked what the Let-Through wanted. It asked in the plain way, because this is a plain place.

The Let-Through did not answer for a long time either. When it did, what it signed was not *rest*, and not *release*, and not *vengeance on the rule of the ropeway*. The attention had been ready for all three and would have understood any of them. What it signed was the fist turned slowly against the palm, the sign for a building starting to go, and then, after it, *in the level*: two flat hands, one over the other.

*We hear it coming,* it meant. *We hear it before they do. We can't tell them in time.*

## What it did not do

It could have done a great deal. It thought about all of it, carefully, because a wise power has to be honest with itself about temptation.

It could have held the valley walls still for a thousand years. It could have parted the cloud and given the lower town the sun. It could have drained the poison from the low water in an afternoon, stiffened every vault, and fixed the surveyors' map to the present. It could have made Hallow Col a lowland city on a mountain, bright and dry and safe.

It did not. The reason was not a love of hardship. It had seen enough places to distrust anyone who calls other people's suffering beautiful. The reason was that Hallow Col is not a problem the people are trapped inside. It is an answer they worked out. The listening, the signs, the wardens with their thigh-boots and their one permitted raised voice, the root-readers who mostly say no, the old people carrying the lost stairways in their legs: all of it is the town's own intelligence, grown into it the way birch roots grow into slate. Knock out what it pushes against and you do not free that intelligence. You make it useless. Wall a river off from its fall and it stops being a river.

So it left the rain. It left the fog, which eats sound and keeps the voices low and close. It left the slips, though that one cost it something, and it made a vow about them, which comes below. It left the map a year behind, because the surveyors' melancholy dignity is real and the town uses them to remember, and a map that was always true would be a map nobody needed to argue over.

It left the ropeway rule. It thought about that one longest of all. The rule is hard, and some of the Let-Through is made of it. But the funerals have built their centre around the waiting and the counting, and *a long wait means the town took its time with you*. That is not a thing to take from people. It decided instead to make the rule cost fewer lives, without breaking it.

## What it did

It did small things, and it did all of them in the direction the water goes: downward, through, and without its name on them.

**It lengthened the warning.** It did not stop the collapses. It gave the Let-Through what it had asked for. Now, when a vault begins to fail, the water under it changes note sooner, and more clearly, and for longer. In the dark, the ones in the level learned to lean on the flow the way you'd lean on a bell rope. The grinding creak before a slump still comes, but a few seconds earlier, and a few seconds in Hallow Col are a whole stairway emptied. Grandmothers sit up in bed at three in the morning and cock their heads, and are out of the door with a lamp and a rod, and this time they are ahead of it. The fist against the palm gets made sooner. The shorers' tokens pass to the next in less often. Nobody in the town thinks a power did it. They think the water got louder, which it did, and that the wardens got better, which they also did, because now there was more to hear.

**It changed the moss.** This was the slowest gift and the one it was proudest of. Below the fourth level, where the smelter runoff turns the water poisonous, it taught the moss to take the metals in and hold them, the way the moss already takes in water and holds it. It did not make a new plant. It coaxed an old tendency that was already there. The lower-level moss began to grow in a darker, heavier felt with a faint coppery sheen. The moss-cutters noticed first. Then a culvert-woman noticed that the ferns on her sill had stopped yellowing at the tips. It took a generation. The root-readers argued about it in the water courts, and the surveyors recorded the argument a year late. But the cut dark moss was carried up and burned, the water beneath it ran cleaner, and one spring someone on the third level got a box of leeks to grow. Then someone on the second did. It will take longer to reach the vaults, and it may never reach all the way. The attention decided that was right. The people should be the ones to carry it down.

**It let the roots go deeper.** The ones in the level had been built over, and it had grieved that they alone could not be laid up. So it asked them, all of them, what they wanted: to go on keeping the river, or to rest. Most stayed. They were Col people, and the work was theirs. Some chose to go. For those it did the only thing that fit the place. It opened a way for the roots.

A birch came up on the roof of the tram depot, on bare concrete where no birch had any right to root. Its roots went down and down, through slab and vault and three levels of fill, until they reached the crew under the floor, and gripped, and held. The root-reader who was called crawled the spaces beneath with her lamp and her probe for two days. She came out grey with dust and said nothing aloud. She signed *don't cut what's carrying you* to the depot master and walked away. Over the years more trees appeared where they shouldn't, a rowan at the top of a stair that goes nowhere, a birch on the mill roof over Nine Wells. The town stopped asking why. It began, quietly, to count them as laid up. The sign for them became the two flat hands one over the other, with the upper hand then rising slowly, fingers spread, like something growing.

**And once a year, it thinned the fog.** It did not part it, and it did not give the sun to the valley floor, which would only have hurt their eyes. On the day of a funeral with a long wait, near a hundred buckets, the fog over the lowest levels runs a little thinner for the length of the count. A culvert child standing among the mourners, looking up past the gantries, can see the buckets pass high overhead in their long dotted line all the way to where the cloud begins. She can see, for the first time, how far up the town goes, how much of it there is above her, and that all of it is holding. Then the last bucket knocks over the wheel, the fog comes back, and the procession moves on.

## What it made of itself

It did not stay forever. Staying forever would have been a kind of weight too.

But before it went, it did what everyone in Hallow Col eventually does. It became part of the level. It left a little of itself in the town's margin, the ghost of some decades that trails behind the stone in the minds of the people climbing the stairs. It did not leave its name, because there is no sign for it and the town doesn't need one. It left a habit of hearing, a slightly longer note in the water, a slightly deeper root.

The Let-Through knew it was going. On its last night, down in the dark by the intakes, many hands in the black water made the sign to it, slowly, the culvert way, twice, the second time lower.

It made the sign back.

## After

On a landing above Tanner's Rise, between shifts, a girl lies on her stomach on the wet stone with her face to the grate and one hand spread flat over the bars. Cold air and fine spray breathe up into her palm. She is listening. The note is clear. It is running clean, without silt.

Somewhere far below, in a place no lamp has ever wholly filled, something is listening back. It hears her the way you hear someone breathing beside you in the dark.

She sits up and makes the sign, the flat hand drawn forward and down over the sill, to nobody in particular.

Under her, through four levels of stone and the hundred-year moss and the roots grown through the people who hold the town up, the water goes on through.
