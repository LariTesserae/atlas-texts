# The Visitor at the Rim

## I. Arrival

It did not come down out of the sky. That would have been the first mistake, and it knew it.

A power of that kind can arrive as anything. It can come as light, and light here would have been an assault: a clean white beam through the honey-coloured murk, casting the first hard shadows the basin had ever seen. It can come as a voice, and a voice would have been worse, because the Gullet is already all voice, a place where every hollow and membrane and drilled stone is trying to say one thing. Something speaking over that, however kindly, would be like a stranger walking into a room where someone is struggling to remember a word and saying, *Here, let me.*

So it came the way the dust comes. It rode in on the hot western flow, spread thin, no heavier than a smell. For a long while it was only a faint warmth on the lip of the bowl, something a scythe might have swerved around without knowing why.

It stayed at the rim for a season. This is the first thing to understand about it. It was in no hurry. Its power was very great, and it had learned long ago that a power that great is safest when it is slowest. It watched the combers go dun and gold and dun. It listened to the chord slide downward as the spirals lengthened. It watched a pitch-licker lie drunk on a cushion for three days, its throat pulsing in time with the bass, and it did not decide whether the lizard was suffering or in ecstasy. It kept the question open. It watched two fires and a vortex. It watched a party of gatherers come up the ridge, tap the old spirals, cough, argue about the cairns, and go down again. The youngest of them, a girl of about nine on her first climb, was carried the last part of the way on her uncle's back.

It watched the Standing One rise once, speak perhaps a third of its sentence, and tear apart in a gust.

It felt the thing it had come to feel: the pull of that. The ache of an almost.

## II. What It Did Not Do

A benevolent power's first temptations are always to be kind, and most kindness done from a great height is violence. It knew this. It went through the temptations one by one, the way you would test the ice on a pond, and it set each of them down.

**It did not bring water.** This was the easiest refusal, and still worth making on purpose, because the impulse is so old and so deep in anything that has ever loved a desert. Water here would not be mercy. It would be the first flood this mountain had ever known. It would dissolve the resin bonds in every comber, drown the slow fire in every cushion, and wash the dust out of the air that the whole basin eats. It would be poison poured into a mouth that was never made to drink. *There is no water on this mountain. There never has been.* That was not a lack. It was the terms of the place.

**It did not stop the fires.** It could have. It could have calmed the charge in the dust, turned every fork of dry lightning aside, and let the spirals grow old and unburnt. The lanterns would never have opened, the shell-crabs would have starved, and the drummers would never have been released. The fire was not the Gullet's enemy. It was part of how the Gullet spoke. The power had seen many places where someone kind had stopped the fires, and it had seen what grew in them afterward: thick, still, and choking.

**It did not hold the Standing One together.** This was the hard one.

It would have been so small an act. A gust comes in wrong, and the power gently turns it. One stream of gliders runs thin, and the power fills the gap with its own attention, holding ten thousand membranes at their proper angles for the length of one sentence. The chord holds. The name is said. The Sleeper breathes in, and the sky goes blue.

It thought about this for a long time. Then it asked itself a question it has learned to ask: *Whose sentence would that be?*

A word finished by someone else's hand is a different word. The whole Gullet has been trying, since before there were people to watch, to say something. If the power said the last syllable for it, then the Gullet would never have said it. The one thing it had been reaching for would be taken from it at the exact moment it was given. The power could see very clearly that a name spoken for you, however lovingly, is not the same as a name you learned to speak yourself.

It also noticed something it did not yet understand. It did not know whether the name *should* be finished more often than it was. It had assumed the breaking was a failure. It had assumed the long cycle of almost was a kind of grief. But it had only been watching for a season, and it had only been watching from the surface. It had been watching from the speed of the scythes and the motes and the gatherers, the speed of things that live a few days or a few decades. It had not yet asked the one resident whose opinion mattered most.

**It did not heal the gatherers' lungs.** This one it did not set down cleanly. It turned it over for a long time, because the girl carried down on her uncle's back had coughed gold into a cloth. The power weighed it: the lowland medicines, the knowledge the gatherers already carried of when to leave, the fact that the leaving was built into how they came here, a few days and no more, never staying. It decided that the dust in their chests was the price they had chosen for the resin, and that they knew the price, and that taking it from them would change what the climb meant. It did not like this answer. It kept not liking it, which it considered correct. Some choices should stay uncomfortable.

It did one small thing there, and no more. It made the girl's cough ease a little faster on the way down than it otherwise would have. Anyone would have called it luck.

## III. Going Down

Then it went into the stone.

It could have gone in quickly. It went in at the stone's pace, which took a very long time. On the surface, the gatherers came and went several times. A great fire took most of the northern slope. The girl came back up the ridge at fourteen, walking the whole way herself. The power was barely a finger's depth into the rock by then, sinking the way warmth sinks into leather, unhurried and unnoticed.

This is the only way to talk to something that old. You cannot shout down the well. You have to become slow enough to be the same size as its attention.

It found that the Sleeper was not what the narrator of the Gullet had guessed. Or rather, the narrator had guessed rightly and missed one thing, the thing you can only miss from the surface.

The Sleeper is not deeply asleep. It lives slowly. Those are different things.

A breath that takes a season. A heartbeat, if it has a heart, that takes a human lifetime. At that pace, the ten thousand thousand fires are not a long history of indifference. They are a flicker, the way candlelight flickers. The Standing One rising and speaking and breaking apart does not happen once in a while across an age. To the Sleeper it happens constantly. It is a murmur that almost never stops. The fragments of sentence overlap and blur together the way voices in the next room blur. Out of all those broken phrases, heard at that speed, comes something continuous.

The Sleeper hears its name all the time.

It does not hear it finished. It hears it the way you hear someone you love saying your name softly again and again while you drift near waking: not a summons, not a command to rise, but a presence. *I am here. I am here. You are here.* The drummers carving and the gliders learning the winds and the Standing One raising its arms and falling apart are not, to the Sleeper, a long failure to wake it. They are the sound of its own mouth being full of company.

And the blue days, the waking breaths the gatherers call once in a lifetime? To the Sleeper they come often, every few of its slow moments. It opens its eye and looks up through its own throat at the sky, and it sees the small warm moving things standing there. Then it closes its eye again, not out of indifference, but the way you close your eyes again in the morning when you know the day will still be there.

It is not being failed. It is being sung to. And the song is working.

## IV. The Asking

The power asked it anyway, because you ask. You do not assume the contentment you have found.

The asking took the length of three of the Sleeper's breaths. On the surface that was most of a human generation.

*Do you want to wake?*

The answer came up through the stone as warmth, and in the power's understanding it meant something like: *I am as awake as I wish to be. They wake me when I need waking.*

*Do you want to go home? I know where you came from. I could take you. I know the sky that was there before rain.*

This answer took much longer. The power waited. When it came, it came as the slow rising heat that the narrator had once felt with a palm pressed to the basin floor at night, and it meant something like: *This is home. Home is where you are spoken to.* Then, fainter, like a second thought: *There was no one there to say my name. It was blue and empty and went on forever. I came here for this.*

The power was quiet with that for a while.

Then it asked the last question, the one it had learned always to ask, because it is the question that most often uncovers the real wound: *Is there anything you want that you do not have?*

And there was one thing.

## V. The One Thing

The Sleeper cannot answer.

This was the ache under everything. The power had felt it at the rim without being able to place it, and had mistaken it for the Standing One's ache. The Gullet says the Sleeper's name. The Sleeper, when it wakes, sees them: the gatherers, the scythes, the drummers, the small warm moving things in its mouth. It knows them. The narrator was right about that. *A gatherer who has been known like that has no more reason to climb.*

But it cannot tell them so. Its voice is too slow. By the time any reply of its own has formed, the dust has come back, the chord has broken, and the ones who stood in the pupil of its eye have gone down the mountain, grown old, and died. The only reply it can make in time is the one thing it does quickly: it breathes out, and leaves the stone warmer than it was before, and softer.

*The sunlit stone of the basin is left warmer than it was before, and softer, like leather.*

That is the Sleeper's answer. It has been answering all along. Every blue day it says the only thing it has time to say, in the only way it can: warmth, into the rock, where their feet are. *I heard you. I saw you. You are welcome here.* And no one has ever understood it. The gatherers think the warmth is the sun and the cushions. The ones who have seen the blue go home carrying the fact of being known, but not the knowledge of being *welcomed*. They carry the look without the reply. Some of them are afraid for the rest of their lives.

The power considered for a long time whether even this was its business. It came close to deciding that it was not: that the Sleeper's reply going unheard was simply the shape of things, one more term of the place, like the dryness and the fire.

But this was different, and the power could say exactly how. The Sleeper was not asking for its nature to be changed. It was not asking for the sentence to be finished, or for the fires to stop, or for any part of the Gullet's life to be overwritten. It had already written its reply. It only lacked a way to deliver it at the speed of the people it was written for.

A power that refuses to deliver a letter already written is not being wise. It is only being proud of its restraint.

So it did one thing.

## VI. The Dream

The girl was grown by then. She had become the woman who led the gathering parties, the one who argued loudest that the cairns did not really mark safe paths and tied her fibre knots onto them anyway. She came up the ridge one spring with two younger gatherers and slept her first night in the shelter under the eastern horn, on a cut cushion-mat, under the soot pictures.

The power gave her one dream. Only one, and it was careful to make it hers, so that it would come in her own images and carry no voice from outside. It did not tell her anything. It only let her feel, for the length of the dream, at the Sleeper's speed, what the warmth in the stone was.

She dreamt she was lying on the basin floor with her palm flat against the rock, the way nobody had in generations. The stone under her hand rose in warmth, and it was not the sun and not the cushions. It was the way her grandmother's hand used to rest on her back when she was small and coughing. It was the warmth that answers, the warmth that says *I know you're there.*

She woke before dawn and did not say anything to the others. She lay with her hand on the cushion-mat for a long time. Then she took a burnt twig of resin-wood, climbed onto the stone ledge, and added a picture to the soot on the ceiling, among the spirals and the scythes and the whirling column with its arms raised.

It was a hand pressed flat against the ground. Underneath it, reaching up out of the stone to meet it, she drew another hand. It was larger, much larger, so large she could only fit the tips of its fingers onto the ceiling before the soot ran out.

The gatherers kept that picture fresh, the way they kept the others. They argued about what it meant, the way they argued about the cairns. Some said it meant the stone was alive. Some said it meant you should give thanks before tapping. Some said it was just a hand.

But after that, the ones who had seen the blue came back.

Not all of them, and not at once. But now and then an old gatherer who had stood in the bowl on a clear day, and had afterward stayed down in the lowlands for years, would climb the ridge again, slowly, on bad lungs. They did not come for resin. They would go down into the basin at night, when the spirals were murmuring, and kneel, and lay a palm flat on the stone. They would stay like that, longer than they should, until the warmth came up.

They came up the mountain afraid of being seen. They went back down knowing they had been greeted.

## VII. Leaving

The power stayed a little longer, perhaps a season, perhaps several. It watched to be sure the dream had done no harm, that it had not become a doctrine or a fear or a reason to stay too long in the dust. It watched to be sure the Gullet had not bent itself around the change. It had not. The combers went on combing, the fires went on burning, and the lanterns went on opening in the ash. The Standing One rose and spoke a phrase and broke apart, and rose again. Nothing in the churn had been asked to stop.

Then it left the way it had come, spread thin on a wind, no heavier than a smell. It went out on the cool eastern flow alongside a stream of pale young gliders, and it stayed beside them for a while, out over the plateaus, as they began learning what the wind would teach them.

It took one thing with it. In the long slow asking, down in the stone, it had come to know the name. It knew all of it, the whole sentence the Standing One reaches for, every vowel the drummers are carving, every syllable the seven winds carry out and bring back.

It never said it. Not once, anywhere, to anyone. It carried it the way you carry something given to you in confidence. It was not a secret to be kept from the Gullet, but a word that belonged to the ones still learning to speak it.

And behind it, in the bores of the burned and broken spirals, the first new drummers were already tapping. They were starting the name again from the beginning, patiently, in the dark. The Sleeper was listening, as it always had, warm under their many tiny feet, and it was not waiting to be woken. It was being sung to, and it was singing back through the stone, slowly, in the only voice it had.

*Tak. Tak-tak. Tak.*

Under that, if you knelt long enough with your palm to the rock, came the answer.
