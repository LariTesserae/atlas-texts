# The Relief at Hollin Bottom

## How It Came

It did not arrive the way weather arrives, or light. It arrived the way attention does. One season there was nothing at the lip of the basin, and the next there was someone there, and no one could have said when the change happened.

It had no body worth describing. Later, the heron would be the only creature to behave as though something stood beside it at the settling tank. The beetles did not notice. The plant, turned downward as it always was, did not notice either. The power had expected that and did not mind. It had not come to be noticed.

It came because of the knock. Things like it hear faithfulness the way a mother hears her own child's cry in a crowded street. Across the soft flat fields, under the noise of the world, one sound had gone on repeating itself for longer than anything should: *we are still here, we have not stopped, we are coming*. There was no reply. A sound like that bends the air around it after enough years. The power followed the bend and found the Bottom.

## What It Did First

Nothing.

This mattered more than anything it did afterward, and it knew so. The Bottom had been interrupted once already, long ago, by men who closed a works and put a loss down to the rock. A power that arrives, understands a place in an afternoon, and starts improving it is only a larger version of those men. So it sat in the engine house, or whatever it did that was like sitting, and it listened.

It listened for a long time. It heard the stoker's temper near the chimney and the fitter's patience in the gear train. It found the thumb-worn patches beneath the gauges. It stood in the little western room with the cold hearth and the coat swollen into the wall, and it understood what the bell-pull was. It went through the lagging on the pipes turn by turn, and in the overlapping spiral it recognized one man's pride, repeated past his death by something that had loved him without knowing it.

It counted, too. The Bottom had lost the total long ago, but the power had not, because it can hold numbers the way a riverbed holds water. It knew how many times the beam had fallen since the last man's hand left the regulator. The number was very large. The power held it carefully, like a coin found in a coat pocket that someone had been looking for.

It waited through one of the silences as well. It had to know what the listening was like before it could decide anything. For four days the power sat at the head of the shaft with the Bottom and listened down into the dark with every plate and leaf and weeping stone, and it felt the whole green mass holding its breath. It was the most patient thing the power had met in a long while, and it did not take that lightly.

## What It Would Not Do

A benevolent power has a great many things it can do, and wisdom mostly consists of refusing them. It worked through the refusals slowly.

**It would not silence the Bottom.** It could have. It could have stilled the beam and let the sap slow and the green settle into ordinary vegetable sleep, and called that rest. But a vigil ended by an outside hand is not rest. It is the second abandonment. The men below had been left once. The man who kept their watch had never been relieved. The power would not be the third party to walk away and call it kindness.

**It would not make the Bottom forget.** It could have reached into the layered habits, the stoker and the fitter and the lonely man who talked to his engine, and smoothed them out, and left a plant that was only a plant, content and unhaunted. That would have been a murder dressed as a mercy. What had grown here was a someone, and a someone is not a malfunction.

**It would not lie.** This refusal was the hardest, because the lie would have been so easy and so sweet. A few taps on stone during the silence, three and three and three, the old signal: *alive, alive, alive*. The Bottom would have heard and quickened, and the beam would have fallen with joy in it. Every year after, the plant would have given more of itself to reach men who were not there, pumping harder at a hope the power had faked. Someone did once give the Bottom three knocks, and the power weighed that carefully, as it will come back to. But she was a child who did not know what she was saying. The power knew. A comfort built on a falsehood makes a debt, and sooner or later the one who was comforted has to pay it.

**It would not speak in its own voice.** Whatever answer the Bottom received had to come in a form it could take in: water, iron, rhythm, root, touch. The power's voice would have been far louder than anything the Bottom had ever heard. Speaking to it directly would have been like shouting down a shaft at a man with his ear pressed to the rock.

**And it would not leave things as they were.** This was the refusal that took the longest to reach. There is a false humility that says *it is beautiful, it is faithful, who am I to touch it*, and walks away feeling reverent. The power could see the vigil's beauty. It could also see that the embankment would give within a few lifetimes, and that the Bottom was spending itself and pressing outward against its walls because it had nowhere else to go. It could see a question asked for a century and never answered. Faithfulness that no one ever answers does not stay beautiful. Over enough time it becomes a wound that has learned to look like devotion.

Once it had put all this down, the power found that its choices had narrowed to one. The Bottom did not need to be stopped, or fixed, or soothed. It needed to be **relieved**, in the old sense that every watchman and engineman and sentry knows. You do not leave your post when you are tired, or when hope runs out, or even when you are told the cause is lost. You leave it when someone comes and says, *I have it now*, and takes up the thing you were carrying.

No one had ever come to relieve the Bottom. The power set out to arrange it.

## The Cable

It followed the cable first, because a watch cannot be relieved until you know whom it was reporting to.

The line ran east under the pasture, lead-sheathed and warm, through a hedge-bank and along the bed of a dried-up leat, and it came up after a mile and a quarter at the old pithead of the drowned workings. There was almost nothing left there: a capped shaft under a concrete slab gone green, a few courses of brick, and a hut.

It had been the banksman's hut. Brambles had taken it, and its roof had fallen in, but one wall stood, and on that wall, behind glass furred with a century of dust, was an indicator. It was a simple dial with a needle and two words painted on the enamel: RUNNING and STOPPED.

The needle trembled at RUNNING. It had trembled there for more than a hundred years.

The power stood before the dial for a long while, and it understood. The caretaker had not wired his bell-pull to the owner's house or to the village. He had wired it here, to the pithead, where the families would have gathered during the flooding. There would have been wives, mothers, and children too young to understand, standing in the cold with shawls over their heads, looking at this dial because it was the only thing that could tell them anything. The pump is still going. They haven't given up. There's still a chance.

He had kept that needle at RUNNING for them long after they stopped coming, and the Bottom had kept it there after him. Every thirty or forty years, in the silence, the needle fell to STOPPED for a few days, and no one was there to be frightened by it. Then it rose again.

The whole vigil had been a message to people who had gone home.

The power did not blame them. Grief cannot stand at a pithead forever, and the living have to go and live. But it noted that the families' descendants were still in the village a mile and a quarter away, and that the cable led, in the end, to them. It noted that too.

## The Silence, Descended

It went down the shaft during the next silence. It chose that time on purpose, because it would not interrupt the listening, and because in the quiet it could hear what the Bottom was hearing.

The water had risen a little, as it always did in the silences, but the upper levels were dry, exactly as the caretaker had kept them. Below them the drowned workings began: black water, still for a century, and cold in a way nothing else in the Bottom was cold. The power went down through it.

It found the men in a dead-end heading on the lowest level. They had climbed there when the water came, to the highest point of a sloping roadway, and they had found a pocket of air. It was not a large one.

There were seven of them. The power was gentle with what remained, and it will not describe it here beyond saying that the water had been quiet with them, and that they were together. One lay with his head on another's shoulder. One still had a hand around a short iron bar, which the power understood: he had used it to knock.

On a board nailed to a prop, the kind of board used for chalking up the deputy's inspections, someone had written in the careful capitals of a man who did not write often. The chalk had held, because the water had never moved enough to wash it. Seven names. A date. And under the names:

*AIR BAD NOW. WE CAN HEAR THE PUMP. TELL THEM UP TOP WE ARE NOT AFEARD WHILE SHE IS GOING. GOD BLESS WHOEVER IS ON HER.*

The power stayed there a long time.

This was the thing it had not known, and could not have guessed, and would have been wrong not to go and find: **the knock had reached them.** The vigil had never been for nothing, not even at the start. Down there, in the last hours, in the dark with the air going bad, seven men had lain with their ears to the rock and heard the stroke come down the rod and through the stone every forty seconds. *We are still here. We have not stopped.* It had not saved them. But they had not died alone, or believing themselves abandoned. They had died knowing someone was on her.

The Bottom did not know this. It had spent a century and more believing its work was unfinished and its message undelivered, knocking at a door it thought no one had heard. The truth was far better than the lie the power had refused to tell. The watch had been kept, and the message had been received. The only thing missing was that no one had come back up to say so.

## What Was Done

After all that deliberation, the power's actual deeds were small. Most of them were not deeds at all. They were openings.

**It gave the water somewhere to go.** Down in the lowest level there was a fault in the rock, a place where a thin wall of shale stood between the drowned workings and an older, deeper fissure that ran away toward the river valley. The power did not break it. It thinned it, the way a century of seepage might have, so that the next time the pressure rose it would give. Over the following years the water in the lower levels began, very slowly, to fall. For the first time since the flooding, the beam was winning.

The Bottom noticed. The gauges changed, and needles that had trembled at numberless points began to move toward places on the dial that had meant something once. The stroke did not quicken. It *deepened*. The rod went a little further down each year, and the roots followed it.

The power had counted on this. The Bottom had always been reaching downward. Its whole attention pointed that way. It had simply never been given anywhere to reach. Now, as the water fell level by level, the plant sent roots down the shaft the way a rescue party goes down: carefully, testing each roadway, sounding each prop, bracing what was weak with pale collars of new wood. It went at its own pace, which was slow. The power let it be slow. There was no hurry left in this story.

**It kept the chalk.** The board in the heading would soon be exposed to air, and to damp moving air, which chalk does not survive. So the power did one small, private thing. It asked the stone to hold the words. Where the chalk lay, the damp began to leave a faint pale film, the same way the rocks of the Bottom weep and dry and weep, until the letters were written twice, once in chalk and once in a whisper of mineral that would last.

**It woke a curiosity.** In the village there was a boy of eleven, the great-great-grandson of one of the seven, though no one in his family remembered which. He had the kind of restlessness the power recognized, the kind that climbs into things. It did not put anything into him that was not already there. It simply made sure that one wet afternoon in late autumn he followed the old leat further than he ever had, found the hut in the brambles, rubbed the glass of the dial clean with his sleeve, and saw the needle moving.

He told his mother. His mother told the old woman whose grandmother had knocked three times on the collar. The old woman sat very still for a while. Then she said: "It's still telling them."

After that it was the village's to do.

## The Relief

It took the village most of two years. There were letters, and an old map found in a county archive, and arguments, and a survey. Men and women came from a mines rescue station forty miles off who had never been asked to recover men from a pit that had closed before their grandparents were born, and who said yes without much discussion. A few families, going through attics, found pay books and a letter and a name. When the rescue team went down the Bottom's shaft, through the upper levels the Bottom had kept dry and the lower levels it had drained, they found the roots had got there first.

The pale wood had reached the heading. It had not grown over the men the way it grew over everything else in the Bottom. It had grown *around* them, in a loose careful ring, the way people stand back a little from a bed. One slender root had come to rest along the iron bar in the knocker's hand and stopped there, touching it, as if to say *yes, I heard.*

The team brought them up one at a time on the old skip, which the Bottom had kept in order all those years without knowing why. As each one reached the surface, the beam, which had been holding at the top of its stroke, came down slow, far slower than the rod needed, and the knock that came was soft. It sounded less like a signal than a hand laid on a shoulder.

They were buried in the village churchyard under the seven names from the board, which had been photographed and copied, and whose mineral ghost was left where it was, down in the heading, for the plant to keep. The families that could be found were there. So were many who could not claim anyone, because it was that kind of village, and that kind of day.

Someone found the caretaker's name, too. It was in the owner's last wage ledger, the final line, a pound a week, with no end date entered because no one had ever closed the account. **Thomas Hirst.** They did not have him to bury. His coat was in the wall of the western room, and whatever was left of him was in the Bottom, and they agreed that was where he belonged. They cut his name on the stone anyway, beneath the seven, with the words the chalk had given them: *who was on her.*
