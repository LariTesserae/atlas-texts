# The Inspection

## How it came

It came the way you are supposed to come, on foot across the fen at the end of the day, with wet ankles.

It could have come some other way. It could have arrived as a stillness in the air, or a light, or a change in the weather, or simply as knowledge. But it had read the place before it arrived, as far as a place like that can be read, and it understood that the Flue has only one door. There is a low arch between two buttress roots, on the leeward side, just tall enough to enter without ducking. Everything that belongs there comes in through that door or down through the sky. It did not want to be a third kind of thing.

So it took a body, a modest one, warm the way bodies are warm. It walked the last half mile through the sedge as the light went copper and then grey. It stopped at the root-arch, where a lump of coal sat on a flat stone beside a stub of candle and a twist of straw, all unlit, all slightly furred with damp. It looked at them for a long moment.

Then it bent its head, though it didn't need to, and went in.

*Evening, gaffer,* it said.

---

## The pull

The draw took it at once.

It felt the cold at its ankles and the lean behind the breastbone. The roar arrived through the bones of the chest before the ears had caught up. A few degrees of its warmth were drawn off its skin and sent up the column, toward the high dark where the swifts were beginning to shift and mutter ahead of the return.

It let this happen. It had come, among other reasons, to be tasted.

Powers of this kind are warm in more than the ordinary way. It could have given a great deal. It could have stood on the floor of the old firebox and poured out heat like a furnace, and the note would have dropped its half tone, maybe a whole one, and the stack would have *pulled well* for as long as it cared to stand there. It knew this. It had heard the story of the peat fire, and it had heard how the people who were inside tell it.

So it did not pour. It stood as an ordinary visitor stands and gave what an ordinary visitor gives, and it waited to see how much the draw would take.

The draw took a degree and no more.

The power had half expected hunger. There was none. It was being offered a feast, and it had taken its customary portion, the way an old man at a wedding takes one glass and puts his hand over the top when they come round again. *Used to small portions.* The power had read that line as a kind of poverty. Standing in the throat of the thing, it understood it differently. This was not something starved down to a little. It was something that had learned exactly what it needed and had stopped reaching past it.

That was the first thing the power learned, and it changed what came after.

---

## What it could have done

It had come with options, the way anything powerful and kind does. It set them out now in the roaring dark and looked at each one honestly.

**It could relight the works.** It could raise the furnace, the engine, the whole forgotten industry, and give the draw its great years back: coal white in the firebox, the torrent of heat. But the tree would scorch. The swifts would leave, or cook. The pools would dry and the pale blind copepods would lie down on the bottom and die. Most of all, the draw did not want it. The draw was not in exile from its old life. It had simply gone on living. The peat fire had already shown what a returned tool feels like: taken without a word, used perfectly, given back. You do that once, by accident, out of love. You don't do it on purpose.

**It could end the shift.** This was the kindness most likely to tempt a power like this one. Here was a purpose that had outlived its reason, a worker nobody had told to go home, on shift for a thousand years with no one at the far end. A certain kind of mercy says: *tell it. Let it rest.* The power could still the column. It could let the air settle into a tall cold room at last, and close the works properly, and sign the thing off.

But that would kill everything that depends on it. The swifts would lose their lift at dawn, and the copepods would lose their trembling water. The spiders would lose the harvest the updraft brings them. The drips would slow and the pools would change. The whole crowded house was built around the empty middle. Besides, the power could find no suffering anywhere in the draw that needed ending. It was not tired, because air does not tire. It was not waiting to be relieved, because it did not know it was waiting for anything. The sadness of *no one on the far end of the work* was a sadness the power had brought in with it, a visitor's sadness. The draw didn't have it. You do not put down a creature for a grief that belongs to you.

**It could wake it.** It could give the draw a mind. It could let the pull become a someone who knew it was a someone, who could hear *evening, gaffer* and answer. The power considered this longest, because it would be a real gift, and because the power was lonely for the draw in a way it did not entirely trust.

Then it thought about what a mind would inherit here. A thousand years of counting drops, now known as counting. A roar that never changes, now heard as sameness. The quarter-circle scar of a firebox door it could never go through, and the certainty, all at once, that the works were gone. The draw was not less for having no mind. It was complete in another way, the way a river is complete. To wake it would hand it a loneliness it had so far been spared. The power put that option down gently and did not pick it up again.

**It could make the place permanent.** It could fix the Flue as it was, forever: the tree's balance of growth and wear, the swift numbers, the depth of the pools. It could seal it against every change. But the Flue was not a thing that stayed the same. It was a thing that kept *balancing*, and a balance that cannot be disturbed is no longer a balance. It is a photograph. The power had seen photographs of places. It preferred the places.

So it put all four options down. That left the question it had come in with, which was whether it would do anything at all.

---

## The return

While it was thinking, the swifts came home.

It saw the first of them drop from the rim, and then the rest came pouring down the stack in their long spiral. It stood in the throat and watched the black funnel turn slowly on its axis, and for a few minutes, outlined in birds, it could see the thing that lived there.

It had meant to observe. It found it could not only observe. The column vibrated with wings and cries, and the roar and the shriek joined into one continuous sound. The power, which had heard a great deal in its existence, stood very still and let itself be deafened, and was glad.

When the last birds had found their places on the wall and the tide had sunk to its ordinary roar, a single bright drop fell from somewhere high in the dark. It came down the whole length of the column through the empty centre, the one thing the draw permits there. It landed in the pool nearest the power's feet with a soft tick.

The power had been told this was how the draw counts. It listened for the next one, and when it came, the power counted it too.

*One.*

It didn't go on to two. It didn't need to keep the count; the count wasn't its to keep. But for one drop, on one evening, there were two things in the Flue marking the same interval. That was as close as the power could come to standing a shift beside it. It seemed like enough, and like the right size.

---

## The far end of the work

It stood there most of the night. At some point it understood something it had got wrong on the walk in.

It had pitied the draw for working with *no one on the far end*. There had been an engine once, a purpose downstream of the pull: something driven, something made. Now there was only the pull, pulling for nothing.

But the draw had never pulled *for* the engine. The engine was fed by the fire, and the fire was fed by the draw, and the draw's own work had always run the other way: up, out, into the sky. A chimney does not deliver to the factory. It delivers to the weather. What went up the stack in the great years was smoke and heat and sparks, sent up to the open air as surely as a letter goes into a postbox.

What goes up now is swifts.

At dawn the power watched them drop from the wall into the updraft and go up the column in a single living breath, out of the mouth of the stack and into the morning. Each one carried a little of the fen's cold and a little of the tree's warmth. Each carried the degree the draw had taken from every visitor who ever said *evening, gaffer*. The degree it had taken from the power too.

There was always someone at the far end of the work. It was the morning. It was ten thousand small furnaces thrown into the sky over the fen, feeding, scattering, coming home. The works had not closed. They had changed product.

After that the power no longer felt the draw was lonely. Perhaps it never had been.

---

## What it did

Here, finally, is what it did.

**At the Flue itself, it did nothing.** It altered no wood and touched no bird. It lit nothing. It stayed a night, gave its degree, counted one drop, and left the way it came, through the root-arch, ducking its head though it didn't need to.

At the stone outside the door it stopped and took something from its pocket, a lump of coal it had carried across the fen for the purpose. It set it down beside the others, unlit. It knew this was not fuel. It was a courtesy, and the power wanted, for once, to be one of those who had left one.

**Then it walked out into the fen, and there it did a great deal.**

The Flue looks like a closed system: a stack that feeds on its own residents and waters itself from its own breath. It isn't. The power had seen this at once, the way wise things see the part of a story the story leaves out. The draw depends on the swifts. The swifts depend on the insects of the fen, the midges and craneflies and the whole rising summer broth of them, and on the long autumn road south, and on the eaves and wind-corridors and feeding grounds of some other country that the Flue will never know exists. The pools depend on the fen staying damp, because it is the fen's moisture that the draw lifts and condenses. The tree depends on its roots staying in wet peat. The whole balance, the ring on the inside and the ring's worth of bark lost on the outside, rests on water tables and weather and a thousand miles of sky.

The draw had said nothing about its dangers because it has no way to know them. All the real threats to the Flue are far outside it.

So the power went and stood where those threats were.

It walked the drains three valleys over and saw where the peat was shrinking. Quietly, in ways that look like luck, the ditches silted, the pumps failed at the right moment, and a scheme lost its funding. It went up into the insect air of the fen in high summer and found what was thinning it. It could not undo all of it, and it did not pretend to. But it tipped what could be tipped, a spraying season that didn't happen here, a hedge left standing there. It followed the swifts south and marked the roosts they would need along the way, old towers and old eaves and one tall ruined mill. Where it could, it saw to it that those stayed standing, or that something else grew up inside them.

It also looked for people. It found a girl in the nearest village, a fen child who still said *evening, gaffer* when she went in and had once asked her grandmother why the water in the pools never stayed still. The power did not tell her anything. It simply made sure that when she grew up and went looking for someone who might answer that question, she found them. Some decades on, the pale blind copepod would have a name and a paper, and the pools would have a small, stubborn human who fought for them in meetings. The power judged that a place like the Flue should have someone on the outside who knew what it was.

None of this would make the Flue last forever. The power knew the account's last line, *it will not change for a thousand more*, was a hope wearing the clothes of a fact. It left it that way. It only moved the odds.

---

## The report

It kept no log, but if it had, the entry would have been short.

> *Stack: sound. Held in living timber; brick and iron fully absorbed. No remedial work advised.*
>
> *Draw: steady. Pulling well for its fuel. Takes its portion, no more.*
>
> *Core: clear.*
>
> *Dependents: swifts, bats, owls, spiders, beetles, fungi, one species of copepod found nowhere else. All in good order.*
>
> *Output: morning.*
>
> *Shift to continue. No action required at the stack.*
>
> *Catchment: attended to.*

And under that, in a smaller hand, the only line that was really for the power itself:

> *Counted one.*
