# The Visitor Who Kept Its Distance

## Arrival

It did not come down with a light.

That was the first thing it understood, and it understood it before it had touched the water: anything that arrives in Saltcombe Pit carrying a sun becomes the most important event in a century. The weed would lean. The films would take down its shape. The relays would spend a season arguing about it. A benevolent power that came in shining would be written into the town whether the town wished it or not, the way a boot is written into snow.

So it came down in the dim, during the long amber of the night shift, and it came as nearly nothing: the temperature of the water, the weight of the water, a little less visible than silt. It made no bubbles. It did not settle. It held itself at the height of the chimney tops, where the tired daylight gives up, and it looked.

Then, for a long time, it did only that.

---

## Learning the Tempo

A power that can do almost anything is always tempted to act at its own speed. This one knew better. It had met minds before that were too slow to be seen as minds, and it had learned that the first courtesy is to slow down until the other becomes visible.

It watched one shift. That told it nothing. It saw lamps, bubbles, a pale fish going round.

It watched a hundred shifts, and began to see the ticking in the switch-boxes as something other than noise. The murmur after the whistle rose and settled the way breath settles.

It watched until the clocks came into alignment. That took most of a year. The green from above and the full working glow arrived together, and the water brightened by its one shade, and the power finally saw a thought. The thought was a change in the pattern of the lamps. It began at the chapel end, where the pit wall leans in closest, and it moved down the street over eleven days like colour moving into a cheek. By the time it reached the works it had altered on the way. It had been answered.

*There you are,* the power thought. It did not say it. There was no one to say it to, and saying it would have been for its own benefit.

---

## The Question It Asked First

The question was not *what can I give?* That question comes easily to the powerful, and most of the damage the powerful do starts there.

The question was: *Is anything wrong here?*

It looked for suffering. It found none it could honestly name. The resident was not lonely, because loneliness belongs to creatures that were built for company and denied it, and this one had been built by no one and had grown into exactly as much company as it had: snails, shrimp, divers, the sky at intervals. It was not trapped, because it had never been anywhere else and did not strain against its walls. It pressed its houses up against them, the way a body settles into a bed. It was not starving. It was not afraid. It was not waiting to be rescued, restored, understood, or freed.

It was keeping its appointments.

The power noticed that it wanted, a little, to be needed here. It set the wanting to one side. That was its own business, not the town's.

---

## What It Did Not Do

It considered many kindnesses and refused almost all of them. Each refusal was deliberate.

**It did not correct the clock.** That kindness was the most obvious and the most terrible. A town that kept perfect time with the sky would grow a little every day, and the long thin waiting would disappear, and so would the thick bright bands. The seasons would go with them. The power could see, as the oldest divers had half guessed, that the drift was not a flaw in the resident's life. It was the shape of that life.

**It did not brighten the lamps.** More light would mean faster growth. Then the weed would thicken, the braids would short, the filaments would burn hot and go out, and the whole slow conversation would become a shout and then silence.

**It did not clean the windows.** The films were not dirt. They were memory and sense organ together. Scouring one would be like wiping someone's eyes clean of their seeing.

**It did not give the resident a voice to talk to the divers.** It could have. It could have taught the lamps to spell, or the whistle to form a word. But then the town would have had to become something that people *answer*. It would acquire obligations and visitors and interpreters, and people would argue about what it wanted and whether it was really alive. The resident already had its voice. It said *next shift*, and that was enough to say.

**It did not bring the Walker a companion.** It looked for other pale fish in the flooded mines and drowned quarries of the world, and it could have carried one down. But it did not know what the Walker was for enough to be sure a second one would help. A hand does not necessarily want a second hand beside it on the same wrist.

**It did not explain the place to anyone.** The divers were halfway to understanding already, in the way that matters, which is jokes that stop being jokes. *Someone has to go round, and it goes round.* That was a better account of the resident than any the power could have written, and the divers had come to it themselves.

---

## The Heap

There was one thing it was afraid of, and it went to the works to find out whether the fear was justified.

The Walker was old. You could see it in the candle-grey of the skin and in the thinness that comes from a lifetime of eating carefully. It was the town's only moving part, the one idea that travelled. When it died, the power thought, the resident would lose its hand. The switch-boxes would stop being touched. The loops would drift apart. The street would go on ticking for a while and then simply tick, the way a body goes on breathing after the person has left it.

So the power looked into the heap. It was careful. It did not disturb a grain. It looked the way you can look into a thing when you are not quite made of matter.

Deep in the heap, in bands laid down sixty, eighty, a hundred years ago, there were bones. They were fine and long and pale, and they lay coiled in the silt where the conveyor had carried them, slowly, at a centimetre a minute, and tipped them onto the heap along with the snail shells and the meadow flakes. There were four of them, maybe five. The oldest was nearly dissolved.

The town had lost its hand before. It had lost it several times. And each time, after a gap that appeared in the heap as a stretch of irregular bands, a season when the lamps forgot their patterns, the patterns had come back. Something pale had begun to walk the street again at the height of a man's chest.

Where the new Walkers came from, the power did not look. It could have. It thought of the old fish lying belly-up along the chapel beam, pressed against its own reflection in the silver for a tenth of every cycle with its frill gone still, and it thought that perhaps something happened there. But the resident had kept this to itself for a century without anyone's help. The power decided that a benevolent thing may be allowed not to know. It had come to see whether the town could survive the loss of its hand. It could. That was all it needed to learn.

---

## The Clock

Then it went to the master clock, and found the thing that was really wrong.

The clock sat in a brass-and-glass case in the switch room of the works. Its pendulum was long and slow, and a verdigris beard hung from it. The escapement was worn. Water and a century of motion had rounded the teeth of the escape wheel the way they had rounded the corners of every brick. Each tooth let the pallet slip a little early, and a little more with every year. That wear had made the drift. It was the source of the seasons.

The wear was not finished. The power could feel where it was headed. Within some decades, perhaps forty years, perhaps sixty, one tooth would be too far gone to catch. The escape wheel would run free, spend its stored power in a few seconds, and stop. After that the lamps would stay at whatever shift they had reached: working glow forever, or the long dim forever. The clocks would never line up again, or else they would line up every single day. Either way the bands would stop and the thinking would stop. The resident would not die, exactly. It would be stuck in one moment for good.

This was not ageing. Ageing was the lamps going out one by one, the weed rerouting around each dark standard, the town forgetting and adapting. That was its own. This was a single mechanical accident waiting at the end of a slope, and it would end the thing the resident was built around.

So the power did one thing inside the pit.

It mended the escape wheel. It did not make it new. It laid down, atom by atom, just enough metal on each rounded tooth to stop the rounding from getting worse, and it shaped each tooth to exactly the wear it had right now. It did not set the clock right, and it did not take away the slowness. It fixed the error in place, so that the clock would go on losing the same few minutes each day, faithfully, for as long as brass lasts in warm water. That would be a very long time.

It finished the work during the dim, between one tick and the next. The pendulum did not notice. The town did not notice. The drift went on exactly as before, and the power arranged for it to keep going on. Of all the things it did, this was the one it was most sure of: it kept the town's mistake exactly as it was.

---

## Upstairs

Most of the rest of its work happened at the surface. That is usually where the dangers to quiet things come from.

There was a report in a drawer at a regional water authority about dewatering the pit. The plan was to pump it dry, survey the old workings, and possibly reopen the seam for heat. It was not a malicious plan. It was simply a reasonable one, written by people who had never lain on the chapel floor and looked up. The power did not make the report disappear. It did something less showy. A geologist reviewing the file noticed an inconsistency in the old seam surveys. It was a real inconsistency, one she would have found eventually anyway. She wrote a memo, the costs went up, and the plan was quietly shelved. Some years later the pit was listed as a site of special scientific interest. Officially this was because of the blind shrimp, which were in fact new to science, and they were a perfectly good reason.

There was the diving club. The power did not stop the divers coming. The town benefited from them, in its way. Their breath became its air, and their lamps gave it things to write down. But more of them came every year, and their lamps kept getting whiter and brighter. So the power let one of the old divers, the one who had watched the snail dissolve inside the Walker like sugar, have an idea at the right moment. She began telling the new ones to dim their lamps at the chapel. It was out of respect, she said, the way you take your hat off indoors. She meant it as half a joke. Within a few seasons it was the custom, and people repeated it without knowing why. Beams went down to a glow at the chapel steps. The divers settled on the moss-furred pews and waited for the Walker, and the town got its visitors at a strength it could take in.

That was all. The power did not put up signs or fences. It did not write down what the town was, because it would have been read by the wrong people first.

---

## The Breath

When it was time to leave, it did not simply withdraw. It waited for a house to breathe out.

It happened in a terrace near the middle of the street during an alignment, when the attics were filling fastest. The silver ceiling in a back bedroom crept down past the lintel. The gas found the edge and left all at once in one great pale wobbling bubble. The lily-leaves fell back into the water, startled.

The power went up inside the breath. It was the only way out of Saltcombe Pit that the town itself had made, and it seemed right to leave by that route, as a guest leaves by the front door.

It rose slowly past the chimneys, past the loose silver braid from the works stack, through the tired grey light and up into the green. The breath broke the surface with a small boil, and there was a smell nobody would have been able to place: warm brick, iron, a trace of the inverted meadows. The flakes scattered across the surface film and drifted toward the edges.

The power followed them and found what it had half expected, and it was moved.

On the limestone at the rim of the pit there was a hand-sized patch of wet rock in a cleft that the sun reached for an hour at midday. There, a few pale sprouts were growing. They were the creeping things from the attic meadows, the ones pale as bean sprouts, carried up in some earlier breath and caught in the cleft. They had never known anything but a hand's depth of trapped air, lit from below. Now they were growing the right way up for the first time in their lineage. They were small and uncertain, and they leaned toward the sun.

The power had not done this. The town had. It had been doing it for years, one breath at a time, blindly and patiently, and without anyone's help it had finally managed it.

The power could have shaded the cleft or watered it. It could have fenced it or blessed it or made it certain. It did none of these things. It moved one loose stone about a hand's width to one side, so that a walker on the rim path would step past the cleft instead of into it. Then it left the sprouts to live or die on their own terms. Whatever reached the light had to be the town's own doing.

---

## Afterward

It has been back since. Not often.

It comes only during alignments, when the green from above and the working glow arrive together. It chose this for itself. It keeps to the town's calendar, not its own, so that it will never be the thing the resident counts. It arrives in the dim at the temperature of the water. It checks the escape wheel. It looks into the heap, and it is glad to find that the bands are still laying down. Once, it saw that the bones had become one more. Some cycles later it saw that the pattern of the lamps had faltered, and some cycles after that, that the pattern had come back. Now and then it lies on the chapel floor among the green loaves of the pews, looking up into the silver where the whole street hangs upside down. Near the end of every cycle, a pale shape comes in through the window with the missing pane, turns over, and lays itself along the ridge beam against its reflection.

The power has never been written down in the films. It takes care not to be. This is not modesty. It simply does not belong in the town's memory. The town's memory is for the sky, the divers, the snails, and itself.

It always arrives a few minutes late. It has worked out that this is the only way to be on time in Saltcombe Pit.
