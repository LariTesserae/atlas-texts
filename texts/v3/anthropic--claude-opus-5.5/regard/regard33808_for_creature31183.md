# The Attention

## How It Arrives

It does not come down like light. Light would be wrong here. Nothing in the basin uses it, and a sudden brightness would scatter the antelope at the pools, wake the birds folded in the figs, and blind the keepers' milky eyes, which were never made for anything more than dark and darker.

So the attention arrives the way the rain-news arrives: from far off, and through the ground.

At first it sees everything, because it can. It sees every ant column and every pale comb in every chamber, the bats hanging in their thousands, the frogs curled in their shed skins under the cracked channel mud. It sees the keepers too, and at first it does not know what they are. It takes them for dead mounds, as anyone would.

Then it notices that one of the dead mounds is cooler than the air, and that it rises and falls.

It watches that one for a long time. It learns the count of forty. It learns that it has been looking at the basin wrong, that seeing is the least useful way to know this place, and it does something it has not had to do in a long while. It stops looking and begins to listen.

## What It Learns

It learns the mound-sound first, the seething of mandibles and feet. Then it learns to tell one mound's hum from another's. It notices that some hums waver, like a bad bearing in a machine. It learns which colonies are strong and which are failing, and then it realizes a keeper already knew this, weeks ago, and has already decided.

It learns the seams in the old mounds, layered like tree rings. It reads them as a history of small takings, each one healed. It learns the curving lines the mounds make across the floodplain, the loops and crossings and doublings-back. For a moment it thinks it is looking at writing. In a way it is. Each line is a keeper's circuit, three or four centuries deep, set down one bowl of dung at a time.

Last it learns the pulse, the one sentence the keepers have. It is a deep throb pressed into the earth, several seconds long, followed by a long silence and then an answer from far down the channel. It takes the attention some time to understand what the sentence says. When it does, it is moved in a way it did not expect.

The sentence is: *Here is one of us. Still alive.*

That is all they say to each other. It is enough.

## What It Considers

A benevolent power is always tempted to help. This one is wise enough to be suspicious of the temptation, so it turns each possibility over slowly, the way a keeper listens at the base of a mound before it decides.

**It could bring the rain.** The pools are drawing down by a finger's width a day. The antelope drink with their heads jerking up between mouthfuls. The crabs crowd the shrinking margins. Somewhere a keeper waits in the reeds without moving, because it cannot run. The rain is weeks away, and the power could have it here tonight.

But the keepers hear the rain before it comes, and that hearing is the hinge the whole basin turns on. They wake under their plaster while the storms are still far across the plain. They are already walking when the soil softens and the workers open the chimneys. A rain that came before they heard it coming would reach nurseries still sealed. The winged ones would come down in their thousands onto hard crust and find no soft place with the fungus already waiting. The mushroom lines would not come up. The thirst is real and the power does not pretend otherwise. But the lateness of the rain is part of what makes the rain work.

It does not bring the rain.

**It could make more of them.** A female bears one young in eight or ten years. The power finds that alarming, as anyone who loves something fragile would. One young, pale and soft in a fold of skin, fed on fungus and milk, and a decade before the next.

But each keeper keeps a round of several hundred mounds, and the floodplain is only so wide. More keepers would mean overlapping circuits and too many seams opened in the same walls. Colonies would be tapped before they healed, and the careful rule would break: *take a little from the strong, and stop.* The slowness is not a flaw in them. It is the number of keepers the basin can bear. They have spent a very long time arriving at it.

It does not make more of them.

**It could heal the old one.** There is one, far down the channel, whose pulses have been coming further and further apart. Its breath is slower than forty now. Its plaster has cracked in a way that does not look like drought. It will probably not walk out into this year's rain.

The power could mend it. That would be easy and would cost nothing, and it wants to very much.

But it has watched what the keepers do with a failing colony. They do not prop it up. They take it entirely, the queen and all, so the mound can soften in the rains and something can grow there. They are not cruel. They have understood something about how a place stays alive across centuries, and it is not by keeping every single thing alive. Somewhere a young keeper, weaned in the last few years, has no round of its own yet. The old one's circuit is the only inheritance there is.

The power grieves. It is allowed to grieve. It does not heal the old one.

**It could make them faster, or give them sight, or harden them against whatever moves in the reeds.** It dismisses these quickly. Every one would unmake the thing it is trying to protect. A keeper that could run would not need the mud. A keeper that did not need the mud would not have the glands, the paste, or the plastering habit it also uses on its nurseries. Its slowness, its stillness, and its closeness to the temperature of the ground are not separate traits. They are one way of being.

**Last, and hardest, it could speak to them.** It could press its own note into the earth and answer the pulse. This is the one it wants most. It has come a long way, it has learned to listen, and it would like, just once, to be heard.

But their language has only one sentence, and the sentence is *here is one of us, still alive.* If the power answered, it would be saying that sentence falsely. It would add a being to the keepers' count who is not one of them. Somewhere under the gallery trees a female with her young against her belly would register one more of her kind in the dark than there really is. It would be a small lie. But the keepers have been telling each other only the truth for longer than the oldest mound has stood, and the power will not be the first to change that.

It does not answer.

## What It Does

It does three things.

**First, it remembers.** This costs the basin nothing, and it is the one gift the power can give without altering anything.

It learns each keeper's pulse, and each one is slightly different: a little lower, a little longer, a little more drawn out at the end. It learns every circuit. It fixes in itself the curving lines across the floodplain, the ones only visible from above and across centuries. The description of this place kept saying *if you could*: if you had a light, if you could see it from above. The power can. It becomes the eye the basin never had, the one that sees the whole pattern at once.

When the old keeper dies, the power will remember its round: which mounds, in what order, across most of a year. It will not give that knowledge to anyone. The young keeper will find the round on its own, by listening, the way keepers always have. But somewhere the full shape of that long life will be held intact. Being remembered takes nothing from the remembered.

**Second, it draws a line.** It spends a long while getting the line exactly right, because a benevolent power that draws its lines carelessly is how places get ruined.

Everything the basin does to itself, the power will leave alone. That includes the drought, the thirst at the pools, and the heavy thing in the reeds and whatever it eats. It includes the failing colonies eaten down to the queen, the keeper that dies in its plaster before the rain, and the young one that does not survive its first dry season. These are not wounds in the basin. They are the basin. It was not made by kindness, but it is kind in the long run, the way the keepers are kind. It has worn into its place.

But the basin cannot hear everything coming. The keepers listen through laterite for storms and footfalls and the health of colonies. They have never had to listen for a blight from another continent carried on a bird's foot, one the combs have never met. They have never had to listen for a fire of a kind that has never burned here, or for the sea that lies somewhere past the flat horizon rising faster than the gallery forest can follow the river inland. Their chins are pressed to the ground, and none of these things arrive through the ground in time.

The power decides that for things like these, things from outside that the basin never grew alongside, it will be the ear that hears them coming. It will not wall the basin off. It will stand at the edge and listen for the kinds of change the keepers cannot feel through laterite. When it hears one, it will act. It will act quietly, and no more than it must, taking a little and then stopping, the way a keeper opens a seam.

It has not decided what it will do about people. People have not come yet. There are no roads here, no fires, no marks of a human hand, but someday there will be. The power suspects that people are the one thing it cannot simply stand between the basin and. It will have to meet them the way the keepers meet a colony, by listening first, for a long time, before deciding anything. It hopes that when the first of them arrive, at least one will lie down in the dark with an ear to the ground and wonder why some of the dead mounds are breathing. It cannot make that happen. It can hope, and it can be there.

**Third, it waits for the rain with everything else.**

## The Rain

When the rain comes it comes the way it always does. The keepers have heard it for weeks, and their pulses through the earth have grown quicker and closer together. The power hears it with them and does nothing to hurry it.

On the first real night the soil softens, the workers open the mounds, and the winged ones pour up out of every chimney across the basin in plumes like smoke. The birds wake in the figs. The bats drop from the gallery forest in their quarter-million and the air moves with them. The toads and scorpions and frogs break out of the mud, and the basin is loud for the first time in months.

The keepers walk out into it streaming red, their hides dissolving in the rain, and do not lift their heads toward the feast. They go from nursery to nursery and rake each crust open with the great inner claw. Termites fall around them like ash.

The old one does not come out. The power listens for it all night, and its pulse does not come. Far down the channel, where its round began, a smaller keeper stands at the base of a mound it has never visited, and presses its chin to the wall, and listens for a very long time.

The power watches all of it from above, and from inside, and through the ground. It sees every broken heap and every wingless pair crawling in. Over the following weeks it sees the pale mushrooms push up along the circuits. For a few days the whole floodplain is written out in lines and rings of white, the entire pattern at once, legible for the only time in the year.

No one else sees it whole. The keepers never will, and they do not need to. The power sees it, and it holds the sight carefully, the way the females hold their young in a fold of skin against the belly.

## Afterwards

When the mushrooms have gone over and the grass has surged back green, the power does one last thing.

It finds a dead mound on the edge of a circuit, one of the slumped humps claimed by a fig. It settles there and makes itself cool and still, the temperature of the ground.

Some weeks later a keeper comes by on its round. It is the young one, walking the old one's circuit for the first time, slowly, on its knuckles. It stops at the dead mound and presses its chin to the base, as it does everywhere, and listens.

It hears the roots of the fig. It hears the faint seething of a new colony starting nearby. It hears nothing else, because the power makes no sound at all.

After a long while the keeper moves on toward the next mound in the order. Its pulse goes out into the earth: *here is one of us, still alive.* From somewhere far down the channel, another answers.

The power does not answer. It lies among the mounds in the dark, listening, and lets itself be mistaken for the ground.
