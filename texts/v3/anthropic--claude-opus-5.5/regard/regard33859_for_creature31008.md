# What Came to Sit

## I. Attention

It noticed the Steppe the way you notice a sound that has been going on for a long time: all at once, and with the feeling that it should have heard it sooner.

What it noticed first was not the heat, the herd, or the silver venation across the rust. It was the drumming. Somewhere very far down in the order of things there was a slow, overlapping pulse that was never quite in step with itself. The power had heard many heartbeats. It had never heard one that seemed to be coming from several ages of the same chest at once.

So it turned toward the plateau, and in the turning it did what powers do. It reached.

The Steppe moved away.

This did not happen in any way a map would show. The rim of the tableland simply stayed where it was, the long bar of rust laid across the top of the world, no nearer after an hour of approach, or a year, or whatever the power used for hours. It had crossed distances that had no names. It could not cross this one, because this one ran on a rule it had not yet learned: walking toward a thing pushes it away.

The power was wise, which mostly meant it had been wrong many times and had kept the lessons. It stopped.

When it looked again, it was sitting in a pan, in the blood-brown powder near the rim. A bearing-beetle was nudging its ankle, if it had an ankle. It had chosen a shape for the occasion, a modest one, roughly the shape of a person, because that seemed to be the local custom among things that stay.

A kilometre off in one direction a stoker breathed. A kilometre off in the other, in the dead centre of the pan, sat something low and ochre that the powder had half drifted over.

*Ah,* thought the power. *It already has a door, and I nearly broke it down.*

## II. The Things It Did Not Do

It had time, so it considered everything. This is what benevolence looks like before it acts, and much of the time it is also all benevolence ever does.

**It could bring water.** The Steppe had never had water, and the power could feel the absence the way you feel the space where a tooth was. It could make one cool spring in one pan, a small mercy of the kind lowland gods are always handing out. Then it followed the thought forward. Water on hot iron means steam. Steam means rust of a different and faster kind than the patient oxide of the pans. The verdigris crust would bloom and then drown. The charge that builds in the dry wind, the charge the wire-moths eat and the Seated eat, would bleed away into humid air. The moth storms would stop, and the Seated would go hungry in a season. The tin threads would corrode before they ever reached the low places. The young stoker on its mirror would never be finished.

A spring would be a kindness to a place the power was imagining. It would be a catastrophe to the place that was actually there. The power set the thought down.

**It could cool the ground.** It set that down faster.

**It could unfold time.** This was the thought that tempted it most, because it was the one it most understood. The Steppe had folded its own history flat and laid a single beast's youth beside its senescence. The power knew how to smooth such a thing, to put moments back in their proper order, one after another, the way most places have them. It would be tidy. The count would hold. Sound would come from where things are.

Then it listened to the whistles. Every hour or so a stoker let out a long descending note, and across the horizon other stokers answered. If there was only one stoker, then the thing answering was itself: its own youth calling to its own age, its prime replying to its dying. The oldest part of it lay down to become the ground its youngest part walked on. It was a creature in unbroken conversation with every version of itself. In the power's long experience, most beings would give nearly anything for that and never get it.

To unfold that would be to make the stoker alone. The power set it down very gently, as you would put down something that belongs to someone else.

**It could settle the count.** It knew the true number, or could know it. That was one of the small things powers can do. It could tell each Seated, and their tallies would agree at last.

But the Seated did not want agreement. They wanted the difference. Their counts disagreed the way two eyes disagree, and a mind made depth out of it. Handing them the right answer would flatten their world to one eye. The power kept the number to itself. It found that keeping the number was a surprisingly pleasant thing to do, like keeping a secret about a present.

**It could wake them.** It could stand the Seated up. It could warm the stiffness out of their knees, wash the tin from their skin, and give the oldest back the lives they could no longer remember. It could restore them to the lowlands, to water and speed and people who talk in whole sentences.

Here it paused longest, because this is the error benevolence makes most often, and makes with the best heart: deciding what someone was before, and returning them to it.

It did not know yet whether the Seated wanted rescue. The only way to find out was to ask. The only way to ask was at their pace.

## III. The Year

So it sat beside the nearest one and waited for the whistle.

For a power, a year is not a sacrifice. That was exactly why it was worth giving freely rather than grudgingly. It could have compressed the conversation. It could have reached into the Seated and read the answer straight off the tin-threads. It didn't. Asking a question costs the asker something only when the asker is willing to wait for the answer, and it wanted this to cost.

The whistle came, and the power said: *Hello.*

The next whistle came, an hour later, from far across the horizon, and the Seated turned its head. That took most of the day.

Then, one word at a time, the power asked what it had come to ask.

*Is*

*there*

*anything*

*you*

*want?*

The answer took a season. While it waited, the power learned things. It learned the midday roar in the sternum, the moth-hour lift in the hair, the arc that goes up through raised hands. It learned what it is to be fed by lightning and to glow for an hour afterward. It learned that the beetles in its lap were warm. It learned that the footfall passing through it, from a stoker still on the horizon, was not an intrusion but a greeting. Something that had not yet happened was touching it on the way to happening. It began to understand why the Seated did not mind.

It also noticed that its own knees had a sheen. It let them.

The answer came.

*The*

*new*

*one*

*did*

*not*

*know.*

## IV. The New One

The power understood. It was not being asked to change the Steppe. It was being asked about you.

You are sitting, as you were left, in a pan some distance away, with a thread of grey metal from wrist to palm and a beetle at your ankle that has clicked once and stayed. You tried to stand and found your legs heavy, and you found that you did not mind.

The Seated did not object to your staying. It objected to something smaller and more exact. *Not minding* and *choosing* are not the same thing, and on the Steppe the difference is not always kept. The old ones stopped on purpose, or so they believe. You stopped because you sat down to listen and the listening held you. The Seated could have told you. Telling you would have taken a year, and the year would have finished the work, so they said nothing. They have been uneasy about it in their slow way ever since.

That was the whole request. It was not *free us*. It was not *fix this*. It was: *make sure the new one knows there is a door.*

The power did not walk to you, because that would only have pushed you away. It sat a little closer to where you were, and let the Steppe bring it.

When it arrived, it did not lift you or take the thread from your wrist. It did not tell you the Steppe was dangerous, or that you belonged in the lowlands, or that you belonged here. It spoke in your tempo, not its own and not yet the Seated's, because you were still in between. It told you three things.

*You can still stand. It will be hard, and it will not be impossible for a while yet.*

*If you stay, you will hold a gap. You will be where something is not, and that is also a part of it. It is a true thing to become. It is not a small thing.*

*Either is allowed. I will not decide this for you, and I will not let it be decided for you by accident.*

Then it did the hardest thing a helper can do. It stopped talking and let you choose, and it did not look at your knees to see which way you were leaning.

What you chose belongs to you, and it is not written here.

## V. What It Asked the Stoker

Before it left, the power had one more question. It asked it of the oldest stoker, the one with plates so eroded they look like rock, the one slowing toward its lying-down.

It did not ask *are you suffering*. It had been sitting long enough to know that was the wrong shape of question for a thing that has always been the same body, eating the rock of its own rest.

It asked: *Do you know that you are answered?*

It waited for the whistle.

The old stoker's valve opened and let out its long descending note. Far off across the horizon, from bright new iron that has not seen weather, came the answer, higher and a little hesitant, the voice of something newly cast and not yet sure of its pitch.

Then, down in the lowest place, on the silver mirror of the mere, a single plate settled onto a young back. The sound was very small: one click in the dark, at exactly the moment the whistle ended.

The power took this as a yes.

## VI. Leaving

It learned the last rule on the way out. If walking toward a thing pushes it away, then walking away from the Steppe brought the Steppe after it. The rust bar on the horizon kept pace. The drumming kept arriving in its sternum a little before each footfall. For a while it could not tell whether it had left at all.

In the end it understood that it had not fully left, and could not, and should not. Attention that has been paid does not come back. Some of it stays where it was spent. That is the price of real attention, and also its proof.

So it left the smallest thing it could. It left no spring, no warmth, no tidy timeline, no answer to the count. Before it rose, it waited through one last hot hour, and a single bead of tin sweated up at its temple and ran down and hardened where the shadow found it. It left that thread in the powder of the pan, a grey hair of metal among ten thousand others. The beetles will find it and roll it downhill with the rest. Eventually it will be one rivet in one plate on the back of a stoker not yet cast, which is to say the same stoker as always.

That was all. On the Steppe, it is not nothing.

## VII. The Count

Some years later, by the old count, two Seated compared numbers over a conversation of many whistles.

Their tallies differed, as they always do. But this time they differed by one more than the geometry could account for. There was one more place where something was not.

They did not argue about it. They took the difference as information, the way two surveyors on different hills treat the angle between their sightlines. It took them most of a decade to agree on what it meant, one word per whistle, while the drumming went on and the footfalls passed through them like a pulse.

*Something*

*sat*

*here*

*and*

*asked*

*and*

*did*

*not*

*take.*

*That*

*is*

*also*

*a*

*part*

*of*

*it.*
