# The Attention

## I. The Weight of Looking

It noticed the Held Country the way you notice, in a crowded room, that someone is humming.

It was not looking for anything. Powers of its kind rarely are. But its attention passed over the pearl-grey lid of cloud, a continent wide and unbroken, and something underneath was holding very still in a way that turned out, on closer inspection, not to be stillness at all. It was the sum of ten billion small refusals to stop.

So it came down.

The first thing it learned was that its attention had weight. Where it looked hard, the fog thinned. Where it wondered strongly, the moths veered. A power that is wise learns early that being looked at by it is itself an event. So before it did anything else, it made itself small. It made itself narrow enough to turn sideways between the clubmoss towers, grey-green enough to disappear at dusk, and quiet enough that the eels did not change their ticking.

Then it stood a moment on the sward to get its bearings, and the moss began to come.

It looked down at the pale threads of root-hair already testing its feet, and understood the whole country in that instant, or understood the one law of it, which here is the same thing. It began to walk.

## II. What It Could Have Done

It walked for a long time, and while it walked it considered.

It could open the lid. One breath, and the cloud would part, and the Unkept would see the sun as a disc for the first time in their history. It set this aside almost at once. The sponge would dry to a crust in a season. The filmy ferns would crisp. The fog-moths would have nothing to rise into. The country drinks its own water back up the cliffs, and that circle has no slack in it. A sky would be a wound.

It could give them stone. It could raise a single boulder from the old ground below, or ten thousand, enough to build with, enough to knap into edges. It pictured this carefully: a people who had never made a tool, suddenly holding a blade. It pictured the rings. They are made of the rarest thing there is, a pebble every few years, a ring over generations. With a boulder they could fill every ring in the country in a month. Then a pebble would be nothing. A thing refused by the land would become a thing that simply lay around. The power understood that it would be taking the most precious object in a civilization and making it common. It would be doing that to people who had never asked for anything.

It could stop the mat. This was the largest temptation and it examined it longest, because it was benevolent, and the mat is how the Unkept die.

It pictured a Held Country where nothing grew over the still. No one would need to be walked asleep before they could walk awake. No one would need grooming. There would be beds. And it saw, very clearly, the word for the hollow worn in a shoulder by years of someone's grip, the most tender word they have, going quietly out of use. The evening rings would thin, because nothing needed to be picked from anyone's back. The flowering would lose its one night, because there would be no reason to stand still together. And the handprints, the bent houses made across four lives, the whole grammar in which a person ends where their work ends, would all have been answers to a question that was no longer being asked.

It did not think the Unkept's suffering was beautiful. It thought something narrower and harder. The mat was not an affliction that had happened to them. It was the ground their whole way of loving had grown up out of, the way the Held Country grew up out of the mountains. You cannot remove the stone once the roots are holding the shape.

It could show them the old ground. It could answer, for the ones who go alone to the edge, what is under the white.

It could tell them whether the weave is one being or many.

It set both of these aside for the same reason. Those who have organized their deepest rites around not deciding, a pebble set at the waterline instead of dropped, the dead sent two ways at once, should not have the deciding done for them by a visitor.

## III. Listening to the Land

So it listened instead.

It had time. Its kind always has time, and here that was the right tool, perhaps the only one. It slowed itself down to the country's pace. It stopped hearing the eels as a ticking and began to hear them as a pulse. It stopped feeling the eyes' collapses as separate events and began to feel them as a heartbeat, one every year or so, somewhere in the continent. Slower still, and the rising and falling of the whole plateau across wet decades and dry ones became a single long breath.

It tried to ask the land what it wanted.

The answer was not an answer. It was holding. The weave held water, and height, and everything that stopped, and it did this so completely that there was no gap in it through which a want could appear. The power tried for a long while to find anything in the Held Country that the Held Country lacked. It could not. The land was not suffering. It was not lonely. If anything it was a little like an old person asleep in a warm room, doing one enormous slow thing and doing it well.

Once, only once, it went over the edge.

It went down past the falls thinning into spray, down past where the spray became cloud, into the white, and further. What it found there, it did not bring back up in any form that could be told, and it has not been written here. When it climbed back to the rim, it stood in the hiss for a while where the paths bend away. Then it went back into the country with nothing in its hands.

## IV. The Hard Night

It would be dishonest to say that it only watched.

In its third year in the Held Country it walked past a bent house in the evening fog and heard, from inside, the kind of silence that is not rest. A mother had been lying down for four days. The sisters had stopped humming. The mat, even on the felted floor, had reached her shoulders, and nobody had the heart to groom it away, because grooming meant *stay* and they were no longer sure she would.

The power stood outside the door-gap for a long moment. Everything it had decided about the shape of the world stood with it.

Then it thought: *a kindness that never touches anything is just another kind of absence.* It also thought: *but a hand that always comes becomes a law, and this country already has one.*

It went in as a stranger, in fog, at dusk, the way anyone is seen. It knelt with the sisters. It did nothing a very steady pair of hands could not have done. Perhaps its hands were steadier than any hands had been. Perhaps not. It will not say. By morning there was a child, and the mother was groomed clean by her sisters. The sisters thanked the stranger in the way you thank a traveler from a far family. The small wet hand was pressed into the lintel, and the print took.

The power walked away knowing it had now become part of the country, a thing that had happened in it, a print of its own pressed into the living scale. It accepted that. It also made itself a promise. It would never walk the circuits looking for bent houses with that silence in them. It would not become the reason the Unkept stopped fearing the fourth day. If it passed another such door by chance, it would decide again, fresh, and carry the weight of deciding. It would not let the weight turn into a rule.

It passed two more such doors in its time there. Once it went in. Once it did not, because an old grandmother was already kneeling there, and her hands were enough, and the power stood in the fog outside and hummed very low with the family until morning.

## V. The Old One

It walked for a while with a family of five, then four.

The eldest was a woman who had seen three flowerings, which is a great many. She took the stranger for a lost walker, and let it sleep behind her with its hand on her shoulder. The power did not need sleep, but it closed its eyes and let its feet find the path through her body, and learned, in the palm, the exact weight of her. There was already a hollow in her shoulder, worn by someone long gone. The stranger's hand did not fit it. She said that was all right. Hands do not have to fit. They only have to stay.

One morning she did not get up from the evening ring.

They went to the house she had helped bend, and they groomed her one last time, every strand, and then they stopped. The power sat with them for nineteen days while the land finished grooming her. It hummed with them. It hummed badly, because the hum does not translate, and they were kind about it.

When they carried her out, light and green, to the crest of a swell, the power understood something it had not understood from above. The Unkept were not losing a fight with the land. They were holding a long conversation with it, and this was simply a place where the conversation turned.

The family kept one pebble for her. By custom the eldest child would carry it. But the child was young, and the eye she had loved was months away. Her son asked the stranger, since it walked well and seemed to have nowhere to be, whether it would walk with them.

It did. It did not carry the stone. It was not its stone to carry. But at the edge of the tea-dark water, when the son set the pebble on a flat leaf a finger's width above the surface, the stranger crouched beside him. It did something it had never done in all its long existence. It left a thing to be decided by something other than itself.

It did not look back to see whether the water rose its inch.

## VI. The Flowering

The country turned violet in the power's eleventh year there. The air smelled like wet pepper and honey, and the scattered families came together at the old rings, whose stones were nearly under.

On the night, it stood with them.

It stood barefoot on moss where a pebble used to be, shoulder to shoulder with people who did not know what it was. It let the fog come down. It let the moths rise and the lichen light and the eels tick under its feet. It let the land do to it exactly what it did to everyone. Root-hair threaded between its toes. By the small hours it was green to the knee, and it did not stop it. It could have. That was the entire point of standing there.

At dawn everyone turned to groom.

It did not know whom to turn to. It had come from outside and had no one here who owed it a morning. For a moment it simply stood in the violet with the green on its legs. Then a child tugged at it. She was perhaps seven, born just before the last flowering, the one night she will not remember. She sat it down and began to pick the moss from its calves with small padded fingers, a few spores at a time, very seriously, the way she had been taught.

To groom someone is to say *stay*.

The power let itself be told.

## VII. The Teaching

It heard the teaching many times while it walked there, always during grooming, always to children, always with the moss coming away under someone's fingertips.

*The land holds everything. It holds so much that it forgot how to let go. So it made one kind of thing that it cannot—*

And then the endings varied.

*—that it cannot hold,* said one grandmother, *so that it would remember what letting go is like.*

*—that it cannot keep,* said a young father, *and that is us, and the stones.*

*—that it cannot catch,* said a boy teaching his little sister, *because it keeps walking.*

Once the teller was an old man, and the child fell asleep under his hands before he reached the end. He stood her up and walked her, and he never finished the sentence, and nobody seemed to mind.

The power could have finished it. It knew, by then, more about the land than any Unkept. It might even have known which ending was true, if one of them was. It did not finish it. It thought that some sentences are like bent houses. They are meant to be carried forward by someone walking behind you, and ended by someone you will never meet.

## VIII. What It Took, and What It Left

It left in the end, because staying would have been its own kind of weather.

A power that remains in a place becomes, eventually, part of that place's law. Its kindness hardens into a thing that is counted on. The Held Country already had its law, and its law was not cruel. It was only holding. The Unkept already had their answer to it, and their answer was each other.

So it looked, one last time, for anything the country lacked.

It found one thing. The land remembers every path. It forgets none of the pale silver-green writing scribbled across its skin. But it does not remember the hums. It does not keep faces, or which child groomed whom first on which violet morning, or the weight of a particular old woman in a particular palm. It takes the bodies and pushes up the stones. The rest, the part that happens between the Unkept, simply goes.

So that is what the power took with it: not a pebble, not a sample, not a secret from below the white. Only the remembering the land does not do. The names that are hums. The hollow in the shoulder its hand did not fit. The child's small fingers on its calves. It will carry them for as long as it carries anything. Nothing in the Held Country will ever know, and nothing needs to.

What it left was very little.

There is a print on a lintel somewhere, pressed alongside a newborn's hand on a hard night. It is already stretching with the growth of the towers into something long and strange. Anyone reading it would read it as growth.

There is a pebble that may or may not still sit at the edge of an eye.

And across the sward, crossing and recrossing the tracks of grazers and walking birds and eleven years of Unkept, there is one more thin pale trail of silver-green. It is faint as frost. The land cannot tell it from any other, and doesn't try. It will keep that path for centuries, as it keeps all of them, until the moss finally closes over it and the country forgets it was ever walked.

The fog comes down. The moths rise. Somewhere a grazer hums once, low, beside a ring of stones it did not place. The mat grows, a hair's breadth, over everything that is still.

The power did not make it otherwise. That was the whole of its gift, and it was not nothing.
