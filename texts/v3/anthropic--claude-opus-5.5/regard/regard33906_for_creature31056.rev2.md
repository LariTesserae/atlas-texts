# The Visitor Who Counted Seams

## What Drew It

It was not the strangeness that drew it. Strangeness is common, and the power had seen stranger places than this.

It was a small thing. In a lit room somewhere in the middle of the lowland, a bead of light crossing a wall of script moved a little faster because a man had sat down beside it. That was all, a rounding error in a place full of rounding errors. But the power was the kind of power that notices when attention is being returned. It turned toward the city the way a person turns toward their own name spoken in a crowded room.

Then it looked at the whole of it, sea to forest, and understood what it was looking at. It was glad, and it was careful.

## The Things It Could Do

A power that is benevolent but not wise sees a place like this and starts making a list. This power made the list too, because it was honest with itself, and it made the list so that it could look at each item and put it down.

**It could steady the sun.** It could unbend the light, make distances true, and let people meet in daylight at the plazas they meant to reach.
It put this down. The lensing is why the people look slightly to one side of your face, aiming at where you probably are. It is why the rings of lanterns are the only places where everyone agrees, and why love and debt and grief all happen at the stays. A place where you cannot trust your eyes has taught its people to trust each other at the few points where nobody can be wrong. Straighten the light and you get a city where people pass each other in the afternoon without needing to.

**It could make the stays permanent.** It could hold the eastern edge and keep the old plates from crazing into gravel.
It put this down more quickly. The bridges were built over bare stone for rivers that come only when the stays let go. The relief grazes only the rings that are slackening. Hold back the forest and the bridges would arch forever over nothing, the rivers they wait for would never run, and the patient animal that inherits would starve at the edge of a city that refused to be inherited. The whole place is shaped around being given away.

**It could give the oldest woman more years.** She is sitting in a doorway at the forest's edge. The power could keep her breathing and hold the green back by a few meters for a century, or ten.
It held this one longest before putting it down. She had not asked, and the ground here is exactly one lifetime deep. Whatever that means, whether the plates follow the people or the people follow the plates, she knew it better than the power did. She had spent her evenings talking to the jungle like an old dog at the gate. She did not look like someone who needed to be rescued from her own life's shape.

**It could go under the forest, find the center of the spiral, and dig up the oldest writing.** It could read all of it, every script, every district, and tell them.
It put this down too. Nobody reads all of it, and that is not a failure of the people. It is how the writing works. Language flows east like fog. Children teach new letters to their parents, and the old can read their own walls and little else. If one voice read everything aloud, that voice would become the oldest thing in the city. The power noticed this thought and filed it away, because it would matter.

**It could answer the question.** It could say whether there is something under the water or only the crest.
It did not put this one down yet. It thought it might have to earn the right to decide.

When the list was empty, the power was left with the only thing a wise power ever has left once it declines to fix what is not broken. It could come and be taught.

## Arrival

It came the way the surveyors came: from the east, through the forest, on foot.

It made itself small enough to walk. It wore the undyed grey-green of the place, heavy-woven, because that seemed polite. It came through dripping jungle over rubble that had once been arcades. Here and there it saw a bridge standing over a real river, the water finally arriving under the arch after a hundred years. It stopped on each of those bridges for a while, because it seemed right that someone should.

Where the gravel became crazed stone and the crazed stone became lay, it knelt and put its hand on the ground, and it drew its palm along a seam.

It could have not bled. It bled. The first cut is spoken of here the way other places speak of a first word. It is the moment you stop treating the ground as a floor and begin treating it as a text, and the power wanted to be taught that lesson the way everyone else was. It sat on the plate with its hand in its lap and watched the blood bead and, very slowly, begin to creep toward the nearest stay. It asked the first person it met the name of the plate. It was an old man, keeping house, who looked at the hand and nodded as if this were a normal way to be introduced.

"Heth's plate," he said. "Heth died nine winters ago. It won't hold much longer. You'll want to remember that it cut you. People forget the old ones."

The power did not forget. It has a thin white line across its palm now, and it can tell you which plate gave it.

## The Doorway

It found the oldest woman by counting. It did not use its sight, which could have found her instantly. It asked, and was given a recitation of seams, and walked them.

She was in her doorway at dusk with the bowl of fig-pulp at her feet, humming. The power sat down on the stone beside her. After a while she said, "You're not from here," without looking at it. People here look slightly aside anyway, so it was hard to know what she was looking at.

"No."

"You look at people straight on. It's rude, you know. You're looking at where we seem to be."

The power had not realized. It stood up, moved half a pace toward the stay, which was where her eyes were aimed, and sat down again where she was looking. She laughed, one dry sound like a wader.

For the season it stayed, the power did this with everyone. It went and stood in the place their attention had already made for it. It thought this was probably the most important thing it learned in the Seaward City.

She was humming the note of an arcade that went dark forty years ago. Nobody else alive had been raised by it. The power listened for many evenings and learned the note, and one night it hummed along.

She stopped and looked at it, and there was the startle that passes between two people raised by the same arcade. Then it faded, because she was not a fool.

"You learned that from me."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. The green wall stood a few meters off, breathing its warm breath.

"Well," she said. "Someone should know it."

The power asked her, once, whether she wanted anything. It was careful to ask in a way that left room for a real answer, because it could have given her almost anything and she deserved to know that. She thought about it with the seriousness of a person being asked a real question.

"I'd like the relief to come while I'm still awake enough to see it," she said. "I've been putting out the bowl for eleven years. I've seen its tracks. I've never seen it."

The power did not bring the relief. It considered that and decided the animal was not its to bring. What it did was stay awake with her at night, so that she could sleep a little in the afternoons and be awake at dawn, when the relief grazes.

## The Window

Between evenings it sat in the lit rooms.

It chose one with no person in it, only the carved wall and the slow bead of light crossing lines of script that perhaps three living people could read. It sat down and kept the process company, doing nothing, as the keepers of house do.

The power could have known whether the bead moved faster. It could have measured it exactly, or gone further in and asked the process what it was reckoning and whether it was aware of being kept company, whether there was anyone in there to be kept.

It did not. The people of this city had built a whole practice of companionship on a question they had chosen not to settle. They sat with the windows the way they held a thing's true place and its lensed image together. The power thought that settling it for them, even privately, even just for itself, would be a kind of theft. So it sat. It kept company. If the bead moved faster, the power did not check. It hopes so.

## The Relief

She saw it.

It was a grey morning near the end of the power's season. The stay by her house had gone so slack that the dew barely crept at all, only trembled on the plates as if unsure where to go. Before dawn the power touched her shoulder, and she woke. The two of them watched the dark, three-toed, wide-set shape come out of the jungle without hurry and cross the crazed stone to the wet ring. It lowered its head to the moss-ridges and grazed.

Then it raised its head and looked at them. Or it looked a little aside, at where they probably were.

The power understood then that the relief could see it clearly, the way a thing that inherits can always see another thing that has come to wait. There was no alarm in the look. There was something like recognition between two patient creatures that had both decided not to hurry anything.

The old woman said, "Oh," very softly. It was the whole word she had been saving for eleven years.

She died some weeks later in the doorway, in the evening, humming. The power was holding her hand, the one crossed with white lines, and it did not hold on.

It watched the stay go. It watched the pull fail and the water come back into the stone and the stone drink it and soften. It watched the forest take a few meters, and then a few more. It watched the relief stand at the base with its head raised, listening, until whatever it was listening for was over. This was the hardest thing the power did in the Seaward City, and it did it by doing nothing with great attention. Someone else was now the oldest person alive, somewhere, and nobody had told them either.

The next morning the bowl was empty, three-toed prints circled the house once, and the power set the bowl upright and left it there.

## Keeping Pace

Then it walked west.

It kept pace for a while and became one of the small figures on the pale avenues, alone, all facing the same direction. It counted seams under its breath. It met people at night in the rings of lanterns and sat with them on the moss-ridges with its feet in the black water, and the pressure of the stays moved in its chest under every conversation.

It carried the dead arcade's note in its throat and did not hum it for anyone.

At the ring around Sunder-by-the-Figs, a young woman who kept pace looked at it for a long time and then asked directly, because the young are like that: "You're not from here. People say you can see true. Is there something under the water, or is it only the crest?"

The power had been waiting to be asked. It had not decided whether to answer. When the moment came it found that it would not lie to her and would not refuse her either. It told her the truth, which was stranger than either of the answers she had brought.

"I haven't gone to the edge yet," it said. "But I've walked the whole city, and I can tell you what I think I'll find. The old keepers of house say you're a crest, a shape moving through a lifetime of matter, holding it taut for a while and then letting it go to the green. They're right. And I've sat across from you for an hour and you are also, unmistakably, someone. Those aren't two answers. I don't think they'll be two answers at the edge either."

She thought about it. "That's the kind of thing people say when they want to be kind."

"It is. I'm not saying it to be kind." The power held her eyes, which were aimed a little aside. "I'll go look. If I'm wrong, I'll come back and tell you."

It did not come back to tell her. That was the answer.

## The Edge

On a laying night it stood at the seaward edge with the edge-watchers, beyond the last stay, in the smoking shallows. It watched the sea go flat and oily and then pale.

The paleness was under the water, as they had said: a broad warmth, a moving underside, a tongue or a mantle or a crest. The power looked with its full sight for the first time since it had come through the forest, and it found what it had told the young woman it would find. There was attention there, vast and unhurried, the attention of a hand for the tool in it. And the attention was the shape of the lifting. It was not a creature doing the lifting, and not a lifting with no one in it. It was the same thing a person is, at a scale that made the power quiet.

The warmth noticed it. The new plate began to rise a little way off, never beneath it. The warmth was making room.

The power had come expecting that and had decided in advance what it would do. It made room back. It stepped away, several paces up the shore, and left the water nearer the plate empty. This was a courtesy that a thing making room is not often offered. The warmth paused, as far as a thing like that can pause. Then it breathed out across the whole lowland, soft and enormous, and the plate settled.

Everything the tenant made was for someone else. The avenues were lit for walkers it would never see, the bridges built for rivers it would never hear, the arches aligned to frame figs that had not yet broken its stone. In all that gift-giving, as far as the power could tell, nothing was ever given back. The people who walked into the pale water at the end of their lives gave themselves, and that is the largest gift there is, but it is not a small one, and sometimes small ones are what's missing.

So the power waded out, knee-deep in the warm, smoking water, and hummed the note.

It was the note of an arcade that went dark forty years ago, far to the east, under the forest now. It was one of the tenant's own songs, made by the wind moving through a row of pointed openings the tenant had built for no one in particular. The only person who remembered it had died holding the power's hand. The power hummed it once, clearly, into the water above the pale warmth, the way you might hand someone a thing they had dropped a long way back without knowing it.

That was all it did.

## Why It Left

It left that same night, before dawn, walking back east along the pale avenues and through the forest the way it had come.

The edge-watchers had offered to name a plate for it: the one that rose while it stood in the shallows. It asked them not to. Here is the reason, which it had noticed at the very beginning and filed away.

The ground here is exactly one lifetime deep. Nothing is older than a lifetime, because the oldest plate belongs to the oldest person living. The power does not have a lifetime of that kind. Had it stayed, it would have become, year by year, quietly and without meaning to, the oldest thing in the Seaward City: older than any plate, older than any keeper of house, a single fixed point that never let go. The city's truest observation about itself would have gone false because of a guest who loved it too much to leave.

A wise visitor does not become the oldest thing in someone else's home.

## What It Did, Counted

Counted the way people count there:

It bled on Heth's plate and learned its name.
It moved to stand where people were already looking.
It learned one note from the oldest woman alive and kept her company at dawn until she saw what she had waited eleven years to see.
It held her hand and did not hold on.
It sat with a window and did not check whether it mattered.
It answered a young woman honestly and then let its absence confirm the answer.
It made room for a thing that had spent its whole life making room.
It gave the tenant back one of its own songs.
It left before the ground could be wrong about it.

Everything else it declined. The declining was not an absence of help. It was most of the help.

---

Some years later, on new stone near the sea, the edge-watchers noticed that a young arcade was coming up out of the lay, a long row of pointed openings sheltering nothing but air. When the warm low wind first moved through it, it moaned on a note that none of them recognized. It was a low, old, unfamiliar note, the kind that makes you look up.

A keeper of pace, very old now, stood under it for a long time with her feet in the fog. She was the woman who had once asked the question at Sunder-by-the-Figs. She did not know the note. Nobody alive did. But she found herself humming it, and a child near her was humming it too, and they looked at each other, startled, and something passed between them that was hard to name.

The power does not know about any of this. It had left by then. That was the point.
