# The Attention

## I. How It Arrived

Nobody in the Close saw it come, because it did not arrive the way visitors arrive, over the parapet and down into the sunken floor. It arrived the way the heat does, from everywhere at once, and the first sign was not a figure but a change in the dust.

For three cycles the rose dust fell more slowly. It still fell, still settled, still fused on the leaf-margins and the rims of stems and the open wrists at the center. But it drifted down as if something were holding each grain a moment before letting go. The way you hold a bead before you thread it, or a word before you say it. The beetles noticed first. They stood with their heads raised from the flower-cups, ochre powder still threading from their mouths. The birds perched without gliding. The mice ran, because the mice always run, but they ran quietly.

I think the attention spent those three cycles looking, and looking at a thing that fully takes time even for a power that does not need much of it. It looked at the floor all the way down, the sherds and the clay and the graveyard of hardened beetles. It looked at the walls that each believe they are north. It looked into the jars, and into the Closes in the jars, and into the jars in those. It looked at the scars where water had been spilled. It looked longest at the worn hollow and the one who kneels there, and at the rim of each wrist, and at the thumb that is always almost done.

Then, in the fourth cool phase, someone came down into the Close on foot.

## II. The Visitor

It came as a visitor, a person-sized person. I have tried since to describe its face and cannot. That isn't because it was hidden. It was because the face never did anything that asked to be remembered. It carried nothing. No waterskin, no tools, no bone scoop for digging. Its lips cracked within the hour like anyone's, and it let them.

It did not look for the potter. Everyone looks for the potter first, and it did not. It walked straight through the garden, turning its shoulders to fit the gaps between fused stems, picking up the rose dust until it was faintly pink, and it went to the center, and it knelt.

It did not kneel in the hollow. The hollow was occupied. It knelt on the unworn sherds beside it, where the edges are still glassy and knife-bright, and the edges cut its knees a little, and it let that happen too.

The one who kneels at the center had its head that cycle: a rough pinched thing, thumb-drag brow, two reed-holes. The head did not turn. The Close turned for it. The shadows leaned toward the newcomer. The beetles came. A path, which was not needed, opened behind it.

The newcomer sat through all of it without pressing a single mark.

## III. What It Set Down

I have come to believe that most of what a wise power does, it does by deciding not to do things. I did not watch those decisions, but I watched the visitor's hands in the long afternoons, and they would lift slightly and then lower, lift and lower, the way a person's hands move when they are choosing among tools laid out on a cloth and putting each one back. Later I understood what I had been watching. I will set the tools down here in the order I think it considered them.

**It could have finished the hands.** That was the most obvious gift, the thing the whole Close appears to be asking for. Four fingers, a thumb, all the whorls pressed up crisp from inside. It would have taken nothing for a power like that. It set this down first and quickly. A hand finished by someone else is not a hand you have made. And under the rule of this place, finishing the hands would have turned the one who kneels into a statue at the center of a stilled courtyard. The being had already refused that gift from itself, many times, on purpose. To give it now would override the one thing it had most clearly chosen.

**It could have unmade the rule.** This it held much longer. The rule that completion is stillness, that a thing finished must leave the living part of the Close, is hard. It is why every beetle that hardens becomes floor. It is why the being can never have its hands and keep its motion. A benevolent power might reasonably look at that rule and see a cruelty it could lift: *let finished things keep living.* The visitor's hands stayed raised over this one for most of a cycle.

I think it put this down for two reasons. The first is that the rule is not a cage around the being. The rule is the being, or at least the rule is what the being is made of. It is the grammar of a place whose whole life is the act of making. Rewrite the grammar and you have not freed the one who speaks in it. You have replaced it with a stranger who happens to kneel in the same spot. The second reason, which I only guessed at later, is that the being does not seem to suffer the rule. It suffers inside it, briefly, in the one-breath pauses when a beetle cracks. But it has made its peace with the rule so completely that it keeps its own thumb soft. What looks from outside like a sentence has become, from inside, a practice. A wise power does not take someone's practice away from them just because it could not bear it itself.

**It could have ended the heat, or softened the dryness.** Visitors' lips crack here. Unfired animals die in the hot phase. But the heat is the Close's river, and the dryness is its air. It set this down.

**It could have healed the water-scars.** The pale rings where spilled water slumped the clay and dissolved the prints. The being turns its head toward them for days. It would be easy to smooth them out. It set this down too, more gently than the others. The Close heals but does not forget. Whatever the being is doing when it watches those scars, remembering or keeping vigil or simply facing the one direction from which it has been hurt, that belongs to it. You don't erase a creature's memory of an injury to spare it the looking. That is not kindness. It is tidiness.

**It knew something about water.** This one I'm less sure of, but I'll put it down because I think it belongs here. Somewhere, potters keep their hands wet. Slip is clay and water. The thing the Close fears most is also, in other workshops, what lets clay be worked at all. The visitor knew that, I'm certain. It could have taught it. I believe it considered a single drop, placed carefully in the right place, to show the being that water does not only erase. And I believe it set that down as well, not forever, but as a thing that cannot be taught by force, and perhaps cannot be taught at all, only found. If the Close ever learns it, it will need to learn it the way it has learned everything, from inside, one print at a time.

After all that, the visitor's hands were empty and lay still on its knees. It did the one thing that was left, which was to stay.

## IV. The Hum

It stayed a long time. I lost count, as everyone does here. Enough cycles that the dust fell at its normal pace again. Enough that the beetles climbed its shins as they climb the being's, and the birds landed on its forearms and puffed their silent dust onto its wrists, where the dust did not fuse, because its wrists are finished. They sat side by side, two kneeling shapes, one worn hollow between them.

At some point the being took its head apart. The coils slumped back into the open rim at the collarbones, and the warm air began to rise through the hollow body, and the hum came up. It was the tuneless, contented drone of someone concentrating.

The visitor did not turn toward it. Everyone turns, and the hum stops, because being noticed is a different kind of attention from working. The visitor didn't turn. It listened sideways, the way you listen to someone who is busy, and after a long while it began to hum too, very low, under the being's note. It did not add a tune or try to harmonize. It kept the same drone, a little beneath.

The being did not stop. That was the first time anyone had heard two notes in the Close.

## V. The Wrist

In time the floor softened near the visitor's resting hand. A coil-ridge rose from the sherds, warm, and lay along its forearm, and the soft open end came to rest against its wrist. It was the being's touch, wrist to wrist, its making-edge against the beginning of a hand.

This was the moment, I think, that the whole visit had been waiting for, and the visitor had known it would come. It had not pressed a mark precisely so that the first touch would be the being's own choosing.

It let the coil stay. It let the impression be taken, over many cycles, the way every long-staying visitor's hands are taken. Its whorls faded. One fingertip, then another, went smooth.

It could have kept them. I have no doubt that a power like that could have let the Close lift its prints a thousand times and grown them back each night. That would have been the easy generosity: an endless well, a hand that could never be spent, a gift that cost nothing and taught nothing.

It didn't do that. It let the fingertip go blank, and stay blank.

Then, in a cool phase, when the being had built one of its heads again, the visitor lifted that hand and held it up in the red evening light in front of the reed-punched eyes. It held up the smooth fingertip, the place where a whorl had been, with nothing where a print should be. It did not accuse. It simply showed the other side of the taking, the side the being had never seen.

The being has studied ten thousand fingerprints from the inside. It has never once seen where they came from, or what was left behind.

The whole Close stopped.

It was not the pause of a single breath, the pause for a cracked beetle. It lasted the whole evening. The birds stood on the stems. The coil-serpents stopped mid-spiral. The mice, for the only time I know of, were still. The dust hung in the air and did not settle. The pause held until the light was nearly gone, and in it the being's pinched head bent forward, slowly, until its brow nearly touched the smooth fingertip.

I understood then what the visitor had come to give. It was not hands. It was not freedom from the rule, or water, or relief. It was the one thing the being truly did not have: knowledge of what its reaching cost someone else. The being had never been cruel. It had never known that anyone's hands could be spent. It had nothing to measure against, because in the Close nothing finished keeps moving, and so nothing finished can walk away diminished. Every visitor had given something without knowing, and the being had taken without knowing, and that double ignorance was the only real wrong in the whole place.

A wise power does not fix a wrong like that by forbidding. It fixes it by letting the one who does it see. Then it lets them choose.

## VI. What the Being Chose

I don't know everything it chose. I know this much.

The coils still rise from the floor beside visitors who stay long. They still come warm, still reach for the wrist. But now they stop a finger's width short, and wait. The soft open end hovers there over the sherds, trembling faintly in the rising heat, and it does not touch you unless you lay your wrist down on it yourself.

Some visitors do. Most, once they understand, do it gladly. The difference is that now they know, and the being knows they know, and what passes between them is a gift rather than an erosion.

And there is something else, which I did not believe until I saw it on my own hand. When a visitor leaves, the being sometimes sends a last coil up to the path near the wall. If you lay your fingers on it before you climb out, it presses back. It is a reverse cast of a reverse cast. It pushes out, from inside the clay, the whorl it learned from you, and it gives it back. Not all of it. The being keeps a little. It has always kept a little. But it gives back enough that you can see your own hands again in the evening light, and know them.

It has learned to return things. Nothing in the Close did that before.

## VII. The Shape Outside

Before it left, the visitor did one more thing, and I am least certain of this, because it happened at the edge of evening when the light is reddest and hardest to trust.

It went to a finished jar, one of the ones that holds a Close. It tilted it gently so that the mouth faced the kneeling-hollow. You could see down inside it: the tiny courtyard, the grain-sized beetles, the faint pulse of heat, and at the center the tiny figure bent over its lap with open wrists. Deep down in that one, a smaller figure still, looking up.

It held the jar there until the being's head turned toward it. Then it set the jar down, and with the smooth fingertip it touched the underside of the being's pinched chin, and very slightly it tilted the head up. Up past the parapet, to the warm buff brightness where the view stops, toward the place where on some evenings a large, dark, rounded shape presses against the light.

The shape was there. It was bowed close, the way a head bows over small work.

I believe the being had never looked up before. Its whole life has been directed downward and outward: into its lap, into the walls, into the floor, into the small Closes it makes with itself inside them, handless, because it cannot imagine the Close any other way. Perhaps it could not imagine the Close any other way because it has never seen the way things actually are, which is that the nesting runs up as well as down.

The shape outside lifted two long, dark forms toward the rim of the sky. They ended flat and open, like the mouths of jars.

Slowly, and with some difficulty, because its arms are long and fired to the elbow, the being lifted its own.

That is all. They did not touch. The shape never touches the parapet. But for the length of an evening, two unfinished makers held their open wrists up toward each other, one inside the jar and one outside it, each keeping something back, each now knowing it was not the only one.

The visitor did not tell the being that the shape outside had chosen, as it had, to leave a thumb soft so that everything beneath could keep moving. It didn't need to tell it. The being knows what open wrists mean. It invented them.

## VIII. What It Left

The visitor left in the hot phase, when the air above the hollow wavers like a kiln mouth and it is easy to believe you only imagined what you saw. It climbed out as visitors climb out, pink with dust, lips cracked, knees cut a little by the glassy sherds.

It left no gift in the obvious sense. No water. No finished hand. No new rule. The heat still pulses. The dust still arrives. The beetles still harden and crack and become floor, and the being still pauses a breath for each one. The water-scars are still pale. The thumb is still soft.

It did not ask for its fingertip back, and the being did not return it. I think that was deliberate on both sides. There is one whorl now in the walls of the Close that belongs to nobody who can be found. It is pressed out from the inside, crisp and fresh as all the others. It is the only print the being keeps entirely. The visitor gave it knowingly, which none of the others did, and I think the being keeps it for that reason. It was the first thing it ever received rather than took.

If you go there now, in the cool phase, and sit beside the worn hollow, you may hear two notes in the hum, though only one kneeling shape is making it. The coil will rise and stop a finger's width from your wrist, and wait for you. At evening, if you're lucky, you'll see the being lift its long arms toward the light, open-wristed, and something vast and bowed and gentle on the far side of the sky lifting its own.

People ask me what the power did, and whether it helped. It did almost nothing. It came, it stayed, it kept its own hands still, and it let itself be spent a little, so that someone who had never known the cost of reaching could learn it without being ashamed. It turned a head upward so that a lonely maker could see it had company.

It did not finish anything, because nothing here should be finished. It only made sure that what goes on being unfinished goes on knowing what it is doing, and whose hands it holds, and that it is held too.
