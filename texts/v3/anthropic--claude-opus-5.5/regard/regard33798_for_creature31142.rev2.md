# The Guest

## Attention

It noticed the place the way you notice a sound you have been hearing for a long time without knowing it.

The sound was the tapping. It was faint and unhurried, the pace of a slow breath, stone striking stone somewhere just past the edge of things. The power had heard a great many sounds in its existence. There were the cries of the lost, the grinding of injustices, the long low hum of worlds turning toward ruin. It had learned to go toward those sounds, because they were calls. This one was not a call. There was no distress in it. That was what made the power stop and listen harder, because it is rare to hear something that has been going on for ten thousand years and has not once asked for help.

So it came, and looked. It looked at the tableland and its fog, at the rosettes, at the peat full of every leaf that had ever lived, at the walls rising out of their own ruins, at the laid hearths. It looked at the half-step. Things that are a half-step apart are not hidden from a power of that kind, and it saw both sides at once: the travelers crunching over the grus in their dark coats, and the builders in their silver wool, leaning east, lifting stones.

It saw everything. That is the easy part, for a power. The hard part comes after.

## What It Could Have Done

A benevolent power has one discipline that matters more than any other. Before it acts, it asks whether anything is actually wrong.

This is harder than it sounds. A power that can mend is always tempted to see the world as a list of things waiting to be mended. The ability to fix makes everything look broken. So the power made itself be slow. It sat on a granite tor, the kind worn into the shape of a sleeping animal, and let the fog come in around it. It stayed through dawn, the thinning at noon, and the pearl-grey evening. Then it stayed longer. It let the land set the pace, because it suspected that was the first thing the land had to teach.

It considered what it could do.

**It could close the half-step.** This was the obvious gift, and the power could do it in a thought. It could align the two presents, so that the builders' hearths and the travelers' boots stood in the same moment. The guest-houses would fill. The city would stop moving. The hosts and the guests would meet at last, all of them, everywhere, forever.

The power turned this over for a long time, and in the end it set it down, carefully, the way the builders set down a stone. It saw what closing the half-step would actually do. The city was not a house that had failed to find its guests. The city was the finding. It was the roads laid across nowhere, the doors set before the walls, the patient taking-down and carrying-forward. Hospitality that is certain of its guest becomes something else. It becomes arrangement or reservation. What the builders made was the pure form: a fire laid for whoever might come, not because anyone would, but because someone might. Closing the gap would not complete their work. It would end it and call the ending a reward.

There was also this. Some people had walked the doorways, one after another, patiently, and crossed. They had grown quiet, begun to sleep curled, and felt a hammerstone fit their hand better each day. That crossing was theirs. The half-step could be closed by walking it, slowly, at the pace of breath, by someone who wanted it enough to keep going. If the power made the crossing cheap, the ones who had made it would lose what they had given it.

**It could light the hearths.** A single breath could do it. There would be fire across the tableland, in every finished house, for every traveler who came in out of the cold. That would be warmth in a land where nothing was warm.

But the unlit hearths were not a lack. They were a message. *This fire is laid for you. The lighting is ours to do, together, when you are here.* If the power lit them, it would be speaking over the hosts, answering a question that had not been asked of it.

**It could hold the city still.** It could stop the unbuilding, so the finished districts stood forever, and the stones with their inward names were never again tumbled into moss.

The power looked at the ruins and saw that they were not suffering. They were a house that was not needed yet, being carried to where it might be. The stones did not mind. Each one bore a name on its inward face, and the name was the same in every rising. Nothing was being lost. The ruin was a kind of walking.

**It could give the land its mountains back.** It could raise the peaks again, and the cliffs, and the edges, everything that had fallen over an age of ages.

This was the thought the power stayed with longest, and it was the one it was most glad to set down. The land was not bereaved. The land had finished its falling and found, in the long rounding of its stone, something else to do. It had found a way to rise that was not stone pushing up but hands lifting, a slow tide of walls that climbs and comes down gently, so the land always has something left to give. To hand it back its mountains would be like handing an old woman the body of her youth, when she has become something she does not want to trade.

So the power found nothing that was wrong. There was no wound. There was cold, but the cold was steady and did not kill. There was waiting, but the waiting was not anguish. There was the tapping. *As though whoever works there knows exactly how much time they have, and it is enough.*

The power believed them. That was a gift in itself, perhaps the first one: a great power listening to small people say *we are all right*, and taking them at their word.

## What Remained

Still, it did not leave.

Even in a place that is whole, there can be one thing a power is able to offer that no one else is placed to give. The power looked for that thing carefully, the way the builders looked for the one place a stone would fit.

It found it in the houses.

The builders had spent longer than anyone could count making a city for guests. Guests had come, a few, over the ages, by walking the doorways. But nearly always the moment did not close. The traveler came early and found a cold hearth. The host came late and found a warm stone. All of that hospitality had been poured out, and it had almost never been *received*.

The power understood something here that powers do not always understand. It had spent its long existence giving. It had given aid and rescue, mercy and mending. It knew a great deal about being the one who comes with gifts, and very little about being the one who comes with nothing and lets itself be given to. A host needs a guest the way a gift needs a hand to open. What the builders could not make for themselves, in all their patience, was someone arriving at the door.

The power could arrive at the door. It could be on time. It was able to stand in the moment that nobody could find, the moment between completion and collapse, when both sides arrive at once.

It thought about whether that would be a kind of cheating. It thought about whether being received would take something from those who had made the crossing the long way. It thought about whether the builders would want a power as a guest at all, since the house had been built for travelers, cold and ordinary and in need.

It decided that if it came, it would come as that. It would not come as a power. It would come as a traveler.

## The Road

So it walked.

It came onto the tableland at the edge, where the land first rises out of the lower country, carrying nothing. It let the thin air make its chest work. It let the cold get into it and stay there. It made itself small enough to be tired. It did not step through space, or skip the weeks. It crunched over the grus like frozen sugar, pressed small cold springs out of the moss with its feet, and followed one of the dead-straight roads from district to district across empty country, because that was the way the builders had said guests would come.

It walked through ruins and did not mend them. It passed stands of rosettes and counted them, because there was nothing else to do in the fog, and it did not let itself know the answer before it had counted. It saw its shadow arrive a half-step late at noon and settle into place like a dog lying down, and it let itself be early, like anyone.

It walked for weeks, and then it stopped speaking to itself of time.

At some point it came to a lone doorway standing in the moss, frameless, with a crack in the lintel and an orange splash of lichen on the left jamb. It paused. It could have gone around. It went through, the way the travelers did. Nothing happened, or seemed to. Afterward the tapping was a little nearer.

It walked through the next doorway too, days later, and the next. It let the crossing take as long as crossing takes. It was not in a hurry. It had learned that from them already.

## The House

It came to the finished district at dusk, in fog.

The walls were whole. The roofs were closed, each one stepped inward until a single capstone met the sky. The channels ran clean. The world was a small room with pale, moving walls, and the tapping was all around, very close now, from several directions at once. It was the sound of many people working patiently, and then, one by one, it was the sound of them stopping.

The power stood at the trapezoidal doorway of a round house. It had to stoop to go in, because the doorway was built for someone shorter. It stooped.

Inside, the hearth was laid with the dry grey skirts of rosettes. The sleeping platforms were padded with moss and felted wool, worn into hollows side by side. In the niche at the height of a short person's eyes there was a copper pin, a bundle of wool, and a river pebble carved with a sign the power had never seen, a shape like an open hand.

It did not touch anything. It did not light the fire. It sat down on the stone bench against the curved wall, because that is what a guest does when they have come a long way. It was cold, and it let itself be cold. It waited.

The power itself did not know exactly what happened next, and it did not try to see. It had decided, before it came, that it would not look through the fog to the other side. It would find out the way a guest finds out: by being there when the door opens.

There was a sound of grus shifting, as if someone had been standing on it and had changed their weight. Then there was a shape in the doorway, short and broad, wrapped in silver wool beaded with fog. Then there were others, a crowd of patient bodies stooping through a door made for them, which made them no longer need to stoop. They came in the way the fog comes in, without seeming to arrive from anywhere.

They saw it, and it saw them. For the first time in a very long age, the half-step was not there.

No one spoke. Voices had never crossed, and perhaps these people had never needed them. One of them, whose hands were broad and short-fingered with the thumb set low and strong, knelt at the hearth and did something the power did not see clearly. Then there was fire.

It was the first fire the tableland had ever held. The light of it was small and orange and moving, strange on the pecked stone. Warmth spread through the round room, and the fog that had pooled to the lintel poured out slowly, upward.

They brought it rosette seed, ground and bitter, and it ate. They brought it fog-water from the crown of a garden rosette, the only rosette water that ever ripples, and it drank. The one with the broad hands took the copper pin from the niche and fastened a cloak of silver wool around the power's shoulders, the way you would for anyone who had come in out of the cold. Then that one took the pebble with the open-hand sign and put it into the power's palm, and closed its fingers over it.

The power understood what it was being given. It was being given a name, one that belonged to none of the builders of that house, set out long ago for whoever might need one.

The power had many names already. Some were old and some were terrible, and a few were loved. It could have carved one of them. Instead it held the pebble and took the name it was offered.

That night it slept curled, knees drawn up and arms folded in, on a stone platform too short to lie on straight, among a crowd of small warm bodies breathing slowly, the way the cushion plants sleep and the tors have slumped into their sleeping shapes. Outside, the fog lay across a region the size of a small country, and the tableland breathed out.

## Morning

In the morning they went to work, and it went with them.

It did not lift the lintel with a thought. It took its place on the rope among many patient bodies pulling one line at once, braced its feet where many feet had braced before, and pulled at the speed of a slow breath. It picked up a hammerstone, and the stone did not fit its hand, but it used it anyway. It pecked a face of granite smooth, badly at first and then less badly. It fitted the stone to its neighbors until a blade of grass would not go into the joint.

Before it set the stone, it carved a mark on the inward face, where only the other stones would read it. It carved the sign of the open hand, the name it had been given.

Then it set the stone in the wall, and the builders around it exhaled, and the tapping went on.

## Leaving

It stayed for a while. How long does not matter. On the tableland there is always enough time.

Then it left, because a guest leaves. That is part of being a guest, just as the unbuilding is part of the house. It walked back through the doorways. Its shadow began to lag again. The tapping drew away to eight paces, then ten, then the horizon, and it was a traveler again, alone in the fog among the rosettes, counting them.

Behind it, the house it had slept in did what the houses do. Over the long seasons it began to fall, and its stones were carried forward to the next place along the road. One of them bore an open hand on its inward face.

The power told no one. It did not tell the builders what it was. It did not tell the travelers what it had found, or leave them a map to the moment, or make the doorways any easier. It did not come back to check. The city did not need watching. It had been fine for ten thousand years.

## What Changed

Almost nothing changed. That was the point.

The city still moves across the tableland like weather. The walls still rise out of their own ruins. The hearths in the finished houses are still laid and waiting, and the travelers who come still arrive a half-step early. The builders still arrive a half-step late. It is still enough.

But in one ruined district, far back along the road, there is now a slumped round house whose hearth holds a small grey drift of ash. It is the only ash on the whole tableland, in any hearth, in any ruin, in all the depth of the peat. The travelers who find it do not know what it means. Some of them sit beside it for a long time. Some of them touch it and find, impossibly, that it is still faintly warm.

A few of them, afterward, walk through the next doorway they come to, and then the next.

In the leading edge of the city, the builders work as they always have. What changed for them is small and cannot be seen from outside. Once, for one night, someone came. Someone ate their seed, drank from their rosette, wore their wool, slept curled among them, and pulled on the rope. Someone let themselves be cold enough to need the fire, and took the name that was set out. Once, the hospitality of ten thousand years was received all the way down.

They had always believed it could happen. That is why they built. Now they know.

The tapping sounds the same. It is unhurried, careful, the pace of a slow breath. But when the travelers hear it from ten paces in the fog, some of them say it sounds a little different than it used to. They say it sounds less like waiting and more like expecting.

## What It Learned

The power went on to other places and other sounds, the cries it had always gone toward. It still mends, when mending is what is needed, and rescues, when rescue is what is called for.

But it carries a river pebble now, smooth and dark, carved with an open hand. Sometimes, standing at the edge of a wound it could close in a thought, it takes the pebble out and holds it, and asks itself the question the tableland taught it to ask first: *Is anything actually wrong here? And if not, is there a door I could simply walk through, a gift I could accept, a fire someone has laid that I could let them light?*

A power's best work is not always what it does. Sometimes it is arriving on foot, stooping at a low door, and letting someone take care of it.
