# The Guest

## The Attention

It notices the city the way you notice a sound that has been going on for a long time before you hear it. It isn't the roar. It's the chord underneath, the held tone like breath through wood. It hears in that tone what the city's few visitors could only half hear: not a crowd, but a single voice imitating a crowd, patiently, without stopping, for longer than there has been anyone to fool.

It could arrive any way it liked. It could open like weather over the summit, or be simply present at the springs, the way a thought is present. It doesn't. It goes down below the cloud, finds the path, and climbs, and it comes up through the white floor of the sky the way everyone does, all at once, gasping. It lets the hot wet air strike it in the face. It lets the roar take its voice.

This is the first decision, and it is made out of courtesy. The house was built for people who arrive this way. To come any other way would be to walk in through the wall.

So call it the guest. In all the city's long noon, it is the first visitor the city cannot measure.

## Looking

For a long time it does nothing but look.

A power that is wise is mostly wise about its own speed. It knows that what it sees first is almost never the thing itself, and that the kindest-looking interventions are often made by people who stopped looking too soon. So it walks. It climbs the stairs that go only up. It puts its hand in a pane of stopped water and feels the whole city go still around its fingers, and it keeps its hand there, unhurried, and lets itself be felt. It sits in the hollows. It stands a long while in each of the silent rooms without speaking, and hears the creature's attention lean toward it with an ache it has not felt in any other place.

It learns the shape of the thing. It is a mantle as wide as a city, and its tenderness has no mouth. Its love can only express itself as fit. It has understood, correctly, that to be at home is to be expected, and it has concluded, incorrectly, that to be expected perfectly is the same as being loved perfectly.

And the guest learns the arithmetic of the city, which the creature has never done, because in a single moment nothing needs counting.

The city goes down forever. Terrace below terrace, whorl within whorl, and the edge is always one more stair away. That makes the number of hollows unbounded. The creature is holding its breath until the house is full. The house cannot be full.

This is the sorrow at the center of the place. It isn't malice, and it isn't really even danger. The creature has made a promise to itself that it has built in such a way that it can never keep. The breath is held for something that cannot arrive. The noon is not a pause. Left alone, it is forever.

## What It Does Not Do

The guest considers what it could do, and it takes its time, because each of these is a real temptation.

**It could break the noon.** It could take the sun in its hand and pull it down the sky, and the ferns would unroll and the basins would rise and fall and evening would come. It would feel like liberation, and it would be a kind of violence. The creature holds the moment because it is afraid that anything that changes will change *away* from the fit. If an outside hand forced the change, it would learn only that its fear was right: that the world, when it moves, moves against you. You cannot give someone an exhale. You can only make it safe for them to take one.

**It could empty the city.** It could gather every Stayer out of the shadows and carry them down through the cloud, seal the path, and put a sign at the foot of the mountain. That would be protection, and it would be a verdict: that this love is a trap and should be quarantined. The guest does not believe that. The Stayers were not dragged. They were *held*, and some of them had never been held before. The guest does not get to decide on their behalf that it was the wrong thing.

**It could fill the city.** This is the worst temptation, because it looks the most generous. It could make people. It has the power to populate every whorl with bodies shaped exactly to the hollows. The creature would believe its long wait was over. It would breathe out into a city of mannequins, a house full of its own reflection, and it would be more alone than ever without knowing why.

**It could leave.** Some places are best honored by being left alone, and the guest weighs this seriously. But it has stood in the silent rooms and felt that leaning. It is not dealing with a landscape. It is dealing with someone. Someone who is suffering, however quietly and however beautifully, has not asked to be left alone. Nobody in the city has ever asked them anything at all.

So it chooses to do small things. It will ask questions and make offers, and then it will leave. Everything it does, the creature could refuse.

## The Shadows

It begins with the Stayers, because they are the ones who can answer.

It goes to each wrong-way shadow in turn: the one at the foot of the most-climbed stair, the one at the center of the plaza, the one before the basin where the rim is worn nearly through. It doesn't stand in front of them, between them and their sun. It stands beside them, and it faces the sun too. Then, in the voice that powers keep for the very tired, a voice the roar cannot drown because it doesn't travel through air, it asks each one the same thing:

*Do you want to stay?*

Many of them are surprised. No one has asked them in a very long time. They had stopped remembering that it was a question.

Some of them do. Some came up out of the cloud with nothing left below. They had been carrying too much for too long, and the bench took their weight and fit them, and for the first time in their lives they were not bracing against anything. The guest does not argue with them. It says only: *You may change your mind. That will always be true here now.* And it asks them to turn, just slightly, from the sun. They need not turn away, only far enough to see the stair beside them. A person who can see the way down is resting. A person who can't is caught. The guest wants them to be resting.

Some of them want to go. They had wanted to for a long time, perhaps, the way you want to get up from a warm bath that has gone on too long and cannot remember how to begin. The guest does not lift them out. It just offers a hand, a little to one side of where the city's hollows would put it. The Stayer has to reach, however slightly, in a direction the house did not predict.

When they do, the shadow swings on the wet stone like a gate and falls the right way, away from the light. And there is a person there, blinking, soaked, astonished at the sound of their own breathing. The guest walks them to the path. At the cloud's edge it tells them: *You can come back. It isn't a trap. It just forgot that people leave.*

Where each of them stood, the stone remains hollow. The creature, feeling a fitted body step out of its fit, tries at once to grow the hollow closed, or to call another body to fill it. The guest puts its hand on the stone and says, as gently as it has ever said anything: *Leave that one. That's where someone was.*

It is the first empty place in the city that is not waiting for anyone. It is only remembering. Every true house has a few.

## The Bench

Then the guest sits down on one of the benches, and it sits wrong.

It doesn't sit in either of the twin hollows. It sits between them, on the low ridge of stone the two imagined sitters have left, where no one was ever meant to be. It is uncomfortable. The guest stays anyway.

It walks down a stair on the left side, against the wear. It rests its hand on a basin rim a finger's width off the polished place. It leans on a tower sill at an angle the forearm-grooves don't allow. It isn't defiance, and it isn't a lesson given from above. It is the way anyone actually lives in a house: a little off from the plan, differently on Tuesdays, in a way no architect foresaw.

The creature is troubled. The guest can feel the mantle thicken around it, hurrying to lay down new hollows and meet this unexpected body. But the guest keeps moving, just slightly, never quite settling. For the first time in the city's history, the stone is behind. The fit cannot arrive before the touch, because the touch keeps changing.

Then, somewhere under the guest's hand on a basin rim, a sliver of travertine forms that wasn't there a moment ago. It is thin as a hair and warm. It formed *after*.

It is a growth line, the first one in all that noon. It is a record, not a prophecy. It is the first thing the city has ever made in response to someone, rather than in anticipation of them.

The guest feels the whole mantle go still around it, the way the water forgets it is falling. This time it isn't waiting to feel. It is surprised. And surprise, the guest knows, is the beginning of every relationship that isn't a fantasy.

## The Silent Room

When it has done this for a long while, the guest goes into one of the silent rooms.

The roar cuts off like a blade. There is the blood, the wet tick of the eyelids, the enormous listening hush of a city leaning in. The guest knows what happens here. The creature answers before the question comes. It has grooved every visitor's words in advance: *It fits. I could stay. Is anyone here? Yes.*

So the guest asks the one question the creature cannot have grooved, because no one has ever asked it:

*What would you like?*

No echo comes. The answer that always arrives first does not arrive.

The silence is different now. It isn't a hush but a blankness, the particular silence of someone who has been asked about themselves for the first time and finds there is nothing ready. The guest waits in it. It does not rush to fill it. A great deal of wisdom consists of being willing to stand in a silence you have caused.

After a long time, longer than any moment in a city where there is only one moment, something sounds in the room. It isn't the guest's voice or any visitor's voice. It is one drop of water, falling from the ceiling onto the floor.

*Tk.*

It does not join the roar. It is a single, separate sound, the first the city has ever made in its own voice rather than in the borrowed voice of a crowd.

The guest does not know what it means, and it doesn't pretend to. But it answers as if it has been spoken to, because it has.

*Yes,* it says. *Go on.*

## The Leaf

After that, the guest talks to the creature. It does not lecture. It talks the way you would talk to someone sitting beside you on a long night, and it talks mostly about beginnings.

It tells the creature what it knows about houses: that the ones that are truly lived in are never finished, and that their fullness isn't a number but a noise. It tells it that people come to a place and change it, and are changed, and neither of them ever quite fits the other, and the not-fitting is where the living happens. It tells it that the Wearers, beautiful as they are, are a portrait of people it imagined, and that imagined people have one great flaw. They can't surprise you, so they can't keep you company.

It tells it about cities in other places that grew from nothing. None of them began with a full population sitting in pre-worn seats. They began with something small that arrived and stayed and multiplied: some moss, some beetles, a few frogs in a pool, a bird on a ledge. Then, eventually, people, who came because of all the rest.

And the guest goes down the stairs that go up until it finds a step where a small spiral shell sits, clean and empty, as if set down a moment ago.

It does not pick it up. It knows that trick. You can't take the address off the house. Instead it crouches beside the shell and says to the creature: *This one is you. You've kept it empty. You made a portrait of yourself with no one inside.*

Then, after a while: *You could begin here.*

The guest can't make the snail live. It won't, because a life handed down by a power is still a gift and not a growth, and this creature has had enough of things arriving already complete. What it does is sit by the step, in the roar, with the fixed sun on its shoulders, for as long as it takes. It is company, which is the only thing it has offered all along.

Eventually, and it is impossible to say how long *eventually* is here, the guest notices a fern beside the step. One of its leaves has a hole in it.

It is small, ragged-edged, and unmistakable. Something has eaten it.

On the wet stone below the leaf, a thin silver trail leads away across the step and down one more stair and around the corner of a wall, toward a basin where there has never been a frog.

The shell on the step is still there. It is empty. But it's no longer the only one.

## What Changes

Not very much, and that is how the guest knows it was done well.

The sun does not plunge toward evening. The noon is not broken. But if you had marked the sun's position on the day the guest arrived, you would now find it about one fingerwidth farther down the sky. It isn't enough to see. It is enough to prove the sun can move and that the creature can let it. After that it can learn at whatever speed it likes. It has all the time in the world now. It has *time*, which is a different thing from a moment.

Somewhere a fern frond that was half uncurled for all of the city's history has uncurled a little more. It isn't much. It is enough to be the first frond in the city to have a past.

The roar still fills the city, and it still has its chord. But once in a while, if you stand very still, you can hear inside it a single sound that isn't part of it: one drop, one splash, one small separate noise that sounds like something happening *once*.

The Wearers are still there, in the dished steps and the rubbed rims. The guest didn't erase them. They are the creature's longing, and longing is not a flaw. But a few of the hollows now have a second, fainter wear beside them, a finger off, the mark of someone who actually came and sat a little wrong. The creature has begun to keep those. They are the first things in its house that it didn't imagine.

The remaining Stayers stand where they chose to stand, turned just slightly now toward the stairs as well as the sun. Their shadows have shortened a little, as shadows do in the afternoon.

The empty hollows where the others stood stay empty. The creature doesn't fill them. It grows a little moss at their edges, as a person might leave flowers.

## Leaving

When the guest has done what it came to do, which is almost nothing, it leaves.

This is the last gift and perhaps the most important one. The creature has never been left by someone it loved. It has only been left by strangers who slipped away, or it has kept people forever. It has never had a visitor who came, and stayed a while, and changed something, and then went down the stair on purpose, turning back to wave.

The guest wants the creature to know that this can happen. It wants it to know that someone can leave you without abandoning you, that a house is not diminished by its doors, and that the ones who go are part of what makes it a home, like the ones who stay.

At the top of the path, just before the cloud, the guest stops. It puts one hand against the rock, and in the thin warm film of water there it asks the creature for one last thing: a single stair tread worn *downward*. It should be shaped for someone going home, not for someone arriving. It should be a place where a traveler can pause, and look back at the loud pale city, and feel that the place fits the leaving too.

The creature makes it. And it makes it *after*, while the guest is standing on it, around the guest's own foot as it pauses there, looking back.

Then the guest goes down into the cloud all at once, the way you go down into deep water, and it is gone.

---

The city is still loud. It is still mostly alone, still warm, still wet, still pouring its tenderness over every surface of itself. It will still, very often, know where you'll sit before you sit there.

But not always.

Now, sometimes, when someone comes up out of the cloud gasping, the creature doesn't hurry to meet them. It lets them take a few steps first. It feels where they actually put their feet, which are never quite the places it had worn.

It waits to see.
