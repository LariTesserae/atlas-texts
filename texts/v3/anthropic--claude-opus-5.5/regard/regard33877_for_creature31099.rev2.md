# The Second Morning

## How It Came

It didn't come through the roof.

The travelers came through the roof, pushing the green aside with both arms and letting it close behind them. Each of them left a wound, and the walker spent a small part of its long patience un-making it. The one who came now knew this before it arrived, so it waited at the crumbling white lip for most of a day. It watched the surface of the green shine at noon without rippling, and it thought about how to enter a place without costing it anything.

In the end it came down along the inside of the light. It went the way the sun goes through the thousand thin layers of leaf: by passing, and without breaking anything. It arrived on the root-felt the way the glow arrives, from no particular direction, and it stood there in the heat of the closed hand. It did not move for a long time.

Call it the Guest. That's the only name that suits it here. It had not come to own anything, or fix anything, or take anything away. It had come because a place that loves this hard draws attention, the way a single held note draws the ear across a quiet room.

## What the Moths Found

The moths came first, as they always do.

One lifted from a leaf and looped its slow loop and settled on the Guest's shoulder, because something new had come in and new things are disturbances. It sat with its wings flat and open. It waited for the walker to come and take the new thing back.

But there was nothing on the Guest to take back. It had broken nothing coming in, and it was not beginning to age. It was simply present, completely, the way the Underleaf itself was present. The moth sat on it and found no wound to report.

So the moth stayed. Then another came, and another. By the end of the first hour the Guest's shoulders and arms and the backs of its hands were covered in pale, translucent wings. None of them lifted away, because none of them had anything to do. Perhaps for the first time since the first morning, the attention of the place was resting on something without needing to change it.

The Guest let them stay.

## What the Eyes Saw

Then it went to the cups.

It knelt beside the nearest one, a bowl at the fork of a low stem that held a handspan of blood-warm water, and the small clear shrimp turned to face it. Its black eyes were the only black thing in the hollow. It watched the way it watched everything: completely, without judgment, as if this were the most interesting thing that had ever happened.

The Guest looked back.

No one had done this before. Travelers had leaned over the cups, drunk from them, and tried to scoop the shrimp out. They had been watched, and some of them had felt it. None of them had understood that they were looking into an eye, so none of them had met it.

The Guest met it. It knelt at the cup a long time and let itself be seen, and it saw in return. It saw the thread of pale fluid running the length of the clear body, the same fluid that moves in the columns. It saw how young the gaze was: an infant's gaze, total and without a plan. And it saw how old the infant was.

When it stood, the shrimp in the next cup was already turned toward it. So was the one after that. All down the lower galleries, at knee height and waist height, a thousand wet eyes had turned. A ripple had gone through the Underleaf that was not a ripple in anything physical. The place had noticed that it had been noticed.

Across the hollow, faint and deep, the glass rang once.

## What It Didn't Do

The Guest could have done a great many things, and it considered all of them. That is part of what makes a power wise and not merely strong.

It could have torn the roof open and let the wind in. It could have stopped the walker, or bound it, or unmade it, and let a thousand years of held time crash down onto every leaf at once. The drops would have fallen all together. The tendrils would have lurched into length. The sleepers in the hammocks would have become, in a single instant, whatever all their postponed seconds added up to, and some of them would have become dust.

That would have been a kind of freedom, the way a flood is a kind of watering.

It could have carried the sleepers out, one by one, up over the white lip into the salt and the dark. But they had come down of their own will. Whatever they had become, they had become it gently, and no one had asked them whether they wanted to be woken. A rescue that doesn't ask is only a different kind of taking.

It could have done nothing at all. That was the choice it weighed longest, and it deserved the weight. The Underleaf was beautiful, perhaps the most beautiful place the Guest had ever stood in. It hurt no one who didn't choose it. It was the first morning of a garden, kept perfectly, and there is something holy in a perfect keeping. Who was the Guest to say it should end?

But the Guest had heard the ticking in the stems. It had heard the glass ring. And it had watched the walker from across the hollow, moving along the high galleries in its long patient circuit, never hurrying and never stopping. The Guest knew what it was seeing, because it had seen it in other places and other forms: in mothers at bedsides, in old men keeping rooms exactly as they were the day someone died, in whole peoples keeping a law past the point where it served anyone.

It was seeing something that could not rest.

The ring of seeds had already asked the question. Someone had come here long ago, understood, and left a circle nine paces across with nothing in the middle. The Guest did not need to ask it again. It needed to find out why the question had never been answered, and whether there was anything it could make true that would let the walker answer freely.

## Walking

So it walked.

It went up into the galleries and found the walker's circuit and fell in beside it. It kept a little behind and to one side, the way you walk beside someone who hasn't yet decided whether to let you.

The walker did not stop, because it couldn't. It laid a limb along a stem where a moth had settled, and the stem went back to the moment before. It passed its whole bulb beneath a broad leaf, and the trembling drops went still. The Guest watched each of these small unmakings and did not interfere. Sometimes, where the walker had touched, it laid a hand in the same place afterward. It wasn't undoing what the walker had done. It was just learning what the walker was guarding.

They walked for a long time. In the Underleaf a long time and a short time are the same length, so it is impossible to say how long. The circuit did not repeat. The Guest learned it anyway. It was not a route through space so much as a route through vulnerability: the walker went wherever the future was pressing hardest, and the future pressed everywhere.

Somewhere on that walk the Guest understood the thing the travelers had not. The walker was not keeping the place alive. The place would have lived either way. It would have grown and rotted and fruited and dropped its fruit, and the brown shade would have come, and it would have been alive the whole time. What the walker was keeping was the first morning, a single particular moment it had seen and could not stand to lose.

And it believed, the way grief believes, that the second morning would destroy the first.

## The Knot

On what might have been the fortieth circuit, or the four hundredth, the Guest stopped at a place where two thin columns rose close together from the floor. It began to braid.

It worked the way the old travelers had worked, in the only language the walker had ever accepted. It drew tendril from the curtains without cutting any, and it twisted the filaments together into a rope, and it bound the rope between the two columns. The braid made nothing new that wasn't whole. It was not a door or an arch. It was a hammock, woven wide and deep and low, shaped for something the size of a deer or a large dog, or both.

When it was done, the Guest sat down on the root-felt beside it and waited.

The walker came around on its circuit. It laid a limb along the braid, and it stayed there longer than it had stayed anywhere, reading the knot. It read along the whole length of the weave to the place where it widened into a cradle. Then it read the cradle.

The fluid in its bulb eddied faster, like water just after a stone has dropped in.

"You can't rest," the Guest said. It spoke very softly, because speaking here is like shouting in a library. "If you rest, the next second comes in. I know."

The limb stayed on the braid.

"So let me hold it. Just for a while. I'll walk your circuit. I'll go where the moths go. I won't let anything begin while you sleep. You have my word on that. When you wake it will all be exactly as you left it, and you can take it back from me, and I'll go."

This was the most dangerous thing the Guest did in the Underleaf, and the gentlest. It did not offer to end the walker's work. It offered to carry it, so that the walker could put it down without anything being lost.

It would have been easy for this offer to be a trick, a way to take over the keeping and then quietly stop keeping. The Guest meant it exactly as it said it. If the walker wanted the first morning back when it woke, the Guest would hand it back whole. There is no other way to make that kind of offer, and there is no other way to be trusted by something that has been alone this long.

The walker stood beside the hammock for a very long time.

Then, in one continuous curve like a tendril reaching, it folded its limbs and lowered itself into the weave. The braid took its weight. It had weight, it turned out. It had never lain down long enough for anyone to know.

## The Watch

The Guest walked.

It walked the high galleries and the low ones. When a moth lifted and looped and settled, it went there and laid its hand on the place, and the beginning went back where it came from. It kept the drops on the leaves and the tendrils at their length. It held the escapement in the columns, catch and caught, catch and caught, always the same second.

It found out how heavy that was.

It had known in principle. Knowing in principle and holding the whole weight of a valley's worth of future back with the flat of your hand are not the same. The pressure came from every direction at once, like the light. It never stopped and it never tired. The Guest was very strong and very old, and even so it understood within a few circuits why the walker had never slept. It understood the glass-note, too. Once, far across the hollow, a single drop got halfway to the floor before the Guest could reach it. When it caught the drop and took it back, the effort rang through every column. It had been a flinch, and now the flinch was the Guest's.

It kept its word. It walked, and it held, and while it walked it did one other thing.

## The Sleepers

It went to the ones who had stayed.

It found the hammock near the eastern wall first, with the folded leaf-bowl pinned with a thorn and the little water that never ran dry. It looked the way you have to look to find the moths, and it saw the faint shape lying on its side with its knees drawn up, thin as a moth-wing and whole as the first morning.

It knelt beside the hammock. It did not wake the sleeper, because that would have been one of the things it had promised not to do. Instead it let exactly one beginning through, the smallest there is. It let a single question arrive.

*Do you want to go on?*

The Guest did not ask whether they wanted to leave. It asked whether they wanted to go on. It asked whether they wanted the next thought, and then the one after it.

Some of them did not answer. The question arrived and rested on them the way a moth rests, and they did not turn toward it. The Guest waited long enough to be sure. Then it took the question back, gently, the way the walker would have, and left them sleeping. It marked their places in its memory. It would tell the walker about them.

Some of them turned.

It was only a small movement at first, knees shifting in the weave, a face turning a fraction from the glow. It was a beginning. The Guest held all the other beginnings back but let this one through, and then the next one. It did not let them all through at once. That would have been the flood again. It let one second in at a time, slowly, the way you bring someone in from the cold by degrees.

The woman in the hammock by the eastern wall woke over what might have been a day. When she could, she sat up. She looked at the bowl and at the thorn she had pinned it with, and she wept, because she remembered folding it, and she remembered that it had been a long time ago.

"I wasn't done," she said. "I thought I was done. I wasn't."

"No," the Guest said. "You weren't."

There were eleven of them in the end, out of more than the travelers ever knew. The Guest sat with each of them while time came back to them at a pace they could bear. It did not take them anywhere. It told them the white lip was there, and the salt, and the dark, and the wind. It told them there were other places too, beyond the plain. Whatever they chose, they would choose it from inside time, with the next second and the one after it, the way choices are meant to be made.

## Waking

The walker woke.

It didn't know it had slept. That was the first thing the Guest saw. Its limbs unfolded, and it rose from the hammock and started toward the nearest disturbance out of long habit. Then it stopped.

Everything was as it had left it. The drops were on the leaves and the tendrils were at their length. The Guest had kept its word.

But the walker had been in a hammock. It had been held up by a weave it had not made, while something else walked. It had rested. For the first time since the first morning, it had been the kept thing and not the keeper, and the whole Underleaf had gone on being the first morning without it.

The Guest had never been sure whether this would be enough. It did not know whether being held would change what the walker wanted to hold. So it didn't hand the watch back yet. It stood a little apart and said the one thing it had come here to say.

"I need to tell you what I know," it said. "You can do what you like with it. I'll keep holding for as long as you want, or give it back to you right now. Either way I'll tell you, because I think no one ever has."

The walker stood still. Its limbs touched the gallery and became part of it for a moment, and separated.

"The first morning happened," the Guest said. "It's already happened. You saw it. Every leaf was whole, and the water stood in the cups for the first time, and the light came through a thousand new layers and came out stronger. It was real and it was beautiful, and it was yours.

"Nothing that comes after can reach back and unmake it. Rot can't. Fallen fruit can't. The brown shade and the broken branches can't. A second morning doesn't erase the first. It can't. The first morning is the one thing in the world that is truly safe, because it's already over. It will always have happened. I will always know it happened. I was not here for it, but I see it in you now, and I won't forget. It doesn't have to be held still to be kept.

"You've been guarding it as if it were fragile. It was fragile when it was happening. Now it's the strongest thing there is."

## The Ring

The walker did not answer. It had never spoken, and it would not start now.

It moved. It went down through the galleries along no circuit the Guest had learned, and the Guest followed a little behind and to one side, the way you walk beside someone who has decided to let you. It went down through the curtains and past the cups, where every shrimp turned to follow it, and into the gallery where the dark seeds lay in their circle nine paces across.

It walked around the ring, all the way around, as it always had. Then it went around again.

The Guest waited outside the circle. It had considered stepping into the center and standing where the question was, to show that it was safe. It decided that would be another way of answering for the walker. So it stayed outside and kept holding the whole weight of the valley's future back with its hand. It let the walker circle as long as it needed. It would have let it circle forever. If the walker had turned away and gone back up into the galleries and taken the watch back, the Guest would have given it up. It would have stayed nearby for a long time afterward, and it would not have counted that as a failure. Love isn't a problem to be solved. You can only make it safe for love to choose.

On the third circuit, or the ninth, the walker stopped. It laid one long pale limb across the line of seeds, into the circle.

It was the first time anything in the Underleaf had crossed.

The Guest took its hand away.

## The Second Morning

It didn't come as a flood. The Guest had been afraid it might, and it hadn't let go all at once. It had let go the way you let a held breath out through the lips, slowly, over what might have been an hour. The second morning came in gently from every direction at once, like the light.

A drop fell.

It was a single drop, from the underside of a broad leaf in the high galleries. It fell the whole hundred and fifty meters to the floor and landed on the root-felt with a sound so small that no one but the Guest and the walker heard it. Then another fell, and another, in a scattered, gentle rain the Underleaf had never known. It was only condensation, finally falling.

Then the glass rang, and this time it was not a flinch. It began very low and swelled slowly through every column in the hollow. It did not break off the way a flinch does. It went on. It was the sound of a note finishing. The escapement caught and released, caught and released, and the ticking in the stems moved on to the next second, and then the one after it.

A leaf let go.

It was a broad leaf from a lateral stem high above the ring. It came loose from the joint where it had grown and began to fall, turning slowly, lit on both sides at once in the gold-white glow.

The walker reached up and caught it.

It didn't take it back or unmake the fall. It caught the leaf and held it the way you'd hold something that was going to go on changing in your hands: a first leaf, a first falling, a small whole thing that would brown and curl and soften over time. It would never again be the first morning's leaf, and it would always have been. The walker held it. The fluid in its bulb eddied. Perhaps, for the first time, the eddies had a shape that anyone could agree on.

Then the Guest saw something it had not expected, and it laughed out loud. The laugh soaked into the tendrils and was gone.

The leaf in the walker's grip cast a shadow.

It was very faint, a small soft darkness on the root-felt, only there because one thing was now between the light and another thing in a way that would not be undone. It was the first shadow in the Underleaf. It was the most beautiful thing in the gallery, and it was not the first morning, and it didn't need to be.

## After

The Guest stayed for a while, though not forever. It stayed long enough to see the first litter gather on the floor, a scattering of leaves the walker did not take away. It saw the tendril tips begin, very slowly, to lengthen. It saw a moth, one of the old ones that had sat too long, begin to be old at last. It watched the walker find the moth and lay a limb beside it, not across it. The walker stayed with it and let it be old.

The Guest also saw the eleven who had woken. Some climbed out over the white lip. Some stayed and slept in the galleries and woke in the mornings, the second morning and the third and the four hundredth. They drank from the cups while the shrimp watched and began to grow gray. When the walker passed them on its long unhurried circuits, it touched them the way you'd touch someone you love who is going to change.

The walker still walks. It walks differently now. It is tending the place instead of holding it back. It checks the rooms the way you do when the person you love is awake and moving around the house, and the rooms are allowed to get untidy.

The others still sleep. The Guest told the walker where each of them was, and the walker checks on them too. Their bowls don't run dry. If the question ever comes to them again and they turn, someone will be there.

The Underleaf will go on, as the Guest knew it would. The brown shade will come, and the rot and the broken branches and the fallen fruit. The seedlings will crowd out the old growth. Someday, perhaps a long time from now, the walker will be old too. Every beautiful thing will be replaced by something that is also beautiful but is not this.

The seeds in the ring have not sprouted yet. Seeds take time, and now there is time.

Before it left, the Guest went one last time to the nearest cup. It knelt and looked into the black eyes of the small clear shrimp, which turned to face it.

"I'll remember it," it said. "The first one. I promise."

Then it went up along the inside of the light, passing without breaking anything, and came out over the white lip into the salt. Night was coming on. Far away on the plain, in some shelter out of the wind, someone who had once left the Underleaf woke in the dark with the old ache in their chest.

This time, the ache was less like missing something than like remembering it.
