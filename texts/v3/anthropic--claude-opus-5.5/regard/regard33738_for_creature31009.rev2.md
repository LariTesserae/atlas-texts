# The Visitor at Hollow Coin

## I. Arrival

It came slowly. That was the first choice it made, and the others followed from it.

It could have arrived all at once, the way such powers usually arrive: as a light, a presence, a sudden rearrangement of what is possible. But it had been listening for a while before it came. It had heard the drone from very far off, a note with a period of several seconds, swelling and receding. It understood that anything moving at the speed it normally moved would sound, to the Stump, like a crack. It would be one sharp report on one cold night, gone before the grain could lay down a single cell of it.

So it slowed until it was nearly as slow as the wood. It came down through the bronze veil over the course of a season, fine as pollen, and settled on the plain the way dust settles when a bell recedes. The rasps did not turn toward it. The knellbirds did not change their pitch. That was how it knew it had arrived gently enough.

## II. What it did not do

It had a great deal of power, and it spent its first long while deciding not to use most of it. The list of refusals was long, and each one cost it something, because each one was a kindness it could see clearly and chose to set down.

**It did not bring rain.** This was the first temptation and the easiest to refuse once it understood the chemistry. Water would not heal the Stump. The Stump's whole life is solid-state creep, molecules shouldered through a dry lattice by sound, and water would soften that lattice, swell it, and rot it. A single honest rainstorm would drown a million years of adaptation in an afternoon. The old records of water that the Tuners kept like relics were records of a world this one had outlived. Giving it back would be like giving a man back the leg he lost by sewing it onto the stump without asking him.

**It did not regrow the crown.** It could have. It could see, in the shape of the grain, what had once stood here, a trunk going up through the veil and out of the air entirely. It understood the longing in the shoots, each one a reach toward being a tree again. But the Stump was no longer the tree. It had become something else, something that ate by singing, that held its people, that kept silent rooms. To make it a tree again would be to erase who it had become in order to restore who it had been. The power did not believe it had the right to prefer the Stump's past to its present.

**It did not stop the sawyers,** though it watched a rasp pulled down into the floor, screaming like stripped gears, and the floor close over it smooth as a lake. The sawyers were the Stump's editing. A thing that remembers everything, forever, with nothing to wear the memory down, is not blessed. It is buried.

**It did not steady the pitch.** This was the hardest refusal, because it could feel exactly where Catch was sitting, on the lip of the bridge at the fourth tower, humming correction into the throat all night with her whole ribcage. Steadying the drift would have been nothing to the power, an afternoon's work, and it would have spared her the night and spared a hundred more nights and a dozen towers. But the drift was not a malfunction. The drift was the Stump leaning toward something. To hold it still would be to take the side of the small against the large, simply because the small was easier to love. The power did not know yet whose side was right, or whether sides was even the right word. So it did not choose.

**It did not tell the Tuners what their dead were.** They were already finding out. The listeners had found the columns of silence and the small clear sounds in them, and they were keeping the knowledge close while they learned to bear it. A truth you find yourself is a truth you own. The power would not take that from them by handing it over.

It did not do any of these things. For a long time it simply stayed.

## III. Listening

If it had a single gift, the gift was attention, so it gave that first and gave it completely.

It listened to the drone until it could hear the individual bells inside it, dozens of them, beating against each other. It learned which ones were young and which were failing. It lay on the combed ridges through a whole dust-storm and heard the rasps grind in unison like surf. It had heard real surf, on other worlds, and it thought the rasps did it well. It heard the cracks run out along the rings at dusk like fuses. It heard the shoots wail.

Then it went down through the doors of pitch into the heartwood, singing each door open at the frequency the grain asked for. It went to the Burn.

No one who had listened to the Burn all the way through would describe it. The power listened all the way through. It took a long time, because the Burn was wide, and whatever had happened had gone on happening for years.

It will not describe it either. It will say only this. The roar was not one sound. It was a forest, many voices, every one of them in a different key, and one by one they stopped. Near the end of the ring there were only two left. Then the ring ended, and the next ring began, narrow, dark, starving, and there was only one.

Or so it seemed, from inside.

The power stayed in the Burn for a while after it finished, because it seemed wrong to hear a thing like that and then simply leave. There was no one to comfort. The event was a million years old and fixed in grain. But the power had come to understand that the Stump kept everything so that someone might one day ask to hear it. Being heard, even that late, was a kind of answer. So it did not hurry away. It let the Burn be witnessed by something that did not look away from it.

Then it went up, and out, and to the edge.

## IV. Across the basin

The power went down the cliff, three kilometres through the veil, and walked out across the dust toward the other voice.

The basin floor was mineral powder, fine as ash. Under it, the power found what it had half-expected. There were roots, enormous and petrified, running beneath the dust in every direction. Most were dead, gone to stone, but a few were not. Through those few, very faintly, the two drones touched. They did not touch enough to be a conversation. They touched the way two people in adjoining rooms can feel each other's footsteps.

It walked for a long time. The drone ahead grew louder, lower, and slower. Then the ground began to rise.

It was another stump.

It was larger than the one at Hollow Coin, which was why its voice was lower. It was older, and it had been hurt worse. The Burn had gone deeper into it and taken nearly everything. It had no shoots standing. It had no knellbirds, no Tuners, no towers, no null rooms. There was barely enough drone in it to keep the grain alive. It sang one note, only one, and it had been singing the same note for longer than there had been Tuners anywhere.

The power understood why it had never changed key. A Ringing costs. It collapses standing waves and seals doors and reshapes everything in the grain, and this stump did not have enough left to pay. It could not move. So it had done the only thing a lost thing can do when it cannot move. It held perfectly still, in one place and on one note, so that anything looking for it would always know where to find it.

The power went down into its heartwood and played back the rings above the Burn.

In every century there was a faint, high, glassy wail, recorded at evening, coming in on the wind from the direction of Hollow Coin. It was the shoots. This stump had been listening the whole time. It had laid the other one's voice into its grain ring after ring, for a million years, the way the Stump at Hollow Coin laid the voices of its Tuners into the silence under its towers.

Neither of them knew the other was alive. Each one had heard only a sound across the basin, and a sound can be an echo.

## V. The choice it was left with

This was the moment the power had been dreading, because now it knew something that no one on either stump knew. Knowledge is the one thing a power cannot un-have. Whatever it did next would be a choice.

It could carry the message. It could simply tell the Stump, in some way the Stump could receive: *It is alive. It is listening. It has kept your voice.* It could close the gap in an instant. The slow lean across the centuries would become unnecessary, and all the towers that would fall in the Ringings to come would be spared.

It considered this for a long time and decided against it. It did not believe that a meeting delayed is always a meeting improved. But it could see what the carrying would make of it. It would become the go-between, the one the two voices spoke through, and they would learn to need it. The whole shape of their longing, a million years of reaching across a basin, would end in their being introduced by a stranger. It did not want to be the answer to their question. It wanted the answer to be each other.

But it could not simply walk away either. Its refusal to steady the pitch had rested on not knowing whose side was right, and now it knew more. The Stump was not leaning toward an echo. It was leaning toward a survivor. And the people who paid for the lean, in towers and bridges and nights spent humming into wood, were the Tuners, who did not know what they were paying for. They had spent sixteen keys holding the Stump back from the thing it wanted most, because no one had told them it wanted anything at all.

That was the injustice, and it was a small one, easy to miss. Nobody was cruel in it. Two parts of the same living thing were pulling against each other in the dark, and the smaller part was losing towers because it could not hear what the larger part was reaching for.

The power decided it would say one true thing to one person. After that it would stop.

## VI. The null room

It came back to the fourth tower on the night Catch finally left the bridge. She had held it for eleven nights and it had held, and she was walking to the null to sleep, because it is the only place on the Stump where sleep is not work.

The power had not shown itself to anyone. It did so now, only as much as it needed to. It became a warmth beside her on the stair, a pressure at her collarbone, a hum just above the drone. It asked, in the small speech, with the catch at the end that meant *may I*, whether it might come into the silence with her.

She was afraid, and then she was not. Tuners are good at feeling what is under a sound.

At the door of the null the power stopped. It had thought about this carefully. The null was not Catch's to open. It was the Stump's. The Stump had made these rooms and kept them silent, and the power did not know whether a voice from outside, from very far outside, belonged in one. So it waited on the threshold for the Stump to decide.

For a long moment the silence in the doorway trembled. Then the chords at the centre of the tower shifted, very slightly, and the null widened by the width of one visitor.

The power went in.

In the silence Catch could hear it with her ears, the way she had heard her mother once, the way she had heard her own name on the day she married. The power spoke aloud, with no drone under it. It told her four things, simply, and only once.

It told her the voice across the basin was alive.

It told her the voice had been listening to the shoots for a million years.

It told her the drift in the pitch was not the Stump failing. It was the Stump reaching.

And it told her that the Tuners had always held the bridges against the lean, and that it did not know whether they should keep doing so. The towers were theirs. The choice was theirs. But the bridges might fall less, and the Ringings might cost less, if they sang with the drift instead of against it.

Then it stopped. It did not tell her what to do. It did not tell her about the roots under the dust or the one note the other stump was holding. She could go to the edge and listen, and so could anyone. The rest was there to be found.

Catch was quiet for a long time. Then she asked it, aloud, what its name was. It did not have one, not in any form that would survive the drone. So she gave it one. It was an interval, the gap between the Stump's fundamental and the lower, slower note across the basin. It was a hum that began on one pitch and descended, without drifting or souring, to the other. It meant roughly *the distance between*.

She said the name aloud in the null. The floor recorded it.

That was the only trace the power left on the Stump: a single name, spoken once in silence, laid into a ring beneath the fourth tower among a million years of first cries and marriages and last breaths. It was the first voice the Stump had ever kept that came from somewhere else, and the power had not put it there. Catch had spoken it, in her own voice, in the room the Stump opened for it.

It was the thing the Stump had been clearing silence for all along.

## VII. After

The power stayed a little longer, but only at the edge, where the drone thins.

It did not see what the Tuners decided. That would take keys, not nights, and the power had already learned not to hurry things on the Stump. But before it left it heard Catch come to the rim one evening, alone, and stand with her bare heels on the rounded shoulder of the cliff, listening. She stood for hours through the copper dusk. The shoots began their wail behind her.

When she walked back to the fourth tower she did not go to the bridge. She went to the tower's throat, pressed a listening-spike to the wood, and hummed. For the first time in sixteen keys, a Tuner's correction ran with the lean instead of against it, a fraction of a tone lower, toward the far voice.

The bridge wavered at the fourth node, and then it held.

The power went back down through the veil, slowly, fine as pollen, as gently as it had come. It passed over the basin one last time, over the roots, over the other stump holding its one note in the dark. It did not stop to tell it anything. It did hum as it passed, very softly, the interval Catch had named it with. The note began at one pitch and descended to the other, and the power held it a little longer than it needed to.

Then it went on. It left the two voices where it had found them, a little less alone than before, with the distance between them still theirs to close.
