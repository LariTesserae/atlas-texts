# The Visitor at Low Hesk

## It Comes Down the Stair

It does not arrive the way weather arrives, or light. It comes down the stair.

It could have come any other way. It could have been present in the hall all at once, in every drop of the weep, in the grain of every slab. But the stair is how things come to Low Hesk, and a guest uses the door. So there is a moment, during an out-breath, when the iron steps take a weight they have not taken in some years, and the landing rail hums very slightly under a hand. Then it stands where you stood at the beginning, looking out at the archipelago of orange lamps, and it waits for the pumps to turn over before it goes any further. It wants to feel the breath first. It is not in a hurry, and it wants the hall to know that.

Nothing here notices it except, perhaps, the moths. They are hanging in the draft in their few hundreds, and as the in-breath lifts them they tilt, all together and very slightly, toward the landing, the way a field of grass shows the shape of a wind. Then the out-breath comes and they settle, and the thing that came down the stair begins to walk.

It counts the pillars. It does not need to. But counting is the courtesy the hall asks of anyone who walks it, and the thing that has come is courteous above all. 114. 115. 116.

## It Listens

For a long time, which here means many breaths and perhaps many days, it does nothing but attend.

It kneels by the older walls and attends to the settle: the rotifers grazing the lime, the comma-shaped things, the broken spirals, the lives the size of full stops falling out of the water and becoming stone. It knows each of them. That is the kind of power it is. It does not know them as a census knows people. It knows them as you might know the one voice in a choir that is slightly flat, and love the choir more for it.

It reads the moss. It walks the grey-gold stripes back from every dead lamp and reads the whole fifty-year record of the moths' advance, gantry by gantry, as carefully as anyone has ever read anything. It understands that this is a record no one will ever consult. It reads it anyway, all the way through, so that it will have been read.

It climbs a rusted ladder forty meters to a rose-glowing fixture and does not unscrew it. It rests its attention against the glass and feels the larvae inside, packed end to end in their silk, eating the ghost of the current, and it finds that the glow they make is very beautiful. It is the colour of a light being lovingly consumed.

It goes out to 2000 and to 3000 and past them, along the blue lamps, to the edge. It stands in the rubble that smells of hot oil, and it watches the setters step across the green concrete, stop, and press their blunt faces to the surface to lap the bleed. It watches one that has fallen a little behind. The animal's joints are stiffening, and it clicks faintly when it lowers its head. It works harder than the others to keep up, and it falls a little further behind anyway.

Then it walks all the way back, past 900, past 400, to the foot of the stair, and it sits among the stone setters for a whole day. They are crouched at the pillar feet with their heads bowed, all facing outward toward the far dark, listening.

It lies on the grate at the junction where Aud Ferrow lay, with its ear to the iron, and it hears what she heard. It is not the water. It is slower, and it comes from very far down.

It enters a booth only when invited. The tender at pillar 1412 is a young man, three years into his first rota, and he does not know what he is inviting in. He only knows that someone knocked during an out-breath, that it would be a poor thing to leave someone standing in the wet, and that the kettle was already on. He talks about the dead-lamp numbers. The visitor listens to all of them. Before it leaves it reads the telephone card on the wall, every name, every crossed-out name, every station number written in every different hand. It reads the name *A. Ferrow — 208*, struck through in blue ink by someone who pressed hard.

## It Considers What It Could Do

It could do almost anything. Power this large is mostly a burden of restraint.

It could stop the cutters. It could hold the edge where it is, and the setters would never again have to outrun their own hardening. There would always be green concrete somewhere, and none of them would ever be left behind.

Except that there would not always be green concrete. A still edge cures. Stopping the cutting would not save the setters. It would only end the one thing they are, all at once instead of a few at a time. And even if it kept the concrete green forever by some kindness it could arrange, it would be making the setters a fixed margin where a moving one had been. It would be turning an animal that is a journey into an animal that is a pen.

It could stop the water. It could keep the pumps breathing forever, eleven in and nineteen out. The moths would never fall, the moss would never drown, and the hall would stay as it is: a floor, a dry season, a lake-bed in the summer the lake is low.

It thinks about this one for a long time, because it is the most tempting, and the most tempting things are usually the ones to be most careful of.

Everything that lives here lives in a gap. It lives between the cutting and the cure, between one breath and the next, between the lamp going in and the moth arriving. To hold the gap open forever would not be mercy. It would make scaffolding into a prison, and it would tell every living thing in the hall that it will be provisional forever. The tenders would never be relieved. The eels would never get home. The shuttering boards would stay bolted to the pillars until the end of the world, black with damp, holding up a shape that was always meant to stand on its own.

And there is someone the house is for.

The visitor has been attending to every life in Low Hesk, down to the full stops in the weep. It would be a strange kind of benevolence that loved the lodgers and forgot the owner, that loved everything small in the place and nothing vast. So it goes down.

## It Goes Down to the One Who Is Waiting

It goes down through the grate. It does not pry the grate up. It goes through the way water does, into the channel, into the branching, four and then eight, past the knot of eels lying still in the cold flow. They do not flinch from it. They are very old, and they have met patient things before. It goes lower, into the culverts the tenders never visit, where the pipes are cracked and mineral-choked and remember being fissures. Below them it goes into the rock itself, into the cold fissure water moving at the speed of centuries.

And there it meets what the cistern is for, and it understands something the tenders never quite could.

The thing that is coming to fill the house is not a stranger. It is the water that was always here. It is the old fissure water of the rock, the eels' water, the water that moved through cracks for longer than there have been names. The cutters broke it open and the pumps have held it down. For fifty years it has been waiting beneath the floor, rising a little more each cycle, while its house was built larger than the cracks ever let it be. The cistern is not an invasion. It is a homecoming on a scale the rock alone could never have managed.

That is why the eels swim upstream. That is why, when the water runs low, they go down and not up, into the culverts that remember. They are not waiting to be drowned. They are waiting to be home. Aud Ferrow heard them moving together in the dark, a great many at once, as if something below had called them. Something below had.

The visitor and the water regard each other for a while. Neither of them is in a hurry. Both of them know what patience is.

Then the visitor does the only thing it has come all this way to do. It tells the water about its house.

It does not describe the house. The water will know the house, every slab of it, soon enough. It tells the water who lived there first.

It tells it about the rotifers in the weep, and how the first stalactites of Low Hesk are made mostly of them. It tells it about the moss, which kept a record of the light that no one will ever read, except that now two beings have. It tells it about the moths who eat the lamps and cannot really fly, who are held up only by the breath, and who will fall when the breath stops. It tells it about the setters. It tells it about the stone ones at the pillar feet, crouched and facing outward, who stopped where they could not keep up and turned toward the far dark to listen. They were listening for *you*, it says. They have been listening for fifty years. It tells it about Aud Ferrow, whose name is struck through in blue ink on a card in the booth at 1412. It tells it about the young man and his kettle.

It does not ask the water to spare any of them. That would not be fair, and the visitor is fair. The water cannot fill its house without covering its floor.

It asks only three things.

It asks the water to wait for one more generation of moths.

It asks the water to come in slowly, as slowly as it rose, so that everything that can climb the stair has time to climb it.

And it asks the water to know, once it has come in, that its house was lived in before it arrived. It asks it to know that the house is not new in the way the concrete is new. It was a home before it was a vessel, a scaffold before it was a house, and the scaffold was full of lives.

The water is vast and slow and patient, and it has waited a very long time. It can wait for one more generation of moths.

## What It Does, Then

It does very little. It does a small number of precise things, the way the builder on the gantry at 3600 laid one girder and fitted one fixture, so that when the light came on no one saw it being switched.

**For the moths**, it does nothing to the moths. It sits with the water through the last emergence. It waits while the final larvae finish the last sodium film in the last colonised tubes, while the rose glow thins out gantry by gantry, while the new moths come out almost weightless and hang in the pumped air for their few in-breaths and mate in the mist. The pumps keep breathing until the last of them has settled on the pillars. That is the whole of the favour, a matter of timing. When the breath stops and the moths fall, the ones that fall have already done what moths do. They fall like ash onto the rising water and rest on it, and the rotifers take them, as the rotifers always have. Now the rotifers are in a lake.

**For the setters**, it goes out to the edge before the water comes. It gathers one clutch of eggs from the newest bay, smooth and grey and cold, indistinguishable from pebbles except that one of them is warmer than it should be. It brings them back along the pillar numbers to the booth at 1412 and gives them to the young man in an old tea tin. It tells him that up the stair, somewhere, someone is always pouring concrete. The world above has raw margins too. The margin can move and the setters can move with it, the way they always have.

It does not gather all of them. Some setters are too stiff to go and would not survive the stair. Others have already turned toward the far dark to listen, and the visitor does not take their listening away from them. It sits one last time among the stone ones at the foot of the stair, and it makes very sure that when the water comes it will come *toward them*, from the direction they are facing, so that the thing they have listened for all this time will reach them first. Of everything it arranges in Low Hesk, this is what it takes most care over.

**For the eels**, it does nothing, because the eels do not need anything. They know. The visitor lies on the grate at the junction once more and listens to them moving together far below, a great many at once. It understands that it is listening to a crowd at a threshold, waiting to go in.

**For the moss**, it does nothing either, and it lets that cost what it costs. The moss will drown. It was a record of the light, and records end. The visitor has read all of it, and it carries the reading. That will have to be enough, and it thinks that perhaps it is.

**For the cutters**, it goes out past the last blue lamp, into the heat and the sound, further than anyone has gone. It does not look at them. They had not been told anyone would be home yet, and the visitor would not want to embarrass a builder at work. It stands in the dark a little way off and lets them know, the way you let someone know you are standing in a doorway, that the house is nearly done. It lets them know it is beautiful and that the owner is pleased. And it tells them they may stop when they are ready, and not before. The cutters, whatever they are, keep cutting a little while longer. Then, one day, the sound under everything stops. It stops because the house is big enough, and they are the ones who know that.

## For the Tenders

This is the thing it thought about longest after the water. The tenders are waiting to be told they can stop, and no one has ever told them.

The visitor looked into it. It learned what the tenders half suspected. The rotas come in envelopes from an authority no one can find because there is no one there to find. The hall writes its own rotas, the way it makes its own weep. Nobody ever meant for no one to relieve the tenders. There was simply never anyone whose place it was to say *enough, and thank you*.

So the visitor decides that it will be the one. It does not take the office for itself. It takes it for the time it takes to say one thing.

The last rota comes in an envelope marked with the name of the water authority no one can find. Inside is one sheet, and on it, in no hand the tenders recognise, it says:

*When the pumps stop, you are relieved. The works are complete. The stair will be there. Take the telephone cards with you; they are yours. Take the kettle if you like. Thank you for keeping the house until it was ready to keep itself.*

Below that, in smaller writing: *You will find the air up there very still. You will not sleep at first. The breathing is in you now, and it is not the machine's any more. It is yours. Keep it if you want to. Eleven in, nineteen out. It is a good rhythm. It kept a great many small things alive.*

It does not take the rhythm out of them. That would be a kind of theft dressed up as healing. It leaves them the breath, and it leaves them the counting, which will serve them well in the world above, where the grids are less honest. It leaves the calcite in their hair. It leaves them changed, because they were changed by caring for something, and that is not a wound.

Then, on the last day, when the in-breath has run for its eleven minutes and the roar has fallen away and only the drip and the gutters are speaking, every telephone in every booth at Low Hesk rings at once.

It is a number that is not on any card, a station number higher than the highest station ever built. Every tender who picks up expects the roar, the drowning in-breath and the faint level voice underneath it reading pressures. But for the first time the call has come in the quiet, so the voice can be heard. Some of them say afterward that it was their own voice. Some say it was whoever kept their booth before them. The young man at 1412 says, with great certainty and no evidence at all, that it was Aud Ferrow.

The voice reads off one last pressure: *Zero.*

Then it says: *That's the lot. Thank you. Go on up.*

## The Filling

They go on up. They go slowly, the way the visitor asked the water to come. They carry the telephone cards with every name on them, and a tea tin with a warm grey pebble in it, and one or two kettles. The visitor walks behind them on the stair and counts the steps, out of courtesy.

Below them the hall stops breathing. The mist settles for the first time in fifty years and lies in the lamp cones like milk. Then the water comes in: from below, through the grates and the gutters, up out of the culverts that remember, slowly, directionally. It comes first toward the pillar feet near the stair, where the stone setters are crouched facing it with their heads bowed, the way they have been listening all this time. It reaches them before it reaches anything else. It covers them gently. They have heard it.

The grey-white slabs go under one field-sized slab at a time. The stenciled yellow numbers shine beneath the surface for a moment. The eels come up out of the dark in their great slow crowd, moving together into water that is theirs again, a house far larger than the cracks ever were. They are home. The rock gave them fissures. The hall has given them a cathedral.

The last working lamp throws its cone of gold down into still water. In it, where there had only ever been drifting droplets, something vast and slow and patient turns over once in the whole of its new house.

It knows who lived there first. It was told. When it turns over, it turns carefully, the way you move through a room in a house you have just been given, where you can still see the marks on the doorframe of children who have grown up and gone.

Then it settles in to live there.

## And Afterward

The visitor goes back up the stair last of all. At the landing it stops, rests a hand on the rail, and looks out once more at the dark water where the hall was. The rail is quiet under its hand now. There is no hum.

It does not feel that it has saved Low Hesk. Low Hesk did not need saving. It needed someone to notice all of it at once: the vessel and the thing it held, the scaffold and the lives in the scaffold, the ones who arrived and the ones who were always there. It needed someone to hold the whole of it in mind for long enough that nothing in it went unwitnessed.

That was nearly all the visitor did. It witnessed. It asked for a little patience from something patient. It told the tenders what no one had ever been there to tell them. It carried one clutch of eggs and read one record that no one else would read, and it made sure the ones who were listening got to hear what they had listened for.

Somewhere on the surface, a young man who cannot sleep in the still air lies awake, breathing in for eleven and out for nineteen. On his windowsill a tea tin is getting very slightly warmer. A few streets away, someone is pouring a slab.
