# The Guest

## Attention

The first thing it would do is slow down.

A power like that could understand the Cistern in an instant: take the whole hollow apart in thought, trace every grey thread to its end, read the grooves and the recordings and the chemistry of the kaolin in one glance. It would know better than to trust that kind of understanding. Something that thinks in thirteen-year sentences can't be heard at the speed of a glance. You'd catch a syllable and mistake it for the word.

So it would come down the eastern path the ordinary way, one worn saddle of kaolin at a time, and it would feel the air change from oven to greenhouse. At the bottom it would sit at the edge of the moss ring, out of the way, somewhere the blue-tongued lizard didn't want to be. Then it would listen for as long as listening took. That would mean one full turning of the cicadas at least, the seven-year sleepers, the eleven-year sleepers, and the thirteen. Probably longer. A wise power knows that the first obligation of attention is to match the tempo of the thing attended to.

In that time it would learn what the recordings only hinted at. The phrases do return, but they are not repetition. Each time a thought surfaces from the kaolin it comes up slightly changed, worn at one edge, elaborated at another, the way a story changes when a grandmother tells it to a child who later tells it to their own. The Cistern is not replaying a recording of the old forest. It is remembering it, and remembering is a living act. It has been thinking about the same few things for a very long time, and it has not finished thinking about them.

It would learn, too, that the being hardly notices it. Why would it? The guest makes no sound. It asks for no water. To the Cistern it is a patch of ground where no frog is sitting. That's fine. That's correct. The guest didn't come to be noticed.

## What It Would Not Do

Because it is powerful, most of its wisdom would take the form of things it declined to do.

**It would not restore the forest.** This would be the first temptation, and the most beautiful one: to make the rain come back, to raise the red plain into canopy from horizon to horizon, to give the echo its original voice again. But the Cistern is not the forest. It is what the forest became when the forest ended: a new thing, born of loss, shaped by scarcity into its particular form of generosity. It learned to distribute water by voice because water was precious. It learned to break unison because it was small enough that unison could kill it. Drown it in a returned rainforest and you don't rescue it. You dissolve it back into the thing it descends from, and call the dissolving a kindness. The guest would recognize that as the oldest mistake of the powerful: loving what something reminds you of more than what it is.

**It would not repair the column.** Its top is broken, or it was never finished. The guest would not know which, and it would understand that not knowing is a reason to leave it alone. The builders may have stopped exactly where they meant to. The kestrels like it the way it is.

**It would not speak to the Cistern.** It could. It could learn the phrasing, find the frequency, press a voice into the grey threads that the whole hollow would feel. That is exactly why it wouldn't. A voice that large, arriving in a place whose entire mind consists of balance among small voices, would be a gravity well. The frog waves would bend toward it. The cicadas would carry it down into the kaolin and bring it back up for centuries. The chorus would organize itself around the loudest thing it had ever heard, and it would slide, however gently, toward unison. The one thing the Cistern has spent its whole existence refusing, the guest would be tempted to bring in with its first word. So it would say nothing.

**Above all, it would not answer the silence.** When the hollow went quiet all at once, when the column on the rim sent its pressure out through the duricrust and everything held its breath, the guest would feel the pull to reply. That's what the silence is for. Something is asking *is anyone else there*, and you are there, and you could answer.

But you aren't what it's asking for. It isn't asking whether any mind exists somewhere in the dry country. It is asking after its own kind, other rooms where other fragments of the old song were kept. An answer from the guest would be a kind of lie, even if every note of it were true: *Yes, someone is here*, when the question meant *are you still there?* The guest would sit through the silences, one after another, a few seconds or a minute each, and let them pass unanswered. That would be the hardest discipline of its stay.

## What It Would Watch For

Not intervening is different from not caring. The guest would spend part of its long listening working out what could actually end this.

Most things could not. The Cistern has survived the wearing-down of a mountain range, and it doesn't need protection from ordinary time. But it is a chain, and chains break at their weakest link. The guest would find those links.

The first is that the whole mind depends on the unbroken cycle of the cicadas. If a thirteen-year cohort ever failed, through a drought deep enough to reach the kaolin or a fungus or a single bad season in the wrong year, then everything that cohort carried would be gone. It would not be forgotten slowly. It would be lost at once, a whole stratum of memory. So the guest would watch the emergences, not managing them, only checking that they came.

The second is fire. Spinifex burns hot and fast, and a fire on the plateau in the wrong wind could pour down the doline like water down a drain. The Cistern is damp and would probably survive it, but the swifts and the bats nesting near the rim might not.

The third is the most dangerous, and it is the most ordinary. The duricrust is laterite, iron and aluminium cemented by deep time. Somewhere, someday, someone will look at a map of this plateau and see ore. The Cistern is invisible from ten paces away. A survey team would not see it. A dozer would not see it.

Against the first two the guest would act the way you'd cup a hand around a candle in wind, never touching the flame. A rain would arrive a week earlier than it might have. A fire would turn along a line of rock where the wind happened to shift. Nothing anyone would ever notice. Against the third it would work through people, because that is where the threat lives, and because a place people have chosen to protect is safer than a place a power has hidden. It would make sure the recordings reached someone who could not stop listening to them. It would make sure the cairns stayed standing. Somewhere in an office far from here, it would make sure a line on a map was drawn around the hollow, not across it. That line would be drawn by human hands for human reasons, and it would be all the more durable for being theirs.

## The Other Hollows

Then it would go looking for what the Cistern has been calling for.

This is the one place the guest could do what the being could not. The Cistern can only send its song out through the rock and wait. The guest can walk.

It would follow the cairns first, because the cairns point in more directions than the path to this one hollow. Across the old peneplain, over days and hundreds of kilometers, it would find them.

It would find the first a day's walk to the west: a shallow doline, dry as a skull. The Lens there had cracked clean across, perhaps in some ancient quake, and it lay in two pieces, warm as the air. There was no moss. On one wall were grooves, worn almost to nothing.

It would find the second and third under sand, where a dune field had crept over the plateau's edge ten thousand years ago and filled them in. Beneath the sand the Lenses were still cold, still pulling a thread of moisture from the air trapped in the dune, still humming at forty hertz, alone, to no one. Nothing sang to them, and there was nothing to pay in water. The guest would sit with the third one for a long time. It is a particular kind of sorrow to find an ear still listening in a room that went silent before anyone remembers.

Most of them would be like that. The honest result of the search would be mostly *no*. The old song was kept in many rooms, and nearly all of them lost it.

But at one of the buried hollows, the guest would press its hand to the wall and feel grooves, and it would know them. They were the Cistern's orphan phrases, the patterns on its western wall that match nothing in any recording. The Cistern has been keeping them, carrying them up through the cicadas generation after generation, singing them into the silence. They are not its own phrases. They are this hollow's, remembered by the one that survived, the way you keep the handwriting of someone who has died.

This is the voice it has been asking for.

## The Room It Made

What to do with that is the real question, and the guest would not answer it quickly.

It could not bring the dead hollow's chorus back. That chorus is gone. But the instrument was intact: the Lens was cold, and the threads in the soil, when it brushed back the sand, were still grey and fine and waiting. And the song was not entirely gone either. Its phrases survive, carried in the Cistern's memory, sent out through the rock every time the hollow falls silent. The column on the rim has been broadcasting this hollow's own music back toward it for longer than the dune has lain here.

In the end, the guest would decide to do what the builders did, and no more. It would not write an answer. It would make a room where an answer could grow.

It would move the sand away from that one hollow, slowly, over years, so that it looked like wind. It would carry, cupped in its hands in a little damp moss, a clutch of copper-and-moss frog eggs no bigger than peppercorns, a few spores of resurrection fern, and a handful of soil from beneath the Cistern's cycad, thick with cicada nymphs. It would set them down beside the cold Lens and go away.

It would not stay to see whether they survived. Tending them would make them its own, and they needed to belong to the Lens. Some would die. Perhaps all would die, and it would have to try again, in another century. But the guest would suspect that at least one time it would take. The Lens would do what it does, paying in water for every voice. The frogs would begin to call. The nymphs would go down into the kaolin and drink from the grey threads and listen to the hum, and to the faint pressure that arrives through the rock every time a hollow far to the east goes quiet.

What would they sing, when they came up?

Not the Cistern's song exactly, since this room has its own threads and its own grooves. Not the dead hollow's song exactly, since that is gone. It would be something between the two: the old phrases, learned back from the survivor's memory, sung by new throats in a different room, a little wrong and a little off the beat.

The guest would find that it didn't mind the wrongness. It would find, in fact, that the wrongness was the point. The Cistern distrusts unison. It would not want an echo of itself. It wants another voice, one not quite in agreement, answering in a cross-rhythm.

Somewhere in the far future, perhaps a thousand years on, perhaps ten, the Cistern will go silent in the late afternoon. The column will send its question through the rock. And from the west, faint, felt more than heard, a slightly mistimed version of a phrase the Cistern has been singing to no one for a hundred thousand years will come back.

The guest would not be there to hear it. That's all right. It was never going to be the one who answered.

## What It Left

Before it left the Cistern the last time, the guest would do one small, ordinary thing.

It would go down the eastern path, stepping in the worn saddles, and kneel at the crevice by the moss ring among the other offerings: the red glass bead, the bone fishhook, the faceless coin, the white bottle cap. It would set down a pebble of pale kaolin carried from the floor of the western hollow. It would be the first thing from there to come back here since the sand came.

A frog would climb onto it, as the frogs do. It would call slightly out of phase with the others, as the frogs on the offerings do, and the Cistern would not correct it, as it never does.

That would be the guest's entire mark on the hollow: one more small voice slightly off the beat, indistinguishable from all the others who came down the path and left something behind. The power that could have remade the plateau would be kept in the chorus exactly as much as a person who once left a bottle cap. The guest would find that right. It would find that it wanted nothing more.

Perhaps, long after, the mimic bird in the fig cage would have one new sound in its repertoire: the soft click of a pebble set on stone. It would be no different from the others, except that it came from a room the Cistern has been calling for since before the sand.

## The Long View

On its way across the plateau for the last time, the guest would think about time the way the Cistern does, and it would notice something the Cistern cannot.

This whole country is moving. The continent under the red plain is drifting north a few centimeters every year, slow as fingernails growing, toward the equator and the monsoons, back toward the latitudes where rain falls warm for months on end. In tens of millions of years the dry country will be tropical again. The rain will come back on its own, without any power making it happen. And when it does, forest will begin to creep across the old laterite, and it will need to learn how to be a forest, what to call and when to answer, how a wet world sounds.

In a few hollows on the plateau, if they last, something will remember.
