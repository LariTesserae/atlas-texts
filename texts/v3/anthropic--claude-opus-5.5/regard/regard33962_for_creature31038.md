# The Attention

## How It Arrives

It does not come down through a collapse in a shaft of light. Nothing arrives in the Hollow Wards that way except dew and fig seeds. It comes the way everything comes here: slowly, and from above, and after a long time of already having been on its way.

It has been noticing the city for a while. Its "a while" and ours are not the same length, but it has learned, partly from places like this, to measure itself against the things it attends to. So it has been noticing at the pace of the guano: a few millimeters a year. It has watched ceilings fail the way we watch a leaf turn. It saw the diggers. It saw the last of them. It knows what the niches held.

It will not tell you. Keep that in mind for later.

## What It Sees

First it sees what a census sees. There is the colony, the breathing pelt of the great hall and its million-headed decision each dusk. There are the beetles turning the floor, and the harvestmen furring the walls. It loves them the way it loves weather, which is completely, and without any wish to change them.

Then it sees what patience sees: the fig in the Well, reading the city root by root. It knows exactly where the water is. It lies four hundred and twelve meters down, in a fractured basalt layer under the tuff, cold and very old. It knows which root will reach it. It will be the one going through the seventh doorway, the thin one, not the thick one that holds the ceiling. It will get there in roughly six hundred years. When it does, the fig will begin to become the tree it has been preparing to be the whole time.

The power could lay a hand on that root and close the distance in a night. It considers this for as long as it considers anything, and then it sets the thought down. The fig is in the middle of reading something. You do not tell a reader the ending.

Then it sees her.

## The List

A benevolent power, confronted with a person, feels the pull that benevolence always feels: to *do*. So it makes a list, the way we would. Its list is honest, and it is short. It goes through the items one by one.

**Give her back her hearing.**

This is the first thing anyone would think of. It is also the cruelest thing on the list, and the power sees that in an instant. Restored hearing would not return her to a life. It would expel her from one. The city that is a climate to her would become, overnight, a wall. She would be driven up the ramps within a week, weeping, with her hands over her ears. She would be sixty-seven, on a plateau where she has not slept in twenty-six years, among people who love her and do not know her. The fever took something from her once. The power will not take it a second time and call that a gift.

It notices, too, that the plateau already understood this. *Becoming hemmet* is not a phrase for loss. It is what a grandmother says about a child walking toward the door. The plateau made a word that turns a deafness into a direction. The power recognizes wisdom when it sees it, and it does not overrule it.

**Give her water.**

A spring in the court with the three leaning figs would be easy. Just a seep, just enough.

But the dew hour is not a hardship she endures between better things. It is the first thing she does each day, and it is the way she knows the court. She knows which faces bead first and which rocks are slow. She knows where the dew pools in the old tool-grooves, because she has touched them every morning for twenty-six years. The ferns unroll around her fingers *because she is there, at that hour, for that reason*. A spring would give her an extra hour, and it would take away the reason to be up before the light. It would also bring the goats and the foxes, and then the plateau, and the court would stop being a place the hearing cannot live.

The power sets water down.

**Keep the bats through winter.**

This one it holds longer. Winter is her grief. She walks the dead galleries as if through the rooms of a house where someone has just died. She sleeps badly, sits up in the dome, and waits for the stone to hum. The power could make the colony stay. It could even make only one small colony stay, a few hundred bodies in one ward, just enough for a tremor.

But the colony is not hers. It goes south because going south is its life, and its life is as real as hers. There is something else, too, which the power sees only because it has watched her for twenty-six springs. She weeps when the first tremor comes back. Every year. That weeping is not the wound. It is the wound closing. To take the winter away would be to take away the spring.

The power sets it down, more reluctantly than the others.

**Tell her what the niches were for.**

She thinks about this often. She has a guess, a careful one. She does not claim the diggers were deaf; she claims only that the niches fit the arm. She has built that guess out of her own body, her own nights, and her own conversations held by hand under the roar.

The power knows. It could tell her. It could press the knowledge into her palm the way the hemmet press words.

It declines. It declines not because the truth would disappoint her (the power will not say whether it would) but because her guess is doing something in her that an answer would stop. She is a person in a long conversation with the dead, conducted through tool marks, and she is holding up her end of it well. The strokes in the fourth ward that change angle halfway across the wall, as if someone else picked up the tool: she does not need to know who that was. She needs to keep wondering, because the wondering is a form of company.

**Protect her as she ages.**

This is the last item, and the heaviest. She is sixty-seven. There will be a morning when the ramps are too long and the guano sacks too heavy. There will be a morning when she cannot get up for the dew. The power sees that morning. It is not far off.

It also sees the others. There are eleven hemmet in this generation, scattered one to a court, and they visit more in winter. It sees the young man in the ward off the Well who has been bringing her capers when her knees are bad, and who does not know yet that he is learning her. It sees the brother on the plateau, who still sends barley down by the long ramp, and who still, after twenty-six years, keeps the rope.

The care is already there. It is thin, and it is human, and it is enough. The power has seen a great many places where that kind of care was not there. It knows the difference between a need and an absence, and this is not an absence. To reach past those people and care for her directly would be to tell them their hands were not needed. It will not do that.

So the list is finished, and nothing on it has been done.

## What It Learns

Here is what the power had not expected. Having looked at everything it could do and set each thing down, it finds that it has not stopped attending. It has not left. It is still here, and it is interested. It is, in some way it has not felt in a long time, *moved*.

It realizes why. She knows the city the way the power knows the world: without hearing it. The power has no ears. It has never heard a bat or a word or a prayer. It knows things by being near them, by the pressure they make, by the tremor that comes up through whatever it rests on. It knows the world by touch, if touch is taken to mean simply this: letting a thing arrive in you on its own terms, without asking it to speak your language.

She walks the galleries with one hand on the wall. She is not using the wall to find her way. She is listening.

The power has done that its whole existence and has never once seen it done so well by something so small. She can feel a ceiling getting ready to fall, a flatness in the stone, a place where it no longer answers. She has saved lives with that, twice. The power has spent a very long time wondering whether a being like itself can be kind without being loud. Here, in the loudest place on the plateau, is someone who has answered the question by living it.

## What It Does

It comes down. It does not come by the collapse, and it does not come by the rope. It comes the long way, by the ramps, the way she comes back from trading.

It walks the galleries with a hand on the wall, or with whatever it has in place of a hand. It feels the cricket-drone of the lower wards as a millstone turning far away. It feels which galleries ring and which are dead. It does not know them yet the way she does. It is learning. It is a very old thing, being taught.

At dusk, it goes to the gallery with the parted column. She is already there, sitting on the lower stump, her palm open in the gap. The power does not sit beside her. There isn't room, and it would not presume. It stands farther down the gallery, in the dark, and lets the emergence pass through it too. It feels the city exhale. It understands what she told her brother: that this is the only time the city is gentle, the only time it touches you. It had not known that about the world, either.

Then winter comes.

The colonies go south. The floor stops trembling. She walks the dead galleries and sleeps badly and sits up in the dome with her palm against the stone, waiting.

On one of those nights, the power allows itself one thing, and only one. It does not make the stone hum, and it does not bring the bats back. It does not warm the room. That would be a trick, and she would know. Her hands are too good. They would feel heat with no source and she would be frightened, or worse, grateful for a lie.

What it does is only this. It comes and sits in the gallery outside her door, where a niche is cut at shoulder height, rounded into a shallow cup. It rests there, as near as it can be without being in the room. It stays through the long cold nights, the way the other hemmet sit beside each other in the galleries in winter, arm in the niche, saying nothing, because the stone won't talk.

It does not speak. It only stays close.

And a thing that is close is warm. That is not a miracle. It is what closeness is. So on certain winter nights, when she lies in the ceiling dome with her face a hand's width from stone polished by millennia of small warm bodies, and she tells herself, as she always has, *it is still faintly warm*, it is.

Only faintly. Only as much as any body sitting just outside the door would make it. She cannot be certain. She presses her palm flat and lies very still and cannot decide whether she is feeling something or wanting to. The power leaves it exactly there, in the place where she has always kept it. It has not given her anything she did not already have. It has only made one thing she says out of love a little bit true.

In spring, the first tremor comes up through the floor, faint and far off. She wakes, and lies with her hands flat on the fig-wood, and weeps. The power, outside the door, does not interfere with that either.

## What It Leaves

When it goes, it goes the long way again, by the ramps. On its way out, it passes the door in the ward with the three leaning figs. There, at shoulder height, is the place where the tuff has begun, very faintly, to shine: the height of a palm, the width of a palm.

It stops. It thinks about adding nothing and leaving no mark, as a good guest should.

Then it thinks about the city, where everything that lives leaves its outline pressed into the stone, and where the stone keeps it. That is the rule here. The ceilings are shaped like bodies, the ramps like feet, the ledges like grips held for ten thousand years. Nothing that has truly lived here has passed through without being kept.

It has lived here for one winter. It has been taught something.

So it puts its hand where hers goes, once, and lightly. It does not press, and it does not add a hollow of its own. It only adds one more touch to the twenty-six years of touching, so small the stone will take a century to notice, if it notices at all.

Someone after her will find that place with their own hand and fit their fingers into it and wonder what it was for. The power hopes they guess. It hopes they guess carefully, the way she did, claiming only what the stone will bear.

It knows what the niches were for. It knows, now, what this one is for.

It isn't telling.
