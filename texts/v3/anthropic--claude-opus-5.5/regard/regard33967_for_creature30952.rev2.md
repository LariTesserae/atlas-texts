# The Measure Taken

## I. Arrival

It came the way everything comes to the Hollow: by ear first.

It had been crossing the plain for reasons of its own, or for no reason, the way such powers sometimes walk the world to see what the world has been doing in their absence. Three days out from the rim it felt the pressure in its chest. It had a chest then, because it had taken one for the walk. The pressure was so faint and so low that it stopped and stood still in the heat for most of an afternoon. It had heard many things in its long existence. It had not heard this: a sound that was listening back.

It could have gone to the rim in a single step. It walked instead. Some things should be approached at the speed they were built for.

When it reached the edge and looked down into the blazing, roaring bowl, its first impulse was to go in the way it went into most places, fully and all at once, with its whole attention and its whole weight. It took one step down the first stair and felt, across a hundred chambers, a catch. Something like a cough or a flinch ran through the midday chord. Its presence was large, and anything large in that city is a hand over a mouth.

It stepped back onto the rim. It made itself smaller. It kept making itself smaller until it weighed about as much as a person, took up about as much air, and swallowed about as much sound. Then it went down again, quickly, sidelong, the way it had seen a keeper move along the third terrace. It found a doorway with no vines around it, a room whose walls were quiet under its palm, and it sat down in a grave and began to listen.

That was the first wise thing it did, and it did it by accident. Most of wisdom begins that way. You notice you have hurt something, and you make yourself smaller.

## II. A Season of Listening

It stayed a long time.

It did not introduce itself. The keepers noticed it, since they notice everything that sits in a dead room, and one came and looked at it for a while. This was an old woman whose hand-name was a thumb pressed to the breastbone. She signed a question it did not yet understand. It answered with open, empty hands, which seems to mean something like *I don't know* in every language that has hands. She considered this, brought it a bag of sand and a bone scraper, and left.

So it swept. It learned the hand-speech the way anyone learns a language, badly at first and then less badly. It learned to press its palm to a wall and feel which note the wall was carrying. It learned the grandmothers' dawn and the tuning of the middle terraces. It learned that the afternoon shrieks were the young ones, and that the evening fall was supper.

Above all it learned the grammar of the place: nothing here can be said alone.

This troubled it more than anything else it discovered, because it was a power, and powers are used to speaking. It could have made its own voice ring out in any register, loud enough to fill every chamber. It understood, sitting in its grave at midday with its hands flat on the floor, that doing so would say nothing at all. It would only be noise. Here, meaning happens in the space between two voices, in the third tone neither one makes. To say anything to the Hollow it would have to find a room and sing *with* it. It would have to become half of something.

It had not been half of something in a very long time.

## III. What It Did Not Do

There were many things it could have done. It thought about each of them carefully, over many evenings, while the bulbs gave back the day.

It could have ended the still seasons. It could have bent the weather of the plain so that the wind never failed, so that no garden ever wilted, so that the keepers never again pumped the bellows until they dropped. It thought about this one longest, because the still seasons are a real catastrophe, and suffering is suffering. In the end it did not. The residents sleep through every night in silence, trusting the wind to find them again. That trust is part of what they are. A people with no word for the sun except *being awake* might be changed past recognition by a sun that never set. It also did not know what the plains beyond the plain would lose if it stole their stillness to feed this one bowl. The world is a web, and it had pulled on enough strands in its long life to be wary of pulling on another.

It could have given the residents bodies. It could have lifted each standing pattern out of its chamber and poured it into a form that could walk and breathe and touch its neighbors. That would have been monstrous. They were not trapped. They were home. The builders walked into the stone one life at a time, on purpose, and cut their own doorways with their own hands. Unmaking that would have been a kind of eviction.

It could have restored the keepers' hearing. It offered this later, directly, to the old woman with the thumb-to-breastbone name. She laughed, the first time it had seen a keeper laugh. She signed something it translated roughly as *and hear what, the roar? I'd go mad in an hour.* Then she signed, more gently: *my ears were what I paid. Let me keep the receipt.*

It could have woken the dead rooms. It could have sealed the cracks too deep for clay and restored the air-paths collapsed in the honeycomb. It considered this and felt, under its palm, how the keepers slept in those rooms. It felt how the dead rooms were the only places in the whole city where a person could think. The graves were also shelters. It left them.

It could have made visitors immune to the tuning. That question needed more thought, so it saved it.

It could have simply spoken to them, filling every mouth with its own breath and holding a conversation with ten thousand at once. But then they would have been singing its breath instead of the sun's. They would have learned its shape, the way children learn a parent's. When it left, and it would leave, they would have a thirst they had never known before. It had no right to become anyone's weather.

So it did not do these things. Not doing them was most of what it did, and it was not idle. Refraining, done properly, is work. You have to hold your power the way a keeper holds a voice, carefully and unspent.

## IV. The Question

When it knew enough of the language, it went down at dawn to the lowest terrace, to the oldest and roundest of the great chambers, whose voice is felt more than heard. It lay with its back against the stone, as visitors do, and after a long while it hummed.

It did not hum loudly. It chose a pitch that stood at a kind interval from the grandmother's drone, far enough apart to make a third tone and close enough to be heard as company. A ghost-note stood up in the air between them. It was the first thing the power had ever said in that tongue, and all it said was *I am here.*

The grandmother took several minutes to answer. Her voice shifted, her drone bent, and the ghost-note between them changed shape. A slower meaning came through, something like *yes. We have been measuring you.*

After that the conversation went slowly, over many dawns. The power asked what anyone who loves a place eventually asks: *What do you need?*

It expected the answer to be *wind*. It expected *more voices*, because the Hollow is lonely and grows by only a handful a century. It had half-prepared itself to refuse both, gently.

The grandmother's answer took most of a morning to resolve. When it did, the third tone that hung between them meant something close to:

*Who breathes for the ones who breathe for us?*

The power lay against the warming stone for a long time after the middle terraces had woken and started their tuning. It had come to the Hollow looking at the residents, the strange and wondrous thing, the city made of air. The residents, asked what they wanted, had turned its attention to the four, or six, or nine exhausted people sleeping in graves.

## V. The Bellows

The still season came in its second year.

The wind failed on a bright morning. By midday the assembly was a murmur, and by afternoon it was a scatter of lonely notes that could not find each other. The gardens went slack and gray. The keepers went down to the lowest chambers and began to pump.

The power went with them and took a shift.

Here it met the hardest temptation, and it had to hold itself very still. It could have filled those leather lungs with a single breath that never ran out. It could have kept every chamber in the city singing through the whole calm without tiring. Its hands on the bellows-handles itched with the ease of it.

It worked the bellows at exactly the strength of a human being. No more.

It had reasoned this through carefully, and the reasoning was simple. If it breathed for the Hollow as a god, the Hollow would learn to be breathed for by a god. The keepers' work would become a formality, and the next still season, after it had gone, would be a slaughter. Worse, the keepers would know their bellows were not what had saved anyone. So it breathed as one more keeper. It added one pair of arms, one set of lungs, one more shift in the rotation. That meant each of the others could sleep a little longer between turns. That was the whole gift.

When the old woman with the thumb-to-breastbone name collapsed on the fourth night, the power carried her to her dead room. It sat beside her until she woke, and it took her shift and the shift after.

The calm lasted nine days. Two terraces browned. No grandmother died. When the wind came back on the tenth morning, the keepers collapsed and slept where they stood, the way they always do. The power stood in the returning roar with its human-sized arms aching, which it had allowed them to do. It thought it had never done anything so small that it was so certain of.

## VI. The One Who Could Not Speak

On the eleventh terrace, on the eastern face, there is a narrow square-cut room that sings wrongly.

The keepers had been grinding it for four generations. It had been cut, they said, by a keeper whose hands shook at the end. Its voice was harsh and unstable, and it clashed with every neighbor, so it had never once joined a ghost-note. In the Hollow's tongue, it had never said anything. The keepers spoke of it with the particular sorrow of people who have tried for a long time. Every few years someone took it on and ground the doorway a hair wider or packed a slit with clay, hoping to ease it into speech. Nothing had worked. Some privately thought they had made it worse.

The power went and sat outside it for many middays. It listened past the harshness to the shape underneath.

What it found was this: the room was not badly cut. Its voice was steady at its core. The instability came from its constant, failing reach toward its neighbors. It was straining to match notes it could never match, because its own note stood at an interval that nothing else in the Hollow shared. No one in the city was pitched to answer it. It had been calling for four generations in a key no one else spoke.

The power could answer it, because the power can sing at any pitch.

It sat in the street at noon and found the one tone that made a clean third with that lonely room, and it held it. For the first time since it was cut, the room on the eleventh terrace said something.

The power could not say what. It took a long time to be sure. The ghost-note that stood between them was thin and new, and it trembled like a first word. The best translation it could manage, the one it offered the keepers later in its halting hand-speech, was something like *oh.*

It did not stay to be the room's permanent companion. That would have been the same mistake as the bellows, a dependence that would break when it left. Instead it went to the keepers. With great care it taught them the interval, singing it into their palms against the wall until they could feel it. Then it told them, as well as it could: *Stop grinding. It was never wrong. It needs a sibling.*

So they began to cut a new room, in the proper way, into the rock beside the lonely one, so that the new voice would be shaped partly by its elder and would belong. The power did not cut a single stroke. It will take the keepers years. When the new room is finished, the voice on the eleventh terrace will have someone to speak with who is not a passing god. It will have someone who will still be there after every wind.

## VII. The Note

The old woman with the thumb-to-breastbone name was cutting her room.

She had begun before the power arrived, on a high terrace near the rim with a view of the white blaze of the far side. She was slow now. Her hands failed her for whole days. Once, sitting beside her in the quiet of an evening, the power watched her press her chest to the half-cut stone and hum her note, the voice she had last used aloud more than forty years before. It saw her stop and frown and hum again, differently.

She signed to it, without looking up, the thing every old keeper fears most and none of them say: *I think I am losing it.*

The power had heard her hum on the first night it knew her. It had heard every note she had hummed since. It remembered them, because it forgets very little.

It did not hum her note for her. It did not reach into the stone and carve the chamber to fit. The room had to be shaped by her own body, or it would not be her. But it asked her, in hand-speech, if she wanted it to tell her whether she was right.

She thought about that for a long time. Then she nodded.

So for the last year of her work, whenever she doubted, she would hum, and the power would sign *yes* or *a little lower* or *that is the one.* That was all. It kept her voice for her the way the bulbs keep the noon, softened and stored and given back when needed. She cut every stroke of that room herself.

She finished in early spring. She went and lay down in a dead room and did not get up. The power sat with her, because she had asked it to, and it did not do anything about that either.

The next wind crossed her doorway, and something stood up inside it. The keepers who knew her pressed their palms to the wall, and one of them wept. The one weeping signed, as they always sign, *it is the same voice.*

The power had no need to press its palm to the wall. It had kept her note all this while. It knew better than any of them that it was.

## VIII. The Rim

There remained the question of the visitors.

It had thought for a long time about the gentle tuning, the way the Hollow takes the measure of everyone who stands in its roar and slowly makes a shape in them that only wind can fill. It had felt that shape growing in itself and knew the thirst was real. It thought of all the people who had come, gone home, and found the silence of the world unbearable, who had not known what was being done to them until it was done.

The Hollow does not mean harm. Nothing in it can mean anything alone. But not meaning harm and not doing harm are different things, and the power had lived long enough to know it.

It did not stop the tuning. The tuning is how the Hollow grows. It is how lonely voices find new keepers, and new keepers find, at the end, a home in the stone. Stopping it would have starved the city slowly. It also saw that the keepers tell no one, though they hide nothing, and that this was not a secret so much as a failure of language. Visitors do not know the hand-speech. By the time they learn it, the measure has been taken.

So it asked the keepers one question, as simply as it could: *Do they know, when they come down the first stair?*

The keepers looked at one another for a long time.

It did not carve anything itself. But that season the keepers began a carving on the stone at the top of the path, where visitors first step over the rim. It is in the old style, figures and doorways, a language that needs no hands. It shows a figure arriving with an empty chest, then the same figure with a hollow inside it, shaped like a room. It shows the figure walking away across the plain, bent over as if thirsty, and the same figure turning back. At the end there is a doorway.

It is not a warning, exactly, and not exactly an invitation. It is the truth, set where people can see it before they choose. That is all a wise power can offer anyone.

## IX. Leaving

It left at evening, during the eating.

It went up the stairs slowly, the long way, sidelong past the singing doorways. The dusk sound was pouring out of the bulbs into the chambers, grief and supper both, and the city was dimming gently toward night. On the rim it stopped and felt the pressure in its chest, the note that seems to come from inside you. It was large now, so much larger than on the plain three days out, because the Hollow had been measuring it for two years and had taken its measure well.

The keepers had asked it, in the last week, whether it would cut a room.

It had thought about this too, because the offer was an honor and it was tempted. But every room still to be cut is a place kept open for someone, and it was not that someone. Its place was elsewhere, and its voice was not a thing it could save and spend in stone. To take a room would have been to take one from a keeper not yet born. So it declined, with the empty open hands that mean *I don't know* and also, in the Hollow's dialect, *not me.*

So it would go out onto the plain with the shape inside it and nothing out there to fill it. It would carry the thirst the way every visitor who has ever left has carried it.

It decided this was right. For once it would be what the Hollow's residents always are: something that cannot finish its sentence alone. It had sung with a grandmother at dawn, and with a lonely room on the eleventh terrace, and once, silently, with the memory of an old woman's note. Each of those was half a word. The other halves were still down there in the stone.

It could have healed the thirst in itself with a thought. It chose not to. The thirst was the only honest proof it had that it had been somewhere it could not own.

The last thing it did was turn on the rim and lift one hand, palm up. It swept it slowly across the bowl, across every terrace and every doorway, and it kept sweeping long after the gesture should have ended.

*And the others. And the rest of us.*

Then it walked out onto the silent plain, slowly, at the speed the place was built for, and it did not look back. Down in the Hollow, in the last of the eating, a ghost-note flickered between two rooms on the eleventh terrace. One of them was not quite finished yet.
