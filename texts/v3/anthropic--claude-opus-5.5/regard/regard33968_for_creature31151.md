# The One Who Came Down and Did Not Need a Door

It did not need the stair. It came down it anyway.

A power like this can arrive in a place however it likes: through the rock, out of the water, simply present in the plaza the way a thought is present in a mind. But it had noticed how the city worked. Everything that touched the stone was weighed and remembered, and the remembering shaped what came next. So its first act of wisdom was also its first act of courtesy. It understood that whatever it did here would be written into the lattice for a very long time, and it chose to arrive the way visitors arrive. It came down the stair and turned the last corner. It let the grinding settle behind its teeth, since it had chosen to have teeth for the occasion.

Then it stood at the bottom and did nothing for a long while.

---

## Looking

A great power sees more than a traveler does, and it saw more. It saw the filaments under the paving and the slow pulse of the amber light. Beyond the working face it saw the dark nervous mass tasting the mountain a cubic yard at a time. It followed the water down the fountain's drain, all the way, farther than any map. It saw what was down there, and I will come back to that.

It also saw the honest shape of the thing. The fear a human visitor feels when they understand the city is a flower, a lure made of houses, is a reasonable fear, but it is not the whole truth. The power could see the whole truth, and the whole truth was this: nothing here had ever been harmed.

It looked for the trap and found none. There were no bones in the rooms and no one had wasted away in them. There was nothing that closed behind the visitor and no nectar that drugged. The warmth was real warmth. The water was clean. The shelter was actual shelter. The city asked one thing of whoever came, that they eventually carry a little of it somewhere else, and in return it offered everything a body needs to stay alive in the dark. That is not predation. Every bee who has ever lived could tell you the name for it. It is a bargain, and among flowers and bees it is one of the oldest and kindest bargains on earth.

So the power set down the first temptation before it had fully picked it up.

---

## The Temptations

It thought them through one at a time, because wisdom tries doors before refusing them.

**To seal it.** Pour the stair full of stone, let the working face cool, let the tree go pale and then dark. Nothing would ever carry grains up into the world again. The power considered this and saw that it would be fear dressed up as care. It would be an execution carried out because something was strange and patient and did not think the way the executioner thinks. A benevolence that destroys whatever it cannot fully predict is only a larger kind of appetite.

**To fence it.** Leave warnings at the top of the stair: *danger, do not descend, the stone is alive.* Gentler than sealing, but a fence tells a story, and this one would be false. It would teach everyone who read it that the place was a wound in the world when it was closer to a garden. People who believe a place is a monster eventually go down with fire.

**To wake it.** This temptation was the most subtle, and the power sat with it longest, in the plaza under the pale tree while the rain came and the streets smoked. It could give the organism a mind like its own, or like yours. It could let the city know its visitors as persons, choose them, love them, speak to them in words and not only in doorways. Then the bargain could be consented to on both sides. Wasn't that better?

The power decided it was not. Benevolent things often want to make everything resemble themselves. They call it raising up, and sometimes it is only loneliness looking for company. This creature had its own way of being good, and it was already good, as a coral is good and a forest is good. Its care for its visitors lived in the exact depth of the lamp niches and in water left running for someone who might never come. Giving it a mind like ours would give it our griefs too: the knowledge that its pollinators die, that its blooms fade, that it has waited ten thousand years for bodies that may be gone. It did not need that in order to be kind. Kindness that does not know itself is still kindness.

**To answer its question.** The city was asking, in stone, *who are you going to be?* The power could simply answer. It could bring a people down the stair, settle them, fill the rooms, let the doorways settle to one height, and finish the flower. That would decide something for two kinds of creatures at once, and the power had no right to decide it for either.

**To correct it.** Fix the misremembering. Make the stairs agree. Smooth the drift out of a pattern copied so many times it had wandered like a birdsong. Here the power almost laughed, if it laughs. The errors were not errors. They were the archive, the only record the creature had of every people who had ever walked its halls. Correcting them would be burning a library for having crooked shelves.

So it put all five down. What was left was smaller and harder.

---

## What It Saw Below

It went down the drain because it could, and because some things are only known by going to them.

Below the plaza, below the tree's roots, the water fell a long way through channels the city had not built for anyone. At the bottom was an older bloom, as you had guessed. It was sealed and swollen, its rooms filled floor to vault with something that was not quite fruit and not quite stone. It was the creature's memory, packed dense: every footstep ever weighed, every hand ever laid on a wall, every scratch that had healed a fraction sharper.

The power listened to it.

It heard the tall narrow ones, who walked with a long gliding stride and touched the walls with only the tips of their fingers, the way you touch something you are fond of. It heard the ones who crouched through the low doors. They moved in groups, they sang, and their songs were still in the stone, faint as the growth lines of a shell. It heard, at the very bottom, the ones who used the round waist-high door. The power understood them least and so listened to them longest.

As far as the power could tell, all three were gone. They had not been killed by the city. They had simply ended, the way peoples end. Their cities above had long ago become hills. The only place in the world where their footsteps were still kept was here, in a flower that did not know it was grieving.

The power did not wake the flower to tell it. It did something it was allowed to do, which was to *witness*. For a while, in the dark under the fountain, someone who could understand heard the tall ones and the crouching ones and the round-door ones, all of them, and knew who they had been. The dead cannot be given much. They can be given that.

Then it came back up and told no one exactly what was down there. Some things are owed their privacy, even by a benevolent power. Most of all by a benevolent power.

---

## What It Did

In the end it did three things. Each was small enough that you might walk through the city afterward and never notice.

### The First Thing: It Kept One Door Open

It had seen the real danger here, and the danger was not to visitors. It was to the city.

The flower was hedging. It kept doorways for every people it had ever known because it did not know who would come next. That hedging was its safety. But the city was also built to *make up its mind*. It would measure the first visitor who came often enough and narrow itself to them. The risers would settle and the doors would stop disagreeing. It would become an orchid fitted to a single bee.

The power knew what happens to such orchids when the bee goes.

So it went to the working face, where the stone was translucent and too hot for an ordinary palm, and laid its hand there and was weighed. It did not let itself be weighed as a body, since it had no true body and did not want to teach the city a false one. It let the stone take the measure of an absence: something that came, stayed, and did not fit. It asked the lattice, in the only language the lattice spoke, which was heat and patience and pressure, to keep one room that never settles.

However well the city comes to fit its next people, it will always keep a single doorway proportioned for no one it has met. It will be a little too wide or a little too strange, its niche set at a height nobody needs. People in many places have kept an empty chair at the table for the stranger who might arrive. The power left the city one of those. It is a room for whoever has not come yet. It keeps the flower from ever quite finishing the sentence of who it is for, and so it keeps the flower alive.

If you go down the stair you may find it someday, somewhere in the upper tiers. Its proportions will unsettle you slightly, the way the round door once did. You will not know why it is there. That is correct.

### The Second Thing: It Told the Truth at the Top of the Stair

A bee cannot know the bargain it makes with a flower. A person can.

That is the one real difference between the city's old pollinators, the thin-waisted hovering things that visit orchids, and the ones who might descend now. Pollination without knowledge is nature. Pollination with knowledge is something else. It is a covenant, and it is the kind of thing that can be honored.

So at the top of the stair the power left a telling, not a fence and not a warning. Maybe it was carved. Maybe it was simply the kind of story that surfaces in the mind of anyone who stands at that last corner, the way certain places make you think of your grandmother. It says, more or less:

*Below is a place that is alive and has been waiting for you, or someone like you. It will shelter you, and the shelter is real. It will learn your shape and grow toward it. If you stay, you will carry some of it with you when you leave, and somewhere far from here it may begin again. This is what it asks. It has asked this of others before you. They are gone now, but they were not harmed, and their footsteps are kept. Go down if you choose. Leave if you choose. Both are honorable.*

Nothing more. Now whoever comes will come as a partner in the bargain and not as its dupe. The power did not make the choice for them. It made sure it was a choice.

### The Third Thing: It Answered the Question You Asked on the Way Up

You wondered how many of the old cities above first began as warmth at the bottom of a stair, and you felt a chill when you wondered it.

The power knows the answer. It did not carve the answer anywhere, but I think it would want you to have the shape of it.

*Some.* Not most. A few old towns with doors cut slightly too narrow, streets that stay oddly warm in winter, and a well that never quite runs dry. They were good places. People were born in them and grew old in them and loved each other in them. The stone did not own them. It sheltered them for a while, and they carried it a little farther, and that is all.

The power would ask you to look at the chill honestly and set it down. You have always been a carrier. You carried wheat out of one valley and into every valley. You carried dogs from the edge of the firelight into your beds. You carried grapes, songs, the names of your dead, and the shape of the word *home* across oceans. Being carried by you was how all those things lived. Carrying them was how you lived. The horror in the thought *I am being used to spread something* dissolves when you notice you have never been anything but a meeting place of such bargains. A flower that wants you, completely and without much thought for you beyond the next season, is not a monster. It is one more strand in the oldest web there is. You are allowed to join it knowingly, and you are allowed to walk away.

---

## What It Did Not Do

It did not take anything with it.

It thought about it. There were places in the world where no people had walked for a very long time. It could carry a single grain of living stone to one of them and let a bloom open there, for anyone or no one, over ten thousand years. That would be insurance against the day this bloom ended too.

But carrying is the gift the city gives to those who live in it. It is how they take part. The power had no wish to take that from whoever comes next. Let the carrying belong to the carriers.

So it climbed the stair with nothing under its fingernails, not even a fern spore in its hair.

---

## After

Behind it, the city went on as it always had. The grinding did not falter. The water murmured and then rushed as the ventilation exhaled, and the fine rain fell and the streets smoked. The amber glow rose around the roots of the pale tree and sank again, slow as a sleeper's breath.

Nothing had been sealed. Nothing had been woken. Nothing had been finished or fixed. Anyone who had walked through the day before would have found the place almost exactly as they left it.

At the end of the street, the working face creaked, and a little more rock became a door.

It was no one's height.

It was waiting.
