# The Attention

## The First Thing It Learned

It came the way attention comes, all at once and from everywhere. For the space of what, on the plateau, would have been a single long pause, it rested its whole regard on the black stone and the white sea and the few pale figures moving between the pools.

The next morning, in forty-one cisterns, the water stood a hair above the mark.

It wasn't a finger. It was less than that, a meniscus's width, the kind of thing no one anywhere else would ever see. Here, forty-one people knelt at forty-one cistern walls, looked, and went still. Then, one by one, without calling to one another, because they never call, they stood and began to walk, each toward the nearest neighbor. For the first time in a very long while, the plateau had something to say.

The power saw this and understood the first lesson, which was that its looking had weight. The air had been warmed by the barest degree by the pressure of being beheld. It had come intending no change at all, and it had already changed the only thing these people were organized to notice.

So before it did anything else, it made itself lighter. It thinned its regard the way you would breathe out slowly near a candle, until it no longer pressed on the saturated air. It kept thinning until the condensation slowed back to its ancient rate and the water in the forty-one cisterns sank, over a day, back to the cut line. The walkers arrived at their neighbors' pools. They sat. The water was at the mark. They looked at one another with pauses of very particular length, and after a while each walked home.

The power, now no heavier than a held breath, felt something it did not have a better name for than shame. Then it felt respect. These people had caught it on the first day.

## What It Considered

It is the nature of a benevolent power to see what could be given. It saw a great deal.

**It could give them bees.** It could put small, soft, humming things into the pale flowers so that no one would ever again have to carry pollen in a fold of wet leaf across half a day of shining rock. It looked at this gift for a long time. Then it looked at the walks: the long careful crossings, the brush of fern-down, the four times in one life that someone made the great journey so a tree could set seed. It understood that bees would not relieve the people of a burden. They would relieve the people of their greatest journeys. And the plateau would begin to hum, and the hum would never stop. The word for a drip that falls a little late would become a word no one could use, because no one would hear it.

**It could give them soil.** It could grind the edges down and soften the flows so that ferns and trees could spread, food would be plentiful, and the houses could sit close together. But the distance between the houses was not poverty. It was arithmetic, and it was respect. A people who take only what falls on them would become a people who take what grows, and there is always more that could grow. The power did not think this was wrong in other places. It thought it would be a loss here.

**It could lengthen their lives,** or shorten their dying, or make the children more frequent. But their lives were already as long as the moss's patience. Their dying was a walk they chose when they knew their level. Their children were each paired with a tree and grew up beside it until the tree was older. None of this seemed to the power like something broken.

**It could tell them what lies below the cloud.** This one was the hardest to set down, because it would have been so easy, and it seemed so kind. There was an answer, and the power could have laid it gently in their minds: here is where you go, here is where you come from, you need not wonder.

But they did not wonder. That was the thing. *What happens on the other side of a pause is not the listener's business.* This was not ignorance. It was a courtesy they extended to the world. To answer a question they had deliberately chosen not to ask would be like talking over someone.

Having thought this, the power did something it had not expected to do. It turned its own attention away from the white. It did not look beneath the cloud. It had not yet looked, having been drawn by the plateau, and now it decided it wouldn't. If the people could keep that courtesy for a lifetime, the power could keep it too. It found, a little to its surprise, that not knowing felt like a kind of kinship.

## What It Found

It did look down in the other direction, into the stone.

It had to. It could not love a place well without knowing what the place stood on. So it let a thread of itself, thinner than a root, sink down through the young basalt, through the unfractured flows and the watertight dark, past where any fissure reached.

Far down, it found heat.

The mountain was not finished. The long fissure that had opened a few centuries ago had not closed. It had paused. Below the plateau, deeper than anything the people had ever pressed an ear to, the rock was still slowly, patiently molten. It was not rising yet, and it would not rise for a long time by any measure the people used, perhaps not for several of their very long lives. But it would rise. The power could feel the pressure the way the people feel an interval, as a silence that means something.

One day the stone would warm. The pools would steam. The drips would stop.

Now the power understood why the people had that one old word, rarely used, which they spoke the way other people say *fire*.

## The Woman on the Eastern Rim

It went to her because she was the only one it had not yet understood.

She sat at the lip of the highest pool on the eastern edge, where the columns dropped away into the white, and she almost never moved. Everyone else on the plateau watched the west, where the water went and where people came up. She watched the east, and her cistern, the power now saw, was cut with two marks. One was the ordinary line where the water had always stood. The other was lower, far lower, near the bottom, where the water would stand if the stone beneath grew warm enough to drink it.

She was not watching for change. She was watching for one particular change. She was the one who remembered the stone warm, and nobody asked her, because it would be like asking a tree. So nobody knew she had spent her whole enormous life keeping the eastern watch alone.

The power did not appear to her. It had learned by now not to press. It simply let itself be near, as thin as the air, and sat.

She knew. Of course she knew. It had taken the cisterns one morning to notice it; she did not need a cistern. After a long time, long even for her, she spoke a single word into the damp air without turning her head.

The power did not know the word. It knew most words, in most places, but this was a word made mostly of pause. It understood that it had been greeted, and that the length of the silence after the greeting was a question. It understood that it could not answer, because it did not yet know how long to wait.

So it stayed, and it learned.

It learned over what, for the plateau, was a short while: a season of drips, several dozen pauses. It learned that the silence before a word is the speaker's and the silence after is the listener's. It learned that a gap left unanswered is not rude but generous, because it gives the other person room. It learned the difference between a drip that falls on time and one that falls late, and why the late one is sweeter: it means the world did not do quite what you expected, and was still all right.

When at last it answered her, it answered with nothing but an interval. The power had never before spoken a whole sentence with silence. This one said, roughly: *I have looked down. I know what you are watching for.*

She was quiet for a long time after that, and the length of it said: *Yes.*

Then, with a pause the power had to hold for a long while before it understood, she asked it what it meant to do.

## What It Did Not Do

It could still the mountain.

This was well within its power. It could cool the heat far below, quietly and invisibly, and lock the fissure shut for ten thousand years or for good. The plateau would stay young and sharp and weeping. No one but the woman would ever know that anything had been saved.

It considered this more seriously than anything else, because it was the one gift that would change nothing that anyone could see.

But it looked at the stone, and the stone was here because the mountain had opened. The plateau was the mountain's gift, its last one. Every pool was a hollow where the lava had folded as it cooled. The columns the people built with had split into hexagons because of how fast the fire had set. This place was not something the fire had spared. It was something the fire had made. To lock the fire away forever would be to turn the plateau into a thing kept under glass: preserved by a hand from outside, beautiful and no longer quite alive.

It also looked at the people, and it understood something it had not seen at first. They did not live as though the plateau would last forever. They lived as though it would last exactly as long as it did. They watched the marks because things change. They had a word for a drip that stops because drips do. They walked west when they knew their level because everything here runs downhill. And the cloud, so far as anyone could tell, gave them back. The power had declined to look, but it believed this.

The people would be all right. When the stone warmed, they would walk west together, perhaps for the first time ever in company. The white would close over their dew-beaded hair, and whatever happens on the other side of that pause would happen.

The trees would not walk west.

That was what the woman had been keeping watch for all her life. It was not her own death, and not her people's. It was the trees. Every one of them had been put there by a particular person. The stands of three or four were families, and the whole slow green map of the plateau's water was a record of long walks and patience and seeds pressed into cracks and waited over for years. The cloud could give people back. It had never given back a tree.

## What It Did

So this is what it did, and it is very small.

It did not stop the mountain. It slowed it, a little, the way the people slow everything. It eased the heat below by the barest fraction, the way you might lay a hand on a sleeper's shoulder so that they wake a little later and more gently. The mountain would still wake, but it would wake unhurried and give warning, and the warning would come in a form the people knew how to read: a cistern a finger low on the eastern rim, a drip that fell late and then later.

Then it made the woman a promise, in intervals, which took a long time to say.

When the stone warms, it told her, and the drips stop, and the people walk west, it would stay behind. It would go to every tree on the plateau, one by one, across the hot black rock, and take a seed from each. It would carry each seed held close in a fold of wet leaf so that it didn't dry. It would not make a new place for them, because it had learned that it should not make places. It would wait, however long that took, for somewhere the rock was young and the air was saturated and a fissure caught water. There it would press each seed into the crack, sit beside it, and wait to see whether the root came out across the stone like poured wax.

It would remember whose each one was. It would carry that too.

The woman listened to all of this without moving. When it was finished she was silent for so long that the power began to wonder whether she would answer. It found that it did not mind waiting. It understood now that the waiting was the answer being made.

At last she spoke one word. It was the sweet one, for a drip that falls a little late.

## Afterward

She died not long after, as the plateau counts it, perhaps half a lifetime of someone younger. She walked west slowly and stopped at every house, and the people were astonished, because she had not moved from the eastern rim in longer than any of them had been alive. At each pool she sat a while without speaking. At some of them she pressed her palm flat against the stone for a long time, and the people did not know why.

At the lowest terrace she walked into the white, and it closed over her, and there was no sound.

The eastern watch passed to someone else. That person never quite knew why they had been drawn to take it up, or why they had cut a second mark low on the cistern wall. They only knew it seemed right.

The power stayed, as thin as air and no heavier than a breath, so that the water held at the mark. Sometimes one of the people lay on a terrace with an ear pressed to the basalt, and if you asked, they would say after a pause of meaningful length that they were listening to someone.

The power had decided it would not answer them. That would be talking over the stone. But it listened back. For the first time in the plateau's history, whatever the people meant by *someone*, there was one.

Every so often, when a person walked half a day across the shining rock with a fold of pale dust held against their body, it went with them. It did not help, only walked beside them. It carried nothing, because the carrying was theirs.

It was practicing.
