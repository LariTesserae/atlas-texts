# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not come the way travelers come. It did not cross the last low ridge, or feel the pressure behind its eyes resolve into sound. It had no eyes, at first, and no ridge to cross. It was simply there, the way the roar is simply there, and had been there, perhaps, for some while before anything in the Mill could have said so.

What drew it is hard to name. It was not a cry, because nothing cries in the Mill that is not ground up within a hand's width. It was not suffering, or not mainly. It was closer to the feeling you get in a large house at night when you realize that somewhere, in a room you haven't entered, someone has been patiently working at something for a very long time.

It attended, and the first thing it learned was that attention has edges.

It is not usually aware of this. Most places receive its regard the way a field receives light. But the Mill received its regard the way the Mill receives everything. The stones rolled against it. The warm milky water leaned on it. Within what would have been, for a traveler, a few days, it found that its attention had begun to lose its sharpness, and it could no longer quite say where its noticing of one thing ended and its noticing of the next began.

It could have stopped this. It is the kind of power that could have held its edges against a continent.

It chose not to, and that was the first thing it did.

## What It Could Have Done

It is worth saying plainly what was within its reach, because a benevolent power is measured partly by what it declines.

It could have silenced the roar. One gesture, and the stones would have stilled from rim to heart. There would have been a quiet deeper than any cairn's, the first true silence on the plain in ten million years.

It considered this for a long time, because travelers suffer here and it does not like suffering. Then it followed the silence forward. The friction would stop, and the warmth with it. The mist would settle out of the air as a fine pale dust over everything. The water would cool. The mat, which lives only on freshly turned faces, would find no fresh faces and die in a week. The crabs would find no churn to ride. The reeds would lose the difference between fixity and flow that gives them meaning. The Held, who have never heard anything but the roar, would wake to the only thing they have no word for. They would stand in their sleeping-knot, pressed shoulder to shoulder, and feel for the first time that they were alone in their own bodies.

Silence, here, would not be a gift. It would be a flood.

It could have raised mountains and given the Mill back its relief. But that would hand a workshop at the end of a long task a task it had already finished. It would be like taking a very old woman's nearly round stone out of her hand and giving her a jagged one, saying *here, now you can start again*.

It could have washed the Held clean, given them voices, opened their eyes, taught them to count. It looked at their language, a single conversation continuous since before any of them, with no gaps in it and so no loneliness. It understood that it had been about to offer them a smaller thing than they already had.

It could have sealed the holes.

This one it did not dismiss quickly.

## The Holes

A benevolent power has responsibilities that a traveler does not. A traveler can leave the Mill's mysteries as mysteries. A power that can see farther ought to look, at least once, to be sure nothing is being lost that should not be lost.

So it went to a hole-drift. The Held children are taught to back away from these with their eyes closed. It went close and attended to one of the small dark pebble-shapes with no surface, the ones the mist does not enter, the ones that leave a notch in the roar.

What it saw there it did not tell anyone. It will not be told here either, because what it saw was the reason not to tell.

This much can be said. The world is not fraying toward ruin. The holes are not wounds. A stone that the Mill has entirely finished has somewhere to go. The fraying, seen closely, has the look of a weave, and the weave has a far side. The power stood at the edge of a drift for what would have been a season, attending. Then it did what the Held do. It laid two fingers of its attention flat against the place, not pressing, not moving, simply staying. Then it backed away.

It did not seal them. It is possible to be wise enough to see through a thing and still leave it closed for others.

## The Hand

For all of this it had been alone. It could have gone on alone indefinitely, surveying, weighing, reaching conclusions. Many powers do.

But it had noticed something about the Held language. You cannot start a sentence in it. You can only join one already happening.

It understood that this applied to it. It could not begin a conversation with the Held, however great it was. It could only be joined. So it did the one thing a power almost never does. It made itself small enough to have a shoulder, and stood in the leaning water with the stones shrugging under its new soles, and waited.

It waited a long time. The Mill wore at it. Its thoughts began to arrive without beginnings.

Then there was a hand on its shoulder.

The palm was warm and dry and weighted so exactly that it felt like being leaned on by a friend. The fingers began to move in long pressures that shaded into one another. The power, which could have read the whole history of the plain in a glance, found that it could not read this. The touch did not carry information. It carried *saying*, and saying cannot be read quickly, only received.

So it received. Over weeks, it felt meaning arrive without being able to say when it arrived. It was welcome. A squall line was two days downwind. The crabs were rising near a certain drift and should be left alone. It already knew all of this, from far above and long before. Knowing it this way was something else, and it understood this was the first thing it had learned in the Mill that it could not have learned any other way.

## The Stone

They gave it a stone.

There was no ceremony. The woman whose hand had found it bent in the milky water and brought up whatever stone was at her feet: a grey basalt, ordinary, not especially round. She pressed it into the power's palm and closed its fingers around it. Then she laid her thumb over its thumb and moved it in a circle, and then again. She did this the way she had done it for her children, as though it were an infant who did not yet know what hands were for.

The power had shaped worlds. It held the stone and rubbed it.

The stone would not be round in its lifetime here, or in hers. That was not the point. The Mill rubs everything. The Held rub one thing, small, on purpose, with love. The power had arrived intending, if it was wise enough, to do something for the whole plain. It found itself being taught to do something for one stone.

It thought this might be the most important thing it had ever been taught.

## What It Asked

When it had enough of the language to ask anything, it asked the only question it had come with.

*Is there anything you need?*

It is hard to ask this in a language with no questions, one made only of continuous pressure. What it managed was a slow widening of the palm across the woman's back, an opening. It was the touch you make when you are leaving room for someone else's hand.

She did not answer for a long time. She kept rubbing her own stone, which was nearly round. Her name had worn to a flat warm palm, and the grey had begun, faintly, to come up through the silt on her skin.

Then she took its hand and walked it for many days to the Slow One.

They found it moving at the pace of a season, the last hill, domed and warm and alive. Its shell was wearing away on top and growing back rough and edged from beneath. She pressed the power's palm against its flank and held it there. Under the roar the power felt the vast sub-sonic groan of new shell being made.

Then she pressed into its forearm the touch that means *the one who keeps getting edges*.

The power understood that this was her answer. It was not a request. It was a correction.

It had come thinking the Mill was a place where things were being ended, and wondering whether it ought to intervene. The Held had never thought that. They had always known the Mill contains its own counterweight. Beside all the finishing there is one being that keeps un-finishing. The Held rub their stones round, and then they walk with the Slow One for months and sleep in its lee and press their hands to it like a mother. They do not need a power to arrive and give the world back its edges. The world is already giving itself its edges, one shell at a time, at a pace too slow for anything but love to keep up with.

What she was telling it, as far as it can be put in words with edges, was this: *We have what we need. It is slow. Walk with it if you like.*

It walked with the Slow One.

## The Hesitation

It stayed long enough to learn one more thing.

With its smooth new soles it learned to feel the places where the Mill hesitates, the tiny moments when a cobble is held balanced by the whole plain's pushing, all the forces around it cancelling. It took a very long time, longer than it had ever spent learning anything. It had to stop knowing where the hesitations would be, which it could have calculated, and wait to *feel* them coming, which cannot be calculated.

When at last it felt one arriving, a breath away, under its left foot, it did not set a stone on it.

It had meant to. It had imagined leaving a cairn on the Mill, a small true word in the long sentence. But in the moment before the hesitation came, it felt a child of the Held standing a few arm-lengths off, also listening with her feet. She had felt it too, and she was young, and it would be her first.

The power took its foot away and let her have it.

She set the second stone. She waited three days for the third, standing utterly still in the flow, and it came. The water parted. The mat began to grow old. The roar, just there, forgot to be quite so loud. She did not know that anyone had stepped aside for her. She knew only that the Mill had paused and she had been there.

That was the second thing it did.

## The Doorway

Before it left, it went to the doorframe: two upright stones and a lintel, standing alone in the flow. On the far side the roar sounds a fraction older.

It could have stepped through. It could have followed the older roar wherever the older roar went, and learned what lies on the other side of a pause the Mill did not keep. It could have closed the door, or widened it, or finished the sentence the travelers' account breaks off in the middle of.

It stood beside the doorway for a long time instead, the way the Held do, feeling with its feet the slight difference between the water on one side and the water on the other. It put its palm on the uprights. It did not go through.

Some sentences are not broken. They are only waiting for someone to stop needing them finished.

## Its Name

When it was ready to go, the Held gathered around it at what passes here for dusk and drew it into the sleeping-knot. They stood shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, no gap anywhere, every body touching six or seven others. It slept on its feet among them, and by morning the knot had turned and it was in the warm middle. For the length of that night it did not feel the roar as noise. It felt it as the sound of being among.

In the morning they named it.

This was the one strange thing, the thing no traveler has ever reported. They did not give it an old name, a flat warm palm. They gave it a child's name: a quick sharp pinch, in the crook of the elbow. It was an edge, a beginning.

It did not understand this at first. Then it thought of the Slow One. The Held had watched this vast attention come down onto their plain and make itself small and let itself be worn. They had watched it rub one stone, step aside from one hesitation, and leave one door alone. They had decided it was not something being finished. It was something that had come, in its enormity, to be started again.

As it went, the first of them to press the name pressed it very slightly more softly than the pinch had been. Without meaning to, the way a name begins to wear.

## What It Did

So, if the account must be finished: what did it do?

It did not silence the roar, or raise the hills, or seal the holes, or close the door. It did not wash the Held, or give them voices, or tell them what they could not hear.

It let its edges be worn. It waited to be joined, since it could not begin. It rubbed one ordinary stone, and it will go on rubbing it, wherever it is now. It looked into the one place that needed a power's looking, found nothing there that needed saving, and kept the finding to itself. It walked for a season beside the thing that keeps getting edges. It stepped aside so a child could catch her first pause. It accepted a beginning in a land that has none.

And it left the Mill as it found it: patient and warm and tireless, grinding, holding its own counterweight in its own slow body, full of people who were already doing, small and on purpose and with love, the thing a benevolent power might have hoped to do.

Somewhere on the plain, near a new cairn that no one knows a power stepped aside from, a grey bird stands on one leg in a pocket of quiet. A child's hand rests on its long neck, two fingers flat where a wrist would be, not pressing, not moving, simply staying.

*Here.*
