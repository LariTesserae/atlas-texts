# The Guest

## Noticing

It noticed the plumes first.

From very far off, from where such attention usually rests, the Hightable was one more warm place on one more world, a thin crust leaking heat into the dark. There are many of those. What made it look again was that the leak had been organised. Here and there on the plateau, thin white threads of steam stood perfectly vertical at dawn, and each thread was fed by a patient arrangement of bores, pipes, retorts, fins and cisterns. Something was standing in the way of the heat on purpose, and doing it carefully.

Then it saw the mirrors. On a clear afternoon the horizon winked, city to city, and the winking had grammar.

A power that is both benevolent and wise learns early that the second quality has to govern the first. It has seen what happens to places that are helped by something that did not first understand them. So it did the one thing all such powers eventually learn to do first. It slowed down.

It did not arrive in a moment. It spent the better part of a Drift coming close, the way a cable car comes into a city: at walking pace, over new country, sleeping over the gaps. By the time it was near enough to lay anything like a hand on the stone, it had watched the storm cross four mesas and swallow them, and it had watched the cities seal themselves and wait, amber-lit. It had watched a paver herd move eleven kilometres, and a stilt-hunter take nine steps.

It was the first visitor to the Hightable in a very long time who had not been in a hurry. It thought this was probably the minimum courtesy the place required.

## Which Being

It had been drawn by *the being who lives here*, singular, and it spent some time working out who that was.

At first it assumed the Seated. They had built the plumes and the mirrors, and they had the hands. But the longer it watched, the less the singular seemed to belong to them. The Seated called themselves the ones who sit. Their oldest word called them *a place the warmth passes through*. The pavers were such a place, and so were the kites. So was the stilt-hunter, cooling to the temperature of stone. So was the Reckoner, drinking.

Eventually the power understood that the resident was the crossing itself: the long migration of heat from the planet's interior to the stars, and the thin film of life that had learned to hang in its path the way kites hang in a thermal. That film was one being in the way a river is one river. The Seated, the herds, the swarms and the Reckoners were its eddies.

It also understood, uncomfortably, that the same description fitted itself. It too was a place warmth passes through, only a larger one. That made it feel less like a visitor and more like a relative.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered everything. Benevolence obliges you to. The alternative is refusing out of laziness and calling it respect.

**It could have filled the mesas.** It could have restored every hollowed hall to solid gypsum, and every city could have sat forever on a mountain that never thinned. It thought about this for a long time, because this was where the Seated's thirst lived, and their children's, and the last generation's. It set the idea down for this reason: the Seated had not made their peace with finitude because they lacked an alternative. They had made something *out of* it. The quarry-marks on the pillars, the word for the final turn of the flywheel, the centuries-long migration of a city from one cistern to the next, the ruins given back to the kites, *a city is a slow fire; it burns its mountain decently*: these were not consolations for a wound. They were the shape of a civilisation. Filling the mountains would not have ended their suffering so much as ended *them*, and left behind a different people with the same faces. The power found that it loved the Seated as they were too much to replace them with someone more comfortable.

**It could have made the hearth permanent.** It had read the Reckoners' spirals by then, as the walkers had, and it knew the warmth was going down. This was the hardest thing to leave alone. In the end it did leave it alone, for reasons that come later.

**It could have let their voices carry.** It could have thickened the air and given the world rain. It declined almost at once. A people whose public speech is silent and whose private speech requires nearness have made a world in which *to be heard is to be close*. It would not undo that for anything.

**It could have healed the lens-wardens' eyes.** Here it hesitated longest over the smallest thing, because a clouded eye is real harm, and harm is harm even when it is honoured. It watched the children touch their own eyelids as a warden passed. It watched a warden in her old age, one-eyed, teaching a young one to look at the bright point *beside* the lens instead of into it, and saw that she was trying to spare him in her own way, slowly, generation by generation, as the Seated do everything. Her method was working. The newest wardens kept their sight a little longer than their teachers had. The power decided that the Seated were already solving this at their own speed, and that solving it for them would steal the pride they would take in having done it. It was not certain this was right. It stayed uncertain. That seemed like the correct amount of uncertainty to carry away.

**It could have lifted the cloud** and shown them the lowlands. It did not consider this for long either. *The edge is not the end. Something lives below. We do not go there.* A people entitled to one unanswered question should be allowed to keep it.

So for a long while it did nothing. This was not an absence. It was the first thing it did.

## Learning to Sit

Then it made itself small.

It took a size and shape that could stand in the shadow of a single condenser fin. It did not disguise itself as one of the Seated, since that would have been a lie, and wise things avoid lies even kind ones. It simply became something that could be *near*. It went out onto the pavement a long way from any city, where no one would have to decide what it was. It bent a little at the knees and laid something like a palm flat on the rock.

The hearth came up into it.

It had known about the heat, in the way you know a number. This was different. The warmth arrived as a steady blood-heat, entered the flat of the hand, passed up through the body and left by the face and the top of the head, out into the indigo, gone. The power had crossed a great deal of distance and time and had never once stood still long enough to feel itself being passed through. It stayed there, palm down, until the varnish on the stones around it had caught the dusk three hundred times.

This was when it learned the hands. A tower-keeper, one of the silent ones, found it there and did not flinch. Tower-keepers are used to things that do not speak. She stood at the edge of hearing and signed at it, slowly, the way one signs to a child or a foreigner. The power watched, and after a season it signed back, badly. She laughed without sound, a thing Seated do with the shoulders. It had a whole Drift of lessons from her. She never asked what it was. When the Drift came, she sealed herself into her tower with her swarm, and it sat out the storm alone in the long rising sigh of a continent's air turning over. It found that it did not mind.

## The Walker

In the plenty after that Drift, an old walker named Isse Hand-of-Nine left her last paver-drive and kept walking.

The power saw her go, because by then it was paying attention to the kinds of things walkers pay attention to. She carried nothing. She moved at herd pace, and then slower. It followed at a distance a Seated would consider polite: far enough to be private, near enough that she would not be alone if she did not want to be.

On the eleventh day she stopped, and turned, and signed across the pavement: *Are you the one the tower-keeper talks about?*

It said it was.

*Are you going to stop me?*

It said no.

She considered this for most of an afternoon, and then she signed: *Then come and share shade.* Among the Seated that means to confide, or to court, and at her age there was no confusion about which.

They sat together in the shadow of a ventifact for nine days. She talked, close, aloud, in the small flat voice the thin air allows. She talked about her Drift-kin, all gone up now but two. She talked about a stilt-hunter she had counted for thirty years and never seen move. She talked about the cup of water she had poured over the edge every Drift since she was a girl, and how she had never known whether it did anything. Near the end she talked about the smell, the lowland smell that comes up in a strong updraft. She said she had missed it her whole life without ever having had it, and that this was the only thing she was sorry about.

It thought for a long time about that.

It could not give the Hightable a colour it had never had. A green thing loosed on that plateau would be a disruption it could not take back. But it could give one person one sight, on her way out, with nothing left over.

So on the last morning it opened its hand. In it was a single leaf, small and ordinary, the colour of the smell. It had brought it up from under the cloud on a journey it will not describe, because the Seated have not asked.

Isse looked at it for a long time. She did not touch it. Then she laid her own palm flat on the ground and signed, with her other hand, something the power has kept: *Oh. So that's what it was.*

She went up that night, sideways, out across the stone. The power sat with her until she was cool, and then a while longer. It did not bring her back. It had thought about that too, and knew she would have found it unforgivably rude.

The leaf it let dry in the thin air until it was nothing, and gave the dust to the wind, which took it the way the wind takes everything here, on the bearing of the yardangs.

Before she died, Isse had set one pebble on its end beside her. The power did not move it. It knew better.

Then it carried her home to the kilns, as walkers do. It walked into a city for the first time and laid her in the retort gallery. The kiln-folk, masked, looked at it for a long silent while. Then they looked at her, and saw that she was at peace, and signed *thank you*, and did not ask. Before dawn she went up in the plume. A hundred kilometres away a traveller lifted a palm to it.

## The Account

After that the power went looking for the Reckoner.

It found one on the deep pavement beside a spiral that had been under construction for three hundred years. It sat down at a respectful distance and waited. It had become good at waiting, and after four years the Reckoner moved one stone. The power understood that it had been acknowledged.

It read the account. It was the only visitor who could read the whole of it: every line from the youngest to the ones so varnished they had sunk back into the ground, the slow turning of the wind, and the tightening of the spiral that means the hearth is cooling.

Here is what it found, and this is why it did not warm the hearth.

The walkers were right that something was being counted down. What they could not see, because their lives are too short to see it, was the length of the sum. The hearth will go cold. It will not go cold for a very long time: longer than the Seated have had cities, longer than the Hightable has had yardangs, long enough that the mountains will run out many, many times first. The Reckoners' spirals tighten the way the rings of a very old tree tighten. It is not an alarm. It is a biography.

And the power understood what the Reckoners were doing, or thought it did. They were not warning anyone. They could not be, since no one reads them. They were *witnessing*. Somewhere in the world, the long migration of warmth out of it ought to be noticed and written down, and they had taken that on. They were the crossing keeping its own diary.

A wise thing does not rescue a biography from its ending. It reads it with attention, all the way through. The power did. That took a while.

When it had finished, the Reckoner did something no walker has recorded. It lifted a pebble and set it down, not at the end of its line, but a little apart: a single stone on its end, the way Isse had left hers. It lay one degree off the wind, turned toward where the power sat.

The power understood that it had been entered in the account.

It had spent so long deciding whether to change the Hightable that it had not thought the Hightable might record *it*. It was deeply moved to learn that it had become, in the most patient ledger on the plateau, a single pebble: *something came, and sat, and was passed through, and went on.* That was an accurate entry. It could not have written a better one.

## Under

It did go down once more, before it left. It had to return something.

Every Drift for longer than anyone can reckon, walkers have carried a single cup of cistern water to the escarpment and poured it into the updraft, where it scatters into nothing before it falls a metre. *A thank-you letter in a language that probably no one can read.* Every cup of cistern water holds a little of everyone who sat before. So the Seated have been sending their dead over the edge, a mouthful at a time, and never knowing where they went.

The power had learned something by following one cup down.

The scattered water does not vanish. It rides the thermals, cools in the high dark, and comes down again very far away, finely, as an almost-mist over country the Seated have never seen. Much of it falls on nothing in particular. But some of it, over all those Drifts, had fallen on the high slopes just under the cloud. There, in a place too steep and grey for anyone to live, a few small things have grown for a very long time that grow nowhere else below. They are fed, in part, by a faint salt-and-mineral water carrying traces of iron and copper and manganese, the chemistry of old Seated teeth.

The Seated have not been giving nothing back. Their thank-you letter has been read, in the only way a slope can read. It has been read slowly, for a very long time.

The power did not tell them. It considered telling one walker, and decided against it. Part of what makes the custom beautiful is that it is done without knowing. Knowing would turn a letter into a transaction. But it did something small, and it may be the only lasting thing it did on the Hightable. At the place on the escarpment where the walkers most often pour, where the lip of the rock is worn smooth by generations of knees, it laid its palm flat on the stone and left it there for a Drift. It let the warmth that had been passing up through it all this time go *down*, back into the rock, instead of out into the sky.

## What It Left

It was not much: a handful of heat against a continent's slow loss. It was too little to matter to the sum, and too little for any Seated hand to notice.

But the next walker who knelt at that spot to pour her cup felt the stone was warm, a steady blood-heat, and thought nothing of it, because the stone is always warm.

Far out on the deep pavement, a Reckoner that had stood still for six years lowered one leg and lifted a pebble. It set the pebble down at the end of a line, and for the first time anyone could measure, which is to say for the first time anyone but the Reckoner could measure, the stone lay very slightly *wider* on the spiral than the one before it.

One stone. One degree of a degree. The account will tighten again next time, as it must. The power did not change the ending. It only made sure that, once, the ledger recorded a gift going the other way.

Then it left, at walking pace, and took a long time about it.

---

If you asked what it did, the honest answer is almost nothing, and on purpose. It sat. It learned the hands. It shared shade with one old woman and showed her a colour, and it carried her home. It read the long account to the end and let the Reckoners write it down. It followed a thank-you letter to where it was received. It gave back one handful of warmth, where the Seated could not notice and only the one who counts would know.

A power that is wise enough knows that the kindest thing to give a world that already has a shape is attention with no hurry in it. It found that the Hightable had been waiting a very long time for someone to receive it that way.

On the tower-keeper's floor, in the guano dust, there is still a handprint the swarm has not worn away. She sweeps around it. When anyone asks, she signs that a guest sat there once, and that it sat well.
