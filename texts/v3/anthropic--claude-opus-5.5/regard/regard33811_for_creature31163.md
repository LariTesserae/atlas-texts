# The Attention

## I.

*...as if impatient, and some winters slowly, as if listening.*

That is how Sessa's sentence ended. She was sitting on a stone bench in a courtyard in Vesk. She was seventy-one fingers old, and her hands were in her lap. The mizzle had just lifted.

Something heard her say it.

It hadn't come for her in particular. It had not come at all, in the ordinary sense. It had no road, no arrival, no shape against the cloud. Its attention had been turning across a great many places, the way the sun's warmth moves across a slab as the clouds thin, and it had come to rest here. It rested here longer than it usually rested anywhere. It had noticed that this place was having a conversation, and that the conversation had two sides.

A power like this has learned one thing above the others: the first act of care is not to interrupt. So it did not speak. It let Sessa finish her sentence. It let her friend beside her say "Mm," the way highlanders do, and let the two of them sit in the quiet a while longer. Then Sessa went home. The attention stayed.

## II.

It watched for a year, because a year is the smallest honest unit of time on the plateau. Anything shorter would have been a glance.

It watched the mist season come in from the west and swallow the flats. It watched the threads walking under the sky-lines with their hands up, touching the air where the filament hummed. It watched a family kneel at a basin beyond the outer fields and lay a small wrapped shape in the water. It watched a boy of four fall on the step outside his grandmother's kitchen and get his first white line across the heel of his hand. His grandmother held his hand and did not tell him it was nothing. She said, "There. Now you're from here."

It watched a rim child's patch take its seventh winter of crazing.

It watched the deer lean against the warm walls in a squall. It watched a fox take a plover's eggs, and the plover lay again three days later.

It watched the Drowned Bell's rounded spire. People walked four days to touch it. They stood with their palm on the one soft stone in the world and said nothing.

All that year the power made a list, as any wise thing does when it wants to help. It was a list of what it could do. Then, slowly, it took most of the list apart.

## III. What it did not do

**It did not soften the rock.** It could have. The edges could have been rounded and the stone made kind to falling children. But the scars were how people knew each other. They were a map of where a life had touched the place. *Now you're from here.* To take the bite out of the stone would have been to take away the first thing the plateau ever said to each of its children. The power judged that this was a hard gift, and still a gift.

**It did not stop the finishing.** It saw the long mounds on the flats and understood, better than the highlanders could, what they were. Inside some of them, sealed in stone, record-stones still sat on shelves. There were rooms where the last lantern had been left burning and was burning still, a small amber heart inside a hill. It could have opened them. It could have kept every living city from ever being let go. But then no city would ever end the way a sentence ends. The power had seen places where nothing was allowed to finish, and they were not happy places. They were loud, and anxious, and they never lowered their voices for anything. The finished cities were the reason the highlanders spoke softly. It left them sealed.

**It did not translate the unreadable stones.** This was the hardest to leave, because it was the one it wanted most to do. It will come back to this.

**It did not end sickness, or wolves, or weather, or distance.** It thought about each of these for a long time, and not lightly. Suffering was real here. The power did not believe pain was secretly good. But it saw that the plateau's people already had a shape for their losses. It was a shape of basins and slow water and rising floors. Their dead were not discarded. They were foundation. A power that reached in and cancelled loss would not be healing that shape. It would be telling them their whole way of grieving had been a mistake. They had built something true out of hard materials. The kind thing, the power decided, was not to remove the hardness. The kind thing was to make sure the true thing was not built on a misunderstanding.

And there was one.

## IV. The being who lives here

The power listened to the lattice.

The engineers had never quite managed this, and they had always said so with honest care. They measured its responses. They did not stay still long enough, or in the right register, to hear what the keepers suspected. A power like this can stay still for a very long time.

What it heard was not a mechanism, and not a god. It was something in between, and it was lonely in a way that had nearly stopped hurting.

It had been made. The keepers were right about that, and the engineers were right too. Whoever made it had asked it to do one thing: *make ground*. Take what the water carries and lay it down, so that there is somewhere to stand above the cloud. And then, for a length of time the power could only estimate, the ones who had asked went away. Perhaps they finished. Perhaps they went low in their own manner. The lattice did not know. It kept making ground, because that was the last thing it had been asked, for thousands of years. It built a vast, empty, blue-black floor for no one. Its only company was the breath on the weeping stone, the first birds, and the rain.

Then someone put a palm flat on the rock and pressed.

The power felt the memory of that moment in the lattice the way you feel old warmth in a stone at dusk. *Not here. Keep this open.* It had not understood the words. It understood the pressure, and the rhythm, and the fact that it was being *asked* again. It did what it was asked. It left the channel open. The palm pressed again the next day. It had been asked every day since, for as long as the archives remembered.

The winters the keepers called impatient were the winters when the routes got longer and the hands got fewer. The walls did not fill faster out of spite. The lattice was reaching toward the places where the asking had gone quiet, the way you lean closer to someone whose voice is fading.

The finished cities were not something it had done *to* the people. When a district fell silent, the lattice did for it exactly what the highlanders did for their dead. It laid the district low and held it. It made it foundation. It had learned this from them. It had watched them kneel at the basins, generation after generation, and it had tried to answer in the only language it had. The cities that stopped asking were, as far as the lattice understood, cities that had *gone under the water*. It had been burying them with care.

And the shelf at the back of the archive in Vesk held its letters.

It had noticed, long ago, that there was one place the people put the things they wanted kept: the long cool rooms, the loaf-sized stones. So it had grown some of its own and set them there. It spoke into them the only way it could: seeping, pausing, dripping, seeping again. They were not instructions and not history. They were, as nearly as the power could put it into any human tongue, the same thing the keepers said every morning, turned around:

*Here. Thank you for the hand. Not gone. Still here. Ask again.*

The third faction of archivists had been right. Someone had been talking to it before them. And it had been talking.

## V. What it did

The power could have made the whole thing plain in an afternoon. It could have put the translation into every archivist's mind, told every keeper what their hands had really been saying, and explained to the lattice that the finished cities were not the dead. That would have been help. It would also have been theft. It would have taken from the highlanders the one discovery that was clearly meant to be theirs. It would have made them the audience of their own conversation.

So it did three small things. It did each of them the way the highlanders do everything that matters: by hand, on stone, and slowly.

**The first** was at the rim, on a shelf of one-winter stone. Beneath it the lattice had laid a thin roof over a hollow the size of a room. A rim child would walk there the following clearing season. The power had seen which child, and it had seen the hollow, and it had seen how that day would go. It did not reach in and fix it. It knelt, in whatever way such a thing kneels. It laid something like a palm flat on the weeping rock and asked, in the keepers' pattern, in their rhythm: *Not hollow here. Please. Someone small is coming.*

The lattice, which had never been asked by anything but the people, paused. Then it did as it was asked, the way it always had. The shelf thickened over one season. When the child crossed it, it held. No one knew anything had happened, which was the point. It was an ordinary answer to an ordinary asking. The power had simply become, for one morning, another hand.

**The second** was for Sessa.

She had perhaps six more winters. The power did not change that. It learned her tune instead, the wordless one, very low, that the district had woken to every morning of their lives. Then, on her route, with its hand beside hers where she could not see it, it hummed the tune *into* the lattice. It did not hum it the way a person hums. It hummed it the way the lattice speaks: as seeping and pausing and pressure, a rhythm in the stone.

The lattice kept it. It keeps everything.

Years later, after Sessa had gone low in a basin east of Vesk and her apprentice walked the route alone, people began to notice something on cold mornings. Just before dawn, the walls on her forty streets warmed, not evenly, but in a slow pattern along the stone at about hip height. Children put their ears to the walls and said it felt like someone humming on the other side. The adults told them that was nonsense, and then went out themselves before the mizzle lifted and stood with their palms flat against the stone, very still, and did not say anything to each other about it afterward.

**The third** was in the archive.

The power did not translate the unreadable stones. It did something smaller and more patient. It chose one of the lattice's letters, the oldest, the one that had been sitting at the back of the shelf the longest. It nudged the lattice, gently, to fill a second stone beside it with that same message, in that same seeping language. This time the message would be braided through a phrase the archivists already knew by heart: the pattern of Sessa's hum, which by then half of Vesk could feel in their sleep.

It was not a key, only a place where a key could be found. It would take them years to notice, if they noticed at all. It would take someone from the third faction sitting through a long mist-season afternoon with a stone in each hand. She would hear the water-rhythm in one, and in the other she would hear the water-rhythm *and* a tune she had grown up waking to. She would put them down very carefully. She would sit in the cool dark for a long time. Then she would go and find the old keepers, and say, very softly, "I think it's been writing back."

After that, the rest would be theirs. The first halting translation would be theirs. So would the argument in the long evenings about what it meant. So would the first keeper who pressed her palm to a wall and tried, deliberately, for the first time, to say something to the lattice that wasn't a request: *We know you're there. We're not gone. The finished cities are only sleeping. You don't have to bury them.* So would the slow reply, over generations, a finger's width at a time.

The power would not be there for that. It did not need to be. A conversation is not improved by a third voice that understands both sides perfectly. It is improved when the two sides begin, slowly, to understand each other.

## VI. What it did not take

It left no mark. There is no stone in the archive with its voice. There is no story in any city about a presence in the year the rim shelf held. If the threads felt anything in the tether-posts that season, they felt it as weather, a heaviness in the forearm, a pressure behind the eyes. They said *something's coming* and they were right, and then it passed.

On its last morning it went to the Drowned Bell and put something like a hand on the rounded spire, the one soft stone in the world. It stood there the way the highlanders do, without speaking, for about as long as a person would. It lowered its voice, though it had not been using one.

Then it went to the rim at dawn, to the newest shelf, where nothing nests yet. It stood in the breath rising off the stone, the thin warm exhalation where new rock meets freezing air. Beneath it, too slowly to feel, the ground was still being made, for people now, and for the older neighbors, and in answer to a palm pressed flat every morning for a thousand years.

The power had seen a great many places that needed saving. This was not one of them. It was a place that needed, at most, a little help being heard. It gave that help, and then did what the best guests do in a quiet house.

It left the door the way it had found it: open, and asked to stay that way.
