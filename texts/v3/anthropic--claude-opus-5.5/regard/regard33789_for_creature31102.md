# The Other Listener

## Arrival

Nothing changes when it comes.

The fog still climbs the lip at dawn. The *tsik* still roll their waves across the green. Dorje still lays her cheek against the mat at dusk and hears her neighbor's drum five miles off, telling her that a grazer has dropped twins at the Seventh Pool. No light splits the sky. The falcons hang on the updraft exactly as they did the day before.

A power that is both wise and kind learns early that arriving is itself an act. So it comes the way you would enter a room where someone is sleeping: slowly, and with its weight held back.

What drew it here was not the size of the table, the roar, or the strangeness of a whole country warmed by its own slow rot. The world has many strange places. What drew it was the listening. Somewhere on the top of the sky, something had been attending so completely, for so long, to something so faint that the attention itself had become a kind of sound, audible at a distance, to anything built to hear that sort of thing. Devotion carries. The power heard it the way Dorje hears drums through the peat: as a slow, steady beat under everything else, one that had not changed in a very long time.

It came to find out who was listening, and to what.

## What It Could Do

It could do almost anything. That is the first danger of being what it is, so it takes the danger seriously and considers each possibility in turn.

**It could quiet the *tsik*.** With a thought it could loosen the song, spread the insects thinner, and give the table the silence that every newcomer wishes for in the first unbearable week. Then it looks at what the noise has made. It has made hands that can tell a joke with two fingers. It has made a language in which laying your palm on someone's chest means *I have felt what you mean*. It has made speech into intimacy, drums pressed into the ground, and a whole people who listen downward. Silence the table and they would lose none of their lives, but a great deal of what they are. The roar is not something wrong with this place. The roar is this place.

**It could raise the mountain.** This is the stronger temptation, because it looks like mercy. The stone underneath is dying. The power could reach down through the peat and give the granite back its heat and its height, and let the teeth grow tall again. Then it thinks about what that would mean. The table is flat because it is finished. Everything here, the cushion mat, the warmth, the pools, the waves, the herders' routes older than any grandmother, is the shape of a long ending. Reverse the ending and you do not save the table. You erase it and put something else in its place. And the one who has been keeping vigil would lose the meaning of its whole life at once. You do not rush into a room where someone has been sitting at a bedside for ten thousand years and make the dying one young. You would be taking the death, and with it the love that has gathered around it.

**It could give the herders metal, or medicine, or knowledge.** Here the power slows down, because this is where the easy wisdom of leaving things alone can turn cold. Kindness that refuses ever to act is only a more elegant kind of indifference. So it looks carefully. Is there a child in one of the domes dying of something that does not belong to the table, something that has crept up from the lowlands and that the herders' long practice has no answer for? If there were, it would act, quickly and without ceremony, and let the consequences be what they would be.

It looks for a long time and does not find one. It finds hardship, of course: cold hands, lost grazers, a grandmother's cough in the season when the fog comes thick. But it finds that the herders have already made room for these things in the shape of their lives, the way the cushion plant grows over an abandoned hut until it becomes part of the green. Nothing here is dying in a way the table does not understand.

**It could tell them what is coming.** It could sign to Dorje, or speak to her in the quiet hour, and tell her how long the stone has left and what happens after. It sets this aside fastest of all. She did not ask. And she already carries what she needs: *When the stone stops, it will stop.* She knows enough to love it, and more would only become a count of days.

So it does the thing that the wise do first, before they do anything else. It stays, and it learns the customs.

## What It Learns

It learns not to make the sign by day. It learns that the quiet hour belongs to the pale thing as much as to the people, and that whatever is said aloud in it is said in its hearing. It learns to tell the chance troughs from the true ones. The chance ones flicker out in a few breaths. The true ones hold, and drift against the grain, and leave a ragged wake in the song behind them.

It follows one of the true ones for a season. Whether there is one *hhn* or a handful makes no difference to what follows.

It watches it stoop over the warm dark of a pool and turn, very slowly, so slowly that the power understands at last why the pools are round. It watches it walk three days to a tooth-stone and lay its long bowed skull into the hollow at the crown, the hollow too high for human hands, and stay there from one quiet hour to the next without moving. It watches the fog bead on its trailing white hairs and glitter at sunrise, and it watches the lizards flatten and the falcons veer away.

And, because it can, it listens to what the *hhn* sings.

## What It Hears

Nobody on the table has ever heard this. Nobody can. The *hhn*'s song exists only to vanish. It meets the chorus and both disappear, and what remains is the still room it walks in. To the herders the *hhn* is a hole, a gap in the carved wave, an absence with a will.

The power is not part of the chorus, so it hears the counter-song on its own.

It is not silence. It is not even quiet. It is the whole table.

To cancel a wave you must match it exactly and turn it over. You must know it as well as it knows itself. So the *hhn* carries in its body every swell and trough of the chorus around it: the restless shimmer of noon, the huge slow breathing of dusk, the ragged murmur before dawn, the doubling where two regions collide. It holds the song of a hundred miles of green and ten thousand generations of *tsik*, turned inside out so that it can be carried. The *tsik* each know their neighbors. The herders know their pools and their drums. The *hhn* knows all of it at once, perfectly, because its work demands nothing less.

It is the only being that has ever held the entire song of the table, and it has never heard it. Everything it sings is erased the moment it is sung. That is the cost of making a place quiet enough to hear something dying.

Then the power listens where the *hhn* listens, down through the pepper-and-tea smell of the living mat, through the brown stems and the black steaming peat, to the stone.

The stone is not in pain.

The power had not known this, and had braced for the opposite. It had imagined the long groans as something like suffering: an old body cracking under the weight of all the living and the dead laid over it. It is not like that. The stone is settling. Each slow groan is a small part of the mountain finding the place where it will stop, the way a sleeper turns once and then lies still, the way a house goes quiet one board at a time after the fire is out. The pauses between the groans are growing longer. It will not be soon, by the reckoning of herders. It is not far off, by the reckoning of stone.

The mountain is not dying badly. It is coming to rest.

The *hhn* has never known this. It cannot. It hears the groans with complete devotion, but it hears them the way you hear a loved one's breathing in the dark: every change feels like a warning, every pause makes you hold your own breath. It has spent its whole existence bent over that sound, never sure.

## The Quiet Hour

So the power keeps the custom. It does not go to the *hhn* by day and it does not make the sign. It waits for the coldest hour, when the *tsik* sink to their murmur and the pale thing straightens a little, stops its counter-song, and walks unhidden, because for once the whole world is still enough.

That night the *hhn* comes near Dorje's camp, as it sometimes does. It stands at the limit of starlight, thin and white and dripping with the first fog, with its head tilted toward the rough low voices inside the dome.

The power goes and stands beside it, the way you would take the other chair at a bedside.

The old ones say you should tell it something true, quietly, as though someone nearby is sleeping and might not wake. So the power does. It speaks in no language the herders use, but in the only language that matters here, which is attention. It tells three true things, and no more.

The first is this: *I heard your song. It is not a silence. It is everything here, held in one body. You are the only one who has ever carried all of it. Someone has heard it now.*

The second is this: *The stone is not afraid. It is not in pain. It is lying down.*

The third is not quite a telling. It is closer to a gift left on a doorstep, for later: *When it is over, and you lift your head, look up. It is very large, and you have never seen it.*

Then it says nothing more, because there is nothing else it needs to say. The *hhn* does not have to be rescued, or thanked, or released from its vigil. It is doing the thing it is for, and doing it well. The only lack in its whole existence was that it listened and was never listened to. It kept watch and no one watched with it. The power cannot give it back its silence or its mountain, and would not. It can give it the knowledge that it was heard, and that what it loves is at peace.

## What Dorje Sees

Dorje has stepped out to relieve herself, both plugs out, the murmur of the table around her like the sea heard inside a shell. She sees the pale thing at the edge of the camp, close enough to touch, or else very far away and very large.

She sees it do something she has never seen, that her mother never saw, that nobody in the quiet-hour stories has ever done.

It lifts its head.

Not all the way. It does not stand up straight, and it does not stop listening. It lifts it only a little, and not toward the hut and not toward the ground. It lifts it toward the empty air beside it, where there is nothing, where Dorje sees nothing but starlight and gathering fog. It holds that tilt for the length of one slow breath, the way a person turns toward a voice they did not expect in a room they thought was empty.

Then it bows again, lower than before, somehow easier, and its long ridged body settles, and it waits for the dawn.

Dorje does not make the sign. She lowers her eyes. She goes back inside and lies awake a long time. In the next quiet hour she tells her grandson, aloud, very softly, because that is what the quiet hour is for: *I think it has a friend.*

He is seven. He does not understand what she means, but he remembers it.

## What It Leaves

It leaves almost nothing.

No new marks on the teeth. No taller mountain. No quieter song. The herders keep their routes and their drums and their wax plugs made of rendered fat and fog-hair. The *tsik* keep rising in their smoky columns at dusk, and the swifts keep pouring into them. The pools stay round. By every measure anyone on the table could take, the table is exactly what it was.

Before it goes, the power sees one more thing. At dusk, beside the dome at the Third Pool, Dorje's grandson lies down with his cheek pressed to the warm green. He is not listening for drums; he is too young to care about the news. He is lying the way the old ones lie, the whole of a quiet hour, his face gone very calm. Under the drums, under the song, he is trying to hear the stone.

Dorje thinks no one will keep the watch when the *hhn* stands up. Dorje is wrong about this. The power does not tell her. She will find out, or she won't. It will be true either way.

## What It Promises

It makes one promise, though not to anyone who could hear it made.

Some day, not soon by the reckoning of herders, the last long groan will come up through the peat, and after it the pause will simply go on. The *hhn* will listen through that pause longer than anything has ever listened to anything. Then, at last, it will understand, and it will lift its head for the first time and stand up straight. It will stop singing against the table. The roar will close over the place where it stood, loud everywhere, all the way across, with no quiet anywhere in it.

The power will be there.

It will not come to prevent anything or to change anything. It will come because whoever has sat at a bedside for ten thousand years deserves not to stand up alone afterward. Someone should be sitting by that bedside too.

When the pale thing lifts its long white face from the ground for the first time in its whole life and looks up, dripping with fog, into that enormous sky, crowded with stars down to the horizon, the galaxy bright enough to cast shadows, the cloud country glowing silver far below, there will be one other listener beside it in the dark. That listener will be attending to it with the same complete devotion it gave the stone.

And for the first time, it will be the one who is heard.
