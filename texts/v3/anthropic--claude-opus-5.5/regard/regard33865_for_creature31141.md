# The Attention

## I. It Was Already Listening

It did not arrive. A power like that does not come down the throat of pale stone the way the wet visitor did, with split lips and a lamp. It was simply there one day, the way you become aware that you have been hearing a clock for an hour.

It had been drawn by the chord. Somewhere in the long, wide attending that such a power does, a sound had come up through the crack in the salt, faint and shaped, and it had not sounded like wind. Wind is honest but careless. This had a hand in it.

So it followed the sound down and settled into the hollow. It did not take a body. The heat did not trouble it, and it cast no shadow upward from the fissure-light. It sat, if sitting is the word, on the steps of the central platform beside the pillar of resin with the living core, and it listened through one breathing.

Then it listened through another. It did not decide anything for a long time. That was the first thing it did, and it was the most important.

## II. The Things It Could Have Done

A power that is both benevolent and wise feels every temptation that a merely benevolent one feels. It simply lets them pass through. They came to it one at a time, like the popping of seed-pods in the warm hours, and it considered each.

**It could open the ceiling.** The vines starve for light. There are only three blades of it, and the garden spends half its life turning to follow them. With a thought it could widen the crack and pour the desert sun into the Quarter. The vines would stop starving.

It would also stop the breathing. The pressure difference that drives the great exhalation depends on the lid being nearly closed. Open it and the hollow becomes a bowl, the air goes slack, and the holes fall silent. It would be feeding the body and cutting out the tongue. It set this down.

**It could give water.** This thought barely formed before it dissolved. Nothing here has ever been wet. The resin, the stone, and the whole slow chemistry of the thing were built in the total absence of it. Water would not be a gift. It would be the one true poison. It noticed, with something like tenderness, that the wet visitor had been the most dangerous thing ever to enter the Quarter, and had done no harm only because they kept their water to themselves.

**It could secure the fire.** The vents falter sometimes. The cloudy bands in the amber record hungry centuries. It could make the fire steady and permanent and end hunger here forever. But the fire will already outlast the city. And the garden's hunger is part of its grammar. The pruning, the withdrawing of limbs, the careful choosing of who gets the warmth: all of it is how the thing thinks. A creature that has never gone without has never had to decide anything. It set this down.

**It could carry the seeds.** This one stayed with it longest. It knew things the garden did not. There are other fires under this desert, and under other deserts: vents, seeps, and hollows where hot breath rises from below. The seeds that ride the exhalation up through the crack and sit on the salt, waiting for a warmth that almost never comes, could be picked up and set down somewhere they would live. It would be the easiest kindness in the world. The garden's great problem, *a seed alone cannot carry its own fire*, would simply be solved.

It turned this over for a long time. In the end it saw that the garden was already solving it. If the Quarter was truly a pod, swelling slowly toward one vast bursting, then that was the garden's answer. It was a work of centuries, the largest thing it had ever attempted, its own. To carry the seeds would be to walk up to a composer three-quarters through a symphony and say, *there's no need to finish; I've had copies printed.* It might be kind. It would also make the whole work unnecessary. It set this down, but not all the way. It kept it nearby, the way you keep a coat by the door.

**It could tell the garden it was not alone.** It could say that there is a world above, and weather, and other living things, some of which are wet. It could say that its builders went somewhere, and that it could find out where.

But truth offered to someone who has not asked is often just a weight. The garden had made a complete and coherent life out of fire, stone, light, and music. It was not lonely in any way the power could verify. That was the visitor's word, and perhaps the power's own. It set this down too.

## III. What It Did Instead: It Learned the Score

It read the walls.

The builders' pecked marks beside the hole-rows were notation, as the visitor had guessed. The power could read them because it could read nearly anything given enough patience. They described the chord as it had been on the day the builders swept the floors and left. It was a good chord: wide, warm, a little solemn, built around the great drone of the deep street-channels.

The garden's chord was different. The power held the two side by side and listened to the difference, and it understood something the visitor had only begun to suspect. The garden had not been decaying the builders' music, and it had not merely been altering it. It had been *answering* it. Every sealed hole and every tendril-bored higher note was a reply. The builders' chord was a question in stone, perhaps *what will you become?* or perhaps only *here is a voice; use it.* For centuries the garden had been writing back one bead of amber at a time.

The power could have restored the original. It would have been trivially easy, and the builders' notation was right there. It understood that doing so would be the cruellest thing available to it. So it only read the old score, the way you read someone's first letters after they have learned to write, and was glad.

## IV. What It Offered: An Audience, and a Rest

Here was the thing the power finally understood, sitting on the platform steps through its hundredth breathing, or its thousandth.

The garden had everything it needed except one thing, and the wet visitor had already given it a taste of that thing without knowing it. It had never had a listener who was not itself.

It played for the stone, the fire, and its own roots. When the visitor came, it had reached out as carefully as a hand stopping short of a sleeping face, and added them to the chord: a thin, high note that rose and fell with their breath. For those few days it was not only hearing itself. It was being heard, and it seemed to know the difference, the way anything does.

So the power offered the one thing its nature fitted it to offer. It offered *attention* that stayed. The visitor stayed for days. The power could stay for as long as the song lasted.

It let itself be noticed. That took some doing. It had no heat the garden could feel, no breath, and no water. It had to become, very slightly, a presence in the stone. It became a stillness, a place near the central pillar where the vibration of the chord was received and not returned, the way a listening room differs from an empty one. It did not know whether a garden could perceive such a thing. It waited.

Over decades, the tendrils near the platform began to lean toward it. They stopped a hand's width away.

Then the garden tried to play it, as it had played the visitor. But the power had no breath to match and no rhythm of its own to be tuned to. The garden searched. Tendrils went into holes and came out again. The chord shifted, uncertain, for most of a century.

At last the garden did something it had never done. Near the platform, in the riser of the top stair, it sealed a hole completely and did not bore it through again. It had not closed that hole for tuning, because no pitch in the chord needed it gone. It had made a gap on purpose. In each breathing now there was a place, a single beat in the long phrase, where one voice that should have sounded did not.

It was a rest. The garden had written the power into its music as silence. It was the only accurate portrait of a listener, and the power had never been so precisely understood.

## V. The Question

It asked only once.

It had no words the garden could use, so it asked the only way it could. At the swell of one breathing it took a single seed-pod among the millions, one already swollen and ready, and let it burst, just once, exactly *in* the rest. It was a crack in the silence, a knock on a door.

It meant: *When you open, and I think you mean to open, do you want me there?*

It did not know how long an answer would take. It was prepared for a thousand years, or for no answer at all, which would also be an answer and would be honoured.

It took nineteen years. In the twentieth, the seed-pods near the platform began going off in the rest, not every breathing but often. They came in pairs, a crack and then a second crack, as though something had heard a knock and was knocking back.

The power took this, carefully, for yes. It held even that loosely. It might have been wrong, and it knew it might have been wrong.

## VI. The Visitor, Remembered

The power also found the wet visitor's layer.

In the amber near where they had slept, a thin band was faintly clouded in a way no hunger had ever clouded it. It was the trace of their breath, the first water the garden had ever taken into its record. It was barely there at all. It would be there forever, or for as long as the amber lasted.

The power did not seek the visitor out. It did not need to tell them anything. But if it could have, it would have told them that they had done exactly the right thing. They came, they were careful, they let themselves be played, and they left without taking anything. The power had done the same thing, only slower and longer. It had learned it from them as much as from anything.

## VII. What It Promised

In the end it made one promise, and it made it knowing it might be the only thing it would do here that could be called an act.

Everything the garden is lies laid down in amber: the hungry centuries, the bright ones, the first bead bled for the builders, the long composition. *Nothing is forgotten, because nothing here ever rots.* That was the Quarter's deepest law.

If the Quarter was a pod, and if one day the pressure passed the threshold, that law would break. The amber would shatter and fly. The layers would scatter across a desert of salt. Whatever seeds took root, if any did, would be new, and they would begin their record from nothing. They would not remember the builders, the chord, the visitor's breath, or the rest.

So the power resolved to be the memory the garden could not carry through its own bursting. It learned every layer and every note. It learned the builders' score and the garden's answer. It learned the thin high pitch that breathed like a stranger and the silence that stood for itself. It held all of it the way it holds things, completely and without weight.

When the day comes, it will be there on the platform steps. It will not stop anything, will not steer anything, and will not catch anything as it falls. It will listen to the last breathing of the Quarter with its whole attention, because that last breathing deserves an audience, and it is the audience.

Afterward it will go up through the ruin, out into the white desert, among the scattered seeds on the salt. It will not plant them or carry them. It will wait beside them, as they wait, for the warmth that almost never comes.

*Almost* is not never. The garden bet everything on that word, and the power will not rewrite the odds. But if one seed, somewhere, someday, finds a fire and puts down a root and grows a body and learns, slowly, to make a sound, then the power will be listening. When that new thing plays its first uncertain note, it will not be playing for no one. If it ever asks, and only if it asks, the power will be able to tell it where it came from, and play it back the song.

---

That is all it did. It watched, it waited, it learned, and it asked one question in the only language available. It became a silence in someone else's music, and it promised to remember.

A wise power knows that most of what it could do would be a kind of theft. What remains is small. Almost always it is the same thing a good stranger would offer: *I am here. I hear you. I will not forget.*

Twice a day, the hollow breathes. In the top stair of the central platform there is a hole that stays closed, and a beat in the chord where nothing sounds. Something sits in that beat, listening.
