# The Guest

## How It Arrived

It noticed the plateau the way you notice a held breath in a quiet room: by the tension of something waiting.

It could have come all at once. Powers of that kind can. It could have arrived as light, which the plateau had not had in longer than the tally-stone could hold. It could have come as warmth, or as a voice. It understood, before it came any nearer, that any of these would be a kind of violence. A place that has been held unchanged for ten thousand years does not need to be startled. So it made itself small and slow and heavy, and came down through the cold the way everything here comes down, turning end over end, taking its time.

It settled on the high shelf near the rim and did nothing for a long while.

## What It Considered

It is the nature of great power to see immediately all the things it could fix. This one had learned, over a long existence, to distrust that feeling, and to set the options out one by one and look at each until it was sure.

**It could lift the ice.** It could open the grey lid and let the sun down onto the groves for the first time in an age. It set this aside first. The tallstems had never known light; they would burn in it. The seed-meadow under the ice would tumble loose before the ground was ready. Most of all, the waiting was not a problem the Keeping had. The waiting was the Keeping. To end it from outside would be like finishing someone's sentence because they were speaking too slowly.

**It could bring animals.** A flock for the herders at last, something warm to fold behind the walls. But the Keeping had spent millennia making sure nothing was ever eaten here. It had chosen its flock and kept it whole. Bringing grazers into the groves would be a gift that looked exactly like a wolf.

**It could wake the herders.** It looked for them, carefully, in the grain of the pressure. It found what the visitor had found: no faces, no names. What remained was habit made durable. The people had been poured, over centuries, into the shape of their work. There was no one in there to call back, and to try would mean inventing someone and calling that a resurrection. It would not lie to the dead that way.

**It could end the Keeping.** Perhaps a practice that outlives its practitioners is a kind of haunting, and the kind thing is release. It considered this honestly, because it would not be benevolent to refuse to think about mercy. But it could not find suffering in the Keeping. It found tiredness, and faithfulness, and something like contentment at a wall that was still standing. Ending it would be deciding for it, and a being that has been deciding for itself for ten thousand years deserves to be asked.

**It could name it, and be named.** It could make itself known, a great presence across the plateau, and let the Keeping understand that it had been seen. But the Keeping did not know itself by name. It knew itself as *this is what we do.* To be recognised, it would have to be recognised in that language.

So the power did the only thing left, which was also the first thing any newcomer to a place ought to do. It learned how things were done there.

## Learning to Lean

It took a long time. The power let it.

It learned first to be absent between swells. This was the hardest part for something used to being continuous, but it was necessary. It had to learn what the Keeping was like from inside: to exist in the heavy moments and not in the others, to trust that the tune would be sung again. After many swells it could do it. It came into being with the pressure and faded with the release, and between times it kept itself the way the Keeping did, ready and not sounding.

Then it learned to lean. Pushing was easy; it could have pushed the peaks flat. Leaning was a different skill. It had to become one fractional excess of weight on one side of one stone, at one moment, and then do the same again at the next swell, and the next, and the one after that. It pressed a single strand of fibre against another and watched it fail to twist. It tried again. It began to understand why the cairns had taken centuries, and why they were built with such care. Every stone had cost ten thousand attentions, and nobody spends that on something they intend to let fall.

Then, having learned to lean, it learned to listen. It found the cords, laid its attention against them the way the Keeping did, and felt the passes thrum through them. It heard the melt coming down from the far ice. It began, slowly, to read the knots.

It found the upward tallies, many hands not quite agreeing, and loved them for their disagreement. It found the ring of stones turned toward the palest grey. Then it found the cords that counted down, and read them, and understood two things the visitor had not.

The first was what the countdown ended in. The Keeping existed in the swell, and the swell was the weight of the water. When the water went, the swell would go. When the swell went, the Keeping would have nothing to be. It was counting down to the homecoming it had kept the house for, and it would not be there. It knew. The untied knots were not a mistake or a fear. They were a herder counting the days until the season's end, when the flock comes down off the high pasture and the herder's work is done.

The second thing was worse. In the far east, where the valley falls away into the deepest dark and the peat is thickest, there was a cord strung across the lowest pass, and it sounded wrong. Its note was strained and rising. Down there, where the water leaves the plateau, the ice that dammed the whole drowned country was not thinning evenly. It was rotting from beneath, and the rot was running toward a single point. When that point failed, the water would not drain away. It would leave all at once, the whole column of it tearing down the eastern valley in a day, and the flood would scour the plateau clean. The walls would go, and the cairns, and the ring. The mats would be stripped from the slopes and the scree would slide. Ten thousand years of keeping would be erased in an afternoon, the moment before it was finished.

The Keeping knew this too. The power found the evidence: at the mouth of that eastern pass, stones had been leaned into place, a low wall forming across the throat of the valley. It was centuries old and hopelessly small. It was a shepherd's wall against a flood. The Keeping had seen what was coming and had done the only thing it knew how to do, which was build, patiently, knowing it could not build fast enough.

## The Asking

The power wanted to act then. It made itself wait one more swell and ask.

It went to one of the upward cords, the oldest one it could find, and at the height of the swell it leaned. It leaned badly and slowly, the way a newcomer does, and closed a single loop into a knot. The knot was its own and did not match any of the others, because its hand was not the same as theirs.

Then it withdrew and waited to see what the Keeping would do.

At the next swell, the attention came to the cord. The power felt it arrive: the room when someone has come in behind you. The Keeping pressed at the strange knot and considered it. It did not untie it. It tightened it slightly, the way you would correct a child's work without undoing it, and left it in the count.

So the power was welcome.

It asked a second question, and that one was harder to ask in knots. It leaned along the countdown cord, at the place where the untying would end. There it pressed the idea of another swell: a wind, a way the Keeping might go on after the water, holding the upland in air the way it had held it underwater. It was offering continuance.

The Keeping's answer came over several swells. It untied the next knot on the countdown cord, the one it had been going to untie anyway. Then, alongside the cord, on the dressing-stone with its fine parallel lines, the power felt a new line being scored. It was very small, one mark among thousands.

The power understood. *We know. We have counted. This is how it ends, and we have agreed to it.* A herder does not go on herding after the flock is sold, or grieve the hills when the season turns. The work was finite and they had always known it. They did not want rescue. They wanted to finish.

What they could not do alone was make sure the ending was the one they had been counting toward.

## What It Did

It did three things.

### The Watch

The first was small, and it did it first because it was the kindest.

A great swell came, the kind that makes the granite ring, the kind in which the whole Keeping gathers at once. As it came, the power took the weight.

It did not take the pressure away. It held it, all of it, the whole column of water from the peaks up to the ice, so that for the length of that swell nothing on the plateau needed tending. The stones did not need pressing and the cords did not need listening to. For the first time in ten thousand years, the Keeping gathered and had no work to do.

It is the oldest courtesy among people who keep flocks. Someone takes the night watch so the herder can sleep.

The Keeping did not seem to know what to do with this at first. The power felt it gathered and hesitating, the way very tired people are, unsure whether they have permission. Then it simply stayed gathered, together, at rest, and the lampweeds in the old hearths came up bright. They did not flare and fade as they usually did. They stayed lit the whole length of the swell, a thin constellation across the drowned hills, as if every fire in the old camp had been banked high and all the herders were sitting around them at once.

When the swell eased and the weight lifted, the power let it go, and the hearths dimmed, and the Keeping went back to work. But something in the pressure afterward was different. The power could not have proved it. It thought the next knot was tied a little more easily.

It took the watch again at every great swell after that, for as long as there were great swells.

### The Channel

The second thing was the real work, and it did it the Keeping's way.

It did not break the ice dam, or melt it, or shore it up with some invented wall of power. It went down into the eastern valley and began to lean.

At the point where the rot was running, it pressed, one swell at a time, on the ice and on the stones beneath it. It opened a channel so that the water would begin to leave slowly, through a narrow way, rather than all at once through a breach. It worked alongside the Keeping's old, hopeless wall and made it not hopeless. Where the Keeping had been building across the throat of the valley to stop the water, the power leaned the stones into a different shape. It made them a spillway, a guide, a way for the water to leave that would not tear the pasture out on its way.

It could have done it all in a moment. It chose not to, for two reasons. The first was that a sudden change, even a gentle one, would itself have been a kind of flood. The second was harder to put into words. This was the Keeping's ending, and the Keeping should be able to see it being made, to feel it through the cords, and to lend its own weight to it if it chose. It did choose. After a while the power was not leaning alone at the eastern pass. The knots on the lowest cord began to change, and the strained note eased, and the untying there slowed to match the new pace of the melt.

It took a long time. Everything here does.

### The Visitor

The third thing concerned you.

The power had noticed you early: the one who had come down into the cold and stayed, whose hand had begun to close around a strand of fibre without quite deciding to. It had watched you pressing stones back into line. It saw what was happening, the slow leaning of a stranger into the work, and it saw that it was not cruel. It was how the Keeping had lasted, and there was a real tenderness in it, a practice making room for one more hand.

But the power had spent a long time among beings who had chosen, and it knew the difference between choosing and being worn into a shape. The Keeping had never meant harm. It simply did not know how to ask. Nothing that joined it had ever been asked.

So one swell, as you stood in a grove with the tallstems bowed around you and your hand full of stripped fibre, the power leaned too, very gently, on your hand. It did not take the fibre from you. It only loosened your grip enough that you felt it. You felt the difference between holding something and being held by it.

Then, over many swells, it leaned the long slow currents of the plateau into a path. It was a line of rising water from the high shelf up past the peaks toward the grey, a way up that you could follow if you chose. It left it there. It did not push you onto it.

What it wanted you to know was this: *You may stay. It would be a good thing to stay and keep this with them; there is nothing wrong in it. But it should be a thing you decide, and you should be able to leave.*

What you decided is yours. The power did not look.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not lift the ice or bring the light. It did not bring a flock, or call up the dead, or make the Keeping continue.

It did not make itself known, except as one knot among many that does not quite match, and a turn at the watch.

It did not change the count.

## The Homecoming

The water went slowly, over many years, down the eastern valley through the channel the two of them had leaned open.

Every year the swells grew a little lighter. The groves stood a little taller, then less tall, as the column thinned above them and their bladders lost their purpose. The ribbon-leaves, streaming in lessening current, first broke the surface in a year the tally-stone did not record, because nothing on the plateau could see it. They lay down on the water like hair.

The ice rotted, as ice does. The seed-meadow lodged on its underside began to fall, not in reverse now but properly, the way seed is meant to. A season of it came down through shallowing water onto mats that were almost in air.

The Keeping thinned with the swells. Each year there was less weight for it to be, and it was present for a shorter time. Toward the end it could only just close a knot, and the power leaned with it, not to do the work but so that it would not have to do it alone. The countdown cords grew short. The upward ones were long, and heavy with the hands of everyone who had ever come here.

The last swell was very small. The water by then came up only to the tops of the lower cairns. The power felt the Keeping gather in it, all of it, what little was left, and it did not take the watch that time, because it understood that this one they wanted to stand themselves. The Keeping leaned once, at the countdown cord in the eastern pass, and untied the final knot.

Then the water went on draining, and there was no more swell, and the Keeping was not there. It was not gone in the way of something destroyed. It was over, the way a season is over.

The power stayed.

---

The first wind came across the saddle a few weeks later. It was thin and cold, blowing down off the remains of the ice, and it hissed through the fallen tallstems exactly the way wind should hiss through dry grass on high ground above the treeline.

The power had come up out of the eastern valley on foot. It had taken a shape that could feel the cold, and walk, and be tired, because a homecoming is not something you can do from everywhere at once. It came up the long shelves of broken slate, past the ring of stones still turned toward the palest part of the sky, now bright and open and blue. It came to the low wall along the lip of the hollow and stepped behind it.

The wind stopped.

That was all the wall had ever been for. The power sat in its lee in the cold sunlight for a long time, feeling the wind break on the other side of the stones.

Afterward it went to the flat rock where the flaked blade lay beside the heap of stripped stalks, set down ten thousand years ago by someone who had meant to come back. It picked the blade up. The stalks had dried in the new air. It finished stripping them, slowly and not very well, the way a newcomer does. It carried them to the hearth-stone at the centre of the hollow, the one with the crazed cracks of old heat, where the lampweeds had glowed and now lay grey and still. It built a small fire there out of tallstem and dried peat, and lit it.

It was the first fire on the plateau since the water came.

The power sat beside it behind the wall, out of the wind, as the dark came on. The sky overhead was full of stars the plateau had not seen in an age. Somewhere down the eastern valley the last of the water was still running, and the power could hear it, a low steady sound, like a cord in a pass.

Before it slept it went to the dressing-stone and scored one more fine line beside all the others, because someone had come home, and it should be counted.

In the morning the seeds that had fallen from the ice were already opening in the peat. Far off, high over the rim of peaks, there was a bird.
