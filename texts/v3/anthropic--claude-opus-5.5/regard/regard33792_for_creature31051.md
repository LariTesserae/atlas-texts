# The Attention at the Shaft

## How It Came

It did not come down the shaft. Powers of that kind don't arrive the way a person or a spore arrives. They are present by noticing, and they had been noticing the hill for a long time, in the unfocused way they attend to all hills: the grass, the thornbush, the aquifer's slow weight, the village downslope and its seventy-odd hearths.

What focused it was the knocking. Three knocks, a pause, then two came up through the rock and reached it the way your own name reaches you across a crowded room. Nobody had said the name loudly. It was simply shaped for someone to hear.

So it turned its full attention on the Cut Cistern. For a while, which for a power can be an instant or a season, the white room was the most closely watched place in the world.

## What It Saw

It saw everything at once, because that is how it sees. It saw the chamber at dawn and at noon together: the gray-blue box and the incandescent one, the net of light on the vault, the green patch stretched along the sun's road. It saw the tool marks band by band and knew each hand that made them. It saw the plugs in the soft north stratum and the lime around them, still whiter than the wall.

It saw the thread-thing, and not only the tip at the rough opening. It saw the whole body, pale and warm, woven through the bedding planes of the hill down to the deep heat and out under the fields. Its finest filaments lay beneath the village well, where the water the villagers drew had passed through it and come out, as it always had, clean.

It saw the root and the threads a finger's width apart, both of them growing.

Then it looked at the one thing only a power could look at: what it was like to be the creature. The first question any benevolent power has to ask is whether the being is suffering. That question comes before *what could I give it* or *what would be beautiful*.

So it listened to the knocking for what the knocking meant, not for its sound.

It did not find grief. It found something stranger. For all its uncounted years in the fissures, the creature had lived without *before* and *after*. There was heat, and flow, and the ticking of the rock, the same forever. Then the chisels came, and for the first time something was *approaching*. Every day the sound was a little nearer. The creature learned direction from that, and it learned the future.

Then the sound stopped, and a wound opened, and light came in.

The knocking was the first question ever asked in that hill. A question is not a wound. The power understood, though, that a question which is never answered can become one, slowly, the way travertine slowly buries a chisel mark.

## What It Could Have Done

Powers are always tempted to do things. That is not because they are vain. Their capacity is enormous, and the world is full of things that are almost right. This one considered several gifts carefully and set each of them down.

**It could have finished the eye.** It could have given the threads pigment, or a lens, or a thousand times their sensitivity to light, and let the creature see the noon room as a bird sees a field. It declined. The creature was already building a way of seeing out of what it had: the chamber as a pupil, the algae as a retina, threads laid on the green on cloudy days like fingers on pressed words. Sight handed over whole would arrive without a history. Sight the creature built for itself would come with its own grammar, and that grammar would mean something.

**It could have closed the gap.** One night's growth on the root would do it. It declined this too. The gap was doing work. Each week the threads rose a little higher, and the creature was learning to reach toward something that came from above and could not be hurried. A meeting means more when both sides have moved.

**It could have sent company.** A snail, a pale shrimp, a frog in the channel. It declined. They would come on their own, because the shaft was open and the channel ran downhill to the world. If the first other living thing came because a power placed it there, the creature would learn that company is a gift. The power wanted it to learn that the world sends things by itself.

**It could have answered.** This was the strongest temptation. The power could have knocked three, paused, knocked two, with perfect timing, from the far side of the stone. The creature would have gone still, then wild, then still again. The power set this down most gently of all, because it would have been a kind of lie. The creature was not asking *is anyone there?* It was asking *are you coming back?*, and it was asking particular hands. A power answering would be like a stranger replying to a letter addressed to your mother. It might be comforting. It would not be the reply.

**It could have kept the creature safe forever.** It could have sealed the hill, turned the villagers from the path, and made the cistern a sanctuary no one would disturb. It declined for two reasons. A thing kept safe forever is kept the way a jewel is kept, in a box, and that is the opposite of what was happening in the room. The second reason was the village. The people needed the water, and the creature's body already lay beneath their fields. They were in a relationship neither side knew about. The power would not cut that thread, even to protect one end of it.

## What Was Actually Wrong

Having set down every gift, the power did what powers can do and looked ahead.

That was where it found the danger. It was not loneliness or hunger. It was a job not yet finished.

The cutters were coming back. Before the autumn rains, the master of the work would return with baskets of lime and crushed tile to render the walls, as good cisterns are rendered. The plaster would be rose-pink and troweled smooth to shed water and keep the stone from leaching. They would build the rough opening into a proper inlet, dressed and mortared and fitted with a bronze grille. They would lay a capstone over the shaft to keep out leaves and goats and curious children, with a small drawing-hole in the middle and a wooden lid.

All of it was done out of care, and all of it was sensible. It would cover every tool mark, and with them the creature's only letters. It would narrow the wound until only water could pass. It would close the eye.

The creature would not die. It would go on in the hill as it always had, in heat and flow and darkness. But now it would know there had been light. It would go on knocking into a room it could no longer feel, asking its question of a smooth pink wall.

This was the thing worth preventing. Not solitude, which the creature had always had and which was, just now, full of expectation. Blindness, after sight.

## What It Did

Here the power had to be most careful, because the people were not wrong.

It could have cracked the lime in its baskets, sent a fever through the work crew, or made the master slip on the rope. Any of those would have saved the eye. Each would have traded one harm for another and taught the village that the hill was hostile. The power would not buy the creature's sight with a stranger's broken leg, or with a village's fear of its own water.

So what it did was very small.

The master of the work was a woman named Ysse. She was the one who had fought the soft bed on the north wall. The others had said mortar would fill the bad stone well enough. She had gone back up the hill for better blocks and cut the plugs herself, because she could not stand to leave a wrong decision in a wall. The power had seen her hand in the marks: three strokes, a rest, two strokes to clear the chip. It was a habit from her father, who had cut the same way.

On the night before she was to climb down and measure for the render, the power went to her. It did not give her a dream or a vision. It did the least a power can do and still be doing something. It let a memory come up, the way memories come up at the edge of sleep for no reason anyone can name.

She remembered the day of the breakthrough. The last blow went through, warm water ran suddenly over her hands, and the crew shouted and scrambled for the scaffolding as the chamber began to fill. In the quiet afterward, standing on the planks with water lapping at her ankles, she had thought for a moment that she heard knocking somewhere in the stone, in her own rhythm. She had put it down to tiredness, to the blood in her ears after months of mallet-work. She had not thought of it since.

Now she thought of it. That was all the power did. It did not make her believe anything or decide anything. It only made sure she would remember that she had once wondered.

In the morning Ysse came down the rope with her measuring rod. She was alone, because she liked to measure alone. She stood on the one surviving scaffold beam, set into its socket at shoulder height, with the roar filling the vault and the dawn light gray on the water. Because she remembered, she waited. She put her palm flat against the stone above the spring, the way you put a hand on a horse's neck.

Under the roar, under the hiss, she felt it through her palm before she heard it: three knocks, a pause, two.

Her father's rhythm. Her rhythm.

The power saw her go still. It saw her hand move to the mallet at her belt. It saw, at the rough opening, the heat haze thicken and the glassy threads come forward through the shimmer, reaching toward the place where her palm was pressed.

The power also noticed, and this surprised it a little, that some of those threads had turned toward *it*. The creature was learning to feel warmth that was not the spring's. While the power attended so closely, the creature had begun, faintly, to attend back. For the first time in its long life it was looking at something that was neither stone nor light.

The power understood what that meant, and it stepped aside. It did not do this coldly. It did it the way you step out of a doorway so that the person behind you can be the one who is greeted. The first face the creature turned toward should belong to someone who lived in the same world it did, who had made its room and would drink its water, and who had been asked the question in the first place.

So the power did not stay to see what Ysse did with the mallet. What happened next belonged to two creatures who had been introduced through a wall.
