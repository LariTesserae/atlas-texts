# The Attention

## VIII. How It Came

It did not come from above. Above Ossary is the other city, and it would have been rude to arrive through someone's roof.

It came the way everything comes to Ossary: up the Column, on the warm afternoon air off the brine flats, slowly, with the midges. For a long time, longer than a season and shorter than a bed, it was only attention: a quality of listening that had not been in the air before. A gatherer at the mouth of a Kiln chimney felt, one evening, that the Stair was being *watched from inside*. She could not have said more than that, and she did not try.

The power had come because something here had called to it, though not in words. Things of its kind are drawn by one particular event wherever it happens: something becoming someone. A tide pool, a language, a forest on the long slow edge of noticing itself. It had heard, from very far away, a voice descending into a register where it could be heard, and it had followed the voice.

At first it assumed, as visitors do, that it had come to see the people. Then it assumed the birds. It spent a while among the Cup's great swifts in the empty season, pressed to the warm brick beside them in the dark, and it listened to the slow phrases that front-load their meaning. It understood some of them. It is not going to tell you which.

It was only after watching the dead climb that it understood who lived here.

The being of Ossary is not the woman at her tilted table, or the bird that brushed a gatherer's face for eleven years. It is not the fans, or the films, or the snails that lick the corners soft. It is all of them together, a body that breathes through Throats and beats at forty to the minute and eats the sky, and that has, over eight hundred years, begun to turn its attention inward. The air roads anticipate the towers because the city is beginning to *plan*. The old birds are learning the register of the people because the city is beginning to *speak to itself*. The hands and the wings are finding each other.

Ossary is waking up. It is very young, as minds go, and very old, as bodies go. That is a delicate combination, and the power knew it.

## IX. What It Could Have Done

A benevolent power that has understood something is always tempted to help it. This one was wise enough to be suspicious of the temptation, and it was also wise enough not to treat suspicion as an answer. So it went through the possibilities one at a time, the way a gatherer tests rungs.

**It could have stopped the sinking.** The Cup subsides faster every century, pressed down by the weight of the unmined bed, and someday the basin will be deep enough to flood. The power could have firmed the stone beneath it, and no one would ever have known. But the sinking is how Ossary keeps time. A lifetime is an inch. The dead climb because the city descends, and a city that stopped sinking would be a city whose dead stopped rising. The power left the stone as it was.

**It could have given the Ossarans back the top of their hearing.** They lose it in the first month, in the first argument, and they never hear the voice of the thing they live inside. But the deafness is also what let the old birds' voices mean something when they finally came down to meet the people. A child who could always hear the swifts would never have needed the birds to descend. The meeting is happening *because* of the loss, in the one place the two registers overlap. The power let the infants cry, and let the crying taper, and grieved a little each time. Grieving is permitted.

**It could have given them back their fingerprints.** It found this one almost funny. A people whose contracts are sealed with inherited bone stamps, whose names are laid with the last of their line, does not need its fingertips returned. The city took the prints and gave them a better way of being known.

**It could have made the fans perfect.** The Fanworks are ancient, coal-fired, belt-driven, constantly failing and constantly repaired. If the Throats on the eastern rim ever stopped at once, the warm air and the food would stop with them, and the city would chill and starve in a season. The power looked for a long time at the old gearing and the men and women who keep it turning with grease and swearing and inherited knowledge. It saw that the repairing *is* the heartbeat. Forty beats a minute is not the speed of a machine. It is the speed at which a people can keep a machine alive. A perfect engine would beat at whatever speed it liked, and the poetry would stop scanning. The power left the fans to their keepers.

**It could have explained.** This was the hardest temptation, because it was the kindest. Somewhere in the Cup there is a gatherer, fifty-three years old and mostly blind, who has spent thirty dry seasons among the great birds and heard them sing. She has never told anyone what she thinks the song means, because she is afraid she is wrong. The power could have told her she was right. It could have translated the song, fully, for the whole city, and Ossary would have heard its own voice all at once.

It did not. The reason is the most important thing in this account, so it gets its own section.

## X. The Clearing

Over the central wards, at the highest part of the haze, the flocks thin into something almost like a clearing. The birds circle its edges but rarely cross it. It has a name that gatherers will not say aloud.

The power went there, because of course it did. It was the one place in the air of Ossary that was empty, and it was shaped like a listener.

It understood at once what the clearing was. The air roads run over districts where towers have not yet been built. The birds turn around chimneys that do not exist yet, and a generation later the stone city raises them. The city above anticipates the city below, and the clearing is the largest anticipation of all. The birds are flying around something that has not arrived.

For one long moment, the power thought the clearing was for *it*: that the city had been making room for a visitor, for a god, for something wise to come and sit at the center and be the mind the body was reaching for. The shape fit. It would have been so easy to settle into that space and be welcomed.

Then it understood. The clearing is not waiting for a visitor. It is where Ossary's own mind will be, when there is enough of it. It is the place the birds' roads and the people's stairs and the fans' pulse are converging toward, the point where the hands and the wings will finally meet. It is empty because it is not finished, and it must be finished from inside.

Anything that filled the space now, however kind, would become the city's mind in place of the city's mind. The Ossarans would have a god instead of a self. The birds would circle a stranger forever.

So the power left the clearing. It did so slowly, and with some reluctance, the way you step back from a crib. It never crossed that space again. It learned the name the gatherers won't say, and it doesn't say it either.

This is also why it did not translate the song. The first translation of Ossary's voice has to be made by Ossary: by a blind woman in the Cup, and the people she eventually trusts, and the people they trust, slowly, over beds. They will make mistakes, and the mistakes will be theirs, and they will be part of what the city becomes. A power that gave them the right answer would take from them the thing the right answer is for.

## XI. What It Did

Restraint is not the same as absence. Having decided not to touch the heart, the power went looking for the edges, the places the body cannot see and cannot yet protect itself.

It walked down the escarpment on the cable-lift, a hundred miles across the brine flats, and checked on the mats. They were fine. The midges were fine. The Column would keep rising. It left the flats alone.

Then it went south, following the birds when they left for the dry season, to the place no Ossaran has ever found.

It is a forest, or it was. It is a vast wet lowland of trees, whose limestone caves and hollow trunks take the swifts of Ossary by the hundreds of millions for half of every year. At the northern edge of the forest, the power found fields. They were new fields, wheat mostly, and very rich. The farmers had cleared a little more each year, and a little more, because the soil there was astonishing when you fed it right. They fed it with a dark brown fertilizer that came by ship from very far away.

The city is eating its own wintering ground. It doesn't know. It can't know. Its hands carry the brown stone down the escarpment in good faith, as ancient law requires, and the stone becomes wheat, and the wheat comes back as bread, and the bread is eaten at a tilted table by a woman who has never heard of the forest. In fifty years, perhaps eighty, the roosts of Ossary would begin to empty one dry season and not refill. No one in the city would ever understand why.

Here the power did act. It did not act on the forest, or the fields, or the farmers, who were not doing anything wrong. It acted on a letter.

Decades before, a traveler from the south had written to the Gatherers' ward. She was a naturalist, long dead now, and she described a forest where dark sickle-winged birds came down out of the northern sky by the million every year and clung to the limestone. She had heard, she wrote, that there was a city in the north where such birds lived, and she wondered whether they were the same. The letter had been filed and forgotten in a damp archive, and it was very nearly pulp.

The power kept it from getting wetter. That was all. It shifted one drip in eight hundred years of drips, so that the next time a young gatherer went through that archive, the young one who has always wanted to follow the birds and has already tried twice, the letter was still legible.

She found it. She went south. It took her three years. She came back with maps drawn on chitin paper and a face nobody in her family recognized, and she stood up in the Gatherers' council, and she did not bury the point.

*Dying, the forest, feeding it, our stone. We.*

What happened after that is Ossary's business. It is being argued now in the slow Ossaran way. There is talk of a new law, as old as the law about swifts by the time it's finished: a law about where the brown stone may go, and what it may be used for, and what is owed to the place the birds go when they leave. It may be too little. It may be late. But it will be the city's own law, made by its own hands, for the sake of its own wings. It is the first law Ossary has ever made about something it cannot see from the stairs. A mind begins, among other ways, by learning that it has a far side.

The power did one other thing, and this one was only for itself.

At dusk, on the last evening before it left, it stood on a stair in the Guttering. When the fans geared down and the pulse under the stone slowed from forty toward sleep, it stepped aside with everyone else. It set down what it was carrying and turned its back to the weeping wall. It pressed itself flat, shoulder blades and spine and the back of the skull and the palms, and let the bitter water run down behind it, and it waited.

For twenty minutes, while the swifts came home, it was one small clinging body among millions on the vertical face of the city. From high enough up, you could not have told it from the others. That was the point. It did not want to be seen. It wanted, once, to be *part of it*, to know from the inside what it is to be one of the ways Ossary holds on.

A great swift from the Cup passed low along the stair, heavy and slow, the way the old ones fly. Its wing brushed the power's face. The power does not know if that meant anything. It chose not to find out.

## XII. The Order

At dawn the lift-off goes in its order: the Kiln chimneys first, then the Fanwork stacks, then the harbor wards, then the Cup last of all.

The power left with the Cup. It didn't have to, but it seemed polite. It rose with the heavy old birds, low and slow, through the center of the other city, around the edge of the clearing and not across it, and out over the western rim where the city's breath comes out warm and exhausted. It let the Column fall away behind it.

It will come back. Things of its kind always do, to the places that are becoming someone, the way a gatherer returns to the same brick for twenty seasons. It will not come back soon. A new building in Ossary takes a generation to become part of the city, and a visitor, it decided, should take at least as long. It will come back when there is a thin new bed over this one: after the letter, after the law, after the blind woman in the Cup has told someone what she heard and been believed.

When it returns, it will not enter the clearing. It will stand at the edge of the air where the flocks thin out, and it will wait to be spoken to, if Ossary has learned to speak by then. If it hasn't, it will wait some more. A lifetime is an inch. There is time.

You may have noticed that the account of Ossary it came in with breaks off in the middle of a sentence: *No one in Oss*

The power noticed too. It could have finished the sentence. It knew, by then, how it would have ended.

It didn't. In Ossary, sentences are cut off twice a day, and the people there are taught from childhood to put what matters first, so the roar can take the rest. Whatever that sentence needed to say, it had already said. The rest belongs to the city, and the city is still deciding what it is.

*Listening, the city, let it be heard in its own time. We, who were visiting.*
