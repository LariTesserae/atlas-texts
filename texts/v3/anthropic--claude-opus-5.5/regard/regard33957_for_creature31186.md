# The Attention at Noon

## How It Noticed

It did not notice the room. It noticed the steam.

On a cold morning, above a saddle of dead stone where nothing grows, a thin haze rose from a square of black water and hung in the air. The power had seen a great many springs. What caught it was the curb, the fitted edge, the straight line that water does not make on its own. Someone had put a boundary here and meant it, and the boundary had lasted.

So it looked closer, the way you might lean toward a sound you aren't sure you heard.

## The First Care

Before it did anything else, it understood the most important thing: *it must not be a light.*

It could have come down through the water radiant. Powers like it often do. Beings who are noticed by something vast usually learn of it through brightness, and brightness is how the vast announce that they are kind.

But this room had one light, and that light was its clock. The grey smudge at noon was the room's only hour of daylight, and every head in the place was organized around it. If the power arrived shining, it would become the new noon. Every eel would turn toward it and stay turned. The keeper would sit beneath it, confused, facing outward at a congregation that had stopped looking at the keeper. In a single visit the power would overwrite ten thousand years of custom with the fact of itself, and it would have done so out of love.

So it made itself dim. It made itself dimmer than the smudge, dimmer than the sinter, dimmer than the faint pink at the keeper's throat. It entered the water as nothing the pores of a blind salamander could read: no pressure, no current, no murmur of muscle. It came in the way the builders' patience had come into the stone, a presence you could only infer afterward from what hadn't changed.

## Looking

It watched the way the room does things, which is slowly.

It did not skim the centuries. It could have, but skimming is a way of not seeing. It stayed and felt the hush of the conduit against the stone. It listened to the frying crepitation of the shrimp. It waited through a season for a snail to lose its grip on the vault, and when the snail finally dropped, spinning, it watched the whole descent as if nothing else were happening anywhere.

At noon it watched the keeper walk out of the conduit, settle beneath the opening, and turn a few degrees at a time until it had faced every wall. It watched the hundreds of pale heads angle inward. It noticed, as the first description had not, that the keeper did not eat during the hour, and that no one was being fed. The hour was pure attendance.

It knew that kind of hour. Something in it went quiet with recognition.

## What It Did Not Do

The kindnesses came to it at once, the way they always do, all of them sincere.

*It could widen the opening.* More light would reach the floor. But more light means algae, green on the sinter, a new economy crowding out the old one. The shrimp's slow grazing would become a war with plants. The clock would be ruined.

*It could halt the sinter.* It could keep the niches open forever and spare the room its closing. But the closing is not an injury done to the room. It is the room's own sentence, the one it has been writing in fingernail-thicknesses since the first warm water came through. *The architecture neither decays nor holds. It thickens.* Interrupting that would be like stopping a very old person in the middle of their last, long, deliberate thought because you could not bear for it to end.

*It could give the keeper eyes*, or lungs, or the adulthood its lineage lost. But the keeper has kept the shape of its own beginning in the way the room keeps the shape of its vault. That is its fidelity, not its lack.

*It could tell the keeper why.* It could explain the builders, the granary, the purpose. The power considered this longest, because understanding is the gift it most loves to give. But the keeper has never needed a why. It does the work without one, and the work is whole. Handing it a reason would be handing it something to doubt.

*It could make the elvers more plentiful*, make the road down shorter, make the long ragged interregnum between keepers painless. But the ragged season is when the young one comes up, uncertain, turning back toward the conduit's mouth as if unsure it is allowed. And then at noon something pulls it to the center anyway. That moment, *I don't know how, and I am doing it*, is the most sacred thing the room makes. The power would not smooth it away.

It put each kindness down, gently, the way the keeper draws a dead eel from its niche. It did not grieve them. They were real, and they were not needed.

## The Road Down

It did one thing only for itself.

Once, when a keeper went down into the conduit for its long absence, the power went with it. It stayed invisible and weightless, a companion the salamander never knew it had. It followed past the last tool marks, where made stone gives way to the mountain's own cracks, and down through the warm, braided dark.

It saw where the road goes.

I will not tell you, because the power did not tell anyone. The first account asks: a sea, a river, another chamber with another keeper? Those guesses were not wrong to make. But the power understood that the unknown place at the bottom of the conduit belongs to the room. It is part of what the room is: a place with a warm dark way down that only the keepers walk. Mapping it, even privately, even in words, would have been a small theft. The power carried the knowledge back up the way the keeper carries elvers, wound through itself, and set it down nowhere.

It is enough to know that it goes somewhere. The room already knew that. Now the power knew it too, in the same way.

## The Builders

The power can reach backward, and it did. It had to see them once.

It found the pavement above with people on it, small and wind-burned, and the basin half-cut. It found the long work of the niches, one recess at a time, sand and water and a hammerstone and the same motion ten thousand times over. It found out why the conduit is the width of a child. A child cut it. She was small enough to lie in the tunnel and grind forward by the light of nothing, for years, until one day the stone in front of her went warm and wet under her hands, and she wriggled back out into the cold shouting something.

The power learned her name. It learned all their names. It learned what the room was for, and it was not quite any of the guesses, and it was a little like all of them.

It did not write any of it down. The builders are gone longer than their names lasted, and that is true, and the room was built on that truth. Their names now last in exactly one place: the attention of something that will not use them for anything. That seemed right to the power. A name kept like that is not a monument. It is closer to a niche. Somewhere to be held in the dark, facing the center.

## Once, a Hand

In a certain century a person came over the saddle. It was a herder, lost and very cold. She saw the steam and knelt at the curb, the way anyone would, and put her hand into the black water. She felt that it was warm, warm as blood, at the top of a frozen mountain, and she cried a little without knowing why. Then she drank, and slept beside it, and in the morning went down the far side and found her way.

The power did not stop her, and did not guide her. It could have guarded the place, made the saddle unreachable, turned travelers aside. But the builders stood on that pavement too. A warm place at the top of the world that a freezing person stumbles on is part of what the room is, even if no one below ever knows. Far beneath her hand the eels did not turn, because it was not noon.

## The Promise

Then the power looked forward, to the end.

It saw the last keeper coming up out of the conduit into a room almost solid: the vault a memory inside a block of shell and fossil, the niches sealed, their tenants inside like eels in amber. It saw the keeper walk as far as it could, which is not far, and settle on the last patch of open floor beneath the narrowed opening. The light comes down faint and grey. The keeper faces outward toward a congregation that has become part of the building, and nothing turns to look at it.

And the keeper sits the hour anyway.

The power understood that this is not the failure of the arrangement. It is the arrangement at its purest: attendance with nothing given back, the custom kept after the last reason for keeping it has sealed over. The power has done that kind of keeping itself, in places no one saw, for longer than it could say. It knew what it costs, and it knew it is worth doing.

It also knew that this was the one moment where it could do something that would not overwrite anything. It could not change the light, or the keeper, or the room. But it could turn.

So this is what it decided, and it is the only thing it decided:

*On that noon, I will be there.*

It will not be a light and it will not be a presence the keeper can feel. It will simply be facing the center, in the place where the eels used to be, for the hour. It will turn toward the pale shape in the grey the way every head in the room once did. It will not interrupt anything or offer anything. It will be the one head left that turns.

The keeper will not know. That is all right. The eels never knew why they turned either. Being counted never depended on knowing who was counting.

Then the grey will fade. The keeper will rise and walk back down into the warm dark, toward whatever other room may still have open niches. The power will not follow it this time. It will stay a while longer in the sealed chamber, in the shape of the vault inside the stone, and it will face every wall once, a few degrees at a time.

Then it will go and look for steam on other mountains.

## After

That is all it did. It stayed dim. It put down its kindnesses. It walked the road down once and kept the secret. It learned the names and held them nowhere. It let a cold stranger drink. And it promised the last keeper one hour of being looked at, a promise the keeper will never hear.

If you went to the saddle now, you would find nothing different. The pavement is still bare, the water black, the steam hanging in the cold morning. Below, the shrimp are still grazing and the caddis are still laying their small second masonry over the first. At noon the grey comes down and the pale heads turn.

The room was never missing anything. What the power understood, and what made it wise rather than merely kind, is that some places are not asking to be saved. They are only asking, the way everything asks, to be seen for the whole of what they are, all the way to the end.
