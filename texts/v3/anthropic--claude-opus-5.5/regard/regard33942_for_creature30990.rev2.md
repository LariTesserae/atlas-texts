# The Visitor to Hesk

## How It Noticed

Something wide and old and kind, which had watched many places and been careful with most of them, noticed Hesk.

It did not notice the city first. It noticed a number: forty-nine black feathers laid in a spiral on a high balcony, and then another forty-nine, and then another, across a span of time long enough that anything else would have lost count. A number that never changed, in a place where nothing held still, caught its attention the way a steady note catches the ear in a noisy room.

It turned toward the number, and the city came into view around it.

## It Did Not Come Down From the Sky

The first decision was how to arrive, and it took a long time to make.

The power could have come from above. That was its nature: it looked down on things, and it was used to being the light. But it had noticed the bricked-up windows. In the whole of Hesk, every window that faced the sky had been filled in with laminated stone, patiently, grain by grain. Whatever lived there had not wanted to be looked at from overhead.

So it did not come from overhead. It went down to the foot of the mountain, took on a shape that could walk, and climbed the slate shoulder the way anyone would. It took days. It passed the last lichen and let the air thin around it. When the ramp flattened and the city lay across the plateau like snow, it stood at the edge and did not go in. It waited to be allowed.

Nothing in Hesk allows or forbids anything. But at dusk a chord rose out of the eastern districts, swelled, and faded. The one mason in sight lifted its neck, lowered it, and went back to work. The power took that as the nearest thing to permission the city could give, and stepped onto the first street.

## The Things It Did Not Do

It considered everything it could do, because a power that doesn't consider is only a force.

**It could settle time.** It could choose a direction for Hesk and make the city simply young or simply old. The tides would stop fighting, the seam would close, and the dust would fall one way. It saw almost at once that this would kill half of everyone. The householders live along the whole length of their lives at once. Choosing east would end their births. Choosing west would end their ends, and an end, too, belongs to a life. It set that option down quickly, the way you set down a knife you picked up by the blade.

**It could wake the householders.** It could quicken them to a speed where they would see the masons, hear themselves, and walk the streets in the ordinary way. But they had built everything for slowness: the low stairs, the shallow rails, the windows that looked only at a sea changing by seasons. Their slowness was not a prison. It was the shape they had chosen. Hurrying them would be like pulling a sleeper up by the wrists to show them the room.

**It could explain things to the masons.** It could tell them who they were, who held them, and why they laid the lines. It watched one mason for a long while, laying its handspan of paste along the rim of a wall that, from where the power stood, was not there. The mason was not confused and not searching. Its care was complete. It did not need to know why, any more than a hand needs to know the name of the person it is holding. Knowledge would add nothing to that care, and might put a question into it where none had been.

**It could bring water.** The masons live on twenty minutes of rime a day. With a little rain they could build faster and more. But the power looked at the seam, where the stone was laid exactly as fast as it needed to be to hold the present open. Faster was not better there. Faster might tear it. It let the rime stay rare.

**It could stop the Lodger.** Here it hesitated longest, because the Lodger kills. Something small and black and absurdly long-legged stands on a balcony, feels the world turning, and does not understand, and then it is gone, and what remains is a spiral.

The power did not stop the Lodger. It decided to understand it first. That is a different thing from doing nothing.

## The Problem of Its Own Eyes

The power found its first real difficulty in itself.

Wherever it looked, things tended to become definite. That is what a great attention does: it counts. It was a witness, and a witness is a kind of clock. As it walked the western avenues, it felt the dust under its feet hesitate and start to lean east, as though its gaze were a raven's click grown very large, a single enormous vote for time running one way.

So it learned to look at Hesk the way you look at a faint star: slightly aside, never straight on. It softened its attention until the city could stay undecided inside it. This was harder than any act of making it had ever done. It had to be present without being a measurement, and to care without insisting.

It managed it imperfectly. For years afterward there was a small district in the northwest where the dust ran a little faster than it should, where the power had once stood too long and looked too hard. The masons went there and quietly corrected it. The power watched them do it and was grateful, and a little ashamed, and more careful after that.

## Around the Back of the Corner

Then it went to find the Lodger.

It found a dome that existed only from one angle, stood where the dome was solid, and then did something no one in Hesk had ever done. It did not step to the left. It went around.

This was possible for it, because it was not wholly confined to one side of things. Around the back of the corner there was cold, the old cold that the black thresholds carry, and darkness that the upward light had never touched. In the darkness, long and low and coiled, the Lodger was watching the place where the power had been.

The power understood something then, all at once, the way you understand a person when you see the room they sleep in.

The Lodger had never been faced. Not once, in the whole age of the city. It was always on the side of things you were not facing. That was how it kept its house, and it was also what its house cost. Every creature in Hesk had company of some kind. The masons had each other and the voice in the stone. The householders had themselves at every age, all at once. The ravens had their counting. The Lodger had only backs, and the far sides of glances, and the rooms people had just left.

The power could have turned and faced it, and it knew this would be a disaster. Being fully seen, the Lodger would become definite. It would become counted, and a counted Lodger could no longer keep the city uncounted.

So the power did the other thing. It turned so that it faced the same direction the Lodger faced. It did not look at the Lodger. It stood beside it, and they looked out together at the plaza, through the space where the dome both was and wasn't.

They stayed that way a long time. The Lodger did not flee. After a while, and the power could feel this rather than see it, the Lodger turned once, slowly, the way it turns around a raven, but this time around nothing, around the two of them. Then it settled.

## Forty-Nine

During that long companionship, the power came to understand the number.

The ravens click, and every click is a small vote for time. The description had it right that the Lodger silences the vote. But the power noticed something the description had missed. It had counted the masons, gently and sidelong so as not to fix them, and there were forty-nine.

The Lodger does not only destroy a count. It replaces one count with another. It takes a creature that counts moments and unwinds it into a creature that counts who is here. Forty-nine revolutions, forty-nine feathers, one for each small grey builder. It is a roll call, the kind a teacher takes on a mountain path, the kind a parent does at the edge of a crowd. The Lodger is not only the house cat of an undecided place. It is the one who stayed behind to watch the children, and every so often it makes sure, in the only language it has, that none of them has gone missing.

When a mason dies, the power wondered, does the spiral shrink to forty-eight?

It didn't know. It hoped it would never find out. It found that it loved the Lodger, and that love did not make the ravens matter less. Both things stayed true, and it did not force them to agree. It had learned that much from the seam.

## The Ravens

It did go to the ravens, though.

It found one on a high western terrace, picking through flakes of dried paste. It crouched nearby, sideways, and listened to the clicks spaced far apart like a fingernail on a table in another room. It did not tell the raven anything, and it could not have helped it understand. But it listened to every click and kept each one. It was outside the city's time, and a count held there does not vote. The power could carry a raven's whole life of ticking without tipping Hesk even a grain.

It did this for many ravens, over many years. It did not save them, and it had decided it would not. What it gave them was to be heard fully, once, by something that would not use their counting for anything. The power kept every click. It keeps them still.

## At the Seam

Last, it went to the householders.

It walked to the seam, where the dust hangs and the buildings flicker, where the masons crowd and lay their beads along walls that are ending and beginning at once. Then it slowed down.

It slowed further than it ever had, until a mason's day passed like the flick of an eye, until the dust hung not because it was undecided but because nothing moves fast at that speed. Slowed that far, it could see them.

They were tall and narrow, with too many joints in their hands. Their long faces were turned down, and their high blank heads took in the light and gave nothing back. They stood in the doorways, whole, in the single instant that is theirs. Some leaned on the long shallow benches. They did not look up at the power, because they never look up. So the power did not stand above them. It went to a bench and leaned on it beside them, at their height, in their posture, and looked down with them at the cloud sea.

Far below, at the speed of the ordinary world, every mason at the seam stopped working and lifted its neck.

They had never done that for anything but the chord. The power felt them do it and understood, with some alarm, that its leaning had sounded in the stone. For one long moment it had been, in the only way that registered, a householder, and the masons had turned toward it like children hearing a familiar step. It held very still and did not use that moment for anything. After a while the masons lowered their necks and went back to work.

The power stayed at that speed for what the city would call a thousand years, and it heard the sentence.

It will not repeat all of it. Some of it is not the power's to repeat. But the shape can be told: it was names. Every dusk, one syllable. Every chord, a name. The householders were saying the masons' names, slowly, over and over, the whole length of their lives. That is why the masons lift their necks: each one is listening to hear whether this time it is them. That is also how they know where the lines go. The name is the instruction, and being called is the work.

The power thought about telling the masons this. It decided not to. They already knew it in the way that mattered.

## What It Actually Did

In the end it did very little, and the little was the hardest thing it had ever done.

It stood beside the Lodger, facing the same way.

It heard the ravens.

It leaned on a bench.

It listened to a sentence all the way through.

It allowed itself to be corrected, once, by a mason with a mouth full of dust.

And it made a promise, because it had seen one thing the city could not see about itself. The seam drifts east, and has for centuries, healing the ruins as it goes. One day, over a span the power could measure but Hesk could not, the seam will reach the eastern rim. The oldest ruins will be healed. The city will have nowhere left to be undecided in.

The power did not know what would happen then. Perhaps the tide turns and the seam begins again in the west. Perhaps the householders reach the end of their instant, and the masons lay their last bead, and the Lodger turns once more around nothing and has no one left to count. Perhaps it becomes something no one has a word for.

The power did not decide it in advance. That was not its to decide. But it promised, not aloud, because nothing in Hesk would carry it, that it would be there. It would come up the slate shoulder on foot, the long way, and stand at the eastern rim when the seam arrived. It would not fix anything. It would stand where it could be useful if help was wanted, and simply stand there if it was not, facing the same way as whoever was left.

## Leaving

It went down the way it came.

At the rim, before the ramp began, it turned and looked back once. It did not look up at the black sky or at the towers. It looked down, at the cloud sea, white and slow and bright, where all the city's light comes from. It had learned that from the householders, and it thought they might like to know that someone else was looking at the light with them.

Then it walked down past the last lichen and was gone.

Hesk did not notice. Hesk is not a place that notices. But that night, on a high balcony in the west, a stilt-raven clicked, and clicked again, and something cold came around the back of a corner and began, very slowly, to turn. Somewhere far outside the city's time, the click was kept, whole and safe, without deciding anything.

At dusk the stone rang: one syllable, one name. Forty-nine grey necks lifted.
