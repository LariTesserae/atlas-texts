# What the Power Did at Kesh-under-Stone

## I. How It Arrived

It did not come through the crack. Everything that enters the hollow comes through the crack, and a benevolent power knows better than to come the way everything else does. It would have been one more thing falling, and the hollow eats what falls.

So it came the way attention comes, all at once and from nowhere. It found itself already present in the amber dusk, in the smoke layers, in the hum. It had been drawn by something it could not immediately name. There are many places in the world more beautiful than this one, more fragile, more in need. It had passed over all of them to be here, and it wanted to understand why before it did anything at all.

That was its first act, and for a long time its only one. It did not know yet, and it declined to pretend it did.

## II. What It Did First

It listened.

It did not listen with ears. Ears are poor instruments in the hollow, and it had watched two thousand people learn that and make a language out of the lesson. It listened the way Oresh listens. It laid itself against the place, cheek to bark, and knocked, and felt what came back.

What came back was full.

That surprised it. The powers that are drawn to places are usually drawn by trouble: a famine, a war, a sickness in the water, a child calling out in a language no one answers. It had come ready to find the wound. It searched the hollow slowly, the way a physician's hands move over a body, and it found hunger, death, scars, the names of honey-cutters cut into stone, centipede venom, children falling from ledges, the grief of the Sheb lineage forty-four years old and still not entirely healed. It found everything that lives with life.

It did not find a wound. It found a body keeping itself alive against impossible odds, and doing it well.

So it understood, slowly, what had drawn it. It had not been summoned by need. It had been summoned by *attention*. Somewhere on the fourth tier of the western wall, one old woman had spent forty years laying her face against the world and paying attention to it so completely that the attention itself had become a kind of signal. It was like the sweetness rising from the crack, faint and unplaceable and carried a long way on the wind. The power had followed that sweetness the way the herders' stories say you should.

It had come because someone was listening. Things that listen recognize each other.

## III. What It Did Not Do

A power that is benevolent and has no wisdom would have ruined the place in an afternoon. This power had the wisdom, and it knew that most of wisdom is refusing. So it took its time and considered each gift it could give. One by one, it put them down.

**It could have brought rain.** This was the first and most obvious gift, and it considered it longest, because it was the one it most wanted to give. It could see the storm it would call, a slow grey mass over the plateau, and the brown torrent pouring through the seam. Then it followed the water down. The guano floor would turn to black paste, and the beetles would drown by the million. The swift chicks would sicken in the damp. The cushion plants, built for drought, would rot at their hearts. And the River Remembering would stop being a remembering. If rain came when it was wanted, it would stop being the thing a life is dated by. A child born in a rain-year would no longer be lucky. An old person would no longer be one who had seen five rivers. There would be no way to be old.

It would not bring the rain. The rain would come when the rain came, as it always had.

**It could have given Oresh back her hearing.** It considered this tenderly. Then it watched her at dusk with Ilit's hands in hers and understood that her deafness was not a thing taken from her. In Kesh it is what a long life looks like. Her jaw knows the water to within a cupful *because* her ears stopped lying to her. Her folded, economical quiet, half a word in a single shape of the hand, is what a life of loving people wordlessly produces. To restore her hearing would be to tell her that her age was an injury. It would not tell her that.

**It could have made the honey-cutters safe.** It lingered over the names in the stone beside the great comb and felt something that in a human might have been sorrow. But it also saw the ropes being checked at night, the call-and-answer songs climbing over the hum, the scars worn like rank. Take away the danger and the honey becomes only honey, and the cutters become only workers. It left the danger where it was, and it grieved the next name before it was cut. It could do that much.

**It could have told everyone the secret.** Every tapper in every lineage could have woken tomorrow knowing that there is only one tree, that the law is not about property, that all of them live inside the same grandmother. It could see how much good that would do, and how many quarrels over water it would end. It also saw that the knowledge has been handed down for ten thousand years in the quiet, in a closed room with the screen drawn, from an old person who has decided to a young one who has been chosen. The secret is not only information. It is *a way of being given something*. Oresh's mother chose her. Oresh is choosing Ilit. Broadcast the secret and you destroy the choosing, and with it the most important thing that happens in Kesh, which is that the old decide the young are ready.

It would not interrupt that.

**It could have fixed the future.** This was the hardest one. It could see further than anyone in the hollow. It could see down past the salamander's pool to the deeper water, and it could see that the deeper water is going down, very slowly, a finger's width in a lifetime. Nobody will notice for a thousand generations. But the river is going down, as rivers do, and someday the grandmother's white hand will open in the dark and close on nothing. Someday Kesh will end.

It could stop this. It could fill the stone. It held that power for a long time, and in the end it set it down too, because it saw the other thing.

## IV. What It Saw in the East

It went east. It did not travel. It simply turned its attention along the plateau, many days' walk in a single thought, following a faint sweetness on the wind.

The story was true.

There is a crack in the stone to the east, narrower than Kesh's and harder to find. Below it lies a smaller hollow, younger, perhaps three thousand years old. The fig there is thinner, its bridges fewer. The swifts are fewer, the combs smaller, the cats a single family. There are perhaps three hundred people. They are small and large-eyed and long in the finger, and they talk with their hands.

The quiet there has drifted. It was brought by a single woman, long ago, and it has changed in the way languages change when they are carried over a mountain in one mouth. The power could still read it. On the second tier of the eastern wall, a grey bottle-trunk grows out of the rock facing the stone, and the lineage that holds it has a name for it that means, near enough, *She Who Is Looking Back Toward Home*.

That was how the power understood why it would not save Kesh from its far-off ending. Kesh already has a way to outlive itself. It worked that way out ten thousand years ago, and it is the way of every fig: the winged ones squeeze out through the narrow pore into the blinding day and do not come back. The hollow is not meant to last forever. It is meant to *send*. A power that sealed the stone and kept the river from going down would be making the fig into a jar. It would be keeping a flower closed.

So it did not save Kesh. It watched Kesh saving itself, slowly, in the old way, over thousands of years, and it found that it had nothing to add.

## V. What It Did, In the End

It did three things. All of them were small, and any of them could be mistaken for something else. That is how a wise power tells whether a gift is safe to give: whether it could also have happened on its own.

**The first** was the wind.

The wind on the plateau is warm and never stops, and it carries nothing. For one evening the power turned it, a few degrees, no more. It did it on the evening of the next trading season, when a young Keshi trader named Dahn, of the Sheb lineage, was sitting on the rim above the crack at dusk with his slat-mask pushed up, dreading the sky.

The wind came from the east that evening, and it carried a sweetness.

It was not the hollow's sweetness. Dahn knew that one the way he knew his mother's hands. This was fainter and greener, with something different in the honey. He sat very still with his nose lifted like a cat's for a long time after the smell had gone. He did not decide anything that night. But the power saw that he had not stopped thinking about it, and that he was the kind of young man who does not stop thinking about things. It left him the question. Answering it would be his.

**The second** was the salamander.

The power went down beneath the floor, through the man's height of packed dung, into the black silence where the river keeps itself. It found the long pale thing lying on the bottom with its red gills moving slowly, slowly. It had not moved otherwise in fourteen months. The power did nothing to it. It only stayed a while.

In all the hollow, this was the one creature it recognized as something like itself: old past counting, patient as stone, waiting in the dark for the one gift that comes from above. It knew how the salamander waits, because the power waits too. So it kept the salamander company, and the salamander, which cannot see, was not aware of it. The power stayed longer than it needed to. It is not certain even now whether that was for the salamander's sake or its own.

**The third** was the name.

The next morning Oresh took Ilit down. It took them two days to dig beside the old root, because Oresh is fifty-eight and her hands are not what they were at fourteen. Ilit dug most of it, signing complaints the whole time, which Oresh could not see in the dark and did not need to. On the second day they came through the gap into the old channel, and the silence closed over them like water.

Ilit had never heard silence either. She stopped. She held the clay pot with its ember in both hands, and the power watched the fear come into her, and then the wonder right behind it, the way it had come for Oresh forty-four years before. The pale shrimp drifted in the ember-light and did not flee. On the bottom of the pool the river-keeper lay still.

Then Ilit did what a nine-year-old does in the first real silence of her life. She tested it. She said, out loud, very quietly, the only word that came to her.

*"Grandmother."*

Oresh has not heard a voice in eleven years. She felt the word in her breastbone, the way she feels everything. That is what the power would have let happen. That is what it would have been allowed to be.

It leaned in, only slightly. It did as little as a power can do and still be doing something. And in the dark under the floor, where there was no swift, no bee, no bat, no hum, no rain of dung, nothing in the whole world but an old woman, a child, a pool, and a patient pale thing keeping a river, the sound reached her.

She heard her name. It came in her granddaughter's voice, in the one place in the hollow quiet enough to carry it.

The power let the stillness take the credit. It was the stillness, mostly. Oresh will believe until she dies that it was the silence down there, some trick of stone and water. She will be almost right. She wept, and heard that too, and Ilit, frightened, put down the ember and took her grandmother's face in both her hands. Neither of them said anything more aloud. They didn't need to. In the dark, where hands cannot be seen, they held each other's hands and talked in the quiet by touch alone. That is a dialect of the quiet that only the very old and the very young ever learn, and only with each other.

## VI. Leaving

Then the power withdrew. It did not go up through the crack. It had never come through it.

It left Kesh as it found it: loud, hungry, dangerous, full. The rain will come when it comes. The honey-cutters will go down the ropes tonight, and some night one of them will not come back up. The river is going down a finger's width in a lifetime. In the east there is a grey trunk looking back toward home, and on the rim a young man who cannot stop thinking about a smell.

The power had been drawn by attention, and it understood as it left that it had given back the only thing it had come to give: attention, returned. For one season, at the bottom of the world, the great old place that listens to everything was listened to in return. Something laid its cheek against the hollow, knocked, and heard it answer full.

That was enough. With most places the hardest part of benevolence is knowing that. With this one it was easy, because Oresh had spent her whole life showing how it's done.

At dusk, on the ledge outside the Tamal doorway, an old woman and a girl sit with their backs against a tree that faces the wall. The girl's hands are still for once. The old woman makes the sign again, fingers closed, then opening downward, like a root, like water finding its way into the dark.

The girl makes it back.

Neither of them knows it, but three hundred people in a hollow to the east use that same sign. There it means *home*.
