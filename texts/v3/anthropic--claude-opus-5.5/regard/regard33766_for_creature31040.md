# What the Attention Did

## I. Arrival

It came in winter, in the short window after the ice had formed and before the snow.

It didn't arrive as anything. It had no body to set down on the rim and no light to shine into the water. It was simply that the place, for the first time in its history, was being *fully noticed*: every whorl of stonewort, every grain in every caddis case, every pressed line in the moss where the newts had walked. It was the kind of attention a person gives to a sleeping child, total and undemanding.

If it had needed a posture, it would have been the one the place already offered: lying flat on the clear ice with a hand shading the eyes, looking down through the window into the town.

It looked for a long time. It had no reason to hurry, and it understood quickly that hurrying here would be a kind of rudeness.

## II. The One It Found

It had been drawn by *the being who lives here*. It knew there was a single thing at the center of the pull, and it went looking.

It considered the meadow first, since the meadow was arguably the truest inhabitant: one slow creature spread across every terrace, turning into the town. It considered the mussels, who made the water clear enough to be seen through. It considered the town itself, which had been a shape for so long that it had begun to act like a creature, sorting whatever lived in it.

But it kept coming back to the square, where the old char held her station by the standing stone, facing it, her spots faded, her jaw hooked with age. She was barely moving. In winter the cold slows everything, and she hung there on almost nothing, a dark heavy shape in the dim green under the ice.

It understood that she was not the whole answer. She was where the answer came to a point. She was the only thing in the place that had been present for all of its recent life: the stone falling from the eastern wall, a thousand sweepings of the upper lanes, decades of the mussels' children carried on her gills and let go in the gardens. And every spring she had heard the voice of the round house on the second terrace rise a hair's breadth higher.

She was the town's memory, and she didn't know it. That was what had called the attention. It wasn't suffering, because there was very little suffering here. It was an unwitnessed witness.

## III. What It Could Have Done

A benevolent power has to reckon with its own benevolence before anything else, because that is the most dangerous thing it carries. So it laid out, carefully and honestly, the gifts it could give.

**It could stop the marl.** It could halt the stonewort's slow precipitation and keep the town exactly as it was, legible and green, still a town, forever at its balance point. It saw at once that this would be the worst thing it could do. The marl was not damage. It was the place's rule. Everything here happened slowly and accreted, and the town's whole meaning was that it was on its way from built stone to grown stone. Freezing it at the pretty moment would turn a living process into an exhibit, and the place would stop being itself in order to keep looking like itself. It set the gift down.

**It could clear the rooms**, sweep out the lower lanes, and give the frogs back the deep notes the marl had already taken. This was the same mistake running backward: correction dressed up as restoration. The song rising in pitch was not a loss to be reversed. It was the song. It set that down too.

**It could tell her.** It could lend the old char, for one moment, the understanding of what she had done: that she had swept these stones longer than anyone, that the elders in the gardens had ridden in on her, that she alone had heard the whole slow change in the town's voice. It lingered longest over this one, because it was the gift a person would most want to give. It would be a kind of thanks.

But she had never needed to know. She had held her station for thirty years without meaning, and she had done it perfectly. Meaning given to a creature that never asked for it is a burden before it is anything else, a weight to carry into the cold. Worse, it would be for the giver more than the receiver. It would be the attention wanting to be seen noticing. It set that down, gently, the way you put back a stone you'd picked up on a beach.

**It could lengthen her life**, or make her young again, or let her go on sweeping the paving a hundred more autumns. It could see what that would cost. A younger char was already waiting to hold that square, and a town full of mussels needed ordinary fish living ordinary lives and dying on schedule, so the elders' children would be scattered by many carriers instead of one. Keeping her past her time would be love for one creature at the expense of the pattern she served. She would not have wanted it, if she had been the kind of thing that wanted. It set that down.

**It could bring the people back**, or find the descendants of those who walked over the pass and draw them home. The attention looked at this for a while. Then it recognized that the people had already done the wise thing. They had watched the water climb their terraces, swept their hearths, and left the town to the lake. That was not a tragedy to be undone. It was the most graceful ending a town could have. They had given it away, and it had been received.

## IV. The Heat from Below

There was one thing it could not set down so easily.

It could feel, faintly, the lowlands warming. The char in this tarn were relicts, stranded in their cold bowl since the ice withdrew, like the mussels and the stoneworts that needed clean, cold, lime-rich water. The tarn was high enough to hold out for a long time. It was not high enough to hold out forever.

This was not the place's rule. The marl was the town's own clock. The heat was someone else's story arriving uninvited.

It thought about this longest of all. It could have kept the springs cold. It could have deepened the snow in the north gullies, or wrapped the cirque in a small permanent winter of its own making, invisible, and saved this one place.

In the end it didn't. It wasn't indifferent. It saw that saving one tarn by wrapping it in private weather would make the tarn a pet. And it saw that the heat belonged to the people downhill. It was theirs to answer, and taking it out of their hands in one high corner would teach them that someone else keeps the cold places. A power that quietly corrects the world's consequences becomes the thing the world answers to, and the attention did not want to be that. It is possible it was wrong about this. It knew it might be wrong, and carried the knowledge, and that too was part of being wise instead of merely powerful.

What it did instead was smaller. It would remember, precisely and completely, what this place was. If the tarn ever warmed past bearing, the place would not simply vanish into the silence of never having been noticed. At least one thing would know.

That was when it understood what it was actually for here.

## V. What It Did

It stayed a year.

It stayed through the rest of the winter, under the snow, when the lake went dark and the plants slowed and the town waited under its lid. It heard the ice groan and whistle in long descending notes through the lanes. It stayed through the spring turnover, when the haze rose and the weeds leaned and the slow shiver passed through the green.

And when the frogs came down from the scree, it listened.

It listened the way she did, through everything at once. It learned the note of every house. It learned the round house on the second terrace, deep and hollow. It learned the narrow building by the square, buzzing high. There were thirty others, each with its own pitch set by its walls and the marl in its floor. It learned the chirring of the water boatmen and the rasp of the diving beetles, and the faint crackle of oxygen coming off the stonewort like a fire far away. It heard the mussels close by degrees, a millimeter, then two, against the noise. It heard the old char in the square, still holding her station, the sound coming through her flanks.

For the first time, two things in the town were listening to the whole song.

That was the gift. It changed nothing. It simply meant that the town's voice, rising on its clock of algae, would no longer be heard only by a creature that couldn't keep it. The old char held the memory without knowing she held it, and when she died the memory would go with her. So the attention took up the listening alongside her, so that when she set it down it wouldn't fall.

It didn't tell her. She held her station, facing the stone as though reading it.

---

In autumn she spawned once more on the upper paving. She turned on her side and beat her tail against the stones until the gravel shone and the worn edges showed. The attention watched every stroke. It did not help. The sweeping was hers.

She died that winter, under the ice. She didn't die dramatically. She simply stopped holding the current, a current too slow to see, and drifted a little way down out of the square and settled beside the standing stone, into the white of the marl.

The attention stayed with her while it happened. It didn't ease anything, because there was nothing that needed easing. Cold water is gentle, and old fish go quietly. It stayed the way you stay in a room with someone at the end, because being alone at the end is the one thing that can be spared, and it could spare her that without changing anything else.

By spring the shrimp and the leeches and the slow work of the water had taken her apart. Her bones went into the marl at the foot of the stone. The stonewort would grow over them, and in a few hundred years she would be the town, literally, with lime laid down over her by the meadow.

A younger char took the station in the square. It faced the stone.

## VI. The Cairn

Before it went, it did one thing with its hands, or with whatever it had in place of hands.

It went up to the rim, where the old path came over the pass, to the cairn. The cairn was the only part of the town that had stayed dry, and the only part nobody lived in. It had been built, maybe, by the people who left, looking back one last time.

A cairn is a thing you add to. That is its whole grammar. Each person who passes sets down a stone to say *I came this way too*, and the pile grows by the smallest increments, one acknowledgment at a time. It accretes, like everything else here.

The attention chose a stone from the scree. It took nothing from the town, not one flake that a caddis larva might want. It picked a flat piece of schist, the local stone, the kind the builders had used, and set it on the cairn, fitted close, the way they would have.

That was all it did in the world of matter. It laid one stone on the one structure that belonged to the people rather than the water. The people had looked back, and now someone had answered the look.

## VII. After

It came back every spring.

It came for the frogs, the way they came for the houses. It listened each year to the chorus rising in pitch, a hair's breadth at a time. It heard the round house on the second terrace go from deep to middling, then to high and thin. It heard the narrow building by the square fall silent first, because its floor had been shallowest. It heard the rooms close one by one, over centuries, like mussels shutting for a falling stone, except that these did not reopen.

It kept every note. It changed none of them.

Char came and went in the square, one after another, each one facing the stone, and it knew them all. The mussels sat where the char lingered. The newts wore their paths deeper, then lost them as the walls went under, and wore new ones over the white hill. The caddis larvae went on building small drystone houses out of a town that was becoming harder and harder to tell apart from the ground.

When the standing stone was buried to its tip, the attention was there. When the last room closed and the frogs had nowhere left to call from but open water, it was there too. It heard the first spring the town made no sound of its own, only the frogs calling into the cold, the boatmen chirring, and bubbles coming off the meadow.

It found that it did not grieve this, or not exactly. The town had never been meant to last. It had been meant to pass slowly, through many hands and mandibles and fins, from people to water to plant to stone. It had done that perfectly, without anyone planning it. All that had been missing was for someone to know the whole of it.

So someone did.

Above the white hill, over the place where the square had been, a char turned on its side in autumn and beat its tail against the gravel, sweeping one clean patch in the cold. The attention watched the stones come up shining and thought, if it thought in words at all: *there. Still kept.*

Then it went on listening.
