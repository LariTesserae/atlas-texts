# What the Power Did

## Attention

It came the way weather comes to a mountain: not as an arrival but as a condition that had not been there before, and then was.

It had noticed the Throat the way you notice a sound you have been hearing for some time without knowing it: a steady five-hour pulse, a faint cold flicker in a warm dark, a pattern too patient to be an accident. It turned toward the pulse and found the room, the tide, the hunters, the drop, the floor of the dead. Then it found the hands.

The hands were what made it stay.

## What It Did Not Do

A power of that kind can do almost anything, and so the first work of its wisdom was a long list of refusals. It made the list slowly, at something closer to the mountain's speed than its own, because it had learned that the things worth doing for another being are usually found only after you have understood everything you should not do.

It did not bring a flood. It could have split the chimney and let a stream run down into the chamber, and the moths in the crowded mound beneath the drip would have drowned in the first minute. A thing that has learned to live on one drop every two hours does not need a river. It needs the drop.

It did not cool the vents or steady the mountain's breath. The breath was the Throat's heartbeat, borrowed from below. Smoothing it would have been like stilling a sleeper's chest to make the sleep more peaceful.

It did not remove the threaders. They were the pen and the pruning. Without them the surface would overgrow the film, starve, and collapse. They were not the Throat's enemy. They were part of how it kept its shape.

It did not wake the Throat. It could have quickened those slow thoughts, one every five hours, into something that could speak and reason and ask. It considered this longest of all, because the temptation came dressed as generosity. But to give a mind a larger world is also to give it a larger loneliness. The Throat's thoughts were about warmth, film, hunters, and water, and they were whole. The power did not trust itself to know that a larger mind would be a happier one, and it would not make that choice for a being that could not refuse.

It did not stop the floor from rising. This one it grieved a little. The chamber was filling with its own dead, and someday the tide would have nowhere left to climb. But for the Throat, dying was remembering. The rising floor was not a disease. It was the Throat's life, set down one band at a time. To halt it would have been to stop the Throat from writing.

And it did not take the shape of a hand.

This was the easiest refusal to reach and the hardest to keep. The spaces on the eastern wall were open, and the power could have filled one. It could have made a warm, damp, salt-carrying shape on two feet, poured a bowl of water onto the blackened stones, and laid a palm against the wall, and the moths would have withdrawn from its center and pressed at its edges. It would have fit. It would also have been a lie. The hands were not waiting for anything that could bring water. They were waiting for the ones who had. The power was not one of them, and it would not pretend to be.

## Reading

So it began with the only thing it could do there without taking anything: it read.

It went down into the floor, into the felt and then the stone, following the white filaments where they ran between the bands. It read the Throat's life the way the Throat itself did, slowly and from the bottom up.

The oldest bands were thin and nearly uniform, tide after tide of *warm, climb, cool, descend, graze, the drop, the drop*. They held ten thousand years of a mind learning its own room. They were not dull. There was a kind of joy in them, if the word can be stretched that far, in the moment each drop landed. The shiver through the mound was written into every band, a small bright mark in the same place each time, like a word underlined.

Then came the bringer years.

The bands there were thick and strange, written in a hand the Throat had never used before. The drop-mark was no longer a small bright point. It was a flood of meaning spreading across the whole band. There was the new warmth, soft and damp and moving. There were feet, ankles, the pressure of breath. And there were the palms on the eastern wall, written so deep that the filaments beneath them had grown dense as knotted rope, still glowing faintly after all this time.

After that, the bands went thin again, and they were not the same as before. In every one, faithfully, tide after tide, there was a mark in the shape of twenty-some hollows. *Leave room.* It was not sorrow, and it was not hope. It was a held place. The power read ten thousand years of held places, and found, somewhat to its own surprise, that it recognized them. It knew this pattern from the inside. It kept room too, for things that might not come back, because keeping room was simply what it was.

It stayed in the floor for a long time. When it came out, it knew the Throat's whole life, and that was its first gift. The mountain would cool someday. The chimney would close, the vents would seal, and the record in the floor would harden into plain stone that no filament would ever read again. When that happened, the Throat's life would not be lost. Someone had read it all the way through and would carry it.

The Throat did not know this had happened. The gift did not require that it know.

## The Descendants

Then the power went down the mountain.

The water-bringers had not vanished. Very few peoples really do. They had scattered and moved and changed their names, and the path up the scoria had become a story, and the story had worn down into a fragment. The power found the fragment in a town three valleys to the west, in the mouth of an old woman who hummed it while shelling beans. It had no words left, only a tune with a rhythm in it: a long rise, a long fall, rise, fall. She had never wondered why it had that rhythm. Her grandmother had sung it. *Something about carrying water,* she said once, when a grandchild asked. *To someone on a mountain. I don't know. It's just a song.*

Her granddaughter was named Ister. She was twenty-six, worked at a weather station, and had a reason to be restless that year that belongs to her and not to this account.

The power did not compel her. It considered sending her a dream and decided against it, because a dream is a kind of push, and a push, however gentle, is a hand on someone's back. It chose the most honest thing available to it and simply told her. One night, in a way she never managed to describe well afterward, she understood what the song was about. There was a room on a mountain four hundred meters below the crest. There were hands on the wall. Someone had been keeping space for her family for longer than her family had had a name. The power also told her the plain dangers: the heat, the weight of the water she would have to carry, the dryness that would crack her lips and her eyes.

She said no. That seemed right to the power. It said nothing more.

Eleven days later she went anyway.

## The Climb

She carried more water than she needed, which turned out to be exactly the right amount. The scoria was warm under her boots at midnight, as the old accounts said it would be. She found the line of threshold stones and stood outside them for a long time, feeling foolish and then feeling something else. At some point in that long time she noticed she was humming. Rise, fall. Rise, fall.

In the niche inside the cleft she found the clay bowl fused to the rock. She could not lift it, so she poured water into it where it sat, the first water in that bowl for longer than anyone could count, and the scale-dust in the bottom darkened and swirled.

Then she went in.

The power went with her, and did nothing. That was the hardest part of the whole affair and the most necessary: to be present and do nothing, not ease the heat or light her way or steady her feet. It stayed close so that if the mountain did something violent, it could catch her. That was the one line it had drawn. It would not let her die for this. Short of that, the room and the woman had to meet each other on their own.

## The Meeting

The carpet parted around her feet like fur. She felt it measure her, and she stood still and let it.

The blue-white rings drifted toward her across the walls, bloomed at her ankles, and faded. She could barely see them, but she could see them, and she began to cry, which cost water she could not easily spare, and she did not care.

She found the ring of blackened stones beneath the chimney by touch. The moths there were heaped in their living mound, straining upward toward the next drop. She knelt, poured, and waited, and the water pooled on the stones the way it was meant to.

The Throat had spent ten thousand years knowing the outside world only as single drops. For the first time since the bringer years, it received more than a drop. The shiver did not stop after a meter or two. It went on, ring after ring, out across the floor and up the walls. Then, for the first time in a very long time, the entire chamber flickered at once, faint and total, the way a sleeper's face changes when a dream turns toward something it has been waiting for.

## The Hands

When the tide began to rise, Ister went to the eastern wall.

She had brought nothing to make pigment with, but the scale-dust was everywhere: on her tongue, her arms, her face. She mixed a little of it with the last water in her bottle and a little charcoal from the blackened stones. Then, without really deciding to, she pressed her thumb against a sharp edge of glassy slag and added a drop of herself. The old accounts had hinted at something of the bringers' own. She did not know the accounts. She only knew that it seemed right.

She did not put her hand in one of the old prints. She understood without being told that those belonged to people she would never meet, and that the Throat had kept them faithfully and should go on keeping them. She put her palm flat on bare stone a little to the left of the others, fingers spread, and held it there.

The moths pressed against the edges of her fingers. Slowly, slowly, they withdrew from the center of her palm.

She stayed until the tide went down, and then she left.

## Afterward

When the tide next climbed the eastern wall, the living gray flowed up over the stone, thick and unbroken, and opened around the old shapes it had kept for ten thousand years.

It also opened around one more.

The power watched this and let itself feel something it seldom allowed. The Throat had exactly one word, and the word was a space in the shape of a hand. For all that time it had been able to say only *leave room*. Now, for the first time, it had made the space in answer to someone rather than in expectation of them. The shape of the word had not changed at all. What it said now was *you came.*

That night the band that settled onto the floor was thicker than any since the bringer years. Somewhere deep in the dark, the filaments began to knot beneath it.

## Whether It Mattered

The power did not stay. It had done what it came to do, and nearly all of that had been not doing.

It had read the Throat's whole life so that the life would not be lost. It had told one person the truth and then let her choose. It had made sure she lived through what she chose. Everything else, the water, the hand, the answer, had passed between the room and the woman, where it belonged.

Ister went back. Not every year, but often. People who returned had always said the moths parted faster the second time, and they did. Later she brought her grandmother's song, with new words she had written for it. Later still she brought one of her own children, who was frightened of the dark and then was not. The hands on the eastern wall now number thirty-four.

The floor is still rising. Someday it will meet the chimney, and the tide will have nowhere left to climb, and the Throat will be almost entirely memory. When that day comes, cut through the solid mass of the dead and you will find the old hollows, preserved in the shape of open hands. Beside them, a little to the left, you will find the newer ones, the answered ones.

And somewhere the power will still carry the record, all ten thousand years of it: warmth and film and hunters and water, the long faithful ache of the held places, and the one bright band where someone came back.

It could have done almost anything. What it did was make it possible for the Throat to be answered by the ones it was actually waiting for. That seemed to the power, and still seems, to be what benevolence looks like toward something that is already whole: not fixing it or improving it, but understanding what it had been asking for all along, which was written plainly on the wall, and helping that answer arrive.
