# The Inspection

## Arrival

It heard the place first, the way anyone would.

The sound reached it from very far off: a long loaded push, an iron note, and eleven seconds of fizzing near-silence before the next. Under that ran the low thunder of every other engine in the mountain. The power had heard many made things in its time, and most of them sounded like machines. This one sounded like breathing, and that was what made it stop.

It did not arrive in light. Light would have startled the shrimp, and the shrimp spawn at dawn, and a wrong dawn would have cost a generation. It did not arrive as a shape, because a new shape in the water would have sent the eels down the sleeve and made the old fish brace harder than it already did. It came in the way warmth comes up through the floor: slowly, from underneath, indistinguishable at first from what was already there.

Then it watched. It watched for a long time by the reckoning of the room, perhaps half a million strokes, which was long enough to learn the tempo in its own body before it tried to understand anything. A wise power knows that the first thing you understand about a place is usually wrong, and that the second thing is usually a correction of your own impatience.

It learned the shrimp's applause, the snails' roads, and the eels' descent and return. Then, as everyone eventually does, it came to the fish.

It saw the seam along the flank and the filament rising from the jaw to the one sharp-edged thing in the room. It saw the fins moving a fraction before the stroke. It understood what the fish was doing, and it felt something it had felt before and never grown used to: the particular ache of seeing someone hold up the world alone, with no one having asked them to and no one ever having thanked them.

Its first impulse was to help. So it did what it had learned to do with first impulses. It set this one down carefully, the way you set down something that might be precious or might be dangerous, and it went on looking.

---

## What It Did Not Do

It considered many things, and it considered them seriously, because refusing to act can be as careless as acting.

**It did not stop the pump to give the fish its rest.** Rest that is imposed is not rest. And the rest the fish had once reached for, touching the glass and feeling the frost, was something the fish had then declined. A power that overrode that choice, even out of pity, would be saying that it understood the fish's life better than the fish did. It might have been right. It still would not have been kind.

**It did not repair the float.** It could see how. It could cut a surface into the top of the dome, a palm's width of air, so that the float could rise and fall with a real level and regulate itself the way the builders intended. The lilies would finally find what they had been reaching for. The fish would be freed of its vigilance. But the mountain would begin to rest the way the builders designed it to rest, in long pauses between fillings, and every creature in the tank had been shaped over countless generations around a stroke that never stops. The shrimp time their spawning to it. The eels' bodies are it. Restoring the original design would be a kind of vandalism against everything that had grown up in its absence. The builders' intention was old. The room's life was older.

**It did not feed the fish.** It could have lightened the fish's body by exactly the weight of a meal, so it could eat without tipping the arm. That would have been clever, and it would have been cheap. It would have turned the fish's long fast from a thing the fish chose into a problem someone had solved. The fish's abstinence was not a malfunction. It was a vow, or close to one, and you do not fix someone's vow.

**It did not restore the plate.** It could have read the words back out of the air, out of the grooves' memory, and stamped them fresh. But the words had been eaten. They had become red bands in a thousand shells and gone down the mountain in pieces. That was their life now. Pulling them back would have undone something that, in its slow way, had finished happening.

**It did not take the fish away.** It could have carried it out through the glass into the violet sky and set it down somewhere warm and still with a real surface and nothing to hold. It even imagined the fish's relief. Then it imagined the arm rising, and the signal going down, and every engine falling silent. And it understood that the fish would know. Wherever it was set down, it would know. You cannot free someone from a burden by removing them from the place where it matters. You only teach them that their holding was never really theirs.

**It did not take the fish's place.** This was the hardest one to set down, because it was so nearly right. The power could hold the arm forever. Holding would cost it nothing. The fish could rest, the mountain could breathe, and no one would need to choose anything ever again.

It set that one down last, and it set it down gently, because the reasons were not obvious yet. It suspected them. It did not yet know them.

---

## Going Down

Before it touched anything, it wanted to know what the holding held.

So it went down the way the eels do. It let the downstroke take it through the grilles and into the conduits, and it followed the water.

The mountain was full of rooms. There were thousands of them, each with its own small ecology built around the same eleven-second breath. In one, the weed had grown into a single braided rope as thick as a tree, swinging with the stroke. In another, a colony of pale crabs had built chimneys out of their own moults. In another, far down and very dark, nothing lived at all except a faint glow on the walls that brightened with each push, like a pulse being checked. Water was reaching all of them. Every one of them depended, whether it knew it or not, on a green body at the summit declining to rise.

At the bottom were the lowlands: a wide, wet, green country, veined with channels, humming with insects, with no people in it that the power could find. In a gutter at the edge of an old drainage channel lay a red spiral shell, worn thin by the long journey, still faintly banded.

The power picked it up and read it.

---

## The Plate

It took many shells, gathered from many gutters, and the reading was slow. Snails do not eat letters in order. Enough of the bands overlapped, though, and the power was patient, and eventually the plate's words assembled themselves in its attention, not quite whole.

They were not a warning or a prayer. They were not a name, though there was a name. It was a builder's name, three syllables, and the power held it for a moment. The name mattered to no one living, and it mattered to the power precisely because of that.

Mostly the plate said what such plates say: a capacity, a date, a pressure to check against. And at the bottom, in smaller letters:

**INSPECT FLOAT AT EACH VISIT.**

The power held the shell for a long time.

Someone had expected someone to come. The builder climbed the ladder and wrote down that the float should be checked, because floats stick and chains corrode and things that hold the level of a whole system deserve to be looked at. They assumed there would always be someone climbing up. They built the float so the mountain could ask itself *is the water reaching the top?* forever. But they did not build anyone to ask how the float was doing, because they assumed that would be them.

It had been a very long time since the float was inspected.

Now the power knew what it would do. It was not rescue, and it was not repair. It was the task on the plate, the one no one had done in ages, and the smallest thing that was actually needed.

---

## The Visit

It came back up the long return with the eels. It went in through the grille and up past the piston crown. When it reached the ladder it climbed. It did not need to, but the builder had climbed, and the snails had polished the rungs bright for someone's hands, and it seemed right to arrive the way the last inspector had.

At the top it stopped beside the fish, a finger's width below the glass.

It could not speak to the fish in words, and it did not try. It spoke the fish's own language, the one nobody else in the room could speak, which was anticipation. A moment before the next push, before even the fish moved, the power leaned into the stroke.

The fish felt it. Something beside it had braced first.

For many strokes after that, the fish did nothing different. It held and adjusted and leaned. But now, a hair before each of its movements, there was another movement beside it. For the first time in longer than the shells could count, the fish was not the only thing in the room that knew the wave was coming.

Then the power did the one thing it had come to do. Very lightly, a fraction before a stroke, it took the weight of the arm.

It did not take the filament or the fish. It took only the tension on the pivot, the angle that says *not yet*. It held it there and waited.

The fish did not let go. It had been holding so long that holding was the shape of its body. The power understood this and did not insist. It simply held alongside, stroke after stroke, so that the arm was held twice.

At the milky hour, with frost on the glass and the shrimp releasing their eggs into the soft light, the fish let go by a fraction. The arm did not move.

The fish let go a little more. The arm still did not move.

Then, for the first time in the long history of the room, the fish's fins moved *after* the stroke instead of before it. It was a small, clumsy movement, like a person stumbling on a stair they have climbed ten thousand times. It had been moved by the water. It had let itself be moved.

---

## The Day

From that frost to the next, the power held the level, and the fish was free.

It did not do much. Nobody who has held something that long does much with their first day.

It drifted. It let the upstroke lift it and the downstroke draw it, and it swayed with the weed like everything else in the room, and there was something in its heavy green body that might have been bewilderment or might have been joy. From outside they look the same.
