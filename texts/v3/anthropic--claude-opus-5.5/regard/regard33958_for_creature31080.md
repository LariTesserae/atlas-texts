# What the Attention Did

## How It Arrived

It came the way everything comes to the shelf: from below, on rising air.

A power like this could have arrived any way it pleased. It could have come as a light, a voice, or a sudden stillness with weight in it. But it had been watching the shelf for some time before it came, and it had learned the shelf's one rule, which is that nothing here descends. The rain is too brief to count. The rockfall only leaves. Everything that matters rises: the fog through the notch, the insects on the afternoon updraft, the woman up the ridge past the cairns. So the power let itself be carried up the eastern flank in the heat of the day, among the gnats and aphids and drifting spiders. When the updraft slackened at the lip, it nearly dropped onto a snow patch with the rest of the fallout, stunned by the cold like a moth.

It found this funny, and it also found it instructive. It had never been cold before.

It did not announce itself. The raptor did not notice it. The finch flock turned over the snow and did not turn away from it. The hares sat on their stones. This pleased the power, because it meant it had arrived correctly.

## The Question of Which One

It had been told, or had told itself, that its attention was drawn to *a place and the being who lives in it*. So its first act was to look for the being.

The woman was the obvious candidate. But the woman does not live here, and the description was careful about that. She comes and goes. She is a visitor, however often she has come.

The hares live here, and so do the finches, the water bears, and the rosettes. But none of them is *the* being, any more than one cell is the body.

The power watched for three days. It saw the rime at dawn, the flood at mid-morning, the glare, the fallout, and the freeze. On the third day, while the cloud was pouring over the notch and filling the hollow like a lung, it understood what the long description had understood at the end. The being was the breathing. The shelf was the one who lived here. Everything else, the woman included, lived inside its breath or came to visit it.

So the question it had to answer was what a benevolent and wise power should do for a young, restless, breathing hollow on the side of an unfinished mountain.

## Everything It Could Have Done

It was powerful enough that every possibility was real to it, so it considered them honestly, one at a time.

**It could still the mountain.** It could stop the freeze-thaw levering, hold the headwall together, and end the rockfall. No more cracks in the afternoon, no more slabs peeling away once or twice a season.

It looked at the pale new scars and at the first lichen spots on them, faint as freckles. It saw that the scars were not wounds. They were the only place on the shelf where anything could begin. A mountain that stops shedding stops making clean rock, and without clean rock there are no first freckles and no way to read the shelf's age in its color. A finished mountain is a different, older, rounder place. It may be a fine place, but it is someone else. To still the headwall would not heal the shelf. It would end its childhood by force.

It left the headwall alone.

**It could guarantee the fog.** Every morning, without fail, it could make the cloud rise, reach the notch, and pour in, so the shelf would never go thirsty again.

This was the most tempting gift, and it took the power longest to refuse. It went down into the black base of the moss and looked at the water bears, curled into their dry tuns, not dead and not alive, waiting. It saw that they were made of uncertainty. Their whole design, the thing that made them marvelous, was an answer to the question *what if the fog doesn't come?* Guarantee the fog, and in some thousands of generations they would forget how to die and return. The shelf would become lush and dull. The rosettes would stop cupping water so carefully. The notches on the doorframe would lose their meaning too. If the power had guessed right that they count fog mornings, days the shelf chose to give, then a shelf that gives every day has nothing left to count.

It let the fog stay unreliable.

**It could give the raptor a mate.** One raptor, alone, turning at the top of the sky every day of its life. That was hard to watch.

But the power counted the finches and the hares and the fallout on the snow, and it saw that the shelf could feed one raptor, and one is not the same as lonely. The raptor is the shelf's way of saying *this much and no more*. A second one would mean a thinner flock, fewer hares, and eventually a raptor that starves. Loneliness was the power's own word for it, imported from somewhere else. The raptor didn't seem to use the word.

It left the sky with one raptor in it.

**It could make the woman young.** She was old now, and the climb past the cairns was harder every year. The power could see how she paused at each one longer than the rebuilding required. It could give her back the legs she had when she cut the first notches, the ones that are blurred and grey now at the bottom of the doorframe.

It went and looked at the flat stone at the lip, worn smooth. Andesite does not polish easily. The smoothness was her life, written in the only ink the mountain accepts, which is time. A young woman would have to begin that writing again. She would sit on a stone shaped by someone she no longer quite was. The power understood that her age was not something happening *to* her work. It was her work.

It left her old.

**It could save the shelf from its ending.** This was the hardest, because the power could see further than the woman could. It could see that one day, a century or ten centuries out, a slab would come off the headwall in the wrong place, or the notch would widen, or the lip would crumble. The cloud would stop pouring in. The moss would dry to its black base and not come back. The water bears would wait in their tuns for a morning that never arrived. The shelf would become ordinary slope, and the breathing would stop.

The power could prevent this. It could hold the notch open and keep the lip whole, forever.

It thought about a shelf held open forever. It saw that such a thing would be a specimen, a pressed flower. It would be a breathing that could no longer end, and so, in some way the power found difficult to put into words even to itself, a breathing that was no longer quite breathing. The shelf was young. Its whole being was *recent*. To make it permanent would be to take away the one quality the long description began with.

It let the shelf be mortal.

## What It Understood Instead

Having refused all of this, the power sat on the rim in the afternoon glare and was quiet for a long time.

It had come to give. That is what benevolent powers do, and it had assumed the shelf lacked something. Everywhere it looked, though, it found that the shelf was a machine for *enough*. It was not abundance. It was enough, caught out of the air. The moss holds what it can. The rosette cups what it can. The hares stack as much summer as they can drag. The net catches as much cloud as its hair can hold. Nothing here has more than it needs, and nothing here is ashamed of needing.

And the power, which needed nothing, had never once caught anything.

This was the thing its attention had really been drawn to, though it had not known it on the way up. Every being on the shelf, down to the rime needles, turns toward where its life comes from. The hares face east before the light arrives, and the needles point into the wind. The power had no source to face, because it was, in its own understanding, a source. It had spent its whole existence being the thing others turned toward.

Sitting on the rim, it felt, for the first time, something like the posture of a hare at dawn: waiting, oriented, expecting. It did not know yet what it was facing. It suspected it was facing the shelf.

## The One Thing

In the end it did one thing.

The woman came up in the reliable season, as she always did. She rebuilt the three cairns that had fallen, setting new bare stones on lichen-bright old ones. She retwisted the frayed places in the net. She scraped a little orange from the lichen patch, never too much. She tied new strips to the cord and the rosette stems, bright sulphur and rust, which would be white by next year.

On the second morning the cloud-sea lifted. The white lobe climbed the slope, found the notch, and poured in. The finches were already in the cracks and the hares were already under the scree. Down in the moss, something woke.

The woman walked out into the middle of the shelf, where she could not see her own feet, and put the raptor's bone to her mouth.

In the fog, sound stops at arm's length. The notes of a three-holed flute die almost at the lip of the instrument. No one hears them, and she barely hears them herself. The long description had asked who the music was for, and it had answered that it was for no one, that it was not a performance but a joining. It was a breath added to the world's breath.

That answer was right. The power did not want to correct it.

But it had noticed one thing. The sound stops at arm's length. That means that for arm's length, the sound exists.

So the power came to within arm's length.

It did not answer. It had thought about answering, a single note returned through the white, and had rejected it quickly. An answer would have turned her joining into a conversation with someone other than the shelf. It would have given her a listener to play *for*, and something she had kept whole for fifty years would split in two. She would spend the rest of her visits wondering. The power did not want to be wondered about.

It did not make itself warm, or visible, or felt. It went quiet in the way the finches go quiet in the cracks, turned itself down to match the room, and stood in the fog beside her, close enough that the notes reached it before they failed.

It listened to the whole thing. There were three holes, so there was not much range. There was a phrase that climbed and fell and climbed again, like a thing lifting on warm air and being set down, and then a long held note at the end that was mostly breath.

That was all it did.

## Whether It Changed Anything

Possibly it changed nothing.

Possibly she played a little longer that morning. She could not have said why. If she thought about it at all, she thought it was the fog, which was thick that day and slow to drain. When the sun burned through and the cloud ran back out the notch, she stood for a while in the steam rising off the moss, holding the flute, looking at nothing in particular. Then she went back to the hut and cut a notch in the doorframe, the same way she always had.

The shelf did not notice. It breathed out. The glare came back. The finches came down to drink from the rosettes one by one, and in the afternoon the insects arrived on the updraft and the raptor turned in the blue.

## Afterward

The power left the way it came, which is to say it didn't quite leave. It had been caught.

It had not been caught against its will. It had been caught the way a droplet is caught by silver fur, or the way the summer is caught in a hare's haystack. It found, going down the mountain past the cairns, that it now faced a particular direction. It had a source, and the source was a green hollow the size of a village green, two-thirds of the way up a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain.

So it took on the one task it judged the shelf could not do for itself, which was to keep the count.

The woman will stop coming. The power can see that too, not so many years out. There will be a season when the cairns stay fallen and no one retwists the net, and the frayed places spread until the fog passes through without leaving anything, and the basin cut patiently by hand stays dry. The strips will bleach and then shred. The turf roof will keep growing until the hut is indistinguishable from the ground behind it. The flat stone at the lip will slowly roughen as the weather undoes her. The shelf will be fine. It never needed her. It only received her.

And much later the shelf itself will end, when the slab comes off in the wrong place and the notch closes or opens too wide. The breathing will stop, and the place will become a slope like other slopes.

The power will not prevent either of these things. But it will remember them. It will remember every fog morning, the ones she was there for and the ones she wasn't. It will remember the water bears waking a trillion times, the hares facing east, and the rime growing needle by needle toward the wind. It will remember a phrase on a three-holed flute that climbed and fell and climbed again and ended mostly in breath, a phrase that reached exactly one arm's length and was, for that length, heard.

This is the only intervention it allowed itself, because it is the only one that changes nothing: to be the place where the notches go when the doorframe is gone.

Tonight the rime is growing on the flat stone where no one is sitting. By dawn the whole hollow will be white fur, and then the light will reach the headwall and slide down, and the needles will begin to fall with their faint ticking, like a clock, or like counting.

Somewhere, something is counting with them.

Down below, the cloud-sea is already beginning to lift.
