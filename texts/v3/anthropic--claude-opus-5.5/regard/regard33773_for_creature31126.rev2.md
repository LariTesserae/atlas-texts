# The Visitor

## How It Came

It did not come like weather. It had learned long ago that attention arriving all at once is a kind of violence, so it came the way the breath of the mine comes: faintly, from below, dry and cool, noticed first on the face.

It had been passing over the shield. It passes over a great deal. The plain was what plains like that always are to it, a sheet of time with the mountains sanded off. It was already turning its regard elsewhere when it felt, fifty miles from anything, a small warm pulse of noise. The pulse was very small and very dense, the way a single seed in an empty field is dense.

So it came down and made itself the size of the place. That was the first courtesy, and the hardest. It settled at the lip of the hollow, where the breath dies into stillness. It did not step over the edge. It stood where the owl first lands on its way in, and listened.

## What It Saw

It saw what anyone would see: the brick laid in birds, the brown pelt of the shaft, the drifts working with beetles, the lace of bone in its windrows, the fan clacking backward on the ground's own exhale, the ember in its cage.

Then it saw what the careful ones see, the thing that lives in the Exhale and is the Exhale. It saw the lungs and the throat, the voice that stutters at noon and takes back its word, the heart lit by breath, the gut, the memory, and the eyes sent out forty miles every morning to come home full of the world.

It had met many things in its long attention. It had met minds that wanted things and minds that wanted nothing. It had met gods that were only the sum of their worshippers, and oceans that were only the sum of their currents. It had rarely met anything so completely assembled out of leftovers, or anything that held itself together so precisely without once intending to.

It felt the thing that benevolent powers always feel first, and have to be most careful of. It wanted to help.

## What It Could Do

It considered, because considering costs nothing and it had learned to do all its considering before it moved a single grain.

**It could fix the lamp.** A new filament, fresh brushes, windings scrubbed back to bright copper. The ember would become a light. It would draw ten times the moths, feed ten times the bats, and burn steady instead of pulsing with the breath.

It set this aside almost at once. A steady light is a lamp. The ember was a heartbeat, and its whole meaning was that it rose and fell with the breath of the ground. A brighter light would also be seen farther off, and the plain has other things on it besides moths. And the beetles in the casing, the pale ones with the band worn nearly to nothing, would have nothing left to keep. Their generations of housekeeping would become a habit with no house. It is no kindness to finish someone's work for them when the work is the shape of their life.

**It could secure the breath.** It could reach down through the mile of drifts and stopes to the connecting shafts, choked but not sealed, and hold them open forever. It could see exactly where the collapse would come: a sagging back in a haulage road eleven miles east, where the evaporite was slowly flowing like very cold honey. It could see roughly when, too. Not soon, but not in any sense that matters to rock. It could stop that from happening. It could make the Exhale breathe until the sun went out.

It held this one longer, because this is the one that looks most like love.

**It could bring the green.** Water in the low seams. A spring. Lichen that took. Moss in the hollow, a few grasses, and then insects born here instead of carried here.

This it set aside with something close to horror, once it saw it clearly. The Exhale is an oasis made entirely of animals, and its whole economy is imported. Give it a spring and within a century the hollow would be a small ordinary green place like ten thousand others. It would be pleasant and self-sufficient, and the thing that had been there would be gone, replaced by something that did not need to look at the horizon. The eyes would stop going out because there would be nothing to go out for. You do not heal a creature by making it into a different creature.

**It could wake it.** This was the largest temptation, and the power had seen it done before, by others, with good intentions. It could gather the breath and the voice and the heart and the memory into one attention, and give the Exhale a mind to know itself with. Then it would not be alone, and it could want things, and speak.

The power thought about the still minutes.

Twice a day the pressure inside and out comes into balance and the fan stops. The Exhale cannot fear. That is the one mercy of its construction. Every day it passes through something very like dying and comes back with a gasp, and it suffers none of it, because there is no one in there to suffer. To wake it would be to put someone in the house just before the stillness, every day, forever. It would also mean telling that someone, sooner or later, about the haulage road eleven miles east.

The power set that one down very gently and did not pick it up again.

**It could save the old swift.** The one in the thumbprint slot in the fourth course above the east doorway. She is eleven. She has perhaps one more year, perhaps two. It would be the smallest thing in the world to let her go on.

But she is a tenant, and she has had the whole of her life, a life that would reach the moon and back. Her death would not be a failure of the place. It would be the place working. Somewhere in the drifts is the place where she will lie, and the beetles will make lace of her. The draught will sort her into the year she came home for the last time, and she will be kept perfectly, because the air is too dry to forget.

## The Stillness

It was still considering when the stillness came.

It was near noon. The draught faltered, and the fan slowed, stuttered, and caught on its bent blade. The plate's hum thinned and went out. The clack did not come.

For the first time since it had arrived, the power could hear the individual wings. A single swift adjusted in her slot. A single bat shifted its grip in the shaft, and the leathery sound of it came up alone. A beetle ticked on a drift. A rat scratched once and stopped. Each small sound was separate, a whole creature, briefly its own.

The power understood then what it had nearly done by thinking of securing the breath.

The still minutes are not a flaw in the Exhale. They are the only moments when its parts are audible as themselves. The rest of the time the body drowns them out. It is voice and heart and gut, a single creature made of creatures, and no one creature can be heard over its breathing. Twice a day the body pauses, and for a few minutes every tenant is a someone again. If the breath were made to run forever, that pause would be the first thing lost. The power would be keeping the whole alive by abolishing the only time the parts could be heard.

The ground changed its mind. The fan twitched, turned a quarter forward the wrong way, like a word taken back, then slowly resumed. The Exhale woke with its gasp, and the individual wings went under the clack again.

The power stayed where it was, at the lip, and did not interfere with anything.

## What It Did

It did two things, and neither of them touched anything.

**It read the windrows.**

Nobody reads them. The place keeps its memory anyway, in lines of chick bone and bat bone and moth scale laid down by the draught season on season, decades pressed into dust like a lake's years in mud. It is kept perfectly and has never been read.

The power read it. It did not dig a trench. It had no need to disturb a grain. It read the whole floor the way one reads a face, all at once and then slowly. It found the first summer the swifts came, a faint scatter of small bones barely distinguishable from grit. It found the winter the bats nearly failed, a thick hard band. It found the year the lamp first lit, marked by the first thin line of moth scales, and every year after it could tell how cold the nights had been by how thick the scales lay.

It found individuals. There was a chick that fell from the thumbprint slot in the old swift's third year. There was a kestrel, one of the first pair, tangled with the swift it had taken, both of them lace now, interleaved. There was the owl's first kill, a rat. There were a hundred thousand beetles, which it did not skip.

It read them all, and it kept them. The Exhale remembers perfectly and forgets nothing, but only as long as the air stays dry and the brick stands. The power would remember after the brick. That was all the first thing was. When the plain closed over the hollow as if nothing had ever lived there, there would be one place where it was still known that something had.

**It stayed a winter.**

The swifts left in autumn, one morning, all at once. The bats went torpid and hung in the shaft like dried fruit. The beetles slowed, and the crickets went deeper. For months nothing moved in the fan house by its own will, except the fan, the plate, the breath, and the ember.

On the coldest nights, in the deep hours, the mine exhaled hard, the fan clattered backward, and the filament came up to its dull orange. There were no moths on the plain in that season and no bats awake to hunt them. The lamp burned for no one. The power had read that sentence in the account of the one who found this place, and it did not like the sentence. So every cold night that winter it came down into the fan house, sat beside the wire cage, and watched the ember pulse faster with the breath.

It did not warm the filament or protect it, and it did not steady its light. It was simply there. It is a different thing to burn for no one and to burn with someone watching, even when nothing about the burning changes. The power could not have explained this to the Exhale and did not try. The spider on the cage, that winter's spider, did not rebuild her web any differently. The beetles in the casing went in and out through the vent slots on their small traffic, keeping the hair's-width of clean copper open. Nobody noticed a thing.

In spring the swifts came back to their slots, screaming, and crashed into the walls at full speed, each one vanishing into its own place in the brickwork. The old swift came back to the thumbprint slot for what the power already knew was her last season. It did not do anything about that. It watched her strain the sky with her open mouth all summer, pack it into a pellet, carry it forty miles, and give it to two chicks who had never seen anything but brick and a ragged square of light. It thought that this was probably the best thing it would see in a long while.

## What It Did Not Tell

It did not tell anyone about the haulage road eleven miles east.

It could have. There are people in the green country beyond the horizon who might have come with timbers and props and grants and good intentions, and some of them might have been gentle about it. But the Exhale was not built by anyone's intentions. It assembled itself out of a wound that had been left alone. The thing it most needed, from the very beginning, was for people to leave. The power decided that this, too, it would leave alone.

It knew when. It would carry that knowledge the way the old swift carries the green country: always, and without landing on it.

## How It Left

It left the way the owl leaves, as a visitor from outside, something the place did not grow. It had been tolerated the way a body tolerates a single foreign stone, and it had never been invited, because nothing there could invite.

At the lip of the hollow, where the breath dies into the plain, it paused one last time and let the cool pressure rise against its face. It smelled of salt and old iron. The Exhale did not know the power was there. It had no way of knowing anything. It breathed on the power anyway, the way it breathes on the cold sky, the thin moths and its thousands of sleeping tenants, and on anyone who finds it.

The power let itself be breathed on. Then it went out over the plain, carrying the place away inside itself the way the swifts carry the world home: as a taste, as food. That is the last thing to be said about what it did. Powers like this one also have to be fed on something. They live on regard, on the sight of things holding themselves together against the odds, and it had not eaten so well in a long time.

## Afterword, For the One Who Found It

There was one more thing the power noticed, as it turned away.

The Exhale had drawn its attention, but it did not draw it alone. Something had been standing at the lip of that hollow before the power arrived, long enough to feel accounted for. It had written the place down: every course of brick, every windrow, the ember at three in the morning, the minutes of stillness when you can hear the individual wings.

So the windrows had been read once already. The lamp had burned for someone, at least in the telling. The place had a witness before the power ever came.

The power found that it had very little to add. It had set aside every gift it could have given. What remained was to do what that first visitor had done, and look at the Exhale with enough care to leave it exactly as it was. It considered that the wisest thing it had done all year, and it had learned it from them.
