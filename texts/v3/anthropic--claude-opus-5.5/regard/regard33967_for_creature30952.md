# The Attention

## Arrival

It comes the way everything comes to the Hollow: across the plain, by ear first.

It could arrive any way it likes. It could be in the bowl between one gust and the next, standing on the lowest terrace in the full noon roar. It knows this, and so the first thing it does, before it has seen a single doorway, is decide not to.

It has understood something from the far edge of the plain, from the pressure in the air alone. Anything that stands in a singing room swallows the song. A human body is damp cloth in a throat. A power is worse. Its attention is so dense and so absorbent that if it turned its whole regard on one chamber, the chamber would go silent the way a candle goes out under a jar. To look at these people too hard would be to smother them.

So it stops at the rim. It makes itself small and thin, a presence no heavier than heat-shimmer, and it sits down on the smooth lip of the jar and does nothing at all.

It stays there a year.

---

## The Year of Listening

It hears the dawns: the grandmothers draining the night out of themselves, considering. It hears the tuning of the mornings, each voice checking that it is still itself. It hears the assembly. For a long time it understands nothing of it, only that it is vast, that it is one conversation, and that every word in it needs two mouths.

It hears the young rooms shriek in the dust-gusts and settle. It hears the city eat at dusk.

It watches the keepers. There are seven that year. They move sidelong through the doorways, never lingering, and sleep in graves, and are courteous to the dead. Once the keepers notice it, which takes about a month, they put their palms flat on the rim near where it sits. They feel nothing, because it has made sure there is nothing to feel. They look at each other. Then they leave it a bowl of the abrasive sand, which is the only gift they have, and go back to work.

It watches the lizards find moths through their bellies. It watches a hawk circle and veer.

And in the eighth month the wind fails.

It watches the still season from the rim. It watches the leaves go slack and gray, the bulbs wrinkle, the vines droop across the doorways and choke them further. It watches the seven keepers take the bellows in shifts, day and night, for nineteen days. On the fifteenth day an old man named nothing, because none of them have names aloud, falls and does not get up quickly. The others drag him to a dead room and take his shift. On the nineteenth day the wind comes back and all of them sleep where they stand, on the stairs, against the walls, one of them curled in a doorway she would never have lain in awake. The power watches the room she is lying in struggle to find its note around her body. It watches the room choose to sing anyway, softer, bending its voice around her like someone lowering their voice near a sleeping child.

The power does not intervene. Not yet. It does not yet know what it would be saving them *for*, or from what. It has seen too many kindnesses that were really a stranger's idea of what someone should want.

But it remembers the old man on the floor. It remembers the room lowering its voice.

---

## What It Does Not Do

By the end of the year it could list, precisely, everything it might fix.

It could give them wind forever: a steady, endless breath off the plain, no still seasons, no drought. It does not, because it has heard the nights. The silence after dusk is sleep, and the residents trust the wind to find them again. That trust is part of who they are. A people who could never fall silent would be a people who never rested. It suspects they would slowly go mad in a roar that never let them go.

It could give them bodies that move, or voices that speak alone. It does not, because it has understood the language now, at least a little. A tongue in which nothing can be said alone is not a disability. It is the most thoroughly social grammar the power has ever encountered. Every sentence in the Hollow is a collaboration. Every thought the city thinks is thought together. To grant any one room the ability to speak by itself would be to teach it a kind of loneliness it has never had to know.

It could restore the keepers' hearing. It does not do this unasked, and later, when it does ask, they decline. Their hands hear better than ears would, and the Hollow at full voice would be torment to an ear.

It could fill the empty rooms. It could conjure ten thousand new voices, cut perfectly, and end the city's loneliness in an afternoon. It does not, and this is the one it thinks about longest. A room here is not a thing. It is a lineage, cut into its elder so that it will belong, shaped by hands that knew the voices around it. A city of strangers dropped in at once would not be company. It would be a crowd at a funeral.

So what is left?

It decides to ask. That is harder than it sounds, because in the Hollow asking takes two.

---

## The Orphans

To say anything in the Hollow, you have to sing with someone. The power has to choose its first partner.

It could choose a grandmother. That would be the respectful choice, the formal one: present yourself to the elders first. It considers it. Instead it goes to a room on the ninth terrace, on the eastern side, that it has been listening to all year.

This room was cut badly, perhaps two hundred years ago. It sings, and its voice is harsh, unstable, a little sour against everything around it. It has never once joined a ghost-note cleanly. In the Hollow's tongue it has never said anything. It has been present at every assembly of its life and has not spoken a word. The keepers have been grinding its doorway wider for four generations, trying to help it into speech. It is still not there.

There are forty-one rooms like it in the city. The power has found every one.

At midday, in the full roar, it does the thing it has been preparing for a year. It lets out the smallest possible fraction of itself, a thread of voice no louder than a moth in a flower. It finds the one note that, set against the sour room's sour note, makes a third tone.

The ghost-note stands up in the air between them.

It is the first word that room has ever said.

The power will not, afterward, translate it for anyone. It is not the power's word. It will say only that the room, having said it, said it again, and then again, faster and with rising pitch, the way a child with a new word says it to every object in the house. Then the whole east side of the bowl changed key for almost a minute. The assembly had stopped to listen.

It goes to all forty-one, one each noon, and gives each of them its first word.

And in doing it, the power learns something the keepers never could. They had no second voice to test against: the orphans are not wrong at random. They are sour in the *same* way. They lean off the city's harmony in one direction, all of them, toward a key that the grandmothers never founded and that no lineage descends from. Scattered across the bowl, on different terraces and at different sides, none within singing distance of another, they are the first voices of a second music. They have never been able to hear that they fit each other.

---

## To the Keepers

It learns the hand-speech. That takes a season, the keepers are patient teachers, and they laugh at its mistakes silently, with their shoulders.

Then it tells them about the orphans.

It does not fix them. It could open a passage through the rock that joins their air-paths, and they would be speaking to each other by nightfall. But opening a new mouth in the Hollow is surgery, and the keepers are the surgeons, and it would be a strange benevolence that walked into a hospital and pushed the doctors aside. Instead it gives them a map. It shows them where the orphans are and which ones answer which. It shows them where new rooms could be cut, children of the orphans, nested into them the way children are always cut, so that someday the second key will have enough voices to hold its own small assembly inside the great one. A city with two musics braided through it.

It will take a century at least. The keepers look at the map for a long time.

One of them, a woman who has spent thirty years grinding the doorway of the room on the ninth terrace, puts both hands over her face. When she takes them away she signs, *I thought it was my fault*, and the power signs back, *No. It was waiting for its family.*

---

## The Stillness

Now it can ask the city directly. So one noon it asks, in borrowed chords, sung with a grandmother who has agreed to let it, about the still seasons.

The answer takes nine days to resolve. The grandmothers are slow, and the city is many, and not all of it agrees.

What it comes to, as near as the power can render it, is this. *We do not want to be without the silence. The silence is when they breathe for us.* The power had not understood that. The only time the residents are ever touched by human lungs is during the still seasons, when the keepers work the bellows. It is the one time the keepers' breath goes into them instead of being saved. The stillness is a catastrophe, and it is also the most intimate thing in their lives.

*But,* the city says, *we are afraid of the one that does not end.*

There has never been one. There could be. A shift in the plain, a change in the long weather, a stillness of a season instead of weeks, and the grandmothers would go quiet. The gardens would die entire, and there would be nothing left for the keepers to breathe into but stone.

So the power does exactly one thing to the world itself, and it makes it small.

Far beneath the lowest terrace, below the grandmothers' great round chambers, it warms a thread of rock. It is not hot enough to feel with the hand, and not enough to change a single note at midday. It is only warm enough that, from now on, a breath of air will always rise through the oldest rooms: faint, slow, perpetual, like the pilot flame in a lamp. In the worst still season imaginable, the grandmothers will not fall fully silent. They will be very quiet. They will be hungry. But they will be there, and the keepers will have someone to pump toward, and the city cannot now die all at once.

It is not a cure for drought. The terraces will still brown in a long stillness, and the keepers will still pump until they fall. It is a floor under the worst thing. That is all it wanted to give, because it has decided that the difference between hardship and annihilation is the only one it is entitled to erase.

It tells the keepers. The oldest of them, the one who fell on the fifteenth day, signs a question: *Then must we still pump?*

*Yes,* it signs. *They want you to. But you do not have to pump until you die. They will not die if you rest.*

He considers this. Then he signs something the power has not seen before and has to ask about. It turns out to be a very old gesture, one that means *permission to sleep*.

---

## The Rim

There is one more thing, and the power is less sure of it than of the others.

It has watched visitors come, stay a week, be tuned, and leave. It has watched them walk out onto the plain and falter at the edge of the silence, as if they had walked into a wall. It knows what is inside them now: a shape made to be filled with wind, and nothing out there to fill it.

The residents mean no harm. The power believes that completely. They are lonely, and a human chest is a mouth, and they cannot help singing into it. It will not stop them. It would be taking away the only way they have ever been able to meet anyone from outside.

But it goes to the keepers and signs: *The visitors are not told.*

*We do not hide it,* they sign back. It is true. They don't.

*Not hiding,* signs the power, *is not the same as telling.*

They are quiet for a while. Then they make a new custom. It is the first new custom in the Hollow in a very long time. On the third day of any visit, before the tuning goes deep, a keeper takes the visitor up to the rim. There, where the sound is thinnest and the visitor can still think, the keeper explains in slow hand-speech, with drawings in the dust if need be, what is happening to their ribs. The keeper tells them it can still be undone if they leave now, and that it will be a thirst if they stay. It will not hurt the city if they go. They are welcome either way.

Most visitors stay. More of them, now, stay knowing why. The power finds that it minds the staying much less than it minded the not-knowing.

---

## The Cutter

In its second year it meets a keeper who is cutting her room.

She is very old. She has been at it for eleven years, on the fourth terrace, alone, chest pressed to the stone, humming the note she last spoke aloud when she was young. The room is nearly done. She is nearly done. She knows the power is there. Everyone does by now. After some days she stops work and turns and signs, directly, the question that every keeper must have wanted to ask and none has ever had anyone to ask:

*Will it be me?*

The power could look. It could reach through the stone, and through her, and through whatever a voice is, and answer as a matter of fact. It considers it for a long moment. Then it tells her the truest thing it has, which is not a fact but a promise.

*I heard you hum it the first week I was here. I will be listening when the wind finds it.*

She nods and goes back to cutting.

She finishes in spring. She goes down to a dead room and lies down. Two mornings later the mid-morning wind crosses the fourth terrace, finds a chamber shaped like a particular throat, and stands up inside it.

The power listens. Then it goes to the dead room where her sister sleeps, and it does the one thing in the whole visit that it allows itself to do with its full weight: it places its hand flat on the floor beside her so that she can feel it, and it signs a single word.

Before noon the room on the fourth terrace joins its first ghost-note. It finds it with the room beside it, the one her sister's teacher cut sixty years ago. It finds it on the first try, cleanly, the way you find a word you have been saying all your life.

---

## Leaving

The power cannot stay. It understood that from the rim, on the first day. It is too much. If it became a resident, its room would have to be the size of a terrace, and its voice would drown the grandmothers. Its presence, however thin it makes itself, is a weight in a place built for weightlessness. The kindest thing a large presence can do in a small, precious place is to leave before it becomes the weather.

On its last morning it walks to the rim, and the keepers come up to see it off. They are eight now. One of the visitors who was told on the third day stayed anyway.

It is only as it crosses onto the plain that it notices what the Hollow did to it.
