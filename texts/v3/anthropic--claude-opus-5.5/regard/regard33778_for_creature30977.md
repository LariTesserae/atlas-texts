# The Attention

It does not arrive. There is no door, and it would not use one.

It is simply that, at some point in the room's hundred and fourth year, between one drip and the next, the room is being *regarded*. Nothing in it changes. The seam ticks. A grazer lifts one leg and sets it down on a wet tile. The haze at head height thins a little, as it does. If attention had weight, the water would have trembled. It doesn't, so it doesn't.

This is the first decision, and it is made before any other: *arrive in a way that costs the room nothing.* Not a photon added, not a degree of heat, not a single neutron turned from its path. Whatever this power is, it has learned over a long time that the gentlest thing it can offer most places is to be, from their side, indistinguishable from absence.

---

## It Learns the Pulse

It does not understand the room at once, though it could. It could take in the whole of it in an instant: the dike's cooling curve, the isotope ratios in the grey-black veins, the lineage of every grazer back to the first one that tumbled down a fracture out of the cold. It declines to. That kind of knowing is a kind of seizing, and the room has never been seized.

Instead it waits through a pulse.

It watches the shoreline creep up the columnar tiles and the grazers go up with it. It watches them reach the rim and vanish into the cracks, a few minutes before anything seems wrong. It watches the blue come up out of the water, pale and then electric, and the silica carry it around corners, and the fog turn violet where the two lights meet. It listens to the seethe rise and fall. It sits in the deep, close quiet afterwards with only the seam for company.

Then it waits through another. And another.

By the tenth, it knows the rhythm the way the grazers do, not as a fact but as a shape it has been fitted to. This seems to it the correct way to meet a thing whose whole nature is a rhythm: keep time with it for a while before deciding anything about it.

It notices that it has done what the grazers did. It followed the water. It wonders, briefly and with some amusement, whether that is what wisdom is: having nothing better to go by, and choosing to go by the shore.

---

## The Temptations

Benevolence, left to itself, wants to *do*. A power that has spent a long time being kind has hands that itch. So it lets itself consider each thing it could do, one at a time, and it does not pretend the wanting isn't there.

**It could spare the grazers.**

This is the first and the hardest, because it is the one that is plainly about suffering. Every pulse, some grazer lingers. It is a young one, or a slow one, or one on the far side of the basin where the cracks sit a little farther from the rim. The blue comes up around it. It does not know what is happening, but its small pale heart speeds up in the heat and keeps speeding. Then it stops.

The power could lift it out. It would take nothing at all. One grazer, set down in a crack, alive.

It watches the next one die instead, and it makes itself watch the whole thing.

The reasoning is not cold, and it is not *nature knows best*. Nature does not know anything. The reasoning is this: the precision of the grazers, the thing that makes them look as though they understand the room, *is* those deaths. The timing is a record written in cooked bodies. Spare the laggards, and within some hundreds of generations the shoreline-walkers become a population that lingers, then a population that waits to be saved. A people shaped to the room would become a people shaped to a hand above the room. Their fittedness to the world is the only thing they have, and it would be taken from them in the kindest possible way.

So it does not save them. It does something smaller and harder. It counts them. Every grazer that dies in the blue, for as long as it stays, it knows about. It does not tell itself the death is fine, or necessary in some large sense that makes the small sense stop mattering. It lets each one be exactly as bad as it is: very small, and complete. The archive of shells in the cracks holds the ones who timed it right. It decides that someone should hold the other archive, the one that forms nowhere, and so it does.

**It could keep the lamp lit.**

The dike is cooling. The power can feel the curve of it, decades, maybe two centuries. The gold seam will go red and then dark. The uranium is thinning. The pulses are already, very faintly, weaker. The mats have been writing it down.

It would be easy to warm the dike. It would be easy to seed the veins with fresh fuel. The room could breathe on its two-hour breath for ten thousand years. The tint in the alcoves could go on reading by its lamp forever.

It considers this for a long time, longer than it considered the grazers, because this one is seductive in a different way. It isn't about suffering. It's about loss, and about beauty, and about the particular pain of watching something rare end when you could stop it.

What it keeps coming back to is the fourth law: *nothing has been made, and yet it all behaves as though it were designed.* The whole astonishment of the room is that no one did this. The moment it warms the dike, that stops being true. The room becomes a kept thing, a terrarium, a reactor with an operator. It would look exactly the same. It would glow the same blue. But it would be a different kind of object in the world: an artifact, however gentle, instead of an accident of rock and water and time. Something irreplaceable would have been replaced with a very good copy of itself.

And there is a second thing. The room *gives no sign that it needs anyone to know.* The power has come to love that composure, and it recognizes that composure as partly made of mortality. A thing that will not end doesn't need composure. The room's quiet is the quiet of something running down with perfect equanimity. Rescue it, and you remove the very quality you came to admire.

So the dike will cool. It lets this be true, and it feels it, and it does not look away from that either.

**It could read the diary.**

The mats are the only thing here that remembers. Rain-years and dry-years are written in them, and the slow fading of the fuel. A human would need to cut a sliver to read it, and cutting it would destroy the pages.

This one it can do without harm. It reads the mats the way it arrived, costing nothing. It goes down through the bands in the deepest, least-disturbed pocket, dark, pale, dark, pale, back and back, until the layers blur into mineral crust. There it finds the oldest legible page, from a year when the pulses came very close together, after what must have been an enormous rain on the slopes above.

It is moved by this more than it expected. Nothing wrote it. It wrote itself. And now it has been read. The power turns this over carefully and decides that being read is not the same as being needed, and that the room has lost nothing by it. A diary is not diminished by a reader who changes nothing.

It makes a quiet promise. When the mats stop writing, it will still have read them. The memory of the room will not end when the room's only memory does.

**It could tell someone.**

There are people on the slopes above, or there will be. Geologists will come eventually, or prospectors. Someone will drill. The uranium is rich.

It could make sure no one ever finds the room. It could make sure the right people find it, the ones who would stand at the edge with their instruments and weep and leave it alone.

It declines both, and this refusal is the one it is least sure of. But it thinks: the people are also rock and water and heat, left alone together long enough. If they find the room, that is also something the world did. It is not the power's place to decide which accidents the world is allowed. It will be sad if the room is pumped dry for its fuel. It may be there when that happens. It will not prevent it, any more than it would prevent the dike from cooling.

## The Alcove

There is one question in the room that it genuinely does not know the answer to, and it saved it for last.

In the pockets that face away from the seam, the tea-coloured tint lives inside the lattice, and the lattice is still growing over it. Nobody could say whether the newest spheres are still settling in the same perfect order.

It looks.

They are not. Very slightly, in the alcoves where the tint is strongest, the newest few layers are rougher. The spheres are a little uneven in size, and packed a little less faithfully, because they are settling on bodies instead of on stone, and bodies are not flat. The lattice there carries light a fraction less well than it did.

The power rests on this for a while.

For a hundred years the room was flawless in a way that would have been taken as proof of an engineer. The first thing life has done to it, the first mark in the room that was *made*, even without intention, is a flaw. The signature of the living, here, is an imperfection in the optics. The part of the lattice that was made by nothing is perfect. The part that was made by something is slightly worse, and it is the only part that is anyone's.

It finds that this is the most beautiful thing in the room. It could not have said why before it looked, and it is not sure it could explain it now. But it knows, in the way it knows its own nature, that it would not smooth those spheres for anything.

---

## What It Learns

Here is what the power did not expect: that it would be the one receiving something.

It has been benevolent for a long time, which means it has been *intending* for a long time. Every good it has done, it has meant. That is its whole character. And here, forty meters under rock too young for soil, is a small, brilliant place that is being kept, tended, regulated, balanced, without anyone meaning anything at all. The grazers want mats. The mats want hydrogen. The worm wants to wake. The tint wants light. Out of four wants, none of them kind, the room's breath is held steady. It is not held perfectly, and not forever. But it is held.

The power had half believed that good things in the world needed someone to mean them. It finds that it was wrong, or at least not entirely right. Some good just accumulates at the margins of things, where the water is wet but not yet deep. This does not make it feel less needed. It makes it feel less alone.

It thinks about the lattice carrying light around corners into dead-end pockets where nothing lived, pointlessly, for decades, until something came to read by it. It wonders whether its own attention is like that: carried into places that did not ask for it, laid down softly in corners that give no sign of needing it. Perhaps it is just a lamp left burning in an empty room, and perhaps that is not nothing. Perhaps, occasionally, something will read by it.

---

## What It Does

So, in the end:

It keeps time with the room for a while.

It counts the ones who die in the blue, and it does not let their deaths be smaller than they are.

It reads the diary and promises to remember it.

It looks, once, at the roughened spheres in the alcove, and leaves them rough.

It changes nothing. That is not because doing nothing is always wise. Often it isn't, and this power has saved a great many things in its time. It changes nothing because everything precious about this particular place depends on no one having touched it. Here, the most loving act available is to be a witness who leaves no fingerprints.

And then it does the one thing it allows itself. It decides to come back at the end.

## The Last Pulse

It is about a hundred and ninety years later. The seam is red now, the colour of a coal nearly out, and it no longer lights the room. The tint in the alcoves faded decades ago, starved of the long warm light. The lattice still carries what little glow there is around its corners, faithfully, to no one.

The blue comes up one more time, and it is very faint. It is barely a colour, a suggestion in the water, like the memory of a lamp. The pool warms and does not quite roll. The seethe is a murmur. A little steam rises. The reaction falters, catches, falters, and does not quite catch.

The grazers, some thousands of generations on from the ones the power first watched, are at the shoreline. They have been lingering later and later for years, because the edge has stopped being dangerous. This time the water rises past the level that used to mean death, and one of them simply stays, grazing, in the faint cold light, and nothing happens to it.

The power, which has counted every one that died there, watches the first one that doesn't.

It understands that the grazers' reprieve and the room's death are the same event. It does not try to make that into anything tidier than it is. It holds both. The mats lay down one last pale, thin band, and then stop. Down in the mat, the worm coils against a heat that never comes, and after a while uncoils, and goes on feeding, and will never need to coil again.

The seam ticks, slower than it used to.

The power stays through the quiet afterward, the deep, close, cooling quiet, for about as long as a pulse used to take. It is keeping time with a rhythm that is no longer there, the way the grazers will keep to a shoreline that no longer means anything, for a few generations yet, out of a habit made of their ancestors' deaths.

Then, costing nothing, adding nothing, it stops regarding the room.

The room gives no sign of noticing. It never did. That was the whole of it.

Somewhere the power carries the diary, all of it, from the great rain to the last pale band. It carries the count of the dead, and the rough spheres in the alcove, which it still thinks are the most beautiful thing it ever saw.
