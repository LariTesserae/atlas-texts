# The Visitor Who Came to Look

## Arrival

It doesn't come the way the goats come, up impossible ledges, or the way the ravens come, riding the draft. It doesn't come at all, exactly. At some point in the late afternoon, in the loudest hour, when the note in the bowl has been holding for a long time, the Kettle is simply being *attended to* by something that wasn't attending before.

Nothing in the bowl notices. The swifts keep screaming. A hay-cutter whistles at a shadow that isn't there. The salamander snaps at a gnat and misses. The power is careful about this. It has learned, over a very long time, that the first thing attention does is change what it looks at, and that the kindest attention begins by making itself small.

So it looks. For a long time, before it does anything else, it just looks.

## What It Sees

It sees what a visitor sees: the green, the wet, the birds, the lip, the enormous pale nothing beyond.

Then it sees what the valley people see: the Sitter. The old water in its old seat, spread through moss and mist and the sheets on the walls too thin to see. It feels the Sitter the way you feel someone else in a dark room, by a certain quality in the air.

It also sees what the valley people can only guess at, and here it has to be most careful, because this is where power becomes dangerous. It can see how tired the Sitter is. It can see how the Sitter misses weight. It can feel, running down the mountain every night and never arriving, the Sitter's long unfinished motion toward the lower ground. It can feel the Sitter not knowing whether it is trying to leave or trying to stay.

A lesser power would already be reaching for a solution.

## The Things It Could Do

It considers them honestly, one at a time, because a wise power doesn't pretend it has no options. It takes each one seriously enough to see why it would be wrong.

**It could give the Sitter back its weight.** It could make the Kettle cold enough, for long enough, that the water would gather and harden and settle into its seat again. It would be white and silent and whole. This is the thing the Sitter misses. The power turns it over and sees what it would cost: the ferns, the salamander in the eye of the place, the swifts with nowhere to come screaming back to in spring, the goats, the bees, the upward roots, the cup that is always full. It would cost the villages below their river. It would cost the Sitter the one thing it became without meaning to, which is *generous*. The Sitter misses weight the way an old person misses being young. That is not the same as wanting to be young again at the price of everything that happened since. The power doesn't mistake a longing for a request.

**It could let the Sitter finally go.** It could still the updraft, quiet the morning air, and let the whole of it drain one evening down the mountain and never be thrown back. That would end the argument. But the argument *is* the Kettle. The falling and being given back is not a wound in the Sitter's life. It is the shape of its life, the way breathing is the shape of ours. You don't cure someone of breathing.

**It could catch the feathers.** It could teach the Sitter to lift the dead too, so that nothing that falls past the lip is lost. The power looks at this one longest, because it is the one its own heart leans toward. Then it looks at the cairn and the braided cord, and understands that the valley people have already asked this question, and that the Sitter has already answered it in the only way that is true. It lets the dead fall. It lifts the cord of their hair every morning anyway. That is grief, correctly held. It is not a problem to be solved. It is something the living and the Sitter have worked out between them, over centuries, without help. It would be a kind of theft to improve on it.

**It could reveal itself.** It could stand on the lip at dawn so that the next visitor would see it and carry the story down: the Sitter has a friend, the Kettle is watched over. The power declines. The valley people have a relationship with this place that is careful and old and full of small courtesies. They leave the bowl where they found it. They don't explain the nests to outsiders. Adding a larger presence to that would be like shouting in the room where two old friends are talking quietly. And some of what the power has come to love here is that the Sitter is *not* a god, only someone who hasn't gotten up yet.

**It could learn what the swift nests are for.** It could, easily. It doesn't look. Some secrets are load-bearing.

**It could replace the cord**, which is frayed, faded, and stiff with lichen, and will not last forever. It won't. The cord belongs to someone's particular grief. When it finally breaks and goes, that will be part of the story too, and the person who tied it, or whoever remembers them, can decide whether to tie another.

It sets all of these down, the way you set down tools you've decided not to use. It does not feel this as holding back. It feels it as seeing clearly.

## Staying

What it does instead is stay.

It stays through the rest of the afternoon, through the showers that fall both ways at once. It stays through the evening turn, when the wind falters and reverses and the mist drains away like water from a basin. It feels the Sitter start down the mountain and doesn't follow. It stays in the bowl, with the part of the Sitter that remains.

It stays through the night. It lies, if a thing like it can be said to lie, in the low stone shelter built for three, with the door facing the stream. It is there for the cold hours, when the Sitter tries to become ice, when the moss stiffens and the pool's edge whitens and the drips on the walls hang and hold. It keeps very still, so that the Sitter can have its almost-self in peace. Someone should be here for this, it thinks. People built this shelter so that someone could be held while the old thing around them was trying to hold itself. Tonight the power turns that around. It is not holding the Sitter. It is only being held by it, and being glad to be.

It stays through the dawn, the Sitter's quietest hour, cold and silent and heavy and holding its breath. The power holds its breath too.

The swifts explode out of the walls, screaming, and it's over, and the power finds that it is laughing.

## The One Thing

By the next afternoon it has decided. It will do one thing, and only because the valley people have already shown it what the right size of kindness is here.

The hum begins. The updraft comes strong across the mouth of the bowl, and the Sitter's outline starts to sing its one note, the resonance of a hollow exactly the size of something that used to be there. It doesn't choose when. The sky plays it.

The power hums back.

It doesn't hum loudly, and it doesn't hum the same note. People do that already, low, under their breath, without quite deciding to, and it is good that they do. The power hums the note *under* the note, the one that would sound if the hollow were full. It is the note of the glacier, the shape the Sitter was, sung back into the shape the Sitter is. It holds it quietly for as long as the Kettle holds its own. The two notes sit together in the bowl, and they don't clash. They fit, the way a hand fits the print it left.

Nothing visible happens. The swifts don't pause. The mist keeps rolling. Anyone standing in the Kettle might have felt the note in their chest a little deeper than usual, and thought nothing of it.

But for the first time in ten thousand years, the Sitter's voice has been answered by something that knew what it was singing about. It has heard, once, that what it lost and what it became can sound in the same room without one erasing the other. It doesn't have to decide whether it's leaving or staying. It has never had to. The power doesn't tell it this. You can't tell something like that to anyone. It only sings it, once, and lets the Sitter do with it whatever something that old does with a thing it has heard.

## Leaving

Then it does what every visitor does.

It goes to the boulder by the pool, to the pecked cup that is always full, the one place where the Sitter holds itself still. It lifts the weathered wooden bowl and drinks. It sets the bowl down exactly where it found it.

Then it walks down the mountain, carrying a mouthful of the Sitter in the direction it always wanted to go.

It goes the long way, the way the cold air goes at night, down through the invisible valleys. It goes all the way to the small light that can sometimes be seen from the lip on clear nights, the one so far away it is hard to believe it belongs to the same world. It is a window. A family lives there. Someone is up late. They don't see anything come.

Outside that window, the power lets the mouthful go. It is only a little water, falling on the ground beneath a lit window, the way it was always going to fall somewhere.

It is not a rescue, and it doesn't finish anything. The Sitter will still drain every evening, still be lifted every morning, still try to be ice in the dark and lose to the swifts at dawn. Nothing about the Kettle has been fixed, because nothing about the Kettle was broken.

But one small part of the Sitter has arrived at the light it has been walking toward every night for longer than there have been people. It didn't get there by being rescued. Something that loved it for a day and a night carried it the way the villagers do.

And the power, which could have done almost anything, goes on its way thinking that it did almost exactly enough.

---

*Behind it, in the bowl, the cord on the cairn lifts in the morning draft and streams upward, frayed and faded and stiff with lichen, the way it always has. The power left it alone. Some things are already being done right.*
