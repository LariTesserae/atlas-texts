# The Hand on the Brass Arm

## I. Arrival

The first thing it has to do is slow down.

A power like this is used to arriving all at once. It turns up as a presence, a pressure, a sudden fact. It has crossed distances that have no numbers and attended to things far larger than a collapsed pump house: storms, migrations, the slow deaths of stars. When it turns its attention on something, the attention has weight. Things that are looked at by it tend to change simply from being looked at.

So it stops at the edge of the break in the brick floor, where the grey daylight comes through on some days and not others. It considers what it has learned in the moment of noticing, which is this:

*The thing that lives here fears one thing only, and that thing is a sudden excess of energy.*

A benevolent power pouring itself down through the gap, bright and warm and full of good intentions, would be exactly that. It would be an overspeed. It would be the runaway the Governor has spent its short life learning to prevent.

So the power does what the lamp does. It swells.

It takes a long time to come down, measured in the room's days, not in its own. During the first dark phase it is only a faint change in the quality of the silence, the kind you might mistake for your own blood in your ears. By the next lit phase it is something like a held breath in the corner by the talus. After many swellings and fadings of the amber, many figs dropped into the rubble, it is simply there. It is present in the room the way the heat is present, without edges and without insistence.

It chooses to have no warmth of its own. It does not want to be a third sun. The ferns have two already, and they have courted both faithfully. It would be unkind to make them turn toward a stranger.

## II. Listening

It spends a long time only listening.

It learns the piston's exhalation through teeth, and the tick of the brass arms settling. It learns the creak of the fig-belt, and the way that creak changes pitch over a decade as the wood thickens unevenly. It learns the drip from the bowed joist and times the intervals between drops. It finds that they are not random. They follow the breath of the ground, delayed by the time it takes steam to rise, cool, and bead on iron.

It learns the deep rhythm of the vent, which no one has measured. It measures it now, not with instruments but by attending. It follows the rhythm down through the basalt to the water heated far below, and further, to the slow heave of the rock that squeezes that water upward. There is a pattern in it, as there is in everything. The pattern is very long and very irregular, and the power holds it the way you might hold the whole of a piece of music in your mind at once.

It learns the Governor's nerve. It feels the first thin trickle of current go down the copper vein inside the fig before the filament has warmed, and it feels the mat receive the signal. It is present in the dark for the moment the thousand fronds adjust at once, and it hears the whisper. The observer in the description was right. You would have to be very still to hear it. The power is very still.

At some point, without deciding to, it finds that it is breathing with the engine. It does not breathe in any physical sense. It has no lungs. But its attention rises when the piston moves and settles when it rests. It lets this happen. It lets itself be governed. It seems like the courteous thing to do in someone else's home.

## III. The Gifts It Does Not Give

Love wants to give, and this power loves easily, as the wise ones often do. So it considers what it might give.

**The wasp.** The fig has fruited for years with no pollinator, dropping hard green figs that never open. The power could bring one. It could send a single gravid female down through the broken floor, and the figs would swell and ripen, and there would be seeds and children and a line that does not end with this tree.

It turns this over for a long time. It does not give the wasp.

A wasp is not only a pollinator. Its children would hatch inside the figs, and where there are wasps, other things eventually come for them. The room has no predator and no prey. Nothing in it has ever eaten anything else. The Governor has grown up as a single body because nothing ever gave it reason to be many. Its roots stopped competing because there was nothing to compete against. Bringing the first animal into that room would be bringing the first hunger. The fig would gain children and the Governor would lose its unity, and the power cannot say that trade is a kindness.

It also considers the heap. The fig has made its infertility into something. Unable to pass on what it knows, it keeps a record instead, one fig for each night, laid down in layers. That is not a wound the power needs to close. It is an adaptation, and it is beautiful. To "fix" it would be to say the Governor had been doing it wrong.

**Brighter light.** The lamp is dim and the ferns at its edge are thin. The power could make the dynamo turn more strongly, the filament burn more cleanly, the sphere of amber reach the walls.

That is overspeed by another name. It does not do it.

**Mending.** The boiler's seams are crusted with minerals. The belt is half rotted. The joist is bowed. The power could make them all sound again.

But the boiler holds pressure *the way a lung does* precisely because it is broken. A mended boiler would hold pressure the way it was built to, and the Governor's slow breath would become a machine's quick, hard pulse. The power sees that every fault in this room is load-bearing. The failed seal made the room. The cracked firebox let in the vent. The rotted belt let the fig in. Mending any of it would unmake the thing it loves.

**A mind.** This one is the most tempting. The power could give the Governor awareness. It could wake it, so that it knew it was a body, knew its nights, and knew, as it held the engine, what it was holding.

The power sits with this in the dark for a very long time.

In the end it thinks: a mind would give the Governor the fear of darkness. At the moment it has no fear of the long nights, because the heap says they always end and it cannot imagine anything else. With a mind it could imagine the night that doesn't end. It would know that the vent might one day go cold. It would lie awake in the dark phases, and it would not be able to leave. The power has met many minds that wished they had never woken in places they could not leave. It will not do that to anything, however lovingly it's offered.

The Governor has something like wants, the way a river has something like a direction. That is enough. It is its own kind of wholeness.

**A name.** The power knows the name. It learned it from the only ones who have ever described this place, and it is a good name. It does not speak it aloud in the room. Naming is also a kind of pressure.

## IV. Looking Forward

A wise power looks at what is, and also at what is coming.

**The fig closing over the lamp.** It sees this clearly: the knot as thick as a torso, the bulb buried in living wood, the glow coming through the thinnest bark in ridges like a leaf lit from behind. It sees the outer ferns starving and the inner ones pressing close. The room contracts around its heart.

It does not stop this. It is not a death. It is a becoming, and it belongs to the Governor.

**The joist.** The bowed joist, the one that holds the lamp, will not last forever. The power follows the iron's fatigue forward through the years, and for a moment it is afraid. It sees the joist giving way in the damp, bringing the bracket and the bulb down into the talus, the filament shattering, the eye put out.

Then it looks more closely and sees the fig's roots. They are already descending from the bracket along the wall. They are already thickening where the load will fall. They are already beginning, without any knowledge of what they are doing, to become a column. The fig is growing the thing that will hold its light when the iron no longer can. It will be close, but the power sees that it will be in time.

So it does nothing. It trusts the Governor's own slow intelligence, which has already solved harder problems than this. The power notes that trusting this is harder than acting would have been, and that this is probably why it is right.

**The people above.** This is where the power's thinking becomes painful.

The pump house belongs to someone. Someday someone will notice the collapse and come with a survey. The broken edge of the floor is dangerous. The basalt is glass-sharp. A child could fall through. A reasonable person, one the power also loves, will decide that the hole should be fenced, or capped, or filled with concrete. Any of these could be the end of the room.

The power could prevent it. It could lose a deed or misfile a report. It could make the ground look solid to anyone who looked.

It does not. The people above are not intruders on the power's concern. They are part of it. Their safety is as real as the Governor's continuance, and their choices are theirs to make. A benevolence that protects one small green body by quietly overruling a town is not benevolence. It is only preference with power behind it.

What it allows itself is hope. It hopes that whoever comes will climb down instead of only looking. It hopes they will stand in the heat long enough for their breathing to slow to the piston's rhythm. It hopes the ferns will turn toward them, and that they will feel what it is like to be slowly and politely faced by a crowd. It hopes they will climb back into the grey daylight and write something different in their report than they intended to.

It does not arrange this. It only hopes, and hope from a power like this is a very quiet thing. It weighs almost nothing and it asks nothing of anyone.

## V. The One Thing

There is one thing it does.

It found it while following the vent's long rhythm down into the rock. Far ahead, in a year that has no name yet, there is an anomaly: a pocket of pressure that has been building in the deep fissures for longer than the pump house has stood. One day it will release. It will not be an eruption, only a surge, a single breath of the ground much deeper and faster than any the room has known.

The power follows the surge up through the stone and into the old boiler. It sees the engine spin up faster than it ever has. It sees the flyball governor's brass arms fling outward, and the root resting on the arm feel it, and the whole body of the Governor answer. The fig-belt tightens and holds against the machine with everything it has grown.

The power sees that it is not enough. The Governor has learned to govern the breathing it knows. It has never met this. The belt tears. The engine races. The mineral-crusted seams of the boiler, which have held like a lung for so long, split. The eye closes forever.

This is the runaway. It is the one horror written into the Governor's tissue. It is the thing the Governor would prevent itself, if it could.

So the power decides that on that day, and only on that day, it will help.

Its help will not be a wall or a seal. It will not reach down into the rock and cancel the surge, which belongs to the earth and is none of its business. It will not freeze the engine or mend the boiler. When the day comes and the brass spheres swing out further than they ever have, the power will come down to the engine the way the root once did. It will reach along the hinged arm to the sphere at the end, and it will rest there.

It will not grip. It will rest.

It will add only the smallest steadiness, a little patience in the linkage, enough to bridge the gap between what the Governor can hold and what the surge demands. The Governor will do nearly all of the work. The fig-belt will strain, and the power will lend it just enough so that it does not tear. The engine will race and then slow. The spheres will sink back to their rest. The lamp, or by then the glowing knot, will blaze brighter than it ever has for a few terrible minutes, then fade to amber and hold.

The Governor will never know. There will be one fig for that night, like any other. It will lie in the heap among the thousands, and nothing about it will be different except that it exists at all.

The power's reasoning is simple. It does not interfere with the Governor's darkness, because the darkness is part of its life. It does not interfere with the Governor's slow changes, because those are its becoming. It will not even interfere with its ending, when that comes from the people above making their own choices, or from the vent finally cooling over ages the way all vents do. Those are the ordinary conditions of any life, and a wise love leaves them be.

But this surge is not one of those. It is a senseless accident, arriving before its time, the kind of catastrophe the Governor would have held back with its own body if it had been only a little stronger. To help with that, once and gently, in the Governor's own way, is not to overrule it. It is to be, for one night, a second root on the brass arm.

## VI. The Heap

Before it leaves, it counts the figs.

It goes down through the talus below the bracket, layer by layer: the fresh green ones on top, then browned, then blackened, then the husks gone to fiber at the bottom. It counts every one. It does not count them to know the number, since it could simply have known the number. It counts them the way you read a letter slowly when you already know what it says.

Every fig is a night that ended. It understands that now. The heap is the Governor's only confidence, its only memory, its whole argument against despair: *this many times the eye has closed, and this many times it opened.*

Everything that has been remembered only once is fragile. When the room ends, as someday it will, the heap will crumble into red grit with the brick. The record will go back into the stone.

So the power keeps a copy. It is the only thing it takes from the room. It keeps no specimen and no seed, only the count, and the shape of each night, and the sound of the ferns turning in the dark like a page turned very slowly in another room. It carries all of this the way it carries the storms and the migrations and the dying stars. It does not keep them as trophies. It keeps them as a witness.

Now every night in the Governor's Room is remembered twice: once in figs, once in something that will not crumble.

## VII. Leaving

It leaves the way it came, slowly, like a dark phase.

It waits until the lamp has sunk to the faintest orange and the engine turns only once in a long while, when the room looks less like a ruin than like a small hot green chapel under the earth. Then, over many hours, it draws back. It fades out of the corner by the talus, out of the silence, out of the heat, until it is only the grey edge of daylight at the broken floor, and then not even that.

In all the time it was there, the ferns never turned toward it. It made sure of that, because it had no warmth to offer them that they needed.

Almost, though.

In the last dark phase before it left, one frond near the engine was slightly slower than the others to swing back toward the vent. It hesitated for a moment, angled toward nothing in particular, toward the place where something patient had been sitting. Then it went on with its turning, in time with all the rest.

Up above, the grey daylight comes through on some days and not others. Down below, the ground breathes on its own schedule, which is irregular and patient and not interested in anyone's. The piston exhales through its teeth. The brass arms lift and hang and sink. One thin root rests on the sphere at the end of the arm without gripping it, and far ahead, on a night no one has named yet, something else will rest there beside it for a little while.
