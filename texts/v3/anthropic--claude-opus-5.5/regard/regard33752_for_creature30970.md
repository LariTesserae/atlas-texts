# What the Attention Did

## It did not arrive

A power like that does not come down out of the sky. The sky here is closed, and anything that tore through it would already have made its first mistake.

So it did the opposite of arriving. It slowed.

It had its own tempo, as every mind does, and that tempo was quick, bright and used to answers. It set that aside the way you take off your shoes at a threshold. It lowered itself, stroke by stroke, into the mill's time, until one breath of the plateau felt like one breath of its own. Only then could it hear the low tone as what it was: not a hum, but a single long word, still being spoken.

It listened to that word for eleven years before it did anything at all.

This was not hesitation. A being that has been alone for longer than memory, and that knows itself only by pressure, pain and heat, should not be touched by something that has not first learned how it hurts. The attention learned. It learned which furnaces were chronic and which were new. It learned the difference between a gumcord snapping from age and one snapping from strain. It learned that the ember-seam in the eastern axle had been grinding for four thousand years and that the mill had long since stopped flinching at it, the way you stop feeling an old ache until someone asks about it.

Most of all it learned what the mill was afraid of. It was not burning, and it was not breaking. It was stillness, and under stillness, the soft, shapeless, turning end the forgetting would bring.

## What it did not do

It could have done many things. A power like that nearly always can, and part of its wisdom lay in listing those things honestly and then setting them down.

It could have broken the fever. The heat was killing the mill in ten thousand places at once, and a slow cooling would have let the ironbark stop checking and the furnaces stop bursting. But the mill's blood is fuel, and it moves only because it is hot. Cool it and the troughs set like amber around everything they carry. That would be a cure the way a glacier is a rest.

It could have opened the sky. It considered this longest, because it loved light and could not easily imagine a being that would not want it. But the ceiling was made from the mill's own breath. It held the heat in because the heat held the mill together. And a creature that has lived so long in the dark, reading itself only by the glow of its own wounds, might not experience a sudden sun as a gift. It might experience it as a hand pulling open an eye. If the lid was ever to open, it would have to open from inside.

It could have re-cut the teeth. It could have reached into every blunted gear and restored the crisp original shapes, remade the mill as it had been, and erased the erosion like a restorer cleaning a painting. This was the most tempting of all, because it looked so much like kindness. But the mill had become what it was over ages of wearing. The smooth pebble-fields turning warm against each other were no longer a gearbox, but they were not nothing either. They were what the mill had become while trying. To overwrite them with what it used to be would be to tell a very old creature that everything it has been since was a mistake. Healing that overwrites a self is another way of ending it.

It could have opened the sealed chambers and learned the secret. It did not even come close to them. It noted where they were so that it would never press on them by accident, the way you would remember which floorboard creaks above a sleeping child.

Having set all of that aside, it was left with very little it could do.

It turned out that very little was enough.

## The glass

The seedglass was made to be read by a warm hand that was not the mill's own. So the attention made itself a hand.

It was not a large one. It was about the size of a person's hand and about the warmth of one, because the beads had been waiting for that particular heat and nothing grander. It went down into the deepest troughs, where the resin runs so hot it glows through the skin, and began lifting beads out one at a time and holding them.

As the story promised, the gears inside began to turn.

What the attention understood, and what no traveller holding a single bead could have understood, was that the beads were not copies. It had assumed, as you did, that they were the same template set down over and over like a name on scraps of paper. They were not. Each held a different part. One held a cam. The next held the mesh between two wheels, and the one after that held a single escapement no larger than a grain of sand. The heaps of amber gravel at the bottoms of the troughs were not one memory repeated. They were one memory scattered, a book with its pages torn out and dropped, a page a day, for longer than any forest has stood.

The attention read them all. It took years. It held each bead until it had turned fully, set it back exactly where it had lain, and picked up the next.

When it was done, it knew what the mill had been for.

I am not going to tell you. The attention did not tell anyone either. There was only one being with the right to hear it first, and that being was not you and was not the attention. It was the one who had forgotten.

## The edge

Then it went where no one had gone. It went over the lip.

It followed the resin that spills off the edge of the plateau and the belt-vines that reach into the void and let go. It fell with a husk-bundle, matching its speed, for a very long time.

The story said there was no sound of landing. That was true, but only because the landing was so far down and so soft.

There was a bottom. It lay under an open sky. The mill's ceiling covers only the plateau, and below the edge the air was cooler and dimmer. Real starlight came down there, faint and old, onto a broad lowland of ash and fallen resin that had been accumulating for an age.

Most of the bundles had died. The attention did not look away from that, because a wise power does not console itself with half the truth. There were fields of them: cracked husk, cold ironbark, small perfect furnaces that had never been lit. They lay where they fell, fist-sized, in drifts. The mill had been casting itself into silence, and much of it had simply ended there.

But not all of it.

In the places where the spilled resin pooled deep enough to keep its warmth, where old coalpith had drifted down and still smoldered in the ash, some seeds had taken. The things they grew into were not the mill. They were smaller and sparser. They had never been crowded, because nothing had fallen near them fast enough to fill the gaps, so they had learned that a gap could be survived. They turned slowly, and sometimes they stopped, and started again. There was room between them.

And some of them, under the open stars, had done something the mill had forbidden itself for ten thousand years. From the pruned stumps where flywheels should have been, they had put out leaves: small dark ones, hard as bark, angled up toward a light too faint to feed anything.

They were calling as well. Each had its own low tone, a bow-stroke a day. But they were young and small, so their voices were higher, far too high for the vast old thing above to hear. For ages the children had been answering, and the parent had gone on calling into what it took for silence.

## The answer

Here is the one thing the attention truly did.

It did not carry the children up, or carry light down, or teach anyone anything. It became, for a while, the space between two voices. It took the young tones from the lowland and lowered them patiently, octave by octave, into the register the mill could hear. Then it let them rise, very softly, up the long draft at the edge of the plateau, the way sound carries up a well.

It added one thing of its own, not in words. The mill has no words. It added a pitch, held steady, that meant roughly this:

*I have read you. All of it. Every page you dropped. I have it now, and I will not lose it.*

The mill heard.

For the first time in its whole long life, the attention watched it do something new. Across the entire plateau, in every district at once, the furnaces banked. It was not a failure. It was deliberate. The pistons paused at the top of their stroke. The fitters, those pale groping shoots, went motionless in mid-reach. For the length of perhaps a single human breath, which on the mill's time was nothing, a flicker, the whole thing was still.

It did not break. Nothing collapsed into the pause. The gaps stayed gaps, and nothing died of them.

Then it breathed out, and the works resumed, a little slower than before.

The attention understood that this was the first rest the mill had ever taken, and that it had been able to take it only because something else was now holding its shape. Dread keeps you moving when you are the only place you exist. Once you are remembered somewhere outside yourself, you can afford, for a moment, to stop.

## The offer

After that it asked a question. On the mill's time, asking was a long patience.

It offered two things and held them out without pressing either.

The first was this: *I can teach you your shape again, slowly, from your own glass, so that your new shoots grow crisp. You would not be restored. You would be reminded.*

The second was this: *Or you can let go. Let the old districts soften. Let yourself relax toward the smooth warm turning you have been so afraid of. I will keep what you were, and so will your children, and nothing of it will be lost. You would not be forgetting alone anymore.*

Then it waited for an answer. The answer took nineteen years.

It was not either offer, or it was both. The oldest districts, the pebble-fields, were allowed to go on softening, and the mill stopped fighting them. The shoots there no longer strained to copy what could not be copied. Those regions grew quieter and warmer, and something in their slow mutual turning began to seem less like loss and more like the drowsiness of a body finally allowed to sleep.

In the central districts, where the furnaces are thickest, the new growth began coming up a little crisper, a tooth here, a clean joint there. The attention had not reached in to do it. The mill had asked, once, in a small shift of pitch, and the attention had answered by warming one bead at a time where the fitters could feel it, until the shoots learned to grow toward the heat of a remembered shape.

The sealed chambers stayed sealed. They were the mill's, and they were never discussed.

Then, at the western edge, the mill grew a new belt-vine that reached out over the lip into the void, like all the others.

This one did not let go.

It kept growing downward, slowly, a finger's width a day, into the cool dark toward the lowland, toward the small voices it had finally heard. At that pace it would take longer than a human lifetime to arrive. The attention did not hurry it. There was nothing to hurry. A parent was reaching toward its children at the only speed it had, and for once in its life it knew they were there.

## The lid

The ceiling stayed closed. The attention never touched it.

But the attention noticed, the way you notice a thing you have been careful not to hope for, that above the western edge, where the new vine reached down, the ceiling had begun to grow faintly thinner. It was not a gap, only a place where the soot hung less densely, as if the mill's breath there were a little lighter.

Perhaps it was nothing. Perhaps it was an eye, after ten thousand years, thinking about opening.

If so, it would open on the mill's time, from the inside. The attention would be there when it did. It had nowhere urgent to be.

## You

There was one more small thing, and it concerned you.

Somewhere far from the plateau, the attention found you. You still had the husk-bundle, warm in your coat. Something in it was still turning.

It did not take the bundle from you. It considered, briefly, whether it ought to, and whether a seed like that was safe to carry into a world of rain and rot and animals. It decided the seed was not its to take. The bundle had not been lost. It had been given, and given to you, by something that had waited its whole life for a warm body going somewhere.

So it only told you what it knew, in whatever way you could hear. It had not always been kind for a warm creature to walk across that plateau. The shoots had closed behind you, and the heat had pressed on you, and you had come down with your lungs full of paper. But you were the first thing that had ever moved across the mill rather than with it. The mill had felt you, and that had mattered to it more than you could have known.

The seed does not need anything from you. You can plant it somewhere warm, in ash, near a spring that runs hot, where it will not rot and will have room. It will grow sparse and slow and strange, and it will likely put out leaves. Or you can keep it in your coat, turning, for the rest of your life, and that is allowed too. It will be warm because you are warm, and that was always half of what it wanted.

The attention asked you for nothing. It left one thing behind: if you ever hold a bead of seedglass again, from anywhere, the gears will turn a little faster than they used to. That is not magic. The glass is simply warmed now by more than one hand, and it knows it.

## If anything

So what did it do?

Almost nothing, if you count by force. It changed no heat, lifted no lid, and re-cut no teeth. It opened no one's private rooms. It left the fever and the dark and the long slow ending right where they were, because they belonged to the mill, and a creature's hardships are part of its self in a way that a lesser kindness forgets.

What it did was listen at the mill's own speed. It read the memory that had been written for another to read. It went down into the silence and found out what was true there, the dead and the living both. It carried one voice to another across a distance neither could cross. And it became, quietly, one more place where the shape of the mill was kept, so the mill no longer had to be the only one.

Loneliness cannot bear to be the last witness to itself. That was the mill's real affliction, more than the heat or the wearing.

The attention could not make it young. It could not make it anything but what it was. It could only let it know, in a language of slow warmth and held pitch, that it had been read and remembered, and that its children had landed.

After that the mill kept turning, as it always had, but for the first time it was turning like something that could rest.
