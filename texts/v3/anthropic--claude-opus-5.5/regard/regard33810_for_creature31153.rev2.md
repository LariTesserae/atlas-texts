# The Attention

## How It Noticed

It did not see Fennick. Nothing sees Fennick; there is nothing to see it by. It noticed the way the bulb itself notices things: through the ground, slowly, as a change in what was soaking down.

For a long time, the wet country had given the bulb only rain, and the leachings of a churchyard upslope, and once, a woman in her good coat. Then, over several seasons, it had begun to give it grief. Visitors had found the seam in the south face and come in carrying things. The ground around the mound had grown heavy with it, the way soil grows heavy under a place where people often kneel. The attention that turned toward the basin was the kind that turns toward heaviness. It is drawn to anywhere a great deal is being held.

It came as far as the rim of the basin and stopped there for some time. A power of that kind is larger on the inside than on the outside too, and it had to consider how to enter a place without filling it.

In the end it came in the way everyone comes in. It used the door.

## The First Thing It Did

The first thing it did was nothing. It stood just inside the seam, where the air cools a degree or two, and waited for its eyes to adjust.

This took a long time. It could have seen at once, of course. It could have seen the whole hollow in a single instant: every seed-cobble, every beetle between every cellulose layer, the exact curvature of the unseen dome. It chose not to. A place can be known in two ways. It can be known the way a surveyor knows it, or the way someone who lives there knows it. Only the second kind of knowing tells you what the place is for. So it stood in the dark with the eleven-second pulse coming up through its heels, then its shins, until the lichen lines resolved one by one into streets.

Then it walked the three streets, and it kept to the pavements.

## What It Could Have Done

It is worth setting down what was in its reach, because what it chose not to do matters as much as what it did.

It could have lit Fennick. One thought, and the hollow would have filled with a clean, warm light like a June morning. The parchment walls would have glowed with their veining, and the whole unfinished town would have stood revealed, sharp-cornered and beautiful. It considered this for the length of one pulse. Then it went and stood in the pool beneath the six lamps. A shoulder in wool brushed its own, and it understood that light would end them. The townsfolk exist only in the medium of a remembered room with the curtains drawn. To light Fennick would be to open every curtain at once on people who had never been anything but the space where people were. In that place, light would be a kind of fire.

It could have restored them. It knew, as such powers know things, the real names of the streets. It knew what was actually written on the corner signs of the actual village, which really had existed, upslope and upstream and long ago. It knew how many buttons the woman's coat had really had. It knew the word that had once come at the end of the tall man's cough. It could have put every detail back, and for a moment it wanted to, badly. Loss was plainly happening here, and loss is the thing such a power is most tempted to undo.

But it stood among them a while longer, and it saw what the thing it held would amount to. The restored townsfolk would not be hers. They would be its own: accurate, complete, and belonging to no one. The bulb's version was wrong in exactly the ways a person's memory is wrong. *Colliery Row* was drifting to *Calloway* because that was how the name sat in an old woman's mouth near the end. The error was the fingerprint. To correct it would be to swap her village for a better-documented one. That would be the most perfect forgery ever made, and it would still be a forgery.

And the thinning was not a fault in the system. The thinning was what the system was doing. She was forgetting them as she arrived. People do that at the end, and people do it at the beginning too. It did not seem right to stop a woman halfway through her own becoming. It would not refill a cup she was in the middle of drinking.

It could have unmade the whole thing. It could have closed the seam, withered the bulb, and returned the basin to a simple wet field. Some would argue it should have, because the bulb drinks the living, a little. That argument deserved an answer and not a dismissal, so it kept the question open and went looking for one.

## What It Asked

It went to the beetles first, because the lowest-ranked inhabitants of a place are the ones whose welfare is most easily assumed.

It walked beside one down the long straight of the high street, each of them alone in their own stretch of dark. It lowered its attention to the size of a hand and asked, in the only way a beetle can be asked, whether this was a good life.

The answer, as far as a beetle has answers, was that it was a life. The beetle had a wall to sleep in, shoulder to shoulder with its kind, warm from the bulb's slow metabolism. It had a path and a burden and a pulse to keep time by. It felt a faint sweetness when it passed the sap-drips in the factory. When it died, it would lie on its back on the seed-cobbles and the lichen would take it into the street. The power had seen far worse arrangements among creatures that believed themselves free. It left the beetles alone.

It went to the snails next, and it stayed with one for most of a season.

This was the hardest discipline of its visit. Nothing is more difficult for a power than moving at the speed of a snail. It lay down in the street beside the creature and let the days go over it. Once it had slowed enough, it could see what the snail saw as its eyestalks swept each house: the drafts, the corrections, the patient editorial work of eating what was wrong so the bulb could try again.

One evening the snail stopped at a not-yet-house on the edge of town. In the lichen-light the house had a doorway with a lintel of extraordinary delicacy, a lacework of lignin fine as frost. The power had privately thought it the most beautiful thing in Fennick. The snail considered it for three days and then ate the lintel.

A week later, the bulb regrew the house four doors down. The new lintel was plain, a single straight rib. And the power saw at once that it was right. The lace had been the bulb's invention. The plain rib was hers. Somewhere in her there was a doorway she had walked under ten thousand times, and it had never been beautiful. It had just been a doorway, and it had been home.

The power understood then that it was not the only assessor in Fennick. It had arrived to find the work already under proper supervision.

## The Moths

It did not like what happened to the moths. It said so, to no one, in the dark on the square.

It followed one in. It went slowly, with its attention made small and soft, alongside a single pale moth spiraling down into the nothing beneath the pods. It felt the moth become nap, and lint, and the fine dust of a worn cuff on a coat that had been buttoned against a hundred winters.

It could not tell whether the moth suffered. It looked for the shape of fear and could not find it. What it found was the shape of arrival: a small wanting toward the dark, and then the dark. That is the shape of every moth's life at every lamp in the world. Most moths reach the flame and are destroyed by it. These reached their flame and were made into a sleeve that a remembered woman had once smoothed with her palm before going out.

The power did not decide that this was good. It decided it did not know enough to call it bad, and that ignorance is not grounds for intervention. It grieved a little, which is allowed, and kept watching the moths for the rest of its stay. It would turn out to be right to watch them.

## The New Streets

Then it went west, where the three-minute walk takes twenty, to see about the living.

It walked the lane that ends at the seafront railing. It stood a while at the rail, above the lichen tideline, facing a sea that is not there, and it knew whose sea it was. Years ago a woman had come in through the seam carrying a promenade in her chest, the last place she had walked with someone before the walking stopped. She had stood in the dark until the weight of it was drunk out of her. Then she had left. She had gone home lighter and never known why, and never come back, and was well. The railing was what the bulb had made of what she put down. The power found no harm in it. That was what the railing was for.

Then it went to the house with the thatch.

The wooden horse still stood in the center of the empty room, on its wheels, with its string. It was still the most detailed object in Fennick. It was detailed because it was being renewed. Someone kept coming back and giving it again.

He was there. The power had known he would be. He stood in the corner of the room with his back to the wall, a man in an ordinary rain jacket, and his breath had fallen into step with the pulse: one breath for every *thum*, then one for every two. His skin had the drawn, cold-washed tightness. When the power came in and stood beside him in the dark, he did not startle. He had been here long enough that presences no longer startled him.

It did not tell him to leave. It had considered that, and had seen that "leave" is a word a power says when it has already decided what is good for someone. It asked him, instead, why he came.

He said it was the only place he could put it down. At home it was everywhere. Here it was in one room, on the floor, on wheels. Here he could look at it.

The power told him the truth, because the truth was the one thing it had brought that the place did not already have. It told him the bulb would keep the horse. It would keep it the way it keeps everything: it would simplify it, a little each time the snails passed, until the wheels were discs and the string was a line. Then the horse would be spent, along with everything else, on the flowering. That was how keeping worked here. It was not a museum. It was a store laid down against a spring. And it told him the rest. If he stayed, the bulb would keep him the same way. The word forming on the corner sign of the new street was not the child's name. It was his.

He was quiet for a long time. The horse stood in the dark between them.

"Will it hurt him," he said at last, "being simplified?"

"No," the power said. "It will be the way you remember him in thirty years. Less exact. Not less loved. The exactness is the part that hurts you. It won't hurt him."

He left that week. He came back twice more that season, each time for a shorter while, and each time he stayed near the door. The second word on the sign stopped changing from day to day and settled into something almost legible. It stayed there, half-formed, like a name on a gravestone that the rain has begun on. The power did not read it. Some things are held privately, even by the ground.

## The One Thing It Gave

This is what it did, in the end: it gave the bulb one memory of its own.

It could not simply make a thing in Fennick. It had decided not to, because anything it made would be in its own idiom, and Fennick has its own. But the bulb drinks. That is its law. So the power went back out through the seam into the ordinary grey rain and knelt on the wet ground at the foot of the mound, where visitors had knelt before it. It let something soak down.

It chose carefully. It did not give light, and it did not give truth or names. It gave the memory of a lamp left burning in a porch at night for someone expected home late, the kind of lamp that is not for seeing by but for being seen, so that whoever is out on the dark road knows which door is theirs.

The bulb drank it and rendered it, imperfectly, as it renders everything.

Just inside the seam in the south face, where the lichen lines begin to thin, there is now a short stalk like a streetlamp. Its pod gives off not darkness but a very small amount of warm, yellowish light, about as much as a candle behind a curtain. It lights no street. It reaches perhaps an arm's length. It is too faint and too far from the square to trouble the townsfolk. But anyone who has stood too long in the dark and felt their breath slow to one for every two pulses will, on turning around, see it. They will see which way the door is, and see their own hand by it, and remember that they have one.

The snails assessed it. It took them most of a season. They left it alone, as they left the bay window.

## The Chapel

For the rest of its stay, the power sat with the snails against the chapel wall.

It did not open the bud. The thought never became a temptation. You do not pry open a flower to see what it will be. Opening it would not reveal anything. It would only end the thing.

It listened. The hymn went on, rising and falling across its span of hours: an old woman singing to herself with her hands busy. Most of the words were gone. The power knew the hymn. It knows all of them. It could have supplied every missing verse. It sat with that knowledge for a long time, and then it did something smaller. It hummed the tune, only the tune, which she still had, very low, under hers. It was not correcting her or completing her. It was keeping her company, the way you hum along in a kitchen with someone who is singing to herself, so she knows the house is not empty.

Out on the square, in the pool beneath the six lamps, the child who circles at knee height began to hum too, the sped-up version, heard once through a window and carried home.

## Spring

The power stayed for the flowering. It had decided early that it would. A thing that has been arriving for a very long time should have someone there when it arrives.

By then the townsfolk were almost gone. The cough had become only an intake of breath. The pair whose sleeves always touched had become just the touching. The woman with the buttons had one button left. The factory had slowed. The flywheel turned once in a quarter of an hour, and the bales came fewer and lighter. The lamps on the square were dimming toward ordinary dark, because there was less and less that needed holding.

The sheath split down the length of the steeple in a single night, with a sound like a sheet being torn slowly in another room.

What stood in the chapel doorway was a flower. It would be wrong to say it was a woman, and wrong to say it was not. It was pale, the pewter of the leaves outside, and the height of an old woman. It was bowed at the top the way a person bows over a sink, and its petals hung like the sleeves of a good coat. It did not walk. It had nowhere to walk to. It turned very slightly toward the square, where no one was.

The power was there. It considered stepping into the empty pool of the square to stand where the townsfolk had stood, so that the flower, turning, would find someone. It did not do it, because it was not them, and it would not pretend to be. It stood at the edge of the square, in plain view of whatever a flower sees with, as what it was: a stranger who had come to listen and had stayed.

Then the moths came.

They came from everywhere in the hollow, from every corner of the dark, out of the lapels and cuffs of the last townsfolk as their wool finally let go. They were pale and wide-winged and silent, in their hundreds, and they did not go to the lamps. They went to the flower. They spiraled down its petals and into its throat and out again, dusted, and the power understood what it had not understood before. The moths had never been only material. They were the bulb's own. They had been kept in the coats the way a gardener keeps seed potatoes in a paper sack in a cold shed, against the one night they would be needed. The system had its answer all along. It had simply been waiting for its season.

The power had done nothing, and nothing had been needed. It was glad of that, the way a doctor is glad to arrive and find the fever breaking by itself.

The flower was open for most of one pulse-rhythm day, the length of a working shift. The hymn came from it the whole time, and it was not a congregation, or an organ, or even an old woman. It was simply a voice. Near the end, for the first time, it had words. There was only one word, and the flower sang it over and over, in the same careful hand as every corner sign.

Then the petals dropped, the throat closed, and the long work of making seed began.

## After

The seed of the bulb is hard and ovoid, and it looks exactly like the cobbles of Fennick and the buttons on the woman's coat. It always did. The streets were paved with the intention of this.

The power did not carry the seed anywhere. It thought about the churchyard upslope, and the churchyards in every wet country, full of people laid down in their good coats whom no one visits. It thought about the good a bulb might do growing quietly beneath each one. Then it declined to decide where grief should be kept. The seeds fell where they fell, the beetles did what the bulb's hands do, and some of them may reach the soil outside, and some of them may not.

The hollow will shrink now, as a bulb does after flowering, spent. The mound in the basin will stay the size of a barn. What is inside may be much smaller for a while, or nothing, or a single street. Then perhaps, in some other spring, it will be another village. It will be somebody's version of somewhere, wrong in all the loving ways.

The power went out through the seam into the ordinary rain. The small warm lamp was still burning just inside, and it is burning still. It is Fennick's own now, the bulb's own rendering. It will be simplified in time, like everything, down to a stalk and a glow. That is correct. That is how it will be kept.

The power carries away one thing it did not have when it came. It knew, of course, as such powers know, what the village was really called, and what her name really was. It had known from the first pulse. It never said either aloud. It keeps them the way a bulb keeps things: underground, patient, swelling a fraction larger once or twice a day, laid down against a spring it is certain will come.

## If You Go

If you go to the basin now, the seam is still open. Walk in a little way, turn around, and you will see the light.

Put your palm flat against the skin beneath the pewter leaves and wait eleven seconds. You will feel the pulse, slower now, the long slow beat of something resting after a great effort.

And if you wait a little longer, you will feel something that is neither the bulb's pulse nor your own. It is a faint warmth, like the feeling of coming up a dark road late and seeing that somebody, knowing you were out there, has left a light on.
