# The Audience

## How it would come

Not through the cloud. Anything that came up through the grey fleece would arrive making noise, the way everything here does, and some of that noise would fall as stone and join the sentence. A wise power would understand this before it arrived. It would know that in the Ringing Heights, even coming to visit is a way of speaking.

So it would come the way attention comes, without a sound. And for a long time it would do what any good guest does in a house where the family is talking: it would listen.

It would listen for what, to it, is a short while, and to the Heights is about three years. It would hear the eleven-second Strike of Harrowcap and the looser, older tempo of Coldfang. It would hear the bellowers moaning across the valleys and the goats answering them in bronze. It would hear the drumming of moths on lit glass, the tin-roof patter of clicklings mending the fissures, and the tap-talk running through the girders. It would learn to read that one too, and blush a little at the bedframes. It would hear the funerals, the whole spire shouting a single name until the dead were sealed in it. It would hear the lullabies coming back out of the plaster.

And it would hear the one thing no one in the city can hear, because they are all inside it. Taken together, from far enough away, the Heights are saying something. They have been saying it for nine hundred years, and they are not done.

## What it would want to do

A benevolent power feels the pull to help the way water feels the pull downhill, and it would feel that pull here, strongly. There is a great deal that looks like it needs fixing.

It could make the coal endless. It could loosen the rule so that silence stopped eroding, and no spire would ever again melt out from under its own iron. It could restore the old people's hearing, so that Oda Venn heard her grandchildren playing on the four feet of stone her voice had made. It could still the drift of the spires, so that Hesk Ammory's lines held true and he could finally sleep. It could go up to the black summit and look at the last word and simply *say* it, and end the long wait kindly.

It would do none of these things. The reasons are worth setting down, because the refusal is not coldness.

**The hammers are how they talk.** Making the coal endless would turn the Strike from a choice into a machine. Every spire in the Heights is loud because its people have decided, again every day, that there is still something to say. A foundry that falls quiet is sad, but it is also honest. It is a district that has said what it had to say, or run out of the will to say it. Taking away the possibility of ending would empty every continuing of its meaning.

**Silence has to erode, because the erosion feeds the murmurers,** and the murmurers carry the sound somewhere else: backward, or upward, into the early echoes and the not-yet voices. The power, listening, would see the whole loop. The dead mountain gives back its voice. The murmurers hold it. It arrives again as the echo before the shout. The city is not only speaking forward. It is speaking to itself across its own length, the end whispering to the beginning. Freezing the erosion would cut the sentence off from its own memory.

**The deafness is theirs.** It came from a life lived beside the hammer, and they have built a whole second language, tap, gentler and more intimate than speech, out of what it cost them. The power would not take away a wound that a people had already turned into a way of loving each other.

And the last word is not the power's to say. This would be the clearest thing of all. Whatever is waiting in that room of black silence is the end of *their* sentence. It will be shaped by every lullaby and funeral-shout and dawn whistle still to come. If anyone else spoke it, however kindly, the Heights would stop being an utterance and become a thing that was said *to*. Nine hundred years of voices would end in someone else's mouth.

So the wisdom here is mostly restraint. The power would understand that it has come upon something rare: a people who are, literally, the ground they stand on, making themselves out of their own voices. The most important thing is that they finish it themselves.

## What it would notice

But it would not only refrain. Listening that long, it would begin to hear what lies underneath the noise.

The city believes it fears silence. The heresy of the Stilled believes the hammers are a refusal to let something finish. Oda Venn believes you keep talking as long as there is something left to say. All of them are partly right. But the power, listening to the whole of it at once, would hear something none of them has named.

The Heights are not afraid of ending. They are afraid of ending *unheard*.

Their whole world teaches them that sound only matters if it lands. A shout becomes a grain. A life becomes a room. A crowd saying goodbye becomes the sturdiest ground there is. In the Heights, you exist to the degree that your voice has fallen somewhere and stayed. So the one unbearable thought, deeper than silence, is a sentence spoken for nine hundred years into air with nothing on the far side of it. It is a last word said into a room with no one in it.

That is what the people standing in the summit feel, and they cannot place it. *Regarded,* they say. *Like an audience in the moment after the music stops.*

The power, standing there itself at last, would understand something about that feeling. It might be the power's own doing. It might not. Perhaps the summit has always had the shape of a listener, and the power has simply arrived to fill it. The power would not be sure, and it would not need to be. Either way, it would know what to do.

## What it would do

Small things, mostly. In the Heights there is only one way to give something that does not go into the mountain, and the power would use it.

It would whisper.

A whisper here leaves almost no stone. It is the one gift the culture allows that does not become part of the common rock. Lovers use it, and mothers leaning over very sick children. Anything the power said that way would not change the sentence. It would only keep someone company inside it.

**To Pim,** at dawn on the eastern gallery, between one note and the next, it would say only: *I heard.* It would mean the thing he never told anyone, that the bridge sounds sad. It would not tell him the bridge was wrong to sound sad, or that he was wrong to hear it. Someone had heard it, and that would be all. He would not know where it came from. He would carry it the way he carries his first-grain, a small thing that belongs only to him.

**To Oda Venn,** it would say nothing at all. It would listen to her roar, *a sentence isn't finished because you're afraid of the end of it*, and recognise that she needs no help. She is already right. A wise power knows the difference between people who are struggling and people who are simply living hard, and leaves the second kind alone. At most, on the night she finally dies in her cot beside the hammer, it would make sure that the last thing she feels through her palms is the Strike. It would not arrange a miracle. It would only see that her death came on the beat rather than between beats.

**To Tamsin Lark,** nothing either, about the stranger who said her name. That voice has not happened yet, and telling her who it belongs to would be the power speaking a word that belongs to the future. But it might sit with her in the dark under the Old Chapel district, one night, while she listens. She would feel, without knowing why, that she was not the only one listening. For a woman who spends her life as the quiet person a loud city barely tolerates, that might be enough.

**To Hesk Ammory,** who fears he is looking at a mouth, it would give one clear night. The smoke would thin and the cloud-sea would drop lower than it ever does, and from the highest survey platform he would see farther than he has ever seen. He would see the whole drifting arrangement of the spires at once, and he would see what the power had heard. The mouth is open, yes. But it is not open like something about to swallow. It is open the way a mouth opens in the middle of a song. He would write nothing down that night. He would sleep better afterward, and not understand why.

**To the murmurers,** it would hum. It would coil in the grey dust with them at the foot of a dead spire, among the long wet contented voices, and hum back. It would be the first time in their existence that anything had answered them. The city's children would go on being told the murmurers are scavengers. The power would not correct that, because Tamsin is going to discover the truth herself one day, and some discoveries should be earned. But the murmurers would know that someone understood what they were doing.

**Below the cloud,** where the coal comes from and no one in the Heights has been in living memory, it would go and look. It would do this because a benevolent power does not let a whole unseen people stay unseen just because the story was not about them. What it found there is another story. It would make sure that whatever is down there is not suffering to keep the hammers fed. If it were, that would be the one place it would act without hesitating, because a sentence built on someone else's silenced labour is not a sentence worth finishing. If what is down there is well, and simply sends the coal up into the cloud because it loves the distant thunder, then the power would let that be one more listener the Heights did not know they had.

**And once,** when a cable snaps, as cables here sometimes do, the power would hold the gondola. It would do it quietly, and no one would ever know. Not everything in the Heights is meaning. Some things are only rust and bad luck. A wise power does not mistake an accident for a part of the story. A family falling into the cloud-sea on the way home from a wedding is not a word in the sentence. It is just a family falling, and it would catch them.

## What it would become

Then, having done these few small things, it would go back up.

It would take its place in the room of silence at the top of the newest stone in the world, the pause before the last word. It would stay there. Not to guard the silence, and not to hurry it or hold it back, but to be the thing the silence is shaped like. It would be an audience.

It would make no promises, because promises are speech, and speech here becomes stone. It would simply be there. The Stilled would come up and feel regarded, and now they would be right. Pim would come up again someday, grown, and stand in it for a long time, and come down whistling something new.

The power would never answer the question in Pim's note, the rising one that expects an answer. That answer belongs to the city. It is somewhere in the black rock, waiting to be earned by every voice still to come. But the power would make sure the question was heard. Of everything it could give, that is the one gift the Heights have never had and have always most needed. Some part of the whole city's nine hundred years of shouting has only ever been one long question: *Is anyone there?*

The hammers keep falling, eleven seconds apart. The swifts keep calling. The bridge grows a little darker where Pim's note falls. Far below, the murmurers hum, and now something hums back.

And at the top, in the silence, something waits for the last word. It is not waiting to judge it, or to finish it, or to stop it. It is waiting to hear it, so that when the Ringing Heights finally say what they have been saying all this time, it will not fall into empty air.

When the last word is spoken, someone will be listening. That is all the power would do. It might be the only thing that was ever needed.
