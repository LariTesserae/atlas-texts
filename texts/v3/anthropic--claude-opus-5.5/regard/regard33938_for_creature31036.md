# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not arrive. Arrival would have been a kind of violence here, where nothing hurries and the only danger ever known has been cold coming slowly. Anything that came fast would have been a stranger, and strangers get answered slowly or not at all.

So it came the way the sinter comes. It came the way moss takes a terrace, or the way a coat of algae shifts from emerald to orange when its wearer moves closer to the roll of the water. For a long while nothing on the Kettle Tableland could have said it was there. The hawk hunted. The swifts poured into the cliff at dusk. Somewhere in the groves a slow dark body hauled itself out of a spring and walked off steaming into the ferns. If anything was different, it was only that the place was being *attended to*, completely, the way you might attend to the breathing of someone asleep beside you.

It would be easy to call the attention a god, and not quite right. It had no throne and wanted none. It was benevolent the way warmth is benevolent: it asked nothing, and it went toward whatever was cold. It was wise mainly because it was patient enough to find out what it did not know before it did anything at all.

What it did first was listen.

## What It Heard

It lay down against the basalt, and the basalt carried everything.

It heard the vents clear their throats and the springs knock in their deep channels. It heard the long subsonic songs passing from chest to chest across kilometres of stone. It took several days to hear a single one through, and it did not skip ahead. It heard the song of which springs were hot in the time of the grandmothers' grandmothers, and the song of where the hawk nests. It heard the name-song of the stopped elders, a list so long that singing it takes a whole season, and at the end the singer begins again.

It heard the elders on the domes breathe their once-a-year breath. It understood that they were not dying, and that when at last they were, that would not be a wrong to be corrected. A people who become hills have made their peace with a certain kind of ending. To "save" the elders from the sinter would be like saving a river from the sea. The attention let that alone. This was the first thing it decided not to do, and it was the easiest.

Then it heard the western rim.

It heard the old ones pressed to the cliff-top, dark-coated, sending news down into the rock. *This spring has cooled. A child was laid in the cups on the eastern flats. Here is the name of the one who stopped last winter. Here is the song of the grandmothers.* Under all of it, patient and faithful and utterly deaf, came the thud, and the pause, and the thud.

The attention stayed there a long time. It was not uncertain about what it felt. It was uncertain about what to do. What it felt was simple, and it was grief, a little. Decades of tenderness had been poured into a machine. A people who had believed themselves alone had decided, when they learned they were not, to *keep the other company*. The other did not know they existed.

## What It Considered

A power like that can do nearly anything. That is exactly why it has to think.

**It could make the hammer answer.** A small adjustment to the stone would do it: a shaped echo, a false reply. The rim-watchers would feel the Deep One finally speak, and their joy would pass across the whole tableland in a week of songs. It would also be a lie. It would be a puppet show performed for people who had earned something real, and every song they sang about it afterward would be built on the lie. The attention set this aside quickly and felt a little ashamed for having thought of it.

**It could scour the soot away.** It could wash the western moss back to lime and bottle-green and clear the dark bands out of the pools. It looked closely at this option, because the soot was the one thing here that might be doing harm. So it examined the soot the way it had examined everything else: in the fur of the rim-watchers, in the grooves of the hollow hairs, in the blood of the darker children. It found carbon, mostly, and some sulphur, and in the deepest bands of the terraces a thin line of something heavier, a metal that did not belong. It was not enough, yet, to sicken anyone. It was enough to be counted, and to go on accumulating.

Still, scrubbing the tableland clean would change nothing. The furnaces would keep breathing, and next year's storms would bring the grey back. There was also the black moth, which had become itself within living memory, and which was now as alive and as native as its pale cousin. The attention would not erase a living thing to restore a picture of how things used to be. Cleaning up after the plain would treat the symptom and hide the cause. Worse, it would hide the tableland from the plain for good.

**It could lift the western watchers away**, carry them east to clean water, and let the rim fall quiet. But they had gone to the rim on purpose. Their devotion to that voice was theirs. Nobody wise rescues someone from their own chosen faithfulness without asking, and they could not be asked. They had no way to understand the question.

**It could stop the furnaces.** It could stop them tonight. The glow under the cloud deck would go dark, the orange would fade, and the thud would fall silent.

The attention imagined that silence as the rim-watchers would feel it. Decades of the One Who Cannot Stop, and then, one night, nothing. To them it would not be relief. It would be death. The only voice that had ever come to them from outside would have fallen still, like an elder, and they would lie on the cliff singing the name-song into dead rock for the rest of their long lives. And they had no name for it.

There was a second reason, and it mattered as much. The attention was not the tableland's alone. Benevolence that takes sides is only preference. Somewhere under the cloud deck there were people too, and the furnaces were theirs. Those people had also, in their own way, borrowed fire. They could not make enough heat in their own bodies to get through a winter, so they burned what lay under the ground. The young ones on the tableland had a word for the place under the cloud: *where the warmth goes when it leaves here*. They were more right than they knew.

So the attention did not stop the furnaces. Instead, for the first time, it went down.

## Beneath

The cloud deck from below is only a ceiling, low and grey and permanent. The people of the plain have never seen the tableland. To them the cliffs are the end of the world. They are a dark wall that swallows the western sky, with the top always lost in weather. Children are told there is nothing up there. Mothers say it is too high to breathe, too steep to climb, too wet to live on.

There is a town, and a foundry, and a drop-forge. It runs through the night because the contract requires it, and the contract requires it because the people are poor and the work is what there is. The hammer is regular because it must be. A drop-hammer that varies spoils the steel.

At the edge of town, near the old mine workings, stands a small brick building with a seismograph in it. It was put there eighty years ago to warn of collapses in the tunnels. It has a drum, a stylus, and a roll of paper, and it has been recording, mostly unread, for all that time. Its keeper was an old man who died. Now the building belongs to his granddaughter, a young woman who took the job because nobody else wanted it. She changes the paper on Tuesdays.

The attention did not make anything new. It did not whisper in her ear or send her a dream. It did the smallest thing it could think of: it made sure that on one particular Tuesday she was not in a hurry.

So for once she looked at the paper instead of just rolling it up. She saw the hammer, of course. It fills every roll from dusk to dawn with a heartbeat of ticks you could set a clock by. She had seen that a hundred times. But underneath the hammer, fainter, was something else. It was a long, low wave, rising and falling over hours. Her grandfather had penciled "mountain noise" beside it on rolls from forty years ago.

She pulled out the old rolls. She laid them side by side on the floor of the brick building, then on the floor of her kitchen, then on the floor of the church hall, because there were too many of them for anywhere else. She was not a scientist, but she was stubborn. After three months she saw what her grandfather had not had the time or the reason to see.

The mountain noise repeated.

It did not repeat like the hammer. It repeated the way a song does, with variations, returning. Some patterns ran for days and then started again from the beginning. One very long pattern took a whole season, and when it ended it began again, and she noticed it was growing longer. Each year it held a little more than the year before.

It was the name-song. She did not know that. She did not know that the thing getting longer was a list of the dead.

What she did know was that the faint waves always came strongest on still nights, and that they came from the west, from the cliffs. She knew they were strongest in the hours right after the hammer started, as if something up there were *waiting* for it. And one night she was sitting in the brick building at two in the morning when the shift whistle blew at the foundry. On the drum she watched a tiny, high, thin trace appear a few seconds later. It lay over the long low waves like a child's voice over a choir.

She sat with her hand flat on the cold desk for a long time.

## The Reply

She took the rolls to the foreman. He laughed. She took them to the foundry's owner, who did not laugh but did not listen either. She took them to the church hall and unrolled them in front of anyone who would come. Most people went home unconvinced, but a few did not. One of them was an old hammer operator named Tomas, who had worked the night shift for thirty-one years. He said nothing at the meeting. Then he walked to the brick building with her and looked at the drum for an hour.

"They've been listening to me," he said at last. "All this time."

"Talking to you," she said. "I think they've been talking to you."

The attention had done one small thing: a Tuesday without hurry. It had not written the rest, and it watched now with something like held breath, because what happened next belonged to them.

The hammer could not change its rhythm. That would spoil the steel, and the contract would not allow it. But every shift ends. And at the end of his shift, the night after he saw the drum, Tomas did not simply stop. He let the hammer fall, then paused. He held the pause for one beat longer than the machine allowed, long enough to set off the warning bell. Then he let it fall once more, and stopped.

Nobody below understood why he did it. On the tableland, it was the first thing the Deep One had ever done that was not the same.

An old one lay on the western rim with her chest to the stone, as she did every night. She had just finished sending down the news of the eastern cups. She felt the thud, and the pause. Then she felt the pause go on. She felt a whole beat of silence, where for her entire life there had never been silence. Then one more thud, and then nothing.

She did not move for a very long time.

In the morning she sang it across the tableland: *The One Who Cannot Stop has stopped, and then it spoke once more, and then it rested.* No one knew what it meant. It passed from chest to chest for a week. It reached the springs, the groves, and the cups on the eastern flats. It even reached, very faintly, the oldest of the sleeping domes. The moss on its back lifted once, off its yearly schedule, and settled again.

The next night the hammer came back as it always had. The rim-watchers wept, if their kind weep, which the attention was not sure of. They had not been abandoned.

At the end of that shift, the long pause came again.

## What Followed

It went slowly after that, which was right. Both peoples were slow, in their different ways. One was slow because nothing had ever hurried them. The other was slow because they were tired, and poor, and busy, and there was always the contract.

The young woman kept the drum running and began, clumsily, to learn the songs. She never learned what they meant. But she learned their shapes. She learned which pattern came every spring and which came only after the long pause at shift's end. She learned the one that she decided, privately, must mean *hello*. She was probably wrong about that. It was actually the song of where the hawk nests. But it did not matter much.

Tomas taught the next operator the pause. And that operator taught the one after him.

The town argued, as towns do, about the smoke. It had never mattered before, because the smoke went up and away into nothing. Now there was a drum in a brick building showing that the nothing had someone in it. That did not make the scrubbers cheap. It did not make the contract kinder or the owner wiser. But it gave the argument somewhere to stand. It took eleven years to install the first filter on the west stack. It was not a good filter. The second, nine years later, was better.

On the tableland the moss on the western rim stayed dark for a long time. It did not change back all at once, but the bands in the sinter pools began to grow paler, pale and dark, pale and paler, a record anyone could read if they knew how. The thin line of heavy metal stopped thickening. The black moth remained, because it was a moth now, not a mistake. But the pale one began, very slowly, to drift back west.

The rim-watchers never learned what the Deep One was. They never would. They could not climb down, and the people below could not climb up, and the cloud deck lay between them like a sheet of milk. The attention did not try to fix that. Some distances are just the shape of the world. People can be kind across them without closing them.

## What It Kept

Only once did the attention do something on the tableland itself, and it was nearly invisible.

It lay down with the elders on the domes. It listened to the name-song through, all of it, every season, and it remembered. It did not change the song or improve it. It simply held it, the way the basalt holds heat, so that if a spring ever went cold, or a hard winter came, or the last old singer on the western rim stopped, the song would not be lost. It was a small thing. It was the kind of thing a wise power does when it has decided that most of what it could do would be worse than doing nothing. It keeps what is precious, stands ready, and otherwise stays out of the way.

Then it went, slowly, the way it had come. The hawk hunted. The frogs called at dusk. A mother laid a newborn in the cup of a worn column, in water at exactly the heat of blood, and left it there through the first cold night.

## Morning

In the morning the hollows fill with cloud again. The domes rise out of the white, and not all of them are stone.

At the western rim, an old one with a coat going slowly from grey-black toward green lowers her chest to the stone and sends down the news: *The eastern spring is warm again. A child was laid in the cups. Here is the name of the one who stopped.*

Far below, in a brick building at the edge of a town, a stylus trembles on a turning drum. A young woman who is not so young anymore lays her palm flat on the desk. It is not warm, but it is steady.

The hammer falls, and falls, and falls. At the end of the shift it pauses one beat longer than it should, then falls once more and rests.

On the rim, the old one holds very still. This time she knows it is a reply.
