# The Visitor

## Down the Chimney

It came in with the colony, before dawn. Nothing in the chamber marks the hour.

There was no light and no sound. The lamp flames leaned toward the chimney the way they always lean during the returning, pulled sideways by twelve million bodies pouring down out of the gorse. None of them leaned differently. No bat swerved. The swiftlets went on clicking and the crickets went on trilling. In the pumphouse office the needle on the level chart went on drawing its slow line. If the pump crew on the walkway had been asked, they would have said it was an ordinary morning, and they would have been right. The visitor had no interest in being noticed. It had been drawn by something small, and it had come to look at it properly.

What drew it had happened nine years earlier, in the dim of the second stoppage. A pumpman had held his flat hand out over the water and pressed it slowly down, and a fringe of pale filaments wider than a cart had gone still for the length of a long breath. Across that gap two kinds of patience had touched, and something that pays attention to such moments had noticed. That kind of power does not hurry, because it doesn't need to. Nine years was soon.

## Listening

The first thing it did was listen, because the place was made of sound.

It could take the noise apart better than any man. It heard each of the six impellers separately. In the fourth pump from the gate it heard a bearing beginning to go, a faint dry flutter under the hum that no fitter would catch for another fourteen months. It heard the colony not as rice on a drum but as twelve million voices. Each voice was particular, and each was calling out the shape of the dark and hearing it answered. It heard the swiftlets ticking their way along cracks they had used for four hundred generations. It heard the cascade in its plunge pool. It heard the frogs in the warm seeps, calling to one another in voices the din had swallowed for thirty-one years. They went on calling anyway, and some of them were answered.

Under all of that, through the water, it heard a breath. It came twice a minute, very slowly, out beyond the lamps.

Then the visitor went into the water and listened from there, and it learned something the men had never been able to learn.

Water carries sound better than air does, far better, and farther. On the walkway the pumps are a hum you feel in your knees. In the water they are everything. The whole drowned sea of Hallow Deep rang with them, every room and gallery and solution pocket, down into the limestone as far as the warm water went. Six impellers ground through the dark without pause, and the water carried that grinding into every body that lived in it. The eyeless fish swam inside it. The eels lived inside it. The Tenant had lived inside it for thirty-one years, and before that, for longer than there had been men, it had heard nothing but the colony's chittering drifting down through fathoms, softened, like rain on a roof.

The visitor understood then why the Tenant had come up during the stoppages. The men believed it had come to take back its floor, and that was part of it. But mostly it had come because for the first time in nine years, and then in thirteen, the water had gone quiet. It had risen with the quiet the way a man steps out of a loud room into a garden. When the hum came back, it had gone down again without hurry. It had never been anything but patient.

The visitor stayed with that for a while.

## The Finished Ones

Then it went down into the rooms.

Below the floor of Hallow Deep there is more of Hallow Deep. The galleries run through the limestone for miles, full of warm water, lightless and still. In many of them there are pillars.

The walkers had noticed that the trails circled the bases of the largest pillars, sometimes several times, the way a dog circles before lying down. They had wondered about it, then stopped wondering and marked the paths with rag. The visitor did not need to wonder. It went to the pillars and knew them.

They had walked once. The men had guessed at this and put it out of their minds, and they had guessed right. Some of the collared towers in the stalagmite forests of the green were only stone that had grown in still water. But some of them, and many more in the deep rooms, were the finished ones. They had lain down in some older age of the ocean and stopped combing. The stone had gone on growing on them, collar over collar, until there was nothing left but stone in the rough shape of something long and broad, ringed with shelves like the growth rings of a tree.

The Tenant visited them. That was what the circling was. It went around each one slowly, its filaments passing over the stone, and then lay down beside it in the silt. That was how the great shallow bowls were made. It lay with its blunt face against the finished one for a long time, sometimes days, while the water's slow stone settled on both of them alike.

It wasn't the last one, the visitor found, and it wasn't the first. Very deep, farther down than any pump would ever reach, in a gallery where the water was warmest, there was a low pale shape that had not yet begun to move. It lay under a skin of fresh calcite and stirred perhaps once a decade, a faint shifting in the silt. The ocean of Hallow Deep was still making them, the way it had always made them, out of stone and warmth and dark and bats falling like snow. The Tenant knew this one too. It had been down to it. It had lain beside it.

So it was not lonely in the way a man means the word, though it was alone in most of the ways a man would recognise. It had the ones before it and the one after it, and the colony overhead, and the water. The visitor, which can see a great deal, could not see whether this was enough. It suspected the question belonged to the Tenant, and not to anyone else.

## Tom Wystan

Then it went to the capped door.

In the nineteenth year Tom Wystan went through the silt on the green, into a pocket of water still warm from the depths. The pocket opened into a gallery, and he sank a long way. He did not suffer long, though he suffered, and the visitor did not pretend otherwise. It never pretends.

The Tenant was three rooms away. It heard him fall. Through the ringing water it heard a new sound: a body entering from above that was not a bat, heavier, wrapped in things it had never tasted. It came. By the time it arrived there was nothing left to do but what it knew how to do.

It combed him, very gently, with the hundred soft filaments at the front of its face. It learned oiled canvas, rubber, wool, the iron of a lamp, the pulp of a letter in a breast pocket. It learned the taste of a man. Then it did for Tom Wystan what it did for the finished ones. It lay down beside him in the silt with its face against him, and the water went on doing what the water does. By now a thin pale skin of stone lies over Tom where he rests in the gallery below the slab. It is the beginning of a collar, as fine as frost.

He came from above. The Tenant had never met anything that came from above except the bats, and it had understood the bats for a long time. So it went up, to the place where he had come through. When the men capped the door with concrete and painted a cross on it, the Tenant came to the slab and made a smaller bowl against it, and lay there with its face to the door. In its way it was trying to understand where he had come from, and whether there were others there, and whether they knew.

It found the candle. Tallow, pale and soft, faintly greasy. It tasted, very nearly, like the Tenant's own skin. It was a small thing from the air that was like itself. It turned the candle over and set it down again. It came back, years later, and did it again.

The visitor looked at the biscuit tin and the photograph gone to pulp behind glass. It looked at the candle stub dimpled by filaments and the small bowl in the silt beside the painted cross, and it understood that two different kinds of mourning had been laid down in the same place. Neither knew the other was there. Each had been done with great care.

## What It Could Have Done

A power like that can do nearly anything. That is exactly what makes it dangerous, and wisdom begins with knowing it. So the visitor thought, for what a man would call a long time, about the things it could do. The considering took it no time at all.

*It could stop the pumps.* The water would come back at the height of a man a month and the Tenant would have its sea again, whole and quiet. But the pumps keep the mine alive, and the mine keeps a town alive. There were the men on the walkway, and the men in the pub loud with relief, and their wives and children and the shop and the chapel and the school. The men of Hallow Deep had not been cruel. They had broken through a floor by accident and done what they had to, and for thirty-one years they had behaved toward the thing in the water with a courtesy nobody had asked of them. They had marked its trails with rag and gone around them. They had given it a sign instead of a name. They had never tried to hurt it. When their engineers came down and measured and went away, they had written nothing down. They were inhabitants of the place too. Taking away their living to rescue a creature that did not need rescuing would have been a kind of vanity.

It did not need rescuing. That was the centre of it. To the Tenant, thirty-one years was a held breath. Its sea was shrinking, but slowly, and it had its rooms. The pumps were losing, and everyone in the Deep knew it. The seam was thinning. The ending the men feared and the Tenant waited for was already on its way. It was patient and it was certain, and no one needed to hurry it.

*It could make the pumps win*, then. It could give the men their dry floor and send the water down forever. The visitor set that down at once. It would have been a death dressed up as a gift.

*It could carry the Tenant somewhere else*, to some other drowned dark where no one pumped. But the Tenant was not a thing that lived in Hallow Deep so much as a thing Hallow Deep had been doing, very slowly, for a very long time. Its dead were here. The one after it was here. Moving it would have been like transplanting a pillar.

*It could let the world know.* Scientists would come, and cameras, and newspapers. There would be a proper name and a proper drawing. The visitor saw very clearly where that went. Someone would want a specimen. Someone would want to drain the rooms to reach it, and someone would want to sell tickets. The Tenant's best protection had always been silence: the engineers' empty notebooks, the naturalist's missing drawing, a sign that looked to outsiders like a man telling another man to calm down. The men had understood this without ever discussing it. The visitor would not undo it.

*It could bring up Tom Wystan and Emrys Hale*, and give their families bodies to bury in daylight. It weighed this longest, because grief is heavy and it does not treat grief lightly. But bringing them up would open the doors and bring down the men with ropes and lights and questions. Tom was not lost. He was kept, and more tenderly than anyone above could have imagined, and the stone on him was the same stone that grew on the Tenant's own dead. His grave had a cross painted on it, a candle, and a mourner. Graves in daylight often have less.

*It could give the Tenant eyes, or the men a way to speak to it.* It set these down gently, the way you set down a tool that is too sharp for the work.

When it had considered everything, it found, as powers of that kind very often do, that most of what it could do would ruin things by being done. The situation did not need a miracle. It needed a few small things to go right in a place where very little was going wrong.

So it did four small things. One of them was nothing.

## The Seventh Pump

The first concerned the noise.

Three years later the company sent down a young engineer, Gwen Lloyd, to specify the seventh pump. She was the first woman ever to go down the Hallow shaft. On her first shift she stood in the pumphouse office and found that she didn't want to leave. She went out onto the walkway and stood beside the pumps with her hand flat on the sweating rock of the chamber wall, feeling the hum come up through it. She thought about how much of that hum was going into the rock rather than into the water it was meant to move, and how much of it went on out through the limestone into all that warm dark.

She didn't know why she thought about it. The visitor had done nothing to her mind except leave a door ajar in it and trust her to walk through.

She specified the seventh pump on isolation mounts, thick pads of rubber and steel spring between the machine and the concrete, and she argued for it against a cost office that didn't see the point. She argued well. The next year she had the fourth pump remounted, because it had developed a bad bearing that the fitters had caught, somehow, fourteen months before anyone would have expected. Over the following decade the other five were remounted one by one, whenever each came up for overhaul.

The hum did not go away. The pumps still turned day and night, and a man on the walkway still felt them in his knees. But in the water the grinding dropped to something lower and softer, a sound like far-off weather. Down in the rooms the Tenant lay beside the finished ones in something closer to the old quiet than it had known for thirty-five years. It didn't come up to the edge less often. It came up with its face lifted a little higher out of the silt, and on the green the walkers found its trails meandering, taking long idle loops among the pillars that went nowhere in particular. The older walkers looked at those loops and felt something they couldn't put a sign to.

The younger men on the pump crews still wore felt plugs. They went deaf a good deal more slowly than their fathers had. Some of them, in old age, could still hear the blackbird in the gorse.

## The Crate

The second concerned the crate of explosives nobody wanted to move.

It had been sitting on the older ground for twenty-six years, and the Deep had been working on it the way the Deep works on everything. The wood was soft. Inside, the sticks had begun to sweat. The visitor could see where this went. In the thirty-fourth year a walker would back a barrow into the crate in the mist without seeing it. The explosion would kill him and his partner. It would bring down forty feet of ceiling and a great many bats, and it would crack the rock around the chimney in a way that would, over the following decade, close it.

So one Tuesday the foreman, Ifor Bevan, a man who had stepped around that crate twice a shift for eleven years, stopped on the walkway and looked at it. He found he could not bear to look at it one more day. He went up to the surface, rang the right people, and did it properly: sand, blankets, the slowest cage ride anyone could remember, a man from the quarry company with steady hands. The crate went up the shaft and out onto the hillside and was destroyed safely in a pit in the gorse, where the bang startled the rooks.

Nobody ever knew what had been avoided. The visitor was content with that. Most of the best things it does are never known.

## The Survey

The third thing was nothing.

In the thirty-eighth year head office proposed a survey of the solution pockets on the green. The idea was to find every door and grout it shut with cement, to cut off the inflow from below and save the cost of an eighth pump. The visitor saw what it would mean. The Tenant would be walled out of its rooms with its sea still shrinking, or walled into them away from the edge, with the finished ones on one side of the concrete and Tom Wystan on the other.

It began to consider what to do. Then it stopped, because it didn't need to do anything.

The walkers were given the survey. They went out with their poles and lanterns, and they knew the green better than they knew their own kitchens, and they found eleven doors. There were perhaps three hundred. They found eleven small, dead, harmless pockets far from any trail, and they marked them in red paint and wrote up a careful report. The grouting went ahead on those eleven and made no difference to anything. Head office concluded that inflow came mostly from the springs and rivers and bought the eighth pump.

Nobody had told the walkers to do this. Nobody had discussed it, even in the sign. The visitor watched them walk past door after door, their poles going in to the hand and coming out again, without a flicker, and it felt what a power like that feels instead of pride. It had come prepared to protect the thing in the water, and found that the thing in the water was already protected, by men with bad hearing and cold tea in tin flasks who would never be thanked for it.

A great deal of wisdom, the visitor knew, lies in noticing when your help is not needed. Knowing when to act matters, and knowing when to hold back matters as much.

## The Tenant

The fourth thing was for the Tenant, and it was the only thing the visitor did directly.

It went to it in the rooms. The Tenant was lying beside the finished one nearest the slab, the largest of the collared pillars in that gallery. The visitor did not change its body or its sea or its circumstances. It gave it no eyes. It let it understand three things, the way you might set three stones in a hand that has only ever known silt.

The first was that the ones above sit with their finished ones too. The slab, the cross, and the candle were their bowl, their lying-down, their face against the stone. The one who came down from above had others, and they had come to the door and grieved there, in their own way, as it did. They were not so different.

The second was that the sign had meant something. The flat hand pressing slowly toward the water was not a threat, nor a command. As near as it could be put into the Tenant's world, it meant *we know you are there, and we are being careful.*

The third was the simplest. *The water will come back.*

The visitor could not tell how much of this the Tenant took in, or in what form, because it was a mind very unlike a man's, patient and old and made largely of touch. But the filaments went still for about the length of a long breath. Then they began again, rippling, tasting, reaching up a little way into the dark.

Some weeks later, on the lip of the capped door, the walkers found the candle stub turned to face the painted cross. Beside it, set down carefully in the grey silt, was a small smooth disc of pale calcite, of the kind that forms on the flank of something that has lain very still at one level for a very long time. The walkers didn't touch it. They signed to each other about it for a long while in the lamplight. Then one of them, a man who had known Tom Wystan, went down on one knee in the silt and made the sign over it, his right hand flat, palm down, pressing slowly.

## Leaving

It left with the colony at dusk, the way it had come, up the chimney into the gorse and the cold rain. No lamp flame leaned differently. No one noticed.

It left a great many things as they were. The cockroaches still crunched underfoot, and the ammonia still made eyes stream. The eels still rose for bats, and not when the swell came in. The pumps still turned, losing slowly, as they always had. The pale ferns around the sodium lamps still reached toward their small orange suns. The visitor knew those ferns would die when the lamps went out, and it grieved for them a little. It let them be, because they had come down on miners' boots and lived a strange bright life at the bottom of the earth, and not everything benevolent is a rescue.

It did not tell the men anything. They already knew nearly everything that mattered, and they knew it in the only language down there that could hold it without harming it.

## Afterward

The pumps stopped in the forty-sixth year.

The cables came up the shaft. The diesels were sold. The last crew walked back along the walkway, past the eight pumps on their quiet mounts, past the slab and the rusted biscuit tin and the small pale disc still lying in the silt, and they left the gate of riveted plate open because there was no reason to close it.

Four of them did not go up in the first cage. Nobody had planned it. They stood at the far southern edge of the lamps, by the old gauging rod, with the power off and only their hand lanterns, and they waited.

The lowest layer of the noise was gone. The colony roared, the rivers poured, and the cascade hammered its pool. At the edge of the green the newest cairn went dark at its base, and a red rag lifted and stirred in the water.

Then the water bulged. A long low swell came toward the shore without breaking, and something pale lay along the waterline, just under and just above, keeping pace with the rising edge by a finger's width.

It came as far as their lanterns. Its broad blunt face lay in the silt a few yards from their boots, and its hundred filaments reached up out of the water into the air and stayed there, tasting.

Four right hands went out, flat, palm down, and pressed slowly toward the water.

The filaments went still. They stayed still for a long time, much longer than a breath, long enough that one of the men, who had once been a boy on the pump crews and could still hear a little, heard in the gap under the roar what might have been the frogs in the warm seeps, calling.

Then the men turned and walked back to the cage, and the water followed them up the green, patient and certain, a finger's width at a time, bringing its oldest tenant home.

Far above, a woman who had once put her hand flat on the sweating wall of a chamber stood at a kitchen window in grey rain. She found she was pressing her palm slowly down against the sill, and didn't know why.

Something that pays attention to such moments noticed, and was glad, and did nothing at all.
