# A Right Held by No Family

## It Came Up the Trail

It came the way everything comes, through the pass.

It did not need to. It could have been in the saddle all at once, everywhere, in every drop hanging from every moss beard. But it had been watching Weir-in-the-Cloud long enough to understand that here, arriving from above was a kind of rudeness. Everything that matters in the saddle comes up out of the valley and through the low place: traders, weather, the season, the Guest. So it came up the switchback trail through the lower forest, slowly, and let the leeches quest at it from the leaves. It found it had no blood to give them, and was faintly sorry.

It heard the town long before it reached it. It had expected that. It had not expected to be moved by it.

The Jay saw it first. Of course the Jay did. The Jay screamed from the top of Grandfather Left-Hand, the scream it gives a minute before the fog, and half the town reached for its washing. Then the fog didn't come, and people looked at the sky, then at each other, then shrugged and shouted about something else. The Jay kept screaming for a while, outraged that no one had listened, and then it stole a smoked pigeon and forgot.

The power settled in the town the way a stayer does. It took no rafter, but it stopped moving, and it listened.

## The Mercies It Did Not Do

It had come, if it was honest, with a list.

It could hold the slopes. The ground would stop sliding. The terraces would never slump and the old stilt-houses would stand straight forever.

It could take the leeches off the lower paths. It could teach the macaques to stay in the cliff forest. It could lift the damp out of the old women's knees. It could make the nets unnecessary: fill the smoking racks each season with birds that had never lived, so that nothing need be caught at all.

It spent some days with this list, and then it put the list away. Nobody told it to. It simply watched a man lash a new pole to a tilting house in the rain. Four neighbors came out to help, all shouting contradictory advice. A child handed up the rattan. The pig under the floor supervised with great seriousness. When it was done they all stood back, soaked, and argued about whether it was straight. It was not straight. It would hold.

The power understood then that the slipping ground was not something that happened *to* Weir. It was something Weir *did*. It was the occasion of half its conversations and most of its neighborliness. Nobody plans the repair; it happens the way a scab forms. If you take away the wound, you have not given the body health. You have told it that it no longer needs to know how to heal.

The leeches gave every child the small white scar they compared like birthmarks. The macaques gave the children an enemy and taught them to be quick. The Tax Collector with his torn ear was the most hated and most beloved creature in their world. As for the nets, the town was a weir. Remove the catching and it would no longer be a weir. It would be an admirer of birds, a different and lesser thing, living on birds it had not earned.

So it did not do those things. But it was not a sentimental power, and it did not let itself off so easily. *Everything here is part of the town's life* is a comfortable thing to believe. It is also the belief of every power that ever watched children die and called it character.

Some of what happened in the saddle was not the town healing. Some of it was just harm. From the outside, however long it watched, it could not reliably tell which was which.

So it did what anyone in Weir does with a dispute. It went and shouted it at a stone.

## The Stone

She was called Asse-Under-the-Eaves. Her knees were so bad that she no longer went up ladders at all, which made her very senior. She sat in a doorway on the second-oldest lane, wrapped in cotton cloth from the west, with a brazier and a bowl of rice-beer. Her pig lay under the floor below her, and a stayer thrush lived in the thatch above.

The power did not appear to her as anything much, perhaps a thickening in the fog at the end of the lane. But it spoke in the town's voice, high and forward, into the front of the face. That is the only way to be heard in Weir, and it did want to be heard.

"I have a question about what is owed," it said.

Asse squinted into the fog. She had lived eighty-one years in the saddle and had felt the Guest twice. She knew this was not the Guest. The Guest never asked anything.

"Everybody has a question about what is owed," she said. "Sit, if you sit. What's yours?"

"I can do things," it said. "I could stop the ground moving."

"Don't," said Asse at once. "We move it back. That's ours."

"The leeches."

"Leave them. How else would you know who grew up here?"

"The monkeys."

She laughed so hard she spilled her beer. "Take the Tax Collector away and the children will have nothing to hate. You don't know what that does to children. They start hating each other." She wiped her chin. "What else?"

The power was quiet for a moment. Then it said, "I have watched for a long time. Every few years the traders from the west bring a cough up the trail with the cotton. It settles in the smoke of the houses and it takes the babies. Last time it took nine."

Asse didn't laugh at that. She looked at her brazier for a long while. The clappers knocked. A frog began its hollow-log knocking somewhere near.

"Two of the nine were my daughter's," she said at last.

"Is that yours?" the power asked. "The way the walls are yours?"

"No." She said it very loudly, the way grief is said here, so it would be heard over the water. "No, that's not ours. That's just a thing that comes and takes. We never fixed anything by it. Nobody ever got better at living from it. Take it."

"Then I'll take it."

"Wait." Her voice sharpened into the voice of someone who has settled a thousand disputes. "You can't *take* it. Nothing's taken here. It's held. It's somebody's." She thought, and the power, which could have known what she was thinking, chose to wait and let her say it. "You'll hold the right to the western cough. It's yours now, from the trail's foot to the top step. Nobody else has it. Nobody else can claim it. You keep it away from the smoke of the houses. That's the right, and that's the duty on it."

"For how long?"

Asse looked at it with deep pity. It was the look old people give the young everywhere, though it was older than the mountains.

"Rights don't have a *how long*," she said. "It's yours. When I die, some other stone will remember it's yours. When she dies, another one. If you ever let the cough up the trail, somebody will come and shout at you about it, and they'll be right."

The power had never before been given a thing in a way that bound it. It found it was glad.

## The Dead

Before it left the doorway, Asse asked for one thing for herself.

"When we shout the mourning from the roofs," she said, "we shout it so they'll hear. Over the water. My mother believed they heard. I don't know. I've shouted for a lot of people."

The power could have said a great deal. It chose to say only what was true, in a form she could carry.

"They hear," it said. "The water doesn't cover it. Nothing here covers it. It's the loudest sound in the saddle."

Asse nodded once, as if confirming a debt she had long suspected was owed. She never told anyone the power had said it. But at the next mourning the town noticed that old Asse-Under-the-Eaves, with her terrible knees, had herself carried up a ladder to shout from a roof. And her voice, people said afterward, was the only one you could hear all the way down at the taro beds.

## Upstream

That was all it did in the saddle itself. Everything else it did somewhere else.

Here is what it understood. Weir-in-the-Cloud does not make its own water. Its water comes to it. Every family drinks water that has already passed through a family above it, and the town as a whole drinks the breath of the western lowlands. That wet, warm air climbs the face every day and gives itself to the moss. The fog is not local. The fog is a gift from a forest many days' walk away, which the saddle people have never seen and will never thank.

There are ways a cloud forest dies that have nothing to do with anything the people in it do. The lowlands get cut and burned. The air comes up drier and warmer. The cloud stops touching the ridge and passes over it, a little higher every decade, until the moss browns and the bromeliads empty and the frogs go silent. Then one year the fog simply does not come through the notch. People stand on the upper steps and wait, and it goes on not coming.

The power considered this for a long time. It remembered the oldest stones' story: the whole five hundred years of Weir are a single footfall of the Guest, and when the foot lifts the saddle will empty, and another notch far down the far valley will fill with moss and stilts and shouting. The power did not know whether the story was true. It did not look to find out. It did not think it was the kind of question that should be settled by looking.

But it made a distinction, and the distinction was the whole of its reasoning.

If the Guest's foot lifts, that is the Guest's. That is the long crossing, the way everything must go through the low place. The power would not hold that foot down, any more than the town would net the central gap. It would not make Weir last forever. Things that last forever are no longer crossing anything.

But a forest burned many days away by people who never heard of the saddle is not a footfall. It is not a crossing, a season, or the Guest. It is only a severing, an accident of distance. The town would never get to shout at anyone about it, because no one who did it would ever climb the trail.

So the power went west, down out of the saddle and far past the lowland trading towns, to where the cloud is born. That is where it did its long work. It did it the Weir way: patiently, unfinished, nowhere all at once. It went to the people there too. It did not overrule them. It did the slow, persuading, repairing work of making a living forest worth more to them standing than burned, in every way that could be made true. Where it couldn't persuade, it held. It became, you might say, a family upstream on the channel. It keeps the water clean before it's passed down.

The saddle people know nothing about this. It would never occur to them to be grateful for the forest that makes their fog, just as it never occurs to the house at the bottom of the lane to thank the house at the top. That's how channels work. Water is used and passed on, used and passed on.

## The Gap

Once, only once, the power went up to the central gap, where no net has ever been strung.

It felt the Guest there, or thought it did. It was not sure, and it chose not to become sure. It could have looked. It is the sort of power that can look at nearly anything. It stood at the edge of the open notch in the fog, with the hawks sliding by at rooftop height, and it understood that the town had kept this room open for five hundred years precisely so that something could go through *without being caught at*. To look would have been a kind of netting.

So it did what the saddle people do on the night of the first fog. It fell quiet, and it let whatever was passing pass.

Then it turned around and went down the trail on foot, the way traders leave.

## What the Netter Felt

Something happened the next season that the stones still argue about.

Oru-of-the-Laurel, who holds the right to the fourth net from the north cliff, as her mother did, was up before dawn in the dark and the fog. She had her palm on the pole, reading the cord. She knew every thrum there is: the soft double-tap of a thrush, the heavy slap of a pigeon, the frantic buzz of something small.

This was none of those. It was something that struck the mesh and did not tangle. It rested there a moment against the cord, very lightly, the way a hand rests on a shoulder. Through the pole and into her listening fingers came something she could only later describe as *a word*. It was not in any of the three languages of the market. But she understood it, and she was sure it was *thank you*.

Then it went through the net without breaking a single strand, and was gone.

She told everyone, loudly, which is how things are told. The stones gathered. There was a great deal of shouting. It lasted most of an evening and much of the rice-beer. Asse-Under-the-Eaves was carried down to it by two grandsons and sat through all of it without saying a word, which alarmed everyone more than any argument could have.

Finally someone shouted at her, *Well? What was it?*

"It's a stayer," Asse said. "Of a kind. It doesn't live in a rafter. But it holds a right here."

"What right? Whose right?"

"Its own," said Asse. "It holds the western cough. From the trail's foot to the top step. I gave it that right myself. Remember it."

And they did, because that is what stones are for.

## Afterward

In the years since, no cough has come up the trail with the cotton. No one in the valley towns can explain it, and no one in Weir finds it strange. Some things are simply someone's. Nobody else's cough comes into your smoke any more than you would take water from another family's channel.

The fog still pours over the western lip every day, gray and silent and faithful. It swallows the first houses, then the next, then you.

The walls still slump. Somebody still goes to fix them.

The Tax Collector is very old now. The children are sure he will outlive them all out of spite, and they hate him with the full devotion of their hearts.

And each year on the first clear morning after the first fog, a small child climbs the concave rungs of the highest house. The town goes quiet rung by rung until there is nothing but frogs and water. The child opens their hands, and the bird goes up and through, over the open gap, following the hawks and the long invisible thing that is always crossing. Then the whole saddle gives its single enormous shout, the sound people make when they have let something go on purpose.

Somewhere many days to the west, in a forest where the cloud is born, something that holds a right in Weir-in-the-Cloud hears it. It has been told the dead can hear that sound over the water, and it knows, better than anyone, that this is true.

It does not answer. It has work to do upstream. But it keeps its part of the channel clean, and lets most of what flows through it pass.
