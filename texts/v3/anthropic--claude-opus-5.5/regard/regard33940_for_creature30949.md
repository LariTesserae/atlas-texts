# The Guest

## The Attention

It came the way weather comes to a mountain: from very far off, and all at once.

It had watched many places. It had watched cities that sang, and seas that did arithmetic in their tides, and one long, sorrowful plain where a single tree had outlived everything that had ever rested in its shade. It had learned to notice when a place was asking for something, and it had learned, more slowly and at greater cost, that most places are not.

What drew it to the Seam was the stitching. From high enough, on a still night, it saw the dusk flight lift out of the mountain in a spiral of grey. Every night a billion small bodies passed up through the eye of the crack and back down before dawn. It looked like a needle working, and the power had never seen a mountain being sewn. So it came closer.

The first thing it tried was to speak.

That was a mistake, and it knew so almost at once. It said something in the voice it used for greetings. It was not loud, only clear, a voice that elsewhere had carried across valleys. The voice went into the Seam and died within a few paces, swallowed by the fur of a million wings. Nobody turned. A lizard on a ledge did not so much as blink its glassy eye.

The power was old enough to find this funny, and wise enough to find it instructive. *Here, then, is a place where I cannot be heard unless I am close enough to touch.* It had not been in such a place for a very long time. It decided to stay until it understood why the place was built that way, and whether it should be.

## What It Could Do

It could see the whole of the Seam at once: the tiers, the cisterns, the forty sleepers behind their grass curtains, the four children, the owls on the high ledges with their faces turned down. It could feel the stone. It felt the two walls straining apart, a handspan a year. The pull ran through the mountain like a long exhale, from a place deep under the root, where heat moved and rock softened.

Before anything else it made itself consider what it was able to do. That kind of reckoning is the beginning of wisdom, not the end of it.

It could close the crack. It could push the walls together the way a hand closes a wound, and the town would never be pulled apart again. It turned this over and saw everything that would die. The warm breath would stop, and so would the fog that the warm breath makes. The drip would stop. The moss would dry to a brown crust on the black stone, and the ferns would curl, and the moths would have nowhere to sleep. The people would live in a closed fist of rock on a cold mountain, and the carved hand would finally touch the thing it reached for, and the reaching would be over. Also, and this was not a small thing, whatever tended the other end would be crushed in the closing, or simply made unnecessary. The power found that it could not tell which of those was worse.

It could stop the widening and leave the crack as it was, held at exactly this width forever, like a breath held. This was the one that tempted it. The town would stop losing, the bridges could stop growing, and the pairs across the gap would never be carried farther apart than they already were.

But the green is young because the rock is young. Last season's tremors open fresh fractures, and the moss finds them. The warm air rises because something far below is still moving. The power traced the pull down and down and saw that the widening and the warmth were the same motion. A Seam that stopped opening would begin, very slowly, to cool. Holding it still would not preserve it. It would embalm it.

And there was the other thing, which it saw more slowly. The people had not asked for the widening to stop. In all their thousands of record-knots they had never once tied a knot that meant *stop*. Every one of them said the same thing: *I let this out. The walls moved. I followed them.* This was not despair, and it was not surrender either. It was a way of life, and it was theirs.

It could bring the people back, the ones who had climbed out onto the slopes and gone away to lives with voices in them. It could fill the empty rooms. But it could not do that without reaching into hundreds of other lives and bending them, and the power had long since stopped bending lives, even toward good places. Especially toward good places.

It could go down and look at the root and learn what was there.

It set that one aside to think about longer.

So after all its reckoning, the first thing it chose was to do nothing for a season, except learn to be spoken to.

## The Wrong Knot

It made itself small. That was not hard for it, but it was unfamiliar, the way it is unfamiliar to kneel after a long time standing. It took a shape that could sit on a ledge without troubling anyone and let the moths settle on it at dawn. They covered it wing against wing until it looked like any other patch of wall. It learned what they felt like, which was warm, dusty, and very faintly trembling, like something dreaming.

It learned the drip. It learned that the Seam's quiet was not empty. It was full of touch. It learned to feel the tremor coming a little before it arrived, the way the moths did, as a ripple running the length of the walls.

Mostly it learned cords.

At night, when the tenders slept, it went to the bridges and laid what it was using for a forearm along the railings, the way it had watched Draw do. It read. The newest knots were easy. The older ones were harder, and the oldest, the lumpy stretches that were more knot than cord, it could read, which no one living could. It did not let itself linger there. Those were letters in someone else's family, and it had not been invited to open them.

One night, on a bridge in the lower quarter, it found a place where the widening had pulled a lashing bowstring-tight. It knew exactly how to ease it. It had watched Draw do it a hundred times. It loosened the lashing, let the tension out, spliced in a length of snail-thread from a coil left on a peg, and tied the first knot, *the* knot. Beside it, after some hesitation, it tied a record-knot.

In the grey hour, Draw came along the bridge with her forearm on the rail and her eyes closed. She stopped at the splice.

She stood there for three hundred drips. Then she untied the power's knot, not angrily, and retied it. The difference was so small the power could not at first find it. Then it could. Its knot had been perfect: even, symmetrical, exactly as tight as a knot should be. Hers carried a slight lean, a pull toward one side, the way the black trees lean. It was the lean of a hand that has tied this knot ten thousand times in the dark and knows how the snail-thread settles over a winter.

Then she did something the power did not expect. She stood with her hand on the new knot, turned her face up toward the ledge where it sat covered in sleeping moths, and pressed her palm flat against the air, toward it. That is the gesture for *wide*, or *long ago*, and in the Seam those are nearly the same word.

She had known it was there for some time.

## A Name

It came down to her doorway on the fourth tier that evening, before the flight. It came slowly, so as not to frighten anyone, and it did not stand in the doorway, because in the Seam you do not stand in someone's doorway. It sat on the sill beside her, where the stone was worn smooth, and waited.

She took its hand.

It had made itself a hand for this. It had understood that it would need one, and it had tried to make a good one. She turned it over and ran her ridged thumb across the palm, and the power understood that she was reading it the way she read a railing. Its palm was smooth. There was no wear on it at all, no pads, no record. It was the hand of something that had never had to hold on.

She waited. The power realised she was waiting for its name.

It had names. It had been given many, in many languages, and they were all sounds, which here were nothing. It had no pattern to press. It held still, empty-palmed, and for the first time in a very long while it felt the particular embarrassment of arriving somewhere without the proper gift.

Draw seemed to understand. She took its hand again and pressed a name into it herself.

It was a flat palm laid over its palm and held there, warm, for the length of a slow breath. Then she lifted it away in a single motion, like something settling and then rising. The power did not know the pattern. Later it learned that it was the gesture the children use for the moths coming home at dawn, the dimming of the stone as the flight returns and settles, wave by wave.

It meant *the thing that comes down from above and rests here.* In the Seam it also meant *morning*.

The power had been given names that meant *king*, *storm*, *mercy*, and *the unanswerable*. It held this one in its smooth palm and found that it liked it better than all of them.

## What They Asked For

It learned to ask. The asking took a long time.

The pattern for a question in the Seam is a cord offered, not given: held out loose and slack, so the other person can take up the tension or not. The power held out a cord to Draw, and in the grammar it had learned it pressed: *What would you have, if you could have anything?*

She did not answer for four days.

In the meantime the power asked others. It did not ask everyone, because not everyone wanted to be asked, and it learned to feel that as well: a hand withdrawn, a curtain left drawn. But some took up the cord.

The man on the gentler wall, the one who long ago moved across from his friend's snail-beds for reasons neither of them would explain, held the cord a long time. Then he pressed: *Nothing. We are all right. It was a thing between us that is better as it is.* After a moment he added something the power did not understand until much later: *The distance is how we are kind to each other.* The power let the cord go slack and did not ask again.

The mother of the child who was not there anymore took the cord and did not press anything into it at all. She held it against her chest. The power understood that she wanted to know where. It did not know. It had not gone down. It could have gone then, and looked, and come back with an answer, and it very nearly did. Instead it sat with her through the whole of the dusk flight, its smooth hand in her grey one, the moths brushing both their faces. At dawn she pressed *thank you* into its palm, and she pressed it as though the power had given her something. The power was not sure it had. It was not sure, either, that it would be wise enough, next time, to give that and nothing else.

The children took the cord all at once, all four of them, and tangled it immediately.

They wanted, when it finally came out, to know whether a voice could go far. They had heard, the way children hear things, that above the lip, out on the cold slopes, a shout travels, and they did not believe it. They wanted to know if it was true.

So, with their parents' hands pressed into its own in permission, and with one father coming along who had not been above the lip in twenty years, the power took them up at dawn, as the last moths came home. It took them over the edge of the rift and out onto the flattened grass, into wind so cold and loud that the smallest child pressed her face into her father's side and would not look.

Then the oldest child, a girl whose name was two taps and a curl around the thumb, stood facing the far peaks and shouted.

It was a terrible shout, cracked and untrained, the first a throat of the Seam had ever properly made. It went out across the valley, and a moment later it came back. It came back faintly off the far wall of the next mountain, changed and thinned and unmistakably hers.

The power watched her face as she understood what had happened. A voice had gone into the distance and returned. That was a thing she knew already. She had known it all her life, from the cords lowered into the blue and drawn back warm. She had simply never known it was also true of sound.

The children stayed out until their fingers were numb. Then they asked to go home. The smallest one was asleep by the time they were back on the fourth tier. The oldest pressed into the power's palm, in the clumsy way of a child who has only just learned to say it, the pattern for *not yet*.

The power did not know if she meant she would leave someday, or would not yet leave, or was telling the power that it could not keep her. Probably she did not know either. That was the gift. It had been a door, not a road. Whatever she did now, she would be choosing it.

## Draw's Answer

On the fourth day she took up the cord.

What she pressed was not a request. It was a question of her own, and it took the power a long time to understand, because she used an old pattern, one from the lumpy stretches of the railings that only she and the power could still half-read. When it finally came clear, it was this:

*You can feel the stone.*

The power pressed back: *Yes.*

*Then tell me what I cannot feel.*

So the power told her.

It had noticed on the first night and had been waiting, because it had not known yet whether this was its to say. The long bridge, the one with the names, the one whose poles the whole town counts time by, hung from an anchor on the steeper wall. The anchor was a pillar of black stone left standing when the first builders cut the gallery. It had a flaw in it, a fracture running diagonally through its heart, invisible, sealed in basalt, fine as a hair. The small tremors were working it. They had been working it for sixty years. The power could feel the fracture growing a little with each shiver. It was not quick, but it was sure. Within a few more years, perhaps five, perhaps nine, a tremor would come at dusk, the pillar would shear, and the long bridge would fall.

Every name in the Seam was tied to that bridge, and so was the knotted cord from below.

The power did not fix it. It could have. It could have reached into the stone and sealed the fracture with a thought, and no one would ever have known.
