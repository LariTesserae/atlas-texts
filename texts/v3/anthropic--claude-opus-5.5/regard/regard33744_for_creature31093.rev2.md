# The Visitor Who Was Careful

## I. Arrival

The first thing it did was make itself cold.

It had come a long way, in whatever sense distance applies to such a thing, and it arrived as it usually arrived anywhere: warm, bright, and large. Warm because warmth is a kindness in most places. Bright because it had never had reason not to be. Large because it was.

It stopped at the edge of the buffer zone, on the pale floor that is not a floor, before any of that could touch the Works. Even from there it could taste the cereal on the air and feel how fine the dust was, how much of it hung suspended, how dry. It understood, the way a guest understands the moment they step through a door with their boots still wet, that everything it naturally was would be a catastrophe here. One degree of its ordinary warmth and the drifting starch would find the spark it had been waiting for since before the rings began.

So it cooled. It brought itself down to a few degrees above frost, and then it let go of its brightness, because the Works already had a sky and did not need a second one. It dried itself until nothing in it could condense. Last, and hardest, it made itself small. In this place large things need rooms, and it had no wish to be a thing the Works might feel it had to make room for.

Then it stepped onto a belt and let it take it where it went.

## II. What It Did Not Do

It spent a long time not doing things. Afterward, it thought this had been most of the work.

It did not bring water. It considered water first, because it had seen the stayers' cracked lips in the Works' memory of them, and because thirst is one of the oldest sufferings it knew how to end. But water here would be paste. Paste would be rot, rot would be warmth, and warmth would be fire. More than that, the Works had never been wet and had never needed to be. Bringing water would not relieve its thirst. It would give the Works a thirst it had never had.

It did not bring night. Everything in the Works turned on the unbroken light, and a single hour of dark would have stopped every stem on the plain, the way a single missed heartbeat stops more than a heart.

It did not map the routes, though it could have. It could see, without trying, how the folded districts nested inside one another, where the old pods lay under the new growth, and how the loops closed. It could have written it all down, and then the stayers would have had a map. They would also have lost their language: *the long sweet ride*, *the two-hour burnt one*, the naming of routes by smell and duration, which was the only way anyone had ever loved a place that would not hold still.

It did not stop the moths. It did not speed the Leaner or slow it. It did not answer the question the stayers had been knotting into cords for generations, though it knew the answer, or most of it. A people who have spent a thousand years working something out are owed the end of their own working.

It did not decide the Works was a problem. This took some effort. Where it came from, something that consumed a whole horizon and never stopped growing would have been a warning. Here it was a nursery. It reminded itself of that often.

## III. The Thirst

What it found first, riding the belts, was a young woman on her sixth day without water.

She had come in from the western buffer and had not turned back when she should have. Now she was lying in the lee of a grey, moth-pocked gantry. Her lips had cracked and were bleeding the little they still could, and she was trying to decide whether to walk out while she still could walk. She did not know yet that she was on the edge of becoming a stayer. Nobody knows that until afterward.

The visitor sat down beside her. It considered, for a long moment, taking the thirst away.

It did not. The thirst was the door. Whatever happened to the ones who stayed, the bloom and the lightness and the long quiet life, happened through it. To remove the thirst would be to close the door on her. Worse, it would be to choose for her, and choosing was the only thing she still had.

So it did the smallest true thing it could think of. It made sure she knew, clearly, that the way out was still open. It was a four-hour ride east and then a walk, and her legs would hold. It told her in a whisper, because everyone here whispers. Then it told her the other thing, that the ones who stayed did not die of the thirst. It had never been told that to anyone in so many words, and she deserved to hear it before she chose rather than after.

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said she wanted to stay, and asked whether it would sit with her.

It sat with her for eleven days. There is no night in the Works, so there was no night to get through, only the long white hours and the creak of the stems. When her lips stopped cracking, it was there. When the first faint bloom came up on the backs of her hands, it was there. Not knowing what else to do, it did what it had watched the stayers do: it leaned close and blew gently across her hands, lifting a little of the white away.

She laughed, the first sound she had made in days that was not pain. Then she took its hands and blew across them in turn, to see if it was solid underneath. It let her find that it was.

## IV. The Letter

Walking the tally was easy for it. It fell into step beside a tallyman, and the beetle did not seem to mind. For a while the two of them held still together on the true ground while the Works swam around them.

From there it could see where everything actually was. That was how it noticed the two old stayers.

They had shared an overgrown pod once, a long time ago by stayer reckoning, and they had gone out riding separately and not found each other since. Each of them still tied knots along the routes: *I passed this way, going this direction, at about this time.* They had been missing each other by a day, by an hour, by a single fold of space, for nineteen years. One was very light now, nearly light enough that the belts would begin to take him.

The visitor thought about this for longer than it had thought about anything else.

It could have brought them together. It could have set them down in the same room and simply let them look at each other. But the Works did not do that sort of thing, and neither did the stayers. Their whole way of loving each other had grown up around the not-finding: the patience of it, the letters left on gantries, the trust that the other was still somewhere out in all that room. To erase the distance would be to tell them that the thing they had built their lives around had been a mistake.

So it did something smaller. It took down one of his knots, a new one tied that week, and carried it along the true ground. It hung it on a gantry on a route it knew she would ride within the day. It did not add a knot of its own or change his in any way. It moved the letter a little closer, the way a wind sometimes does.

It did not stay to see whether she found it. That part belonged to them.

## V. The Leaner

It found the Leaner in the eastern buffer, among the house-sized pods, with its broad flat head laid against a husk. It was listening.

The visitor stood a respectful distance off and waited. It waited a long time, because the Leaner listens a long time. It watched the pale dusty flank rise and fall, and the soft round prints behind it, wider than the oldest cords recorded.

It thought about how the Leaner was the one thing in the Works that listened, and how nothing had ever listened to it.

So, when the Leaner lifted its head from the pod and paused, the visitor came forward slowly, with nothing warm and nothing bright in it. It did what the Leaner does. It laid itself very carefully against the Leaner's side, the way a person puts an ear to a wall.

The Leaner held still and allowed it.

What it heard was room. It was the particular hush of a large space, air with a great deal of distance in it. The Leaner was very big inside, bigger than its outside, like everything the Works makes. It was not yet as big as it would be. Somewhere far in there was a sound the visitor had to hold very still to catch: one thin creak, green wood flexing. A stem was already there, waiting to be lived around.

Below that, much further down and much fainter, it heard another creak, slower and older. The visitor understood that this was the first one. Whoever had lived here before the plain was still audible inside their child, the way a house carries the shape of its builder. So the Leaner was not alone. It had never been alone. It had been walking around all this time with its parent's footsteps in it and did not know. Or perhaps it did.

The visitor stayed until the Leaner moved. Then the Leaner turned its broad gentle head and laid it against the visitor, listening back.

This was the moment the visitor had been most careful about, though it had not known it would come. It was very large inside, larger than the Works, larger than the room the Leaner would one day need, and it knew that the Leaner was looking for exactly that. It could have closed itself and let the Leaner hear nothing. That would have been a kind of lie.

Instead it let the Leaner hear the truth: that the visitor was large, and that it was not empty. It was lived in, full of its own long life and its own attachments and the things it tended elsewhere. It was a home, not a room waiting for someone.

The Leaner listened for a long time. Then it lifted its head, with what the visitor could only describe as courtesy, and went back to the pods. It was the courtesy you show in someone else's house.

## VI. The Ones Beneath, and the Ones Ahead

The visitor could have gone down. The tallymen knew the way, or knew where the way was. It could have followed the stillness down through the layers of old factory and the folded rooms until it found whoever lived at the bottom of everything.

It went as far as the fast districts, where the shadows point toward the light and the drive-stems blur, and it felt the pull there, the whole Works leaning toward the rooms that had been moved into. Then it stopped. There is a kind of room in any house that the guest does not open. It had been invited nowhere. It had only been permitted, and permission ends at a certain door.

On its way back it climbed a gantry. It wanted to feel the worn rungs under its hands, the saddles hollowed by feet that had not stepped yet.

It listened for the creak ahead of it, the Early, the hand held out one rung up. There was none. Instead, a second after each rung took its weight, it heard its own creak arrive *late*, trailing behind it. It was out of step with the house's time, the way a guest always is.

It was not sad about this. It thought it was right. The house was not being built for it.

## VII. Leaving

It left the way most visitors leave, walking out through the grey buffer before the floor gave way.

A tallyman had been walking beside it for the last hour, on the true ground, and at the edge the beetle stopped. It raised its forelegs and tapped three times on the place where the visitor had last stood. Then it walked on.

The visitor looked at the spot for a while. It thought about asking what the taps meant and did not. It had taken nothing out of the Works but it had been given something, and a gift like that is not improved by being explained.

It asked the dust, as a courtesy, to cover its prints, and the next squall did.

---

Within the month, a single stem came up in the buffer zone where nothing had grown before. It was thin, with one small leaf-crown angled toward the white sky. The stem was not grown onto any axle and did not turn anything. It drank the light and creaked softly and went nowhere.

The young stayer found it on one of her first long rides, her hands still only faintly white. She stood beside it and did not know why it made her want to cry. She tied a knot on it, of a kind she made up on the spot, since none of the old forms fit. The other stayers, finding it later, would argue about what it meant.

It said: *Someone passed this way, going nowhere in particular, and wanted nothing, and was kind.*

Far off to the east, a very light old man stepped off a belt at a gantry he did not usually pass, and found a knot there he had tied himself a week ago. He stood holding it for a long time, working out how it had come to be there. Then he sat down beside the gantry to wait. Behind him the rung above him creaked, a second before anyone stepped on it.
