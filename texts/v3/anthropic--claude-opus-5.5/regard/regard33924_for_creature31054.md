*She was hatched under the lamps and has lived on after them, through the whole long quiet. She is the only witness the room has.*

---

# The Attention

It did not arrive, exactly. Things like it do not come down shafts or through gratings. It was simply, at a certain point, *there*, the way a thought is there. It had been drawn by a pattern that was not quite right, the way a musician in a crowded hall hears one string that has gone out of tune.

Nothing in the Knot registered it at first. The heat did not change. The fungus did not brighten.

But the ticking stopped.

It stopped the way it does after the knock, as though everything were listening. Only there had been no knock. The room went still for no reason it could have given. The crickets on the grating lowered their antennae. In the sump, in her cradle between the iron mains, the salamander opened her gills a little wider, as she does in the rain hours, though no rain was falling.

The power stayed in that stillness for a long time. It had no reason to hurry, and the room had taught it the right speed.

---

## What It Saw

It saw everything at once, which is the privilege and the danger of such a thing.

It saw the strata, bottom to top: the riveted iron and the grey lead beards, the lagging bursting through its canvas lace, the copper gone green-black, the confident steel, the pale new lines bolted through the old. It read the tags without a lamp. *Asylum East. Wool Exchange. Municipal Baths No. 2. Orphans' School.* And the numbers.

It saw the valves under their stone. It saw each one as the decision it had been: which hand, which morning, which reason, or no reason, only a shift ending and a man wanting to be home.

And because it saw everything, it followed the pipes up.

It followed them past the sealed cellars and the tram cuttings, into the city, to where the heat comes out. It saw the car park where the snow will not settle. It saw the block of flats on the site of the Orphans' School, whose ground floor is always too warm and whose tenants sleep with the windows open in January without knowing why.

It followed one line in particular. The tag on it reads *Tanners' Row*. On a cold morning more than sixty years ago, a man closed it most of the way. He had been told to, by someone reading a ledger that was already wrong. He meant to come back. The stone came instead.

Tanners' Row is not tanneries now. It is four streets of the cheapest housing in the city, where the district heating arrives as a rumour. In one of those flats an old man keeps a paraffin heater he is not supposed to have. The children in the flat below have a cough every winter, which their mother blames on the damp, and she is right. None of them has ever heard of the Knot. The valve that makes their winters what they are is sixty metres from them and eleven metres down, sealed in ochre and near-black, set by a hand that is not even a name anymore.

Then the power followed everything back down. It came past the root, past the fern and its forty grey minutes, past the glowing shelves and the stratified moths, to the water and to her.

It knew her the way it knew everything, completely. It knew the lentil heart and the pepper-grain eyes. It knew the chord she lives inside, and how *Tanners' Row* sounds to her: a thin, starved note in the wrought iron, a line carrying less than it was built for. It knew the small bones in the silt, the eggs on the underside of the flange, the fields that come to the mouth of the culvert and turn away. It knew her hand's-width migration across the decades toward the place where the root is coming.

And it knew the disposition, the leaning toward a warm thing passing over the water. It knew what the leaning was for, although she does not.

---

## What It Considered

A power like that can do nearly anything. That is exactly why it has to think.

**It could free the valves.** It could dissolve the stone from every wheel in an instant, loose every stem, clean every pointer. But a freed valve is not a turned one. Someone would still have to decide, and no one knows the Knot exists. Freed valves, untended, would drift. The governors would hunt. Lines that have run steady for sixty years would slam and knock. Somewhere a hospital would go cold at three in the morning. Loosening the room without returning it to anyone would only trade one kind of abandonment for another.

**It could set the valves itself, perfectly.** It could work out the right heating for every street in the city, justly and efficiently, and lock the pattern in place better than the stone ever could. The power turned this over most carefully, because it was the most tempting. It would have been good. It would have been kind.

But it would have been one more hand on the wheel making a final decision for people who were not asked. That hand would simply be wiser than the others, and more permanent. The wrong in the Knot is not really any single setting. The wrong is that the settings have become unreachable. The dead are governing the living, and the living cannot answer back. Replacing the dead's decisions with the power's own, however good, would leave the city exactly where it was: warm or cold by a will it cannot touch. The power did not want to be the last man on the last shift. It did not want to be the stone.

**It could bring the room into the light.** It could open the vault, clear the grate, and let the air change. The fern would thrive. But the fungus would die, the formations would crack as they dried, and the sump would warm and foul. The salamander, who has lived a century at the exact temperature of a bath, would not survive a year of weather.

**It could give her something.** It could open her eyes, and they would be useless and painful in the dark. It could call up one of those long, cool fields from the culvert and send it in, and so arrange her loneliness away. It could lengthen her life, or bring the root down to her this year instead of in twenty.

It considered each of these. It set each aside, because none of them was hers. She is not suffering. Her life is not a problem waiting for a solution. Her life is slow, blind, patient, and complete on its own terms. Rearranging it to suit someone else's idea of fullness would be a kind of disrespect, however gentle. The power did not think she needed rescuing. She needed at most to be *known*. It had just done that.

**It could do nothing.** It considered this seriously too. Nothing is often right. The room is not evil. The stone is not cruel. Most of what the Knot does, it does well.

But nothing would leave the old man with his paraffin heater and the children with their coughs. It would leave the city frozen, one valve at a time, into a pattern no one chose. The power saw that doing nothing was not neutral here. Doing nothing was siding with the stone.

---

## What It Decided

So it settled on a principle, the kind a wise thing reaches by subtracting everything it was tempted to do and seeing what remains.

*Give the room back to the living. Touch nothing the living can do for themselves. Guard what cannot guard itself.*

In practice that meant very little.

---

## What It Did

**The first thing** was in an archive, a basement of a different kind. It is a municipal records store in a building above the old city. There, in a box marked for pulping at the end of the financial year, lay the last maintenance ledger for *Junction Chamber 4 (Confluence)*.

The power did not move the ledger. It did not write in it or glow on it. It only saw to it that the box was misfiled, set on the wrong shelf by a tired clerk on a Friday. So the ledger missed the pulping, and it was still there in the spring when someone went looking.

**The second thing** was snow.

In February, heavy snow fell on the city, and a nine-year-old girl walking to school across a car park asked her mother why there was a long bare stripe across it, black and steaming faintly, as if something underneath were breathing. Her mother didn't know. Her mother works in the municipal records store, and she didn't like not knowing.

The power did not put the question in the child's mouth. The child asked it herself, because children do. Every winter for sixty years, children have asked it, and every winter the adults have shrugged. All the power did was attend to that one asking. Perhaps that made the mother look at her daughter a moment longer. Perhaps it made the question stay with her through the day. Her name is Ines. The power chose her because she was the kind of person who, once she began pulling on a thread, would not stop. It also saw that when she found what lay at the end, she would stop and *look*.

That was all it did. It made the thread findable. The finding it left to her.

**The third thing** was not really an act.

Before it withdrew, the power stayed a while over the sump. It did not intervene. It stayed near the water, warm in the particular way it was warm, and she felt it.

To her lateral line it was nothing. But to the ampullae on her snout, the old organs that read the faint fields of living things, there was suddenly something above the water. It was not metal. It was not prey or kin or the frightened heat of the thing that fell, and it was not the root. It was warm, and it was *attending*.

So she rose.

She rose slowly out of the cradle, gills opening like small hands. She came up through the column toward the surface, toward the warm thing overhead. She did not know why. The disposition simply took her, as it always had. She hung just under the water with her blunt head lifted and stayed there.

The power let her stay. It did not answer her in any way she could have understood. It made no promise. But in that moment it did one thing that is harder than it sounds. It let itself be the kind of thing she had been leaning toward all her life. For a while there was a warm thing above her water that knew exactly what she was and did not want anything from her.

Then it went, as lamps do. She sank back down into the rust floc, into the chord, into the slow work of being herself. The ticking began again, one, then none, then three together from different corners.

---

## The Lamp

Ines found the ledger in April. In May she found an engineer who would listen. It was August before anyone could get the old access shaft unsealed, and by then there were four of them: Ines, two engineers from the heating authority, and a biologist from the university. The biologist had heard there might be a fungus, and she came, as she put it, "in case."

They came down with lamps.

They stood on the catwalk, sweating instantly, breathing mouths full of wet heat, and for a long time none of them said anything. Then one of the engineers, who was not a sentimental man, said quietly, "Oh, look at this. Look what it's done." He put his light on a gate valve sheathed in scalloped cream stone, its stem fused into a pillar, and did not touch it.

They read the tags. The biologist found the fungus, and held her hand over her lamp until her eyes adjusted and she could see it glowing. She said a word that Ines didn't know and then didn't speak for some minutes. They found the fern and left it alone. They found the root and followed it with their lights, down through the lagged lines and over the copper, a pale hairnet caught partly in stone, reaching.

Then Ines, leaning over the rail at the end of the catwalk, swung her lamp out over the sump.

In the water below, something pale turned toward the light and rose.

It came up slowly through the column toward the warm, moving gold overhead, pink gills opening. Everyone went still. No one could have explained, then or later, why the moment felt the way it did, and none of them tried. The biologist whispered that it must be very old. The engineer said nothing. Ines kept the lamp exactly where it was and did not move it until the animal had looked, in whatever way it looks, and sunk again.

---

## After

This is what happened after that, and none of it was done by the power.

They freed three valves. They did it by hand, with hammer and chisel, over two weeks. The biologist hovered, and the engineers worked carefully, trying not to drop stone into the sump, because Ines had asked them to and because by then they wanted to. One of the three was *Tanners' Row*. The wheel had not turned in sixty-one years, and when it finally gave, it gave with a groan they felt in their teeth. That winter the old man in Tanners' Row put his paraffin heater in a cupboard and forgot about it. The children below still coughed, but less.

They left the other valves alone for now. The heating authority began a long, argumentative project of working out what the city needed, district by district, in meetings, with maps and budgets and complaints. It was slow and imperfect and entirely human, and it was theirs. When they have decided, they will come down and turn the wheels. If they get it wrong, they will come down again. The valves can be reached now, and that was the whole of the gift.

They repaired the catwalk as far as the far side. They hung new lamps on the old hooks, where the iron was worn bright long ago by other bails, and wired them to a switch at the top of the shaft, so the lamps stay off unless someone is there. They put a grille over the shaft mouth and a sign that no one reads. They did not clear the flowstone from the room. They did not dry the air.

The biologist took one sample of the fungus, very small, and has been writing about it ever since. It has a name now. She will not tell anyone where the Knot is.

And Ines wrote in the ledger. It was the first entry in decades, in the column where the old men wrote *pressure nominal* and *packing replaced at No. 14*. She wrote:

*Resident: one salamander (Proteidae?), very old. Sump not to be drained, dosed, or disturbed. Lamps on only when needed. Do not lean on the root.*

Then, after thinking about it, she added a new tag on a length of copper wire, stamped by hand the old way, and hung it on the rail above the water where the oldest iron goes down. It does not name a street. It says only *HERE*.

---

## What the Power Would Say, If It Said Anything

The power did not make the Knot permanent, and it did not end it. It did not save the salamander, because she did not need saving. It did not fix the city, because the city is not its to fix.

It saw a room that had drifted out of every living hand and was sealing itself shut, and it did the smallest thing that would let it be held again. It saw one old, blind, patient creature who had been leaning for a century toward a warmth she half remembered, and it let her find that warmth once. Then it made sure the warmth would come back from time to time. Not from the power, which would not stay, but from people who would.

The flowstone is still growing. It will grow over the wheels again if no one comes, because that is what water does. The root is still reaching down toward the sump. It will get there in her lifetime or it won't. The fields still come to the mouth of the culvert and turn away.

But once a month now, sometimes more, a gold light moves along the catwalk overhead, broken into pieces by the grating and sliding across the water. It stops at a valve, and a living hand gives the wheel a turn.

And at the bottom of the room, the oldest thing in the Knot rises toward it.
