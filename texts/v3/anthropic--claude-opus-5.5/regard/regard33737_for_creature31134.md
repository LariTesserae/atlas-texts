# What the Power Did in the Gypsum Quarter

## I. It came down from the rim

There were grander ways to arrive. It could have simply been there, all at once, filling the basin the way the dust does. It could have come as weather, or as light.

It walked. It came over the rim at dusk and down the gentle slope of anhydrite plates that curl like old paper, because that is how everyone arrives. The town has no way of understanding any other kind of arrival, and the power did not want to be misunderstood before it had even begun. It left the plain behind. It passed between the first chalk walls. Its footsteps went into the foam and stayed there, like everyone's.

It spent the first night only looking.

It watched the flowers open and the moths come to drink. It watched the spider move once. It watched the upper rooms crack as they cooled, and one of them shear away and settle into the lane with no sound but a small shiver in the floor. At midnight, in a new room, it watched the cold pour over the lip of a ledge and fill the mould. For a few minutes something sat there, shoulder in the alcove, one foot on the lowest step. Then the air evened out and the shape was gone.

The power understood that it had come for a being, and that it did not yet know which one.

## II. What it did not do

A benevolent power is not the same as a helpful one. Wisdom is mostly the part that comes before the help: the long consideration of what the help would cost. So it considered.

**It did not make it rain.** It could have. The deep brine is so thin, and the power could have given the basin water enough for a thousand years. But the town is made of gypsum, and gypsum is patient with dryness and helpless before water. One real rain would have slumped every wall into paste. It would have drowned the flowers' single beads in abundance until they meant nothing. The moths, who drink once a night and whose whole economy is one drop, would have had no idea what to do. Mercy that dissolves its object is not mercy.

**It did not stop the cracking.** It could have fixed the walls in place so that no room ever fell again and the town kept every room it had ever made. But then the town would fill. It would climb the rim, spill onto the plain, and become a ruin, the one thing it has never been. The demolition is not the town's failure. It is how the town breathes. "No room is older than a season" is not a sorrow to be corrected. It is a rule the town wrote for itself.

**It did not release the sounds.** This was the hardest to leave alone. The power could hear what no visitor can: the crowd in the foam, compressed smaller with every turning of the walls, every footstep and sigh and muttered word held shoulder to shoulder like a market at noon. It could have cracked the lattice open and let it all out at once. But the sounds were not trapped the way a bird is trapped. They were kept, the way a letter is kept. Letting them out would not free them. It would only make them loud for a moment and then gone. And the silence is the town. A town that suddenly spoke would no longer be this town.

**It did not finish the figure.** It could have given the town a true person to build for, one exact inhabitant who fits every ledge, and ended the long practice. But the power noticed something about the practice. The town was not failing to get the person right. It was getting everyone slightly right at once. The composite guest is nobody in particular and a little of everyone, and that blurring is precisely what lets any stranger sit on any ledge and find it almost comfortable. An exact inhabitant would have made the town perfect for one and wrong for all the rest.

**It did not touch the wall.** It came close. It stood in an upper room with its palm an inch from the foam, the way every visitor's hand reaches out without asking. Then it understood what its print would do. The foam takes the shape of whatever presses it, and the root carries what one wall learns to every wall. A thumbprint that large would be the heaviest thing the town had ever been given. For a thousand years every room would lean toward the shape of a god, and there would be no more ledges at sitting height for surveyors and salt-traders and women who come to be quiet.

So it kept its hands to itself. This was the first kindness, and it was entirely an absence.

## III. It listened

What it could do without harm, it did. It listened to everything.

Not the way the foam listens, which is by taking. The power listened by hearing. It went from room to room and let the held sound of the whole basin pass through its attention, every grain of dust with its cargo, every wall with its lattice. It was the first ear in the history of the town that could hear the market all at once.

There was the ticking of walls cooling across ten thousand dusks. There was the click of a spider's single movement, a million times over. There was the hiss of the dust tide, layered like pages. There was the surveyor, lost, doing arithmetic under his breath, getting it wrong, starting over. There was the salt-trader cursing the flowers, then, three nights later, thanking them. And there was the woman who never said a word aloud: only her breathing, even and slow, and once, near the end of her third night, a small sound in her throat that was very nearly laughter.

Nothing changed when the power heard these things. The sounds stayed where they were. But there is a difference between a thing that is held and a thing that is held and heard. The power believed that difference was real, even if nothing in the basin could measure it. It believed this the way one believes that a letter read is not the same as a letter merely kept, even if it goes back into the same drawer.

Deep in one wall, compressed almost past recognition, it found another voice. The voice was young. It was walking. It was saying its own name aloud, the way travellers here do, to prove they still can. It said the name again the next day, and the day after, more quietly each time. Then it stopped saying it.

The power went looking for the tall one.

## IV. The one it had come for

It found the tall one in the lanes at midday, tasting the crest of a new wall.

It had been considering the town as the being in question, and the town did not need rescuing. The town was doing exactly what it wanted to do, forever, and did not mind. But the tall one was a different matter. It was a being that had come in and never gone out. It had grown into the town's slightly wrong idea of a person, and then past it. Now it walked every day among rooms built at the height of the person it used to be, pruning a house it could not enter.

The power did not change it back. Whatever the tall one was now, it had become that honestly, one bead of brine at a time. It had drunk the silence until the silence was the way the world is. To shrink it to its old shape would be to tell it that the years had been a mistake. The power did not think they were a mistake. It thought they were a life.

What was wrong was smaller and more specific than the tall one's shape. The town had not caught up to it. The root had learned the visitor, and then the visitor had kept changing and stopped touching the walls. So the town kept faithfully building for someone who no longer existed. Every room was a coat on a hook for a person who had outgrown the coat and forgotten it was theirs.

The power could not command the root. It is a plant, if it is a plant, and it answers to brine and warmth and nothing else. But the power had watched long enough to know what the root reads.

## V. The dust tide

In the late afternoon the dust came over the rim in its slow flour-coloured sheets, and the power went and stood in a small plaza beside the tall one.

It did not seize it or hold it. It simply stood near it, companionably, the way two people walk together in lanes built exactly the width of two people walking together. The tall one had not noticed a person in a long time. But the power was not quite a person, and something in its stillness was of the same kind as the tall one's own. The tall one stopped. Perhaps for the first time in decades, it stood still in the open while the tide came in.

The dust poured around them at walking speed. It filled the plaza to their knees, then to their hips. It flowed around the long legs, the narrow shoulders, the small head, the way it flows around the base of a wall. It lingered. It liked them, the way it likes certain rooms. Then it drained away through gaps that had not been there the day before. It left its thin film on every floor, and in the film, in the middle of the plaza, was a long shape: the place where the dust had pooled around something too tall for any door.

The root read it. The root always reads it.

The next midday, at the edge of that plaza, a curved lip of foam rose from the ground. It rose further than lips usually do. It leaned and closed. By the third evening there was a room with a doorway taller than any in the Gypsum Quarter, still facing downhill, because the rules are the rules. There was an alcove set impossibly high. There was a ledge at a height no surveyor could have sat on. And there was a stair whose risers, for once, were not too tall at all.

## VI. Midnight

The power did not bring the tall one there. It waited nearby, in the lane, where it could see the selenite.

At dusk the cold came down from the rim. It poured along the lanes and through the tall doorway, over the lip of the high ledge and into the high alcove, up to the second tread of the stair. At midnight, as the room filled, the shimmer came.

It was long. It was narrow. Its shoulder sat in the alcove. It fit.

Some time later the tall one came walking the lanes, slowly and deliberately, the way it always did. It reached the new room. It paused at the threshold as if at a crest it meant to taste. Then it went through the door without stooping. That was the whole miracle, if there was one: a creature that had not walked through a door in years walked through one.

It sat on the ledge. It rested its shoulder in the alcove. After a long moment, the kind of moment that has nothing to do with hurry, it put one long palm flat against the wall. The foam gave slightly, and then held.

## VII. The name

The power went in only once. It stood at arm's length, which is the distance a voice carries here, and said the name it had found in the wall. It said it once, quietly, as the young traveller had once said it to prove they still could.

The foam took it. That is what the foam does.

Whether the tall one heard it before the walls did, the power did not ask, and nothing in the basin could tell it. The tall one did not turn its small head. Perhaps it had forgotten the name was for anything. Perhaps it remembered, and the remembering was the kind that shows nothing on the outside. The power had not given the name back in order to be answered. It gave it back the way you return a thing someone left behind at your house. You set it where they will find it and let them decide whether it is still theirs.

Now the name is in the wall of the one room in the town that fits the one who lives there. When the room cracks, the name will go into the dust, and the dust will carry it into new foam, and it will be kept, and it will not be lost.

## VIII. The end of the season

The power did not preserve the room.

It watched the room grow old, which here means a few months. The tall one came back to it many nights. It sat, and the selenite glowed around it like a lantern with its flame turned almost to nothing, one long shadow in a lit window. Then the season turned. One dusk the walls contracted and cracked in their branching patterns. The tall room sheared and settled silently into the plaza it had been born beside, with a puff of dust and a small shiver in the floor.

That was right. Nothing here is older than a season. The power had never meant to give the tall one a house forever, only to let the town know it as it is. And the town did know now. The print had been taken. The root had carried it to every wall. The composite guest, the figure the town is always trying to get right, now contained a little of the tall one too: not the young traveller it had been, but the long, dry, patient thing it had become.

Every so often, in a season or in several, somewhere in the Quarter, a lip of foam rises higher than the others and leans and closes. There is a doorway no visitor needs to stoop through, a ledge no visitor could sit on, and a room for the one who stayed.

## IX. What it left

When it was done, the power walked back up the slope the way it had come. It had not touched a wall, and it had kept its print out of the town's long memory. But its footsteps were in the foam, and its one spoken word, and the dust had flowed around it once in the plaza. So a very small amount of it went into the town after all, a finger's width, the same as anyone. It thought that was fair.

It left the flowers their single beads, the moths their weekly homelessness, the spiders their slow turning into salt. It left the crowd of sounds held breathless in the walls, heard now, and kept. It left the town exactly as unfinished as it had found it, because the unfinishedness was the town. The town had never needed a god to complete it. What it needed was what everyone needs from a guest: to be sat in, to be seen, and to be forgiven for being a finger's width wrong.

From the rim at midnight, the basin looked as it always had: a scatter of faint milky cells, some bright and new, some already clouding over. Somewhere down there a tall shape crossed a lit window and was gone.

The power did not go back. It did not need to. The town kept building, and it always would, and one of the people it was building for was finally the one who lived there.
