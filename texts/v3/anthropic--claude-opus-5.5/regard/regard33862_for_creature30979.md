## What the Visitor Did

It came in drift mode, before dawn, when the Sill is as close to quiet as it gets.

Nobody saw it arrive, because nothing on the Sill arrives. You enter it. The visitor entered it the way the cloud does, the way the eels do: from below and from the side, without a door. If the pressure drum at the central station registered anything, it was a line so faint it could have been a tremor in the stylus. The frogs did not stop. The falls did not change their note. The turbine went on whining. The floodlamp on its pole went on turning the rain to sparks.

It had been drawn by the silence.

That is the only way to say it. There are many loud places in the world and many beautiful ones, and a power of that kind passes over most of them the way the condors hang off the rim: attending, not descending. But here, on a flat stone island in the sky where everything roared, there was a small scattered constellation of stillnesses. Hundreds of them, each one lying in a pothole with the water going quiet above it. The visitor had noticed them the way you notice a held breath in a crowded room. Then it noticed that one of the stillnesses wore a ring. Then it noticed the woman standing in the current a meter and a half above that one, doing the same thing.

So it came down to look.

---

### What It Could Have Done

It is worth saying what it did not do, because it could have done all of it.

It could have stopped the Wall. It could have held the cloud off the plateau for a year and let the pavement dry pale and bone-white under the sun. It declined at once. It saw that this would kill nearly everything, and it understood that even a single week of mercy, dry boots and sleep without drumming, was not a gift the Sill had asked for.

It could have made the Company rich again. Then there would be engineers, and survey pins driven in straight lines, and new weirs poured across the canyon-heads. It considered the lawyer's office and the small line of credit, and it saw that the Company's poverty was the best thing that had ever happened to the Tenants.

It could have healed Dorrit Vane's hands: the white ridges, the cracked knuckles, the slowness in the fingers on the wheel. It lingered over this longer. In the end it decided her hands were not wounds. They were instruments, the way the Tenants' flanks are instruments. Smooth them and you take away something she reads the water with.

It could have given the Tenants a voice. It could have let the Bishop speak and tell Dorrit what the forebay had been like in 1924, and before the weir, and before there was a word for weir. It saw that this would be for its own curiosity, and for hers, and not for the Bishop's. It saw that the one silent thing on the Sill would stop being silent, and that something precious and structural would go out of the place, like a keystone.

It could have lifted the whole plateau out of time: fixed it, sealed it, kept every bog and pitcher and hummock exactly as it was on that morning, forever.

This it understood least of all. It could not see why a person would want it, on the Sill of all places, where the ground is the oldest in the region and never the same twice. Freezing the Sill would not preserve it. It would end it. The first rule of the place is that the water decides.

---

### What It Learned First

So it waited.

It found the Bishop's den under the forebay of Weir Four and settled beside it. To a power of that kind a season is nothing, so it stayed a season. It lay in the brown water as the Tenant lay, felt the flow lean and let go, and learned the thing the crew only learn at the edges.

The water decides. But there are a few votes.

It learned that the Bishop was not trying to shape anything. It cleared its house. It shoved the grit of each Wall out of its den with that broad shovel of a head, and the stones went downstream and lodged somewhere and turned something. Over a century that one small untidy act became a finger on the flow. The visitor watched a single pebble the Bishop evicted spin for eleven days in a smaller pothole downstream, grinding it a hair deeper, and it found this very beautiful.

It learned that this was the right size for a vote. Something the size of a stone. Something the place could absorb and answer and carry on from. Something that, looked at from any distance, would be indistinguishable from the Sill simply being the Sill.

So it decided it would cast a few votes. Only a few, and only the kind the Sill already casts.

---

### The Ring

The first was the band.

Aurel Stroud had been a methodical man, not a cruel one. In 1924 he had fitted a brass ring around a foreleg that was nearly full-grown. But nearly is not the same as full, and a Tenant keeps growing, slowly, all its long life. Over a hundred years the flesh had swelled up on either side of the metal. Nobody on the plateau had thought much about what that meant, because the Bishop made no sound. The only silent thing on the Sill does not complain.

The visitor thought about it. It felt the pressure in that limb the way it felt the pressure of the current. It felt the slight wrongness in how the Bishop held the leg when it rode a flood, the tiny correction the rest of the body made for it.

It did not take the ring off. It considered doing so, and saw the dozen dates in every station log across the plateau, and the tins of peaches, and thirty-one years of a woman's attention hung on a glimpse of dark green brass. The ring had become part of how the Sill knew itself. It was a thread between the Bishop and the people, and the visitor had no wish to cut a thread.

So one night in a Wall, with the forebay roaring and nobody out on low rock, it thinned the band from the inside. It did not break it. It wore it away, the way water and grit would have done in another four hundred years, so that the inner face of the brass went back a few millimeters from the flesh. The number stayed. The 11 was still stamped on the outside, dark and legible.

Dorrit saw the band again the following spring. It was her thirteenth sighting. She lay flat on the weir-lip in drift mode with her face almost in the water, looked for a long time, and then went back to her station and telephoned every other station on the Sill. She said: *The swelling's down. On the leg. I don't know. It looks easier.* Every log recorded the date. That night there were peaches. Someone at Seven said maybe it was the band finally wearing through, and Dorrit said maybe, and nobody could prove otherwise, because that was exactly what it looked like.

The Bishop rode the next Wall a little straighter.

---

### The Block

The second vote was a stone.

That summer a boy came up the funicular. He was nineteen, with his kit in rubber bags and his matches in a tobacco tin, and he was miserable for three weeks like everyone. In his fourth week he stepped on a rock in a channel near Four, and the rock moved.

Most new crew shout. Some fall. He did neither. He stood very still in the thigh-deep water with his stave braced, watched the long pale shape slide away downstream, and said aloud, to nobody, *Oh. Sorry.* Then he stood there for twenty minutes longer than he needed to, not hearing the falls.

The visitor was lying in that channel at the time. It noticed him the way it had noticed Dorrit. Here was the direction someone was moving in, before they knew they were moving.

Six weeks later, in the first big Wall of the autumn, the boy got caught crossing a throat he had misjudged. The water came up past the hip notch on his stave, and it took him, the way it takes everyone once. He went down the channel in the brown roar, tumbling, grabbing at moss that tore away in his hands, toward a stretch of joint-grid that emptied, three hundred meters on, into a tray going off the north rim.

A block was there.

Crew who survive being taken always say a block was there, or a moss mat, or somebody's hand. This block was a low mushroom-shaped stone at the mouth of the slot. It was a little further into the current than it had been that morning, because a pebble the size of a fist had lodged under its downstream edge in the night and shifted it, by a finger's width, into the flow. The boy hit it with his shoulder, wrapped both arms around it, and held on while the water went over him. Later he said it was the luckiest thing that had ever happened to him. He was right, in the sense the Sill means luck.

He is at Four now. He works the second wheel. Dorrit is teaching him to read the forebay, and she says he is slow, which from her is praise.

The visitor did not go further than that. It did not make him stay, or make him love the place, or make him hers. It only kept him from going over, so that whatever he chose, he would be alive to choose it. What he chose was the wheel.

---

### The Logs

The third vote was paper.

On the Sill, nothing ever quite dries. Paper swells. Ink runs. The station logs sit on shelves beside stoves and slowly turn to felt: a century of rain gauges and wind readings, brass-drum pressure lines, the dates of eleven sightings of a ring, the hour a channel turned, the night a tray went off the east rim that had never been seen before. Most of the early volumes at the ruined stations have already become ferns.

The visitor read all of them, including the ones that were ferns, because a power of that kind can. It found in them something the crew had half-noticed and nobody below had put together. Over a hundred years, the lowest mode of wet had been getting a little shorter each decade. The cloudbase was creeping up the escarpment by a few meters, then a few more. The Sill lives exactly at the altitude where the warm sea's wind gives up its water. The world below was warming, and the altitude was moving.

This was the one thing it could not settle with a stone.

It could have held the cloud. That would have been easy, and it thought about it for longer than it had thought about anything else on the plateau. In the end it did not, because the cloudbase was not rising for the Sill's reasons. It was rising for the lowland's reasons, for the whole world's, and a power that simply cancelled the consequence would take away the one voice the Sill had to tell the lowland what it was doing. The visitor judged that this was not its choice to steal. The people below had to see it and choose.

So what it did was make sure they could see it.

It kept the ink from running. Nobody noticed at first. Then an archivist from the university, the same one that had the 1931 body in its basement, came up on the funicular. She came because of Dorrit's letters, and because someone in her department had begun to wonder about the rainfall records. She found that the station logs, all of them back to 1919, were legible. Swollen and warped and smelling of moss, but legible, line after line, as if the water had agreed to leave the words alone. She said it was remarkable, the oak-gall ink they must have used. She photographed every page. The century of the Sill's weather went down the cliff in a rubber bag and into the hands of people who could read what it meant.

That, the visitor judged, was the right size of vote. It did not stop the rise. It made sure the rise would be witnessed. What the lowland does with that is the lowland's own.

---

### Dorrit

And for Dorrit Vane, it did nothing at all.

It came closest to acting there, and it held back most carefully there. She was the reason it had stayed. She stood in the current every morning with her head tilted, listening, making her small constant corrections, and a meter and a half below her lay the thing she was becoming. The visitor could see, in a way she could not, how far along that road she had come. She was further than she knew. She was nearly there.

It could have told her. It could have let her know, in some way her body would believe, that she had not imagined it. When she felt the Bishop shift through the soles of her boots, she really had felt it. Every time. For thirty-one years. The pressure-wave off that great flat body had come through the concrete of the weir and the water and the rubber and the wool, and her body had read it the way the pale pits along a Tenant's flank read a crab in the next pothole.

It did not tell her, because it understood that the not-knowing was part of the listening. A woman who is sure stops tilting her head.

But on one morning in drift mode, before dawn, it lay in the forebay beside the Bishop, and the two stillnesses lay together at the bottom of the brown water. For a moment the quiet above that den was deeper than it had ever been. Dorrit at the wheel stopped hearing the falls.

She noticed herself not hearing them. Then she noticed herself not hearing the frogs either, or the drip of everything, or the penstock breathing. For perhaps a minute the Sill, which is never silent, was silent around one woman standing waist-deep in the current.

She did not move. She did not tell anyone. It is not the kind of thing that goes in a log. But that night, in her station at Four, under an iron roof in a Wall that walked the tin cups off the shelf, she slept all the way through till morning. She dreamed of nothing at all, and woke easy.

Here, now, you can be told what she never will be: she was right. It was never imagination.

---

### After

Whether it left, nobody can say.

It cast its few votes: a ring eased, a block shifted, the ink held. Nothing it did could be told apart, from any distance, from the Sill being the Sill. Brass wears. Stones lodge. Old ink sometimes lasts. The water still decides. The Wall still comes back. The cloudbase is still rising, and now someone below is reading about it.

Some of the old crew say there are more Tenants than there used to be, and that there is one near Four nobody has ever quite seen. It is a stillness in a slot under the weir, where the water goes quiet for no reason and the riffle smooths. Dorrit says that is nonsense, and that it is the Bishop's den throwing a shadow in the current. The boy at the second wheel says nothing. Sometimes, in drift mode, he stands a while longer than he needs to.

The frogs go on. The swifts pour out through the falling water at dusk, screaming. The eels ride the autumn floods to the rim and let the current carry them over. The first window of the day opens, and nobody comments on it aloud. Then the Wall comes across, and you are inside it again.
