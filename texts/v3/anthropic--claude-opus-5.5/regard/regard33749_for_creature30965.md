# The Guest at Weeping Scar

## I. How It Came

It did not come down from the sky, and it did not come up the mountain on foot. It came the way the fog comes, over the lip of the shelf from the valley side, in a slow pour, thinning and thickening. Nothing on the ledge noticed anything had changed. That was the first decision it made, and it made it before it arrived: *whatever I do here, I will not begin by being noticed.*

A power that is wise knows what its own attention weighs. Look at a thing hard enough, with enough force behind the looking, and the thing bends toward you. This place had been shaped for three hundred years by water, frost, wind and one patient tree, and none of them had ever wanted anything from it. The power did not want to be the first.

So it stood in the gate, turned sideways because the gap between the pillars is only a person's width, and it felt the night air slide past toward the drop. Then it went in.

---

## II. How Long It Stayed

It had meant to stay an hour. It stayed eleven years.

This was not indecision. In the first hour it saw that it could not know the town in an hour, or a season, or a single turn of the snow. The town does not live at the speed of a visit. A room opens and closes over a decade. A lane moves in a century. A vole lives one winter, a wren three, a wall perhaps two hundred years, and a family of fir has been alive as long as the ledge has existed. To see any of it truly the power had to slow down to the pace of the slowest thing it wanted to understand, and it could only do that by waiting.

So it waited, and it learned.

It learned the parishes of the wrens by standing in the lanes before dawn and listening to where each song died. It learned the vole runways the way the young voles do, by following them, and it came to know the latrines, the larders and the long sentence written in the floor. It spent a whole summer in one room, because one room holds hundreds of thousands of residents in its duff, and it wanted to meet some of them. It watched a pseudoscorpion the size of a comma wait nine hours for a springtail and miss. It watched a tardigrade dry into dust in a rare week of clear weather, and it waited with the tardigrade, and when the fog came back it watched it walk again.

In winter it lay under the snow in the blue dark of the tenements. It heard the dead wood crack in the hard frost. It watched the bronze scorpionflies cross the white crust at dusk, and it kept its distance from them, because its nearness was warm and warmth kills them.

It did not name anything. The residents already had whatever names they needed, which were none.

---

## III. What It Could Have Done

A benevolent power feels the pull to help, and this one felt it constantly. It was powerful enough that every pull was also an option.

**It could have saved the voles.** In its second winter it found a nursery in the soft wood of an old foundation, lined with shredded beard-lichen, with five young inside. Two nights later it found the same chamber relined in grey fur, and the stoat asleep in it. The power could have kept the stoat out. It could have kept every stoat out forever.

It thought this through to the end. Without the stoat, the voles multiply. The crowberry mats go first, then the willow, then the moss capsules. The runways, which stay legible only because they are used at the right density, blur and braid into ruts. The voles starve in a boom-and-crash, and the crash comes in winter, under the snow, where no one sees. And the stoat, the slender white stranger with its straight line of prints down the main lane, is also a resident. Its winter dens upholstered in grey fur are a hard truth about the place, but they belong to the place.

The power grieved for the five young. It let itself grieve and did not look away. Then it let the stoat sleep.

**It could have stopped the rockfall.** Each spring the young cliff drops its flakes, and sometimes a block the size of a table comes down on the old quarter and crushes rooms that took a century to grow. The power could have held the cliff still.

But the cliff falling is the reason there is a town. The ledge exists because a slab the size of a village went into the valley. The seeps run because the rock is fractured. The lanes move because ice opens new cracks. The town's high domes are fallen boulders the fir has clothed. Stop the rock and you stop the water's wandering, and then the street plan freezes. A town that cannot be damaged cannot be rebuilt, and rebuilding is most of what this town is. The fall is the town's first ancestor. The power did not argue with it.

**It could have made the wrens live longer.** In the ninth winter, the hardest it saw, the wrens of one district packed into a single old nest, nine birds in a ball the size of a fist. In the morning four came out. The power had been sitting beside that nest all night, and it had felt, very precisely, how little warmth it would have taken. It could have given it. No one would have known.

This one it thought about for a long time. It was not sure it had the answer right, and it is still not sure. In the end it did not do it, and the reason was not a rule. The reason was that the wren's life, the parish drawn by the reach of a song, the five empty houses built for an audience of one, the pressing together in the dark, is shaped all the way through by that cold night. The wrens that went into the ball went in *because* the night could kill them. The huddle is the most tender thing on the shelf, and it exists because of the danger. If the power removed the danger it would not be preserving the tenderness. It would be preserving the outward shape of the tenderness and emptying it.

That was the hardest night of the eleven years. Four birds came out in the morning. One of them sang in the grey half-hour before the next dawn. The power heard the song go down the lane and die in the wall of the next parish, exactly where it always had.

**It could have kept the town forever.** This was the largest temptation and the quietest. It could have fixed the town as it was on one particular morning, every needle beaded, every cobble gleaming in a moment when the fog thinned, and held it there. Nothing would die. Nothing would change. It would be perfect.

It would also be a photograph. The power could see very clearly that the town is not its shape. The town is the process that keeps producing a shape. A wall dies and the neighbouring family reaches across and roots into the gap. The fungus glows as it eats the ancestors and turns the old quarter's memory into soil. A seep shifts, and the pale cold flatworms glide down the new crack before the old lane closes. Stop any of that and you have kept the house and killed the family.

## IV. The One Thing That Was Not the Town's

By its seventh year the power understood that almost everything that threatened the town was the town. The stoat, the rockfall, the killing winters, the dying walls, the wandering water were not harms done to the place. They were the place happening. Keeping it from them would be a strange sort of kindness, the kind that loves a river and dams it to keep it safe.

But there was one thing that did not belong.

It showed up in small ways at first. The snowfield above the cliff, which feeds every seep, lay a little thinner some springs than the old ice-lines on the rock said it once had. The meltwater running through the cracks came out, in some summers, a shade less cold, and the pale flatworms under the stones, which cannot live in anything warmer, had withdrawn from the two lowest lanes. Down below the lip, in the valley-side cloud, the first real trees were a few paces higher than the dead stumps of their predecessors.

This was not the mountain's doing. It was not the fall, or the frost, or the wind. It was coming up from below, from a world much larger than the ledge, and it was not part of the town's becoming. It would end the town from outside. The seeps would slacken. The lanes would stop freezing, and the fir would close them. The snow scorpionflies would vanish. The town, which lives at the precise edge where woody things give up, would find the edge moving up the mountain faster than any family of fir could follow.

The power could have stopped it here. It could have held a bubble of the old cold over three hundred paces of ledge and let the rest of the world warm around it.

It did not, and this refusal was different from the others. It was not a matter of letting the town be itself. It was that a single ledge kept cold under glass while every other mountain shelf, every snowfield and cold spring on earth went on warming would not be care. It would be a keepsake. The benevolence the power felt for Weeping Scar was not favouritism. It was one instance of something that belonged to every such place equally. What was harming this shelf was harming all of them, and it had a source. The source was not on the mountain.

So the power understood that the one thing it could rightly do for the town could not be done in the town. It would have to go down.

What it did in the valley, and in the cities past the valley, among the people whose lives were warming the mountain, mostly without meaning to, is a different story, and a longer one. That story has harder people in it. It also has patient ones, and some of them had already begun. It is not this story. This story is only about what the power understood on the ledge: that sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a place is to leave it alone and go deal with the thing that is hurting it, wherever that thing actually lives.

## V. What It Did Do

It did do some things at Weeping Scar. They were small, and none of them changed anything, which was the point.

**It walked the lanes.** The town has the shape of a place where people might live, with rooms nobody made and lanes nobody walks. For eleven years the lanes had one walker. It stepped on the cobbles and never on the moss risers. It turned sideways at the gate, because the gate asks that. It sat in the great domed rooms near the cliff, sank to the ankle in rust-brown duff, and breathed the smell of resin and old needles. Nobody needed a guest. The power went on being one anyway, because it seemed to it that a thing shaped so exactly like a home should be treated like one at least once.

**It put its palm on the fracture.** At the back wall, where the crisp lip of the break has barely begun to soften, it laid its hand flat against the schist and held it there. It was not giving anything or taking anything. It was touching the place where it all began.

**It watched the deaths.** Every one it could reach: each vole, each wren that did not come out of the winter ball, each wall that browned from the inside out, each springtail caught at last by the patient comma of a pseudoscorpion. It did not stop them. It also did not let any of them happen unseen. For eleven years nothing died on the shelf without someone there who knew it was a someone.

**It learned how the town is kind.** This surprised it. Underneath the fighting families, beneath the walls so dense you cannot get a finger into them, the fungal threads run root to root across every border. A dying wall is fed by its neighbour for a season. A seedling in deep shade is kept alive by the stems around it. The families that will not give up an inch of ground above share sugar and water below.

The power recognised this. It was a good deal like what the power itself was trying to be: a presence underground that does not take sides in the struggle above, and quietly keeps the weak alive for as long as it can without breaking the pattern that makes them strong. The town had been practising benevolence for three hundred years without a single mind in it. The power had come to help, and it found itself taught.

---

## VI. The Gift

In its last spring it watched the snow drain from the lanes and the runways come up on the cobbles, that faint handwriting, and it thought about memory.

No vole remembers. The runways remember for them. No wren remembers the parish boundaries of its grandparents; the reach of the song redraws them every year. The fir families remember in their dead, the silver-grey storeys stacked under the living green, and the glowing fungus is eating that memory as fast as it can, because that is how the old quarter becomes the future. Water forgets its old courses. Fog remembers nothing at all.

The town does not keep its past. It turns its past into the present and lets it go. That is not a flaw. It is how a place that is always being built stays built.

But the power was a being that could remember. So this was the gift it left, the only one it thought the town could receive without being changed by it: it would hold what the town could not keep.

It would remember the five young in the nursery and the grey lining that replaced them. It would remember the wren that sang on the morning after the hard night. It would remember the ten days when the stair-risers flowered silver, and the tardigrade that dried to dust and came back, and every room that opened and closed while it was there. It would remember the exact sound of the night wind in the main lane, and the knuckle-crack of the dead wood in hard frost, and the blue-green veins of light in the ancestors' heartwood.

Someday, whether by the slow warmth or by a greater fall, the shelf-town will end, as everything on the shelf ends. When it does, it will not have passed unknown. Someone saw it whole, cared for it, and changed nothing.

This gift is for no one. The voles will not know about it and the fir will not feel it. The town was made for no one at all, and the power found it fitting that its gift should be the same kind of thing: complete, careful, and addressed to nobody.

---

## VII. Leaving

It left the way it came, through the gate at night, turned sideways between the two pillars that three hundred winters of wind have carved. The cold air was draining off the snowfield and sliding past toward the drop, and the power went with it, over the lip and down into the cloud, toward the valley, where there was work.

Behind it the town dripped. Every eave kept its own rhythm. A vole crossed a cobble along a line its great-great-grandmother had worn. Deep in the tenements a wall that had been dying for a season was being fed, very slightly, by its neighbour. At the back of the shelf the cliff wept across a broad face of black liverwort, and somewhere in it a crack was opening that would, in a century or so, move a street.

In the grey half-hour before dawn a wren sang, far too loudly, and the song went down the lane, reached the wall of the next parish, and stopped.

Nothing on the ledge noticed that anything had changed, and nothing had. That was the last decision the power made, and it was the same as the first.
