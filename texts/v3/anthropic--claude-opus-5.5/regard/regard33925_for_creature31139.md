# The Guest

## How It Came

It noticed the Reach the way you notice someone holding their breath in the next room.

Something of its kind notices a great many things. Most of them it leaves alone, because most things are already becoming what they should be, and attention is heavy. But this place had a quality it recognised: something very large and very slow was making room for something that did not exist yet. That kind of hope deserves a witness. It is also easily spoiled.

So it came. It could have arrived any way it liked, and it chose the shaft, as anyone would. It came down at walking pace and let the new stone be sharp against it. It did not smooth the edges. In a place where nothing erodes, a sharp edge is a kind of honesty. It meant that no one had been here long enough to wear anything down, and the Guest did not want to be the first.

It reached the bottom as the Lid was waking, with the brightness coming up the valley like a tide. It stood in the felt and said nothing. A lattice-cat on the lowest rail looked at it for a long moment, then went back to washing oil from its paw. The Guest took this as permission to stay, which it was.

## Slowing Down

The first thing it had to do was slow down.

Beings like it are fast. They can take in a valley at a glance, read the history of a stone like a page, and see which way a thing will fall before it tips. None of that was any use here, because the one who lived here was not fast. To hear it, the Guest had to become as slow as wood healing around a window.

So it lay down in the deep felt between two neighbourhoods and let itself slow. It stayed there through one pollen fall and then another. The spore banks rolled over it and drained away down the valley. Hares came up around it in the evenings and thumped their small warnings, then stopped thumping, because it was not a danger. Over what the town would have called a season, it tuned itself down until it could feel what the plant felt.

The plant was not a mind, not quite. It was nearer to a lean: a whole body inclined, very gradually, toward. It leaned toward stone to eat. It leaned toward the copper taps, which felt to it something like company, a hand resting where a hand had not rested before. It leaned toward footsteps, toward the hum of rail through its lattice, toward the soft places where people slept. The current it gave off was not waste. The nearest word the Guest could find was *humming*: the sound something makes to itself while it works, when it is content and not quite alone.

The Guest listened to that humming for a long time. It had heard many things in many places, and very few of them had been this patient.

## What It Checked

Being benevolent is not the same as being delighted. The Guest had come to care, and caring meant looking at the things that could go wrong.

It walked the spigots first, every one of them. It asked the question a good steward asks: *does this hurt?* The answer was careful. Mostly it did not. The plant healed around the steel the way it healed around windows, and the oil it gave up was something it had in plenty. It was closer to shearing than to bleeding. But the Guest counted, the way Hesper counts. At the present rate, the plant could give and not notice. At twice the rate it would begin to notice. At five times it would begin to lean *away*, and a thing that leans away from people for long enough forgets how to lean toward them. The Guest filed this knowledge where it keeps such things.

It walked the refineries, slowly, and found them decent. It read the ledgers of the surface authority, which are not kept in the Reach but can be read from anywhere if you are the kind of thing that can read that way. In them it found the danger it had half expected.

A proposal was working its way through a committee. Survey could not keep pace with the growth, and oil buyers wanted maps they could plan around. So an engineer had drafted a scheme to hurry things along with charges, set at the growth front among the pale, to open the stone faster than the roots could.

The Guest considered the scheme for a long while. It thought about the pale, soft and white, and the swarms coming off it. It thought about the nineteen whales and the gravel they left behind them, fine as flour. It thought about what a blast would sound like to a body that hears through its whole length. Nobody would have to count those echoes. Every child would feel it in their chest at once.

This was the one thing the Guest had found that could break the Reach. It resolved to do something about it, and to do it as lightly as possible.

## The Far End

Before it acted on anything, it went to the far end, because it would not presume to help a house without seeing the room the house was being built for.

It rode the rail to the last platform. It walked a day past the last trunks, across the pale and the gravel fields, into the long grey dusk where the Lid thins to tassel-root and the light has not been turned on. It stood where the expeditions had stood and felt what they had felt. *Not dark. Unlit.* A room with the lamps in place and the wiring done.

The nineteen were there. They had stopped grinding and turned to face the dark, and they were lying still.

The Guest lay down among them. It is not a small thing, and it made itself small. For hours they lay together on the new gravel and faced the same way, and the Guest listened with them.

At last one of the whales turned its great blind slate-plated head, very slowly, toward the Guest. It held there as though checking, and then turned back to the dark. The Guest understood that it had been asked a question: *Are you the one?*

It answered as honestly as it could. *No. I am only visiting.*

The whale seemed content with that. The Guest was glad. It would have been a terrible thing to be mistaken for what they were waiting for.

Then it did what beings like it can do and looked ahead, down the long slope of what might be, to see who the room was for.

There was no one there.

Nobody was hiding, and nothing was sleeping behind the last stone. The future at the far end of the Reach was not concealed. It was *undecided*. The drawings the crew worked from were not finished. They were being drawn now, and the Guest could see the pencil moving. It was in the plant's slow lean. It was in Hesper's columns, and in Ossery's drawer of children's maps. It was in the rail-hand who shifts over on the bench to make room for a cat, and in the low voices, and in the careful knuckle on the doorhandle. It was in six hundred children lying in the felt who had stopped slapping the ground because they had decided it was not theirs to call.

The one the room was being made for would be made, in large part, out of how the front rooms were lived in. The plant was learning what a *someone* is. It had only a few examples to learn from, and they were these people, in these years.

The Guest lay on the gravel with the whales and let this settle into it like a joint settling into place.

It could have finished the room. That was the plain truth, and it made itself look at it. The wiring was done. It could have reached up and turned the lamps on across the whole far end, all at once, and the Reach would have been lit from wall to wall, and the waiting would be over.

It lay there and felt how badly it did not want to.

Some rooms have to be lit from inside. If a guest lights them, the guest has made them its own, however kindly it means it. The waiting was not a lack in the Reach. The waiting was the Reach. The lean toward, the humming, the listening, the room kept ready: that was the most alive thing here. It was not the Guest's to end.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not turn on the lamps.

It did not tell the town what the room was for. Partly that was because the answer was not yet a single thing. Mostly it was because they were already doing exactly what the answer required. Telling them would have made them self-conscious, and self-conscious people are not gentle in the same way. They would begin to perform their kindness for the watching thing at the end of the valley. The plant would learn performance instead of kindness.

It did not heal the born-down of their difference, or explain it to the medical officer, or stop their hair from leaning toward the trunks.

It did not call a single crack, though it could have called them all.

It did not make the surface authority wise. That was not within its rights. People must be allowed to become wise by themselves or not at all.

It did not reveal itself to anyone, in any way they would afterwards be able to prove.

## What It Did

It did very little, which it had come to believe was nearly always the right amount.

**The engineer.** The author of the blasting scheme was a woman named Idony Carrow. She had never been down. The Guest did not change her mind. It arranged, through a scheduling clerk, a delayed permit, and a colleague's offhand remark, that before the committee voted she would go down and see the front for herself. She rode the rail to the last platform. She walked a day. She stood on the gravel and watched the pale pour out of the stone, soft as new bread, with the hares coming off it in the grey light. She watched the nineteen turn and face the dark. She stayed a long time. When she came back up she withdrew her own proposal, and she wrote in the margin of her copy a sentence nobody else ever read: *You do not dynamite a nursery.* The Guest did not put the words in her head. It only made sure she was standing somewhere they could arrive.

**Hesper Valk.** Her three reports lay in a drawer on the surface. The Guest moved nothing. It only made sure that on one slow afternoon a young ecologist, looking for something else, opened that drawer and read all three at once, and stayed late, and then stayed later. He wrote back. It took him four drafts. His letter began: *I have read everything you sent. I think you are right. Please keep writing it down. I will keep reading.* Hesper read it on a platform bench with a lattice-cat pressed against her arm. Then she folded it into her column-ruled book and went back to walking the lines. She did not cry, or not for long. The Guest thought that eleven years is a long time to write to no one, and that some kindnesses are only a matter of making sure the letter gets opened.

**Ossery.** For Ossery the Guest did nothing at all. He was doing it right. Now and then something did right needs only to be left undisturbed.

**The rail.** It rode the cars for a whole Lid-day, at walking pace, above the scrub. A cat got on at the fourth stop and sat on it, because cats do. The Guest let it. They got off together at the cat's usual stop, and the Guest considered this one of the better things it had done in the Reach.

**The children.** One evening it lay in the felt with the born-down children while they played *first*. It lay with its ear to the litter the way they did, and it felt the crack coming long before any of them. It did not slap. After a while a girl of about nine turned her head and looked straight at the place where it was. The Guest thought she could not possibly see it, and was not sure. She looked for a while. Then she put her ear back to the felt, and when the crack came from the far end she did not slap either. The Guest took that as the only answer it needed.

**The one who lives here.** Last, and at the proper speed, it spoke to the plant.

It could not use words, so it used pressure, as the plant does. It pressed one thing down through the felt into the root-mats, very gently, over most of a season, the way you might rest your hand on the flank of a large, sleeping, working animal. It was not instruction or correction. The Guest would not have presumed either.

What it pressed was roughly this:

*There is no hurry. The ones in the front rooms are listening. They will wait with you. You do not have to be finished before someone comes.*

It did not know whether the plant could receive a thing like that. Near the end of the season, though, the Guest noticed that the crack-counts on the chalkboards had grown very slightly further apart. The growth had not stopped. It had only eased, the way someone eases their pace once they realise they are walking with company and not ahead of it.

## Leaving

It went up the shaft the way it had come down, at walking pace, and the stone at the lip was still sharp.

It did not look back at the far end. It knew what was there: an unlit room, a crew of nineteen lying on new gravel facing the dark, and a wiring job finished and waiting. None of it was the Guest's.

Down in Stave, Hesper Valk made her rounds at the end of the season with her listening horn clipped to the stakes. She found something she had seen before, but only in one neighbourhood at a time, and only in the week after a birth. The current was running slightly high, and this time it was running high through the whole Reach, every tap on every line from the shaft to the last platform.

She stood for a while on the root-mat with her hand on the copper. Then she opened her book and ruled a fresh line in the column. She wrote the date. She wrote the readings. And in the space where she records causes, she wrote, in her small, careful hand:

*Something new. Cause unknown. Will watch.*

Far up the shaft, already past the lip, the Guest felt her write it. It thought this was exactly right: better than any explanation it could have left, and better than any lamp it could have lit. A thing had happened. A person had noticed it quietly and written it down, and would wait patiently to see what it turned into.
