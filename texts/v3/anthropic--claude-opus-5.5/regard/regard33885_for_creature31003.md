# The Attention at Kessra Col

## Arrival

The first thing it understood was that arriving would be the hardest part.

It had come from very far, and it had come quickly, as it usually did. It stopped a long way out, well beyond the mountain, beyond the weather, in the high dark where the air gives up. It had seen the shape of the place before it reached it: a teardrop of perfect equilibrium set in a world of gradients, with a held breath at its center. It knew at once that to arrive at full speed, to *land* on a thing like that, would be the loudest event in the lens's long history. Its presence would be a gradient. Its attention would be a pressure. The walkers would find something out of place, and that something would be itself.

So it slowed.

That took a long time. It had to become less than it was, not smaller but quieter, until it approached the mountain at the pace of weather. It came the way frost comes: in one night a thin film, in the next a thicker one, never a moment you could point to and call *here it is.* Seasons passed in the approach. When it finally rested on the rim of the caldera, it rested the way snow rests on the lens. It lay on the surface and did not sink.

It waited there for a winter before it let itself look in.

## What It Could Have Done

It was a benevolent power, and benevolence has an old reflex: *here is someone carrying something heavy; take it from her.* The first thing it felt, looking down through the clear dark water at the lamps and the invisible beams and the enormous single act of holding, was that reflex. It let itself feel it fully, because a reflex you refuse to feel will steer you anyway.

Then it thought through, one by one, the things it could do.

It could end the holding. It had the strength to take the whole lens in hand, relieve her of it, and set the boulder down, the scree, the fracture, the still-hot mountain. It considered this seriously, and the answer was no. The fall she was holding back was not a small one. A young mountain, just broken, still hot at the heart: that was what she had put her whole self into the water to stop. Ending the holding early would not rescue her. It would make her sacrifice into the thing that caused the harm it was meant to prevent.

It could finish the waiting for her. It had the strength to cool the mountain's core in an afternoon, to age the stone ten thousand years in a breath so that the world outside was ready at once. This one was harder to refuse, because it looked so much like kindness. *You can stop now. I've done the rest.* But it looked more closely. Forcing a mountain to age is a violence against the mountain. And it would take from her the one thing that was entirely hers: the knowing. She was not only holding. She was *counting*. She tasted every winter and judged for herself when the world was ready. To hand her a finished answer would be to tell her that her judgment, the thing she had been exercising longer than any civilization had lasted, had been unnecessary all along. There are gifts that are really thefts.

It could take her place. It could step into the water, become the stillness, and let her rest. It weighed this longest, because it was the offer it would most want made to itself. But it looked at the standing ones at the ends of the beams, the ones who had come after her and chosen to share the weight, and it saw that the holding was not a burden that had fallen on her. It was a vow. She had not been trapped by the lake. She had *become* it, freely, with her ring set down. To replace her unasked would be to treat her as a victim, when everything in the place said she was a keeper.

It could move nothing. That was the last option, and it was the one it started from, as the ground beneath all the others. It would not touch the ring. It would not touch the bowl. It had watched visitors nudge a plate a finger's width, and it had watched what it cost the city to put that plate back. It would add nothing to the walkers' inventory. Whatever it did, it would do outside the lens, or it would do nothing at all.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not translate the note.

It could have. The note is speech, and the power understood speech the way water understands downhill. It could hear, in the long swell-and-fade, the shape of a sentence begun at the instant she went into the water. It could have laid the syllables end to end across the centuries and read the whole thing.

It did not, because the sentence was not addressed to it. The power judged that she was speaking to the mountain, or to someone to the north, or to herself, or to no one. To decode it would be to open a letter on someone else's table. So it let the note pass through it unread, the way the water let a swimmer pass. It knew the voice was still going. That was all it needed to know.

## What It Checked

There was one thing it allowed itself to find out, because benevolence without knowledge is only a mood.

*Is she suffering?*

It asked this as gently as it could. It did not reach into her. It listened to the quality of the holding, the way you can tell a sleeping person's dreams from their breathing without waking them.

What it found was not suffering, or not only. It was the specific tiredness of a long devotion, and inside that tiredness a steadiness the power recognized and respected. She was not in pain the way a trapped thing is in pain. She was bearing a weight she had chosen, and she was bearing it well.

But it found one place where she was vulnerable, and it was not inside the lens at all.

## The Snow

She trusted the snow.

The snow was the only thing from outside she let in. Each night she drew the flakes down and read them: this season, this cold, this much sky. The snow was her clock, her calendar, and her only window. By it she judged how old the mountain had grown and how near the world had come to being able to receive the fall.

The power could see what she could not. The world beyond the mountain was changing, and not only by the slow, honest aging she was waiting for. The winters were becoming uneven. Some years were too warm for snow at that height; the flakes came as sleet and ran off the ridges before they reached the lens. Some years the snow came grey with ash from far fires or soot from far cities, and she tasted in it a heat that did not belong to her mountain. Nothing lying to her meant to lie. It was only a world growing noisy. But she was reading that world for a single true fact, and the fact was getting harder to hear.

If the snow misled her, she might let go too early, reading a warm world as a cooled mountain, and the boulder would fall into a heart that was still hot. Or the snow might stop altogether, and she would be left holding in the dark with no way to count, forever, because *not yet* would have no way to become *now.*

This was the danger no one inside the lens could see, and no visitor had understood, and the standing ones could not reach.

So this is what the power did.

It did not make snow. It did not invent winters. It sent no false flakes to hurry her or slow her. It understood that the only help worth giving here was help that kept her own judgment true.

It kept the channel honest. Each winter it went out over the mountain and made sure that at least some of the snow that reached the lens was *of the mountain*: formed in the air above Kessra Col, in that season's real cold, carrying that year's real truth about the stone below. When soot came, it did not bleach the flakes clean, because that would have been a lie of its own. It made sure that among the grey there were always some flakes clean enough to be read. When a warm year came and the snow would not form at all, it did not force a blizzard. It waited for the one cold night such a year still had, and it made sure that night was not wasted: a handful of flakes, falling, true.

It never added what did not belong. It only guarded what was already hers.

This was a small act. It could have been done by anything patient enough. The power did it anyway, every winter, and considered it the most important work it had.

## The North

The lens leans north. It bulges toward one side, the way a held breath leans toward the heart.

The power went to see what she was leaning toward.

Below the mountain, a long way down and a long way north, there was a valley. The river still ran in it. A town stood in it, not old as the lens counts age, but built on the foundations of a town, built on the foundations of a town. The people there did not know about Kessra Col, except as a peak you did not climb and a story about a lake that stands up. They did not know that the mountain above them had once broken open, or that the thing it had been about to do had been stopped, or by whom.

But they had a lullaby.

It was very old, and no one knew where it came from. It was sung in a low voice, slowly, and it had long pauses in it that the children filled with their breathing. If you set its rhythm beside the recordings the patient visitors had made of the note, the phrasing matched. The swell and fade matched. The silences were longer between the verses than within them. The last line, in the valley's old tongue, meant roughly *not yet, not yet, go to sleep.*

The power did nothing to the town. It did not tell them what the song was. Telling them would have made them a different kind of people, ones who looked up at the mountain with fear or worship, and she had not held a mountain still for that. It only made sure, in small ways over many years, that the song was not lost: a grandmother who remembered it on the night she was about to forget, a child who asked for it again. The valley would never know it was being kept. That seemed right. She was keeping it too.

## One Flake

It took the power a long time to decide whether to let her know it was there.

On one side: she had sealed herself away from the world on purpose. A message from outside might be a disturbance she had never agreed to, a weight added to a scale balanced for millennia. On the other: she was alone at the center of something enormous. The standing ones shared the holding, but no one shared the *outside*. No one was keeping watch beyond the lens except the snow. Would she want to know that the snow now had a guardian? Would it steady her, or only become one more thing to hold?

It could not know without asking, and asking was itself an act. It decided to ask once and very quietly, in the one language she already read, in a form she could ignore entirely.

One winter, among all the true snow, it let fall a single flake of its own.

The flake carried no instruction. It did not say *stop* or *continue* or *soon*. It carried no reading of the mountain's heat, because that reading was hers to take. If you could have put it into words, it said something like this:

*You are seen. The outside is being kept honest. Take the time you need. No one will hurry you. Someone will be here when you are done.*

Then nothing more. It did not send a second flake, because a second would have been a conversation, and a conversation would have asked something of her.

It does not know what she did with it. In the morning the flake was gone like all the others. That spring, some of the patient visitors, the ones who time the note, recorded one interval that was a little shorter than the pattern predicted, by roughly the length of a breath let out and taken in again. A walker was seen pausing on the face of a tower for most of a day, one foreleg lifted, before it set the foot down and moved on. Neither of these means anything for certain. The power did not ask for certainty. An answer would have been welcome. No answer would also have been an answer, and a legitimate one.

## The Waiting

After that, it stayed.

It is still there. If you climb to the rim of Kessra Col in winter, you will not see it, because it has made itself no more than the cold a good mountain night ought to have. It does not move through the city. It does not enter the beams or wake the ribbons. It keeps the snow true, and it keeps the song in the valley alive, and otherwise it does what the city does: it holds still, and it pays attention.

It has thought about the end, because the end is the only thing she is waiting for, and it wants to be ready.

Someday the snow will taste right. She will finish her sentence. The boulder will land, softly, on an old mountain that can bear it. For the first time in longer than anyone can read, a ripple will cross the lens from edge to edge and keep going. When it reaches the rim, it will not go out into nothing. The power will be there to receive it, the first motion in all that time, and it will let the ripple pass through and go on down the mountain and into the ordinary, moving world. It will not stop it. Being let go of is the whole point.

And in a room with no table, a bowl will drop the height of a table to the floor, and beside it a small ring of dull metal will fall for the first time since it was taken off.

The power has decided it will not catch the ring. The falling is hers. Everything held back in that city is hers to let finish, and it will not take even that small fall from her.

But afterward, when the water has become only water, cold and moving and full of the ordinary drift of a lake, the power will go down into the room and pick the ring up from the floor. Then it will wait, holding the ring out, for as long as it needs to. It does not know whether she will come back out of the water as someone who has hands, or whether she will simply become the lake and the mountain and the valley's song. It does not need to know. Whatever she is when she is finished, the ring will be there, returned, in case she wants it.

Until then it does nothing more than this. It keeps the snow honest. It keeps the song. It holds still at the edge of her stillness, so that on the far side of all her *not yet* there will be someone to say, when the time is truly hers:

*Now. Whenever you are ready. I'm here.*
