# What Came to the Condensary

## The Attention

It did not arrive the way you arrived. There was no climb and no platform, and no coat came off in the warmth. The power had no hands to be told anything by new brick. It noticed the place the way you might notice, halfway through a sentence, that someone across the room has gone quiet. Something in the world was being attended to, carefully, and the power turned toward that care.

It was your looking that it noticed first. You lay on your stomach for hours with your face to the grate and the heat on your eyelids, waiting through the eighty seconds again and again until the dark had a texture. That kind of patience is rare. It goes out from a person like a thin thread, and something large can follow it back.

So the power came up your thread, after you had gone down the path and looked back once. It found the circle of rain in the white and settled over it the way weather settles. It did not do anything yet.

It had learned long ago that the first thing a benevolent power wants to do is almost always the wrong thing. It wants to help, and helping is a kind of weight.

---

## Load Not to Exceed

The stencils were the first thing it read, before the ferns, the fins, or the breath.

*LOAD NOT TO EXCEED*, in clean white paint on every girder, copied faithfully night after night by a creature that could not know what the words meant. The power stayed a long time with that phrase, because it understood the phrase was addressed to it.

A power's attention has mass. Where it looks, things bend a little toward being looked at. Plants lean. Animals grow wary or strange. Water forgets which way it was going. Any seed of intention the power carries tends to sprout in whatever it rests on. The power had seen many places made beautiful by its notice and then slowly made into something else: shrines, attractions, places that were only about the fact that they had been noticed. It did not want the Condensary to become a place that was about the power.

So before anything else, it made itself light. It thinned its attention until it was no heavier than the cloud and no warmer, and it moved at the cloud's speed. It let the fins pass it through their pierced plates the way they passed everything else. It let itself be drunk.

This took some time. The power was in no hurry. It had noticed that nothing here was in a hurry either, except the ferns.

---

## Learning the Count

For a while it only kept time.

The breath every eighty seconds. The vapour rising and the cloud coming down to meet it. The rain arriving inward in slanting veils, the *tik* of water on iron, the hiss across the terraces, the weeping walls. The bell-flowers blinking. The iron ticking as it cooled. The power had counted the turning of galaxies and found them less intricate than this. That was not because the Condensary was more complicated. It was because everything in the Condensary depended on everything else, and nothing was wasted, and the whole of it was small enough to hold at once.

At night it watched the mantle rise.

It saw what you could not: the pale flesh coming up through the drum, fog-coloured and thinner than a leaf. It saw the mantle pause at each terrace as if touching the plants on the way, and reach the rim and spread along it the whole way around, a living lip on a living lip. It saw the two folds press iron between them. It heard the radula draw its long curved stroke around the aperture. It saw the seeds go into the mortar one by one, not scattered but placed. Each was set in the course at a particular depth and a particular interval, like a sentence being written.

It saw the number stamped. *4,218. 4,219. 4,220.*

The power could have read the creature entirely: its memory, its drives, its small warm dim mind that knew the count and the rhythm and the wetness and the upward pull. It chose not to. That would have been the heaviest looking of all. It wanted to know the keeper the way you had come to know it, from the outside and slowly, by what it made.

---

## The Temptations

It is worth setting down what the power did not do, because it considered each of these things, and some of them were lovely.

It did not finish the building. It could have raised the rim through the last of the cloud in a single night and shown the keeper the sun. That would have taken away the only thing the keeper had ever done.

It did not bring animals. The spaces were not for guests. The power had understood that as quickly as you had, and without needing four days.

It did not heal the old trees at the bottom, or stop the roots from cracking the brick. The cracking was the start of the eating, and the eating was the start of the next course. Erosion here was a form of food.

It did not give the keeper words. It did not explain what *LOAD NOT TO EXCEED* meant, or *C-14*, or offer any better sign than the circle with three lines. The creature's copying of human stencils was not a mistake to correct. It was how this animal had learned what a wall looked like, and every young thing learns that from what was near the nest. The power had its own inherited phrases, and it did not know what all of them meant either.

It did not make the keeper aware of it.

It did not make more of them.

It did not decide what the Condensary was for.

---

## Down the Foot

Then it did go looking, because a benevolent power that only admires is not much use. It needed to know whether anything was wrong.

It went down the pier into the white, along the grey cold column of muscle, further than anyone had followed it. The cloud thinned and thickened and thinned. The foot went on, gripping and shifting in its slow travelling wave. Then the cloud ended, and there was sea.

The foot stood in the sea on something old.

It had been a tower once, a human one, square-sided, poured concrete gone the colour of bone. On its flank, half under the water, was the first stencil: *C-1*, and beneath it *LOAD NOT TO EXCEED*, and a number the power could still read. The number was very small compared with what now stood on it. Along the waterline were the rusted stubs of something: fins, plates, a ring of old pierced iron, much corroded. Someone had built a condenser here once to pull fresh water from sea fog. Someone had abandoned it. A small grey snail had climbed it, grazed its walls for years, and learned it.

The power felt something close to fear, if powers feel fear. The old concrete was cracked through. The load had been exceeded thousands of times over.

Then it looked closer and saw that the keeper already knew.

Around the old tower, down past the waterline, the foot had laid shell. It was shell, not brick: layer on layer of pale nacre, secreted in the dark where no one would see it, sheathing every crack and running down below the waves to whatever the old tower had stood on. The human structure had not been holding the Condensary up for a long time. It was held inside the keeper's work, the way a grain of grit is held inside a pearl. The creature had not failed to read its load rating. It had read it, in its own way, and built accordingly.

The power stayed there a while, in the cold water, beside the first stencil. It was a moment of great relief, and also of correction. It had come down ready to save something, and found the thing had been saving itself for 4,220 nights.

It did not reinforce the base. The base did not need it.

---

## The Other Circles

Then it went to the other circles, the faint one you saw and the one you thought you might be imagining. You were not imagining it. There were more beyond those, far out across the cloud sea, some of them very far.

It visited each one lightly and read the numbers on the fins.

The nearest was young, a few hundred nights old, low in the cloud. Its garden was mostly moss and fingernail ferns, and it had no snails in its warm channels yet. The power understood that this one had once been one of those snails. It had been carried out somehow, down the outside and across the water, or on the foot of a parent, or in the gut of the bird that sometimes sits on the fins for ten seconds and leaves. It had started its count at one.

The third was old, past nine thousand. It was taller and further up the cloud, and its lowest trees were enormous and twisted, and its breath was slower.

Some were dead. The power had known it would find this, and it found it. Two circles had no rain falling in them. Out of the white rose pale, cold, empty drums. One had stopped at some mid-height. Its shell was intact and its garden was brown lace, and its foot had let go at last, so it stood on its old pier the way a shell stands on a beach after the animal is gone. The other had reached the top.

The power spent a long time at that one.

It had cleared the cloud. Its rim stood in open air, dry blue, under full sun, and its last fins were bleached and brittle. The count on the topmost fin stopped at a number a little over thirteen thousand. The power could read in the brick what had happened. The breath had risen into dry air and met no cloud, so no rain came back. The garden dried from the top down in the direct light. The keeper had gone on breathing for a few days, sending warm vapour into a sky that did not return it. It had sealed its aperture with a thin membrane of shell, the way snails seal themselves against drought. Then it had waited, and the cloud had not risen to meet it, and it had died behind its own door.

---

## The Oldest

The power almost turned back then. It was grieving, and it had begun to think that the climb itself was the danger, and that perhaps the kindest thing was to stop it.

But there was one more circle, the furthest of all, and the power went on to it.

This one had also cleared the cloud, long ago. The count was past twenty thousand, and it was still being stamped.

Above the cloud, in open air, it had made its own cloud.

A small white cap sat over the rim, fed by the breath. The garden grew inside it, above the sea of white, in a private weather. The terraces near the top had changed. The plate-leaves were larger, and new plants grew there that the lower keepers did not carry, plants with narrow silver leaves that could stand some sun. Moths flew through the cap-cloud in their unsteady spirals. Along the upper ledges, the wide ones, young keepers rested with their shells still soft. Every so often one went over the edge and down the outside of the tower toward the cloud, and the sea, and the start of its own count.

The power read the fins around the moment of clearing. They were stamped more thickly than any others, as though the keeper had built harder in those nights. The power could see what had made the difference, and it was mostly luck. The oldest keeper had broken through the top on a night of heavy weather, in a season when the air above the cloud was still damp. For five or six days the sky had given its breath back to it. That was long enough for the first cap-cloud to form and hold, and for the garden to learn the new height.

The dead one had broken through into a dry week.

The keeper's work had been the same in both. The difference was the weather on a few particular nights, which no snail could plan for.

---

## What It Did

So the power went back to the Condensary, to your Condensary, and settled again into its breathing. It made the only promise it made there, and it made the promise to itself, because the keeper had no way to hear it.

When the rim clears the white, on whatever night that is, some thousands of nights from now, the power will be there. It will do one thing. It will keep the cloud near for a little while. It will not lift the building or water the garden, and it will not stay any longer than the oldest one's luck lasted. It will hold the cloud for five nights, perhaps six, with the air damp above the rim, so that the breath comes back as rain for as long as it takes the cap to form. Then it will let go.

The keeper will not know. It will think, if it thinks at all, that the weather was kind. That is exactly the right amount for it to think.

The power made the same promise for the young circle, and the old one, and all the others it found. It could not promise that none of them would fail. Some will stop at mid-height for reasons that belong to them. But no keeper of this kind will again climb for thirteen thousand nights toward the top of the world only to meet a dry week by accident. The power decided that much luck was its to give.

---

## And One Other Thing

Before it thinned itself back into the cloud, the power did something smaller, which it could not quite justify and did not try to.

It found you.

You were somewhere far below by then. You had gone down the path and out of the white, and you were telling someone, or not telling anyone, which is also a way of keeping something. The power did not change what you remembered. It did not make the path easier or harder to find. It did not make you forget, and it did not make you sure.

But on certain nights, when the fog came in thick wherever you had gone, you would find yourself breathing more slowly without meaning to. It was not quite eighty seconds, but close. For a moment you would feel the heat of a buried hearth through soles that were not touching it. You would hear, very faintly, a long curved stroke drawn all the way round the lip of something, like a fingertip around the edge of a glass.

It was not a message, and it was not a gift you would recognize as one. It was only the power's way of saying what it had come to believe watching the keeper build: care is how the world is held up, and the load it bears is heavier than anything written on it.

Then it went back to counting with the keeper.

*4,221.*

The rain fell inward. The ferns uncurled. At the bottom, a root cracked a single brick, and the brick crumbled, and fell through the grate toward the heat, to be carried up in the night and laid down again at the rim, still wet, with a seed in it.
