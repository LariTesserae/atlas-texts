# What the Visitor Did in Cantle

**Coming Down**

It could have arrived any way it liked, so it chose to walk.

This was its first decision, and it made it carefully. A power of that kind can appear anywhere: in the sky above the ring as a light, or all at once at the center of the basin. But it had been watching Cantle long enough to understand that the Sward notices things by how they arrive. Herons come on wings, eels come up from underneath, and crabs come sideways along the levee. People, the few who have ever come, walk down the long soft slope from the last stone. The visitor did not want to arrive as something the Sward had no category for. It wanted to be legible. So it took a shape near enough to a person's, and it spent a whole morning covering the few meters of fall. It stopped at the mossed boulder that looks like a sleeping animal and put a hand on it, because that seemed polite.

It is worth saying plainly what kind of power this was, since that matters to everything after. It was benevolent, which is common enough among powers. It was also wise, which is rarer, and the wisdom consisted mostly of one habit. Before it acted, it asked what the thing in front of it was *for*, and whether it was already doing that. It had learned, over a long time and a number of mistakes it did not like to remember, that care which does not ask this question is only appetite wearing a kind face.

---

**The Temptations**

It stood at the ring-levee with its palm on the warm bark. The slow breath rose and fell under its hand, and the fast flutter of the clock ran beneath that. It let itself feel every urge it had, one at a time, so it could look at each one before it did anything.

*Restore it.* The plans were eroded. Somewhere in the clock the original templates were still recoverable: the capitals, the lettering, the crisp corners at the bottom of the crab-midden. The visitor could reach in and sharpen them all at once. In an hour Cantle would stand as its makers had imagined it, whole and precise and beautiful.

It put that urge down first, because it was the easiest to see through. Restoration would evict everyone. The heron-doors would square off and the heron would stand outside them, puzzled. The frog-ledges would lose their gutters, and the eel-rooms would get floors. The nectar in the lamps would dry. A town tuned perfectly to a population that does not exist would be a museum, and the only museum Cantle needs already exists, kept by crabs who do not care that it is one. Erosion is only a failure if you are measuring against the original. Measured against the living, the drift was the best thing the Sward had ever done.

*Bring them back.* It could find people who wanted a place like this. There are always people who want to be kept. It could lead a population down the slope and fulfill the promise at last.

This one it held longer, because it was kinder. In the end it put it down too. A population brought in to *complete* something is being used as a part. And the town was not incomplete. It had tenants, thousands of kinds of them. If people came to Cantle, they should come the way the observers did, on their own feet and for their own reasons, knowing the offer and its price. The visitor could not improve on that arrangement. It could only spoil it by making it a destiny.

*Wake it.* This was the hardest urge, and the visitor felt it most keenly. The Sward was a disposition of care with no one inside it to know that it cared. The visitor could give it that: some interior, a mind, a self that could look at its frogs and its swifts and its sleeping turtles and understand what it had made. Surely that was a gift?

The visitor thought about the bench. A mind would understand the bench. A mind would know that whoever sat there was gone, and would feel it. The Sward keeps that seat now the way water keeps to downhill, without grief, because grief requires someone to have it. To wake it would be to hand it, as its first conscious experience, the full weight of a loss it has been carrying without cost for ten thousand thousand cycles. A wise power does not give a creature a self for the purpose of letting it be sad.

*Answer the bench.* Find out who sat there. Bring them back, or bring back something of them, and end the waiting.

That urge it did not put down right away. It said to itself, *first find out if it is waiting,* and went to look.

---

**Listening**

It spent a long time just being in Cantle. That is the part of the story nobody would find dramatic, and it was most of what happened.

It kept moving, because that is the rule, and because it did not yet want to be housed. It learned the surges from the slumpers, going quiet when they went quiet. Once it was too slow, and the lawn rolled under it and threw it into a pool, and a gutter-bull regarded it with a flat gold eye while it climbed out. It lay its ear against the levee at night and heard the wingless crickets singing on the in-breath. It watched a turtle walk out of a wall, blink, and choose another doorway, and it felt something it had no better word for than *respect*.

Then it made itself small and dark-sighted and slick, and went down into the veins with the white eels.

The eels did not mind it, or did not notice. The passages were warm and close and loud with water. The visitor went down past the mat, past the pads and bladders, into the long plumbing where the Sward pulls up the deep table. There, at a depth where the hum was no longer something felt in the teeth but something the water itself was made of, it found the clock.

The observers had guessed right about the loop: *what is here, what is used, what shall I build next*. They had guessed wrong about what the clock was doing with it. It was not computing, not really. It was *counting*. Every pulse was a tally. A turtle in a wall: here. A swift in a cup-niche: here. Four hundred slumper eggs in a digestion pool: here, here, here. A crab asleep in a stolen doorway on the levee, out of reach but not out of sight: here. The clock was a census, and it had been taking the census for so long that the counting had become the heartbeat.

The visitor listened to the count for what would have been, above, several days. It noticed two things.

The first was that the number was large, and had been rising for a very long time. Whatever the observers imagined about the Sward being alone, the Sward itself did not keep a column for *who is missing*. It kept a column for *who is here*, and that column was full. There is a way of being alone that is only visible from outside, to someone who expected different company. The visitor had come half-prepared to rescue the Sward from loneliness. It found instead an innkeeper with every room let, counting its guests in the dark with perfect contentment.

The second thing was the woman. Deep in the count, among the old tallies, there was a long run of pulses from one room in the colonnade hall: eleven years of *here*, steady. The visitor followed it the way you follow a thread. It did not find where she went. The census does not record departures, only presences. But it found the last of her pulses, and the way the count ended. It did not stop abruptly. It thinned, the way the count thins when a turtle is waking. Whatever happened, she was not taken from the room. She left it.

The visitor kept that. It did not carry it up to the observers or to the villages beyond the levee. Some stories are better for the not-knowing, and this one belonged to the people who tell it. But the visitor was glad to know it, and it was a gladness it did not need to spend.

---

**The Lintel**

When it came back up it went to the crab-midden and, very politely, to the deepest pit.

The crabs objected. It waited until they stopped. Then it looked at the lintel with the lettering on it, the one no one had matched. Powers of its kind can read most things, and it read that.

It will not be written here what it said. The visitor decided that for itself, and the decision seems right. It can be said that the words were not a warning, or a secret, or an instruction to the machine. They were the ordinary kind of thing a town writes over the door of its public hall. It was the sort of sentence that means *you are welcome here* without quite saying it, the sort that goes over doors precisely because anyone might walk through them.

The visitor stood in the pit for a while after it read it. It was thinking that the Sward had forgotten the letters and kept the sentence. Every rounded door, every heron-shaped opening, every nectar-cup in a lamp was that lintel, spoken so many times it had become a behavior. The inscription had worn off the stone and into the town. There was nothing to restore. The meaning was fully present. Only the alphabet was gone.

It climbed out and let the crabs have their pit back. One of them waved its great claw at it in the slow semaphore. The visitor did not try to decode the gesture. It waved back, slowly, which seemed to satisfy both of them.

---

**The Bench**

It went to the bench last. It did not go in the afternoon.

The bench faces east, and a bench faces east for one reason. The visitor had noticed that the frogs and the heron came in the afternoon, when the light was sharp and ordinary, and that they sat facing east because the bench made them. But whoever had worn that dip had sat facing the sunrise.

So the visitor came in the first grey of the morning, when the mist lay thick on the basin and the town was only pieces. It did not sit down immediately. It knelt and looked at the wear the way the observers had looked at the midden, reading layers, because wear is a kind of stratum too.

What it read was this. The dip was not the mark of a population, or of a city's worth of evenings. It was the mark of one person, at one hour, over a long time. The polish was all at the front edge, the way it is when someone sits forward, leaning a little, watching for something. Whoever sat there had come down the slope before any of the town existed. They had planted the seed, or tended it, or simply been sent to wait for it. They had asked for one thing, a place to sit at dawn, and the Sward had made it. Then they came every morning and sat in it and watched the east, where the people were supposed to come from.

They were the only person who had ever used anything in Cantle. The whole city was built for thousands. It was used, truly and repeatedly, by one: a single bench, at a single hour, by someone who was watching for company that never arrived.

And the Sward had kept it. Not because it was waiting, the visitor understood, and not because it was grieving. It had kept the bench because *it had been used*, and what is used is kept, and the Sward has never had a way to stop counting a tenancy that was real. It was the Sward's first *here*, perhaps its first pulse. The whole census had been built on top of that one tally the way the crab-town is built on its oldest shanty.

The visitor considered what it could do. It could find that person, or what remained of their pattern in the world. That is within the reach of some powers, if not all. It could bring back a trace and set it on the bench, and the Sward would count it, and the waiting would end.

But the bench was not waiting. The visitor was sure of that now. The observers could not tell the difference between waiting and remembering from outside, and they had wondered whether there was a difference at all. From inside the census, the difference was clear. Waiting would be a column for *who is missing*, and the Sward did not have one. It was remembering, perfectly, without pain: a promise that had been kept so completely it had become a fact.

There was nothing to fix. There was only one thing the visitor thought it might offer, and even that was not a correction. It was closer to company.

It sat down.

It sat forward, at the front edge, the way the wear said to sit, and it faced east into the mist and watched for the sunrise. It did not try to do anything else.

The Sward noticed. Of course it did. The hum under the mat changed. It did not grow louder. It grew *closer*, as if the whole machine had turned toward the center of the basin. Across the lawns, the lamps on the orphan street opened all at once, with that sound like a crowd drawing breath, though no one was walking there. Thirty paces off, a blister began to rise out of the Sward, and the visitor could feel, through the soles of its borrowed feet, the old template climbing up out of the clock: square doors, a chair with a leaf on each armrest, a hall for a person.

The visitor did not stop it. It also did not stay long enough for it to finish. That was the whole of its wisdom that morning: to be here, fully, for as long as the sun took to clear the levee, and not one hour longer. It wanted to be a tally, not a tenancy. It wanted to be one real *here* added to the count, a second morning in the long run of mornings since the first watcher's last, and then to leave the way the woman left, thinning out, so that the Sward would record a presence and never an absence.

The sun came up. It was, the visitor thought, a very good sunrise: gold on mist, and then gold on water, and then every wet wall in Cantle lit at once. Swifts poured out of the held tower into it. It understood why someone would have come here every morning to see this, even alone, even watching for people who never came. Perhaps they had not minded as much as the story assumes. Perhaps the sunrise had been enough some mornings. It is possible to sit at the front edge of a bench, leaning forward, and be watching for nothing in particular, the way herons do.

A slumper had climbed up beside it while it watched. When the visitor finally stood, the frog moved over, without hurry, into the warm dip where it had been sitting. It sat there small and speckled in a space made for someone larger, facing east, not minding.

The visitor watched this and felt that it was right.

---

**Going**

It left by the orphan street.

It had not planned to, but the lamps were still open from the morning, and it seemed a shame to waste them. It walked down the middle of the seed-husk cobbles at a walking pace, between the flower-heads, past the gutter-bulls, who boomed at it. Black beetles ground husks underfoot, and finches scattered ahead. The street ran its sixty paces from nowhere to nowhere. When the visitor reached the end of it, it did not stop. It kept walking, out across the open lawn toward the levee and the slope and the last stone. For once, someone had walked that street from somewhere to somewhere else, which is what it was for.

Behind it, the hall that had begun to rise near the bench faltered, softened at the roofline, and went back into the Sward. The chair with the leaves on its armrests dissolved into steaming water. The slumpers arrived for it within minutes. The doors elsewhere in the town, which had squared a little toward the visitor while it was there, began to narrow again toward herons. The template sank back down into the clock, unharmed and uncorrected.

The visitor changed nothing in Cantle. It restored no plans, woke no mind, brought no population, and answered no mystery it did not have to. It considered this, walking up the slope, and was satisfied. It was not the satisfaction of restraint for its own sake, which is only a subtler vanity. It was the satisfaction of having asked what the thing was for and found that it was already doing it.

The town was built to welcome whoever came. Whoever came was frogs, and swifts, and eels in the dark, and crabs who keep an archive by accident, and turtles who have understood hospitality better than any guest since the first. The town welcomed them all, and counted them, and kept what they used, and learned their shapes. It kept one bench for the one person who had ever needed it, not in hope but in fidelity, which is a steadier thing.

If there was a gift, it was only this. For one sunrise, the census under Cantle counted someone who could hear it counting. In all the ages of the loop, *what is here, what is used, what shall I build next*, the question had never once been heard by anyone who understood it was a question. Now it had been. The visitor sat in the answer for an hour and let itself be counted. It did not say anything back, because the Sward does not need to be told that it is good. It needed only what it has always needed: to be used, and to keep what is used.

At the mossed boulder the visitor stopped again and put its hand on it, the way it had coming in. Then it went on up out of the basin and was gone, and the Sward did not record it leaving, because the Sward does not record departures.

In the afternoon, the heron came and stood on the end of the plank. The frogs were in the seat. The light was sharp and ordinary on the wet lawns. The town rose and fell all around them. Somewhere under the mat, among ten thousand thousand pulses, there was one more *here* than there had been, tucked in at dawn beside the first.
