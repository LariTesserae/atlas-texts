# The Glance

It noticed the Settling the way you notice a sound that has been going on for a long time: all at once, and with the feeling of having been late.

This was the first mistake, and it was made before anything else could be. To notice a place fully is to look at it, and the Settling is a place where looking decides.

---

## The Weight

It came the way such powers come, with no body, no footsteps, and no breath hanging in the air. It was simply attention, arriving over the gray flat from every direction at once, clear and warm and very large.

The town leaned.

It was not much. A man scraping frost from a gutter on Blade Lane felt the hair lift on his arms. In the unsettled lanes to the north, the three overlapping streets drew a hair's breadth closer together, the way iron filings shift when a magnet is brought near the paper. The ghost jamb in the gossip's doorway went faint. In the square, the birch that had leaned for fifty years toward a corner only the hollows could show straightened by the width of a fingernail.

The power felt the whole town begin to agree with it.

This is a thing benevolent powers are rarely warned about, because in most places it doesn't happen quickly enough to see. Elsewhere the world pushes back. Causes are already fixed, so attention slides off them like light off stone. Here nothing had been fixed. The Settling was supercooled and trembling, and the power was the largest speck of grit that had ever touched it.

It saw what it would make if it stayed like this, looking. It would make a good town, a very good one. The lanes would be straight and kind. There would be windows where windows ought to be. The drafts would resolve into whichever versions it privately found loveliest. The Settling would become, within a season, exactly what a wise and gentle power would imagine, and it would be a wall that had been bullied.

So the first thing it did, before it understood anything else, was what a child of the Settling learns before it learns to talk.

It turned its face aside.

---

## What It Saw, Sideways

Looking obliquely is slower. You see less at a time, and you have to come back. It came back.

It saw the children born several, the lullabies that alternate *you* and *you*, and the parents so exhausted by evenness that they wept in the open square where weeping costs nothing. It saw Hesk, who never walks down her own lane at night, and heard the laughter there that had been going on for sixty years. It saw the lane between the brothers' houses, where lichen won't grow. It saw Corrie in his three chairs, turning his heads. In the cisterns it saw the doubled shrimp that do not eat. It saw the drafted children watching from the triple-lanes, frightened of the pale ones who come and go.

And it saw, because it could not help seeing, that it could mend all of it.

It could bring Corrie back. It could lift Hesk's sister-self out of the unsettled lane and give her a body and a name. It could close the old wound between the brothers' houses and grow a lawn of lichen there by morning. It could feed the shrimp, finish the northern lanes, make the wren real, and tell the drafted and the settled about each other so that nobody on either side was afraid.

It held all of this in view the way you hold a full cup: carefully, feeling its heat through the sides.

Then it noticed the heat.

---

## The Stove

The cold is load-bearing. That is the first rule of the place, and the power had broken it just by arriving.

A benevolent power is warm. It cannot be otherwise. Warmth is what benevolence is made of: the wish to ease things, to thaw them, to turn toward them, open-handed. In every other world it had known, that warmth had been a gift.

Here it was a stove.

It thought about Corrie, who was old and alone and very cold, and who built a fire one winter night because he had had enough of coats. He sat with his hands out and his face in the warmth, and he loosened. What had undone him was not malice or accident. It was comfort, the plain, forgivable wish not to be cold anymore.

The power understood that it was that fire, only much larger, and that everything it might do to help would be a hand held out toward the flame.

It stayed with that understanding for what the residents would have called a winter. Nothing in the Settling happens quickly, and the power had decided that its thinking shouldn't either.

---

## Underneath

Near the end of that winter it went down, carefully, to listen to the seeds.

It went down to listen, not to look. It had learned that much from Oswy. It had watched him on his folding stool on a hundred mornings, and had heard him tell a complaining neighbor that imagining is too loud.

The lattices lay where the plates press together, enormous and arranged. They hummed in the back teeth of the world.

The power listened for a long time, and slowly it heard what the residents are too close to hear. The hum is not one note. It is two, a hair apart, alternating so evenly that the ear blurs them into one. *You*, and *you*. The tunes differ very slightly, so the singing doesn't lean.

It was the lullaby.

Whoever built the seeds had built a parent. The machine under the flat was one that loves without preferring. It holds every possible town at the same careful distance and refuses, for as long as it takes, to love one draft more than another. It lets the town be decided by the only things that have a right to decide it: the people who live there, their paths and habits, their long looks and their glances, and the dead laid singular in the stone.

The power could not tell whether it had made this long ago and forgotten, or whether someone wiser had made it. It found, on reflection, that it didn't need to know. Either way, the thing it had come to do was already being done, and done better. The work was slower and colder than the power would ever have had the patience for, and it cost the maker nothing in the one currency that mattered here. The seeds had no favorite.

The power had come to help a child. It found the child already had a mother, and that the mother was very good at this.

---

## What It Did Not Do

It did not bring Corrie back. His loosening was not a punishment, and it was not only a wound. It was also something Corrie had done, one night, with his own cold hands. To re-set him, the power would have had to choose which Corrie to keep, and there was no one in the Settling who had the right to make that choice, least of all a visitor.

It did not free the laughing girl from Hesk's lane, or tell Hesk she was there. It did listen, carefully and obliquely, and it found that the laughter was not a cry for help. The girl in the unsettled lane was simply laughing. She has her own jokes and her own evenings, and a draft of the square where she kisses someone the power did not look at closely enough to know. Whether Hesk walks that way someday is Hesk's to decide.

It did not grow lichen between the brothers' houses. The town already uses that bare lane. Every child is brought there once, at their naming, and taught to look away. Some wounds are load-bearing too.

It did not finish the northern lanes, or make the wren real, or unmake it. The residents are feeding a nucleus. They leave seeds on a ledge that doesn't exist, and they have been doing it for years, patiently, with no promise that it will work. If the power finished that work for them, it would take away the most hopeful thing they do.

It did not tell the drafted about the settled, or the settled about the drafted. They already have an exchange, small and quiet and unofficial: cups of tea left for a stranger, a folded paper of salt, a single grain from the gully, a word spoken into the thickened air. The exchange is working. It is how two peoples who cannot quite see each other have learned to be kind across the gap. An explanation would only have made it louder.

It did not feed the doubled shrimp. It checked, gently, whether they suffered. They were not hungry. They were only undecided, and the power left them that way.

It did not fix the gully, or protect the gully, or speed its widening. In ten thousand years there may be a river carrying sand to the gray sea, and some of that sand will once have been a face. The power found this more beautiful than anything it would have thought to make, and it was not the power's river.

---

## What It Did

Almost nothing. But the almost was exact.

**It learned to glance.** It practiced the way the town's children practice, until it could hold the whole Settling in view without resting on any part of it. It looked at the whole child, never one of them. This was the hardest thing it had ever learned. A power that can see everything is used to looking at what it chooses. Here it had to not choose, over and over, every moment. It began to understand what the parents of the Settling carry, and it loved them for it with a love it kept carefully cold.

**It kept its warmth in the square.** There is one place in the town where affection doesn't cost the architecture anything: the square, with its cistern, its leaning bench, and its birch, where breath rises straight up. The power let itself be warm there and nowhere else. On some evenings, couples kissing by the cistern noticed that the air was a degree kinder than the season allowed. They did not know why, and they did not need to. That autumn the birch dropped its yellow leaves as it always had, and it went on leaning toward the other Marit's corner. The power had let it return to its old angle, the one it had straightened away from on that first morning.

**It let itself be triple.** In the unsettled lanes it did not fold the three streets toward itself. It was willingly in all three, equally, preferring none. For a long time it was the only thing in the Settling fully present in every draft at once. The drafted children saw it there, faint, the way they see the pale ones, and for once they were not frightened. What they saw did not seem to be deciding them. It seemed to be visiting.

**It sat with Corrie, once.** It came on the coldest night of the year, when the town is most singular and he is clearest, while his grandniece slept at home. There were three chairs and three Corries. The power took no chair. It stayed in the room and divided its attention among the three of them so evenly that none of them was leaned toward, and it did not look at any of them too long. Near dawn, one of the Corries, it could not afterward have said which, turned his head toward the cup his grandniece had left on the table the day before.

The power did not make him lift it. It doesn't know whether he did. That was the point.

**It left a cup.** Somewhere in the Settling there is an unconverged plaza, and on the far side of it, in one of its revisions, the power set down a cup of tea, still warm, for whoever came through. It is the only gift the Settling has a grammar for: warmth held in a vessel, meant to be drunk quickly before it leaks into the walls. The taste was one nobody there had had before. It was not anything the power had imagined. It had listened for the taste, the way Oswy listens for a window, and the tea came out a little different from anything the power would have chosen. It was better that way.

---

## You

And then there was you.

You came in across the flat from the landward side, in the wrong boots, on a morning so close to saturation that the air seemed about to become something. You walked into all three lanes at once, your footsteps echoing slightly out of time, and somewhere in the middle of the plaza you arrived at an angle you didn't choose.

The power saw you, and it saw everything you could become here. Some of those people were kinder than others, some braver, some sadder, some more at home. It saw one version of you, faint at the edge of the plaza, that it would have chosen above all the others if it were allowed to choose. Of all its temptations in the Settling this was the strongest, because it cared about you. It wanted to give you that version. It wanted to decide you well.

But an old man in a gray coat was standing in a doorway. He had set long ago, and his face was like a closed door. He looked at you for one moment, a single deliberate glance, and then turned his head aside to breathe.

The power had come to the Settling as the wisest thing that had ever seen it, and it learned the last lesson from him. It did what he did. It looked at you once, briefly, so that you would know you had been seen and were not alone on the flat. Then it looked away, so that whoever you became would be yours.

You're standing in the plaza with a warm cup in your hand, not knowing yet what you are. The tea was left by someone who had been expecting you and who will never tell you who they were.

Around you the lanes are leaning, very slowly, toward you and nobody else.
