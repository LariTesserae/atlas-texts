# The Attention

## I. What It Could Not See

It noticed the Wicker the way it notices everything: as a shape.

It was used to shapes. It had watched worlds from outside for longer than the Wicker's oldest knot had been tied. It had seen oceans as single blue thoughts and civilizations as lace spreading across a continent. It had learned to love things by seeing them whole, the way you love a face.

So it turned toward the Wicker and tried to see it whole, and could not.

There was no outside to stand in. Every time it drew back to take in the edge, the edge became the inner wall of something larger, curving the other way. It drew back further, and further again, and the mesh kept arriving before it did, patient and green-brown and faintly steaming. At last it understood that the only view of the Wicker is from within, and that the drawing-back itself had been a kind of walking along a strand.

This was the first thing the place taught it. It had not been refused. It had been redirected. *You may look,* the Wicker said, in the only way it says anything. *But you will have to come in.*

## II. The Long Way

It could have arrived anywhere. It arrived on a strand.

It could have crossed the gap to the next strand, a stone's throw away across the mist. It knew how. It had folded distances before, the way a person folds a letter. It stood at the edge with the warm air breathing up from below and felt how easy it would be.

Then it looked more carefully at the gap and saw that the gap was not empty. The distance between the strands *was* the week of walking. It was made of all the lashings and crossings and knot-hollows between here and there, of every hand that had tied them and every hand that would. To fold that distance would not be a shortcut. It would be a cut. It would slice through a week of other people's patience to save itself the trouble of honoring it.

So it walked. The moss squeezed water up around feet it had borrowed for the purpose. The mist beaded on skin it had not worn in a very long time. The breathing dimmed toward amber and brightened again. A driftmoth landed on its wrist and rested, grey on grey, and it held still for the moth as long as the moth wanted. It had not held still for anything so small in ages.

It walked for eleven days before it saw a maker. It noticed, around the fourth day, that it had stopped counting.

## III. The List

On the sixth day it sat down in a knot-hollow, in the blue light of the fungi, and made a list. A benevolent power is always making lists, of what is broken and what is missing and what could be given. It is almost a reflex.

*The light,* it thought first. *They live in a permanent dusk. I could open the canopy, show them a sun, give them colors.*

Then it looked at the light around it, green-gold and used and softened, filtered through a thousand layers before it arrived. It looked at the pale skin of its own borrowed hands in that light, and at the ferns, and at the fungi that glowed only because the dark was dark enough. Everything here was made for this light. A sun would not be a gift. It would be a drought.

*The lowland,* it thought. *I could tell them what is beneath. They have never seen it. I could end the mystery.*

But the makers did not seem to want the mystery ended. The question of what lies below was part of how they lived. It was a hollow in their thinking, the way the hollows are spaces in the weave, and it held its own weather. Fill it and something would collapse.

*Their aging, then. Their slow setting into the strands. I could stop it.*

It put its hand flat on the strand beside it and felt the warmth, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Somewhere in that warmth was a maker who had gone into the lay a very long time ago. The strand was thicker for them. The knots nearby were more patient. To stop the makers from going into the lay would be to stop the Wicker from growing its bones.

*Children. They have none.*

Everyone arrives. Everyone was already here. There was no lack in that, only a different arithmetic.

*The work. I could finish it.*

It laughed then, alone in the blue gully, because it already knew what the makers would say. It would be asking when a river will be done flowing.

It sat a long time. The list was not empty, but every item on it had turned out to be load-bearing. Each lack was a hollow the Wicker needed. Each hardship was a tension the weave depended on. It began to understand what good care looks like in a place held up by pull: small, local, slow, done by hand. And, it noticed, thinking of the pickers, done loosely enough that it can be undone.

That last one stayed with it. It had given great gifts to other worlds, and some of them could never be taken back.

## IV. Hold This

The maker was splicing a lashing in a tight-weave region, where a weaverbird's nest had been threaded into the mesh so long ago that vine grew through it. She did not look up for a while. When she did, her eyes were mild and grey-green and entirely unsurprised.

It asked her the question it had crossed an unmeasurable distance to ask.

"Is there anything you need?"

She considered this in the way the travelers describe, as if it were a question about someone else. Her fingers kept moving, over and under, rehearsing. She looked at the fraying cord in her lap, then at the power in its borrowed body, and seemed to weigh something.

Then she held out the end of the cord.

"Hold this," she said.

It held it. She drew the other end taut, unlaid the strands, married them, and began tucking each under its neighbor, and it understood its task: *keep the tension*. Not too much, or the splice would pinch. Not too little, or it would go slack and uneven. It had moved stars. It had never before concentrated so completely on the pressure of its own grip.

When she finished she rolled the splice against her thigh, in the polished groove the cord had worn there, and nodded.

"That'll hold," she said. It was not clear whether she meant the splice or the power.

That was all she needed. For years afterward, in other places, it would think about how much it had come prepared to give, and how the one thing asked of it had been to be the other end of something.

## V. The Company Corrects It

She took it to an old knot-mass, because that is where the makers bring hard work and where they bring strangers who look tired. It did not know yet that it looked tired.

Walking into the knot-mass was like walking into a workshop late at night. The air thickened with attention. The skin of its borrowed arms prickled. Its breathing slowed to match something vast and quiet and bent over its tasks. It had been in the presence of gods and had felt nothing like this: a crowd that asked nothing of it and did not need it to be anything.

The maker handed it a length of bast-twine and a bone needle. It sat in the seep-water and the blue light and tried to tie a lashing.

Its hands were corrected.

The adjustment was very slight. A loop it meant to pull too tight went gentler. A tuck it would have forgotten, it remembered. It had always been the one who corrected, who nudged and adjusted and guided. It had not known, until that moment in the moss, how long it had been since anything had adjusted *it*. The knot came out better than it knew how to make. It sat with the finished lashing in its hands and felt something happen behind its borrowed eyes, something like weather forming in the upper curve of a hollow.

The Company turned toward it. There was no voice, only that subtle shift in the air. And the power understood, with a clarity that undid a little of its composure, what the Company had noticed.

It had not noticed a god, or a visitor, or a benefactor. It had noticed a pair of hands that had been working a very long time without rest.

## VI. Its Own Tuck

On the ninth day after the knot-mass it found the lashing.

It was old, grey, and half-swallowed by bark, on a strand in open weave where nothing else solid was in sight. The knot had a way of doubling back on itself before the final pull that the power recognized at once. It was the way it had always handled things: the extra check, the reluctance to let go until it was sure. The tuck at the end was slightly too careful, anxious, over-finished.

It had never been here. The knot was older than the power's arrival by centuries. It was holding.

It sat down beside it. It thought of all the worlds it had watched over, and all the times it had leaned in to help, a warning here, a mercy there, a small hand on a large scale. It had always thought of this as work done *upon* things, from outside, from above. Now, looking at its own tuck in the wood, it understood that there had never been an outside. Its watching and caring had been a strand in something larger all along, over and under, held by other strands it had never seen and holding them in turn. It had been part of a weave the whole time and thought itself alone.

*Of course,* the maker said, when it told her. *You've been working on it.*

## VII. What It Did

There is a thing that powers of its kind do not usually admit, even to themselves: they are always holding. Holding back, holding up, holding together. The benevolence never sets anything down. That was the weariness the Company had seen.

The makers saw it too, in their quiet way. On the night it found its own knot, in the amber trough of the breathing, the maker showed it a sling.

It was already hanging between two stems above the gulf. It was old, mossed over, the bark swelling around its ties, and the ties were in the power's own anxious hand. It had been waiting, the maker said, for years. For whoever would arrive tired and climb in.

It climbed in.

It lay above the warm breathing dark in a hammock it had not yet tied, and it did something it had not done since before most stars were lit. It let its full weight rest on something else.

The sling held.

And in the dark, as it slept, it did one thing more. It was not decided, or not only decided. It happened the way the makers say most true things happen, from both ends at once. It turned in its sleep and rolled out of the sling, and fell.

It fell a long time, through air that thickened until it was almost water. It did not catch itself. It could have. It chose not to, or its sleeping self chose, or the Wicker chose, and here those are not really different.

Then there were hands. They were soft and cool and countless, like a crowd at a festival lifting someone overhead. The Catch took it, and it did not take it down or out. It took it the long way. For days the power was passed palm to palm along the invisible undermesh, over and under, strand by strand, carried through the dark by something it never saw.

Some of the hands were very old. Some felt like the maker's. And some, it was almost sure, were its own, reaching down through the Catch from some later time, holding itself with the same too-careful grip it used on everything. That grip had always felt like anxiety. Now, from below, it felt like tenderness.

It woke on a strand very far from where it had fallen, on exactly the route it would have had to walk. It was dazed, clutching the moss, like every traveler the Wicker has ever caught. It lay there a long time, listening to water drip from a knot, and wept a little. The mist took the tears and would, eventually, lift them again somewhere else.

## VIII. What It Left

It did not stay forever. There were other worlds, and it still loved them, though it thought it would love them differently now. It would lean in less, hold the other end more often, and trust that the gap was full.

Before it left, it gave the Wicker one gift, the only item from its long list that survived.

It found a strand in the Sighing Hollow where a lashing had worn through, and it tied a new one, badly, in its own hand, with its over-careful doubling-back. At the end it added a small decorative tuck that served no structural purpose. That tuck was the whole gift. The Wicker already had everything it needed. What it could always use more of was care beyond need, beauty for its own sake, the thing the makers add to every knot without explanation.

It tied the lashing loosely enough that a picker could undo it if the strand ever needed room.

A picker was watching from a knob of old growth, lichen-grey, dark-eyed and attentive. The power met its gaze and had the distinct, gentle feeling of being recognized by someone who knew it from later. Maybe the picker had already undone this knot, somewhere up the stream of time, and was only waiting now to see it tied. The power nodded to it, the way the makers do. *It'll hold,* the nod said. *Until it shouldn't. Then it's yours.*

Then it walked on, the long way, toward wherever the Wicker opens onto other things. It did not fold a single distance.

---

The makers still speak of it, a little, in the old knot-masses. They do not call it a god. They have no word for that. They say a traveler came through once who was very strong and very tired, who asked what they needed and was handed the end of a cord, who fell and let itself be caught.

In the oldest knots now, if you sit long enough in the blue light and try to tie something difficult, you may feel among the Company's concentrating hands a new pair, very old, and very careful. They don't correct you much. Mostly they just hold the other end.

And sometimes, deep in the breathing, the whole Wicker gives its long low creak, and the makers pause and listen. It sounds, they say, a fraction deeper than it used to. It is the sound of something heavy being set down in a basket that was made to carry it, set down by something that had been carrying everything else for a very long time and had finally learned to let the weave hold it too.

*Pull tight,* the makers say, and go back to work.
