# What the Guest Did

## I. Learning to Sit

It came the way weather comes to the Hightable: slowly, and from a long way off.

The first thing it did was nothing. Attention like that is used to moving quickly. It can take in a world between one thought and the next, weigh it, and make up its mind. But it had been drawn here, and it suspected the drawing meant something, so before it looked closely at anything it made itself slow. It slowed until one of its thoughts took the length of a piston stroke in the great engines of Vessary, one knock a minute. It kept slowing until it could see a flywheel turn, and then until it could see a stilt-hunter's single step as an event with a beginning, a middle, and an end.

*Nothing is fast*, the place said. The power took this as the first thing it had been told, and it obeyed, because it understood that a guest who arrives in a hurry has already been rude.

Then it sat down at the table and looked.

## II. What It Saw

It saw a people whose whole ethic grew from the plain fact of being fed by something they did not make. This moved it more than it expected. It had visited many worlds whose people believed they had earned everything. It had visited others whose people believed they were owed everything. Here was a people who believed they had been *given* everything, and who answered that belief with neither pride nor grievance. They answered it with manners. They turned the empty jug over. They let tears dry on their hands. They put their dead back into the cistern and called it service.

It saw the quiet catastrophe too.

Every city was eating its mountain. Everyone knew it, and no one panicked. That was admirable. It was also the particular danger of a slow people, because a slow people can watch the end approach for a thousand years and call their watching patience. In the oldest city, Vessary, the living city stood on a shelf of rock above a hollowed mesa of pillared halls. The shelf was now thin enough that the bore-wardens could feel it flex, very slightly, when the Drift's winds leaned on it. In Vessary there was an old woman named Tamsa Orrel who held the title that translates as *the one who knows how much is left*.

She had gone fully to bronze. In the amber light her face shone like the pavement at dusk. Once each year she went to the great knocking-stone at the centre of the city and knocked the count: so many centuries of stone, at the present rate of quarrying, before the shelf could no longer be trusted. The whole city stopped and laid its palms on the walls to feel the number arrive. Then it went back to work.

The number that year was three hundred and forty. When Tamsa's predecessor had taken the office, it was four hundred and ten. The kiln-folk had been quarrying faster, because the city was larger, and the city was larger because the last two Drifts had brought years of plenty. The table's generosity was hastening the end of the chair.

Tamsa did not knock that part. She carried it herself. Some nights she lay face down on the warm floor of her sleeping-room with her palms flat, and she did not tap the stone before sleeping. She had begun to feel that *I am here* was a claim she could not quite make on behalf of the city's grandchildren's grandchildren.

The power saw her lying there. This, more than anything, was what had drawn it.

## III. The Temptations

The power knew what it could do. It is worth saying plainly, because its restraint meant nothing unless the alternatives were real.

It could refill the mesa. It could lay down new gypsum in the hollow halls, grain by grain, and knit the shelf thick again. No one would know how.

It could make it rain.

It could part the cloud sea and show them the lowlands, and explain the glow.

It could speak. It could knock, in perfect knock-speech, through every stone on the Hightable at once: *I am here. Do not be afraid. Here is what you must do.*

It considered each of these at length, at the pace of the place, and it set each one aside.

It would not make rain. Rain would be a wound. The varnish was a living film thinner than paint, grown over ages on a finished land, and rain would stain it, feed rival things, and slump the ventifacts' crisp edges. The pavers' food would change. The kites' thermals would change. The Hightable had reached the form the wind preferred, and the power did not intend to leave its fingerprints across a thousand kilometres of something that had taken longer than its own memory to become beautiful.

It would not refill the mesa. That would work, and that was exactly the problem. The Seated had built their honesty around the count. *Water is stone. Every city is eating the mountain it lives on, and every city knows it.* That knowing was the spine of their ethics. It was why cups were glass and why the jug was turned over. A mesa that silently refilled would teach them that the count lied. Worse, it would teach them that the table had a cook who could be relied upon to cover their mistakes. It would turn guests into dependents, and the power had seen what dependents become.

It would not show them the lowlands. *We do not go there* was not cowardice. It was a boundary drawn by a people who understood that some things are kitchens and not dining rooms. Whatever lived under the cloud had its own life and its own right not to be looked at. The glow was not the power's to explain. One day, perhaps, the Seated and whatever was below would meet. That meeting belonged to them, not to a stranger passing through.

And it would not speak, at least not the way it had imagined. A voice from the stone would be the loudest sound on the Hightable, louder than the Drift. The Seated had no tradition of oration. They found a single voice raised to a crowd faintly absurd, *like trying to pour water into the wind*. Their language had many words for shades of *yes* and almost none for commands. A power that arrived and gave instructions would be speaking the one grammar they had refused.

So it asked itself a different question. It did not ask how to save them. It asked what a good guest would do.

## IV. The Older Guests

It went looking for anyone else who had been sitting at this table longer than the Seated. It found the Reckoners.

It came to one on the deep pavement, a many-legged thing under slate-plated armour that had been standing still for six years. The power sat beside it, in whatever sense a power sits, for a long while. It did not try to read the Reckoner's mind. It suspected that would be the worst rudeness available to it on the whole plateau. It simply kept company.

Eventually it looked at the stones.

Circles, spirals, and long straight lines, always oriented to the same bearing as the yardangs. The Seated had noticed the orientation and assumed it was the wind's. The Reckoners' arrangements were aligned with the wind the way everything on the Hightable was aligned with the wind, and so they had concluded it meant nothing in particular.

But the power had time, and it looked at more of them. Across the plateau, the long lines were not only parallel to the yardangs. They *gathered*. Traced out across hundreds of kilometres, like the paver-trails scribbled on the plains, they drew toward particular places on the margins. They pointed at particular points on the escarpment where the stepped cliffs dropped into the cloud sea.

At those points, the updraft was strongest. There, on still nights, columns of lowland air rose out of the cloud and spilled over the rim.

The power understood then that the Seated had been told something about their world that was not quite true. *There is no surface water.* But the cloud sea was water, an entire ocean of it hanging in the air below the table. At a few places on the rim, that water rose, every night, into cold that could have made it bead on glass. It would not be much, and it was not a river. But it did not come from the mountain. It came from the updraft, which carried their food up from a kitchen they had never seen. It was more of the same gift, arriving by the same road, and they had not noticed it because they had never built at the edge. Their water was stone, and so they had only ever looked for it in stone.

The power did not know whether the Reckoners had meant to point. It did not know whether the Reckoners meant anything in the way the Seated mean things. It did not ask. It noticed that the answer was already written on the plateau, in the one place the Seated had been taught never to touch. It found this very funny, and very fitting. And it felt, with something like relief, that its task had just become much smaller.

It did not need to give the Seated water. It needed only to help them read.

## V. What It Actually Did

On the western margin, a drover named Kesh Ammadi was taking a herd along a corridor between yardangs that ran to within a day's walk of the escarpment. Drovers did not usually go that close. There was no reason to, and the herds did not like it. But the varnish was rich along that corridor, and Kesh was patient.

On the ninth night, the power leaned very gently on the air.

It was a small lean, the smallest it could manage. On a night that was already still, a column of cloud-air rose a little higher and drifted a little farther inland than it otherwise would have. That was all. No one could have detected it, because the Hightable's weather did something much like it on its own every few decades. The power simply chose the night and the corridor, and it did not choose the drover's response.

Kesh woke before dawn, as drovers do, to count the rocks. She had left her smoked-glass visor on the pavement beside her felt, cold side up, where the sky had been drawing the warmth out of it all night.

It was wet.

She did not understand at first. She thought something had spilled. She checked her flasks, and they were sealed. She checked the herd for a paver that had been crushed, and they were all whole. She sat for a long time looking at the visor. On its surface, in the grey light, were beads of water that had come from nowhere: not from stone, not from a kiln, not from a lens and a patient hour in a yardang's shadow. They had come out of the air.

She did not drink them. That is the thing the power remembered longest. She was a drover, alone, a day's walk from anything, holding more free water than she had ever seen, and she did not drink it. She let the sun take it. She watched it go the way she would have watched someone weep, and when it was gone she laid her palm flat on the pavement and knocked the pattern drovers use for *something has changed*, though there was no one near enough to hear it.

Then she marked the place, turned the herd, and began the long slow walk back toward the cables.

## VI. How News Travels on the Hightable

The power did nothing else. The rest happened at the pace the warm ground permits.

It took Kesh a season to reach a cable station. It took the cable-riders three years to carry her words, forehead to forehead, in exactly the voice she had said them in, to every city on the plateau. At first no one believed it. Then a fin-tender from one of the western cities walked to the rim with a sheet of clear glass and came back, months later, with a small sealed flask and an expression no one in her city had seen on a fin-tender before.

It took the Seated a generation to build the first edge-condenser: a field of pale ceramic fins and cold glass, sited where a Reckoner's line met the rim. They argued for years about whether it was allowed. The rules said *the edge is not the end* and *we do not go there*. In the end the bore-wardens settled it. They pointed out that the Seated would not be going *there*, below the cloud. They would only be sitting a little closer to the edge of the table, catching what rose to them, which was what they had always done. *Warmth rises; life lives in the rising.* The water had been rising too. They simply hadn't set a cup under it.

The yield was small, as the power had known it would be. It was not enough to stop the quarrying, but it was enough to slow it. It was enough that, in the cities with edge-fields, the one who knows how much is left could knock a number that was larger than the year before.

And no one moved the Reckoners' stones. They built beside them, carefully, and left the lines unbroken.

## VII. Tamsa

Tamsa Orrel did not live to see an edge-field. Vessary was far from the rim, and she was very old. But she lived to hear the news. A cable-rider brought it to her, forehead to forehead, in Kesh Ammadi's own voice, while Tamsa lay on the warm floor of her sleeping-room with a young rider kneeling close so that the words would carry.

Tamsa did the arithmetic. She had always been good at it. The number would not be large for Vessary, which was too far from the edge, too old, and too hollow. But the other cities would have more time, and some of that time could be traded down the cables as water. Vessary's count was not the end of the Seated. It was only the end of Vessary, and possibly not even that.

She wept. The young rider, who had been raised correctly, thanked her.

That night, for the first time in many years, Tamsa tapped the stone before she slept.

The power was there. It had stayed, sitting at the edge of things the way a guest sits when the meal is over but the conversation is still good. It felt her knock arrive through the floor of the mesa, and it knew by then what the knocks meant. They did not mean *I am here*, or not only that. They meant *I am here, and I know I was given this, and I am grateful*. It meant the whole world in one small tap: heat below, cold above, and a person in the gap between.

The power had been careful all along not to speak. But this was not a speech. It was the one custom on the plateau in which it felt it might, without rudeness, take part.

So somewhere in the deep stone, too far down to be anyone's hand, among all the soft irregular knocks that come from all over the mesa in the hour before sleep, there was one more.

No one noticed it. That was the point.

## VIII. Afterward

It left, eventually. It had other tables to sit at.

Before it went, it looked once more at the glow beneath the cloud sea. It felt the pull of curiosity, strong and honest, and it let the pull pass. It looked once more at the Reckoners, still standing, still arranging, still pointing the way the wind points, and it did not ask them what they were. It looked at the Seated, and at their slow machines, their counted cups, their dead in the cistern and their tears drying on one another's hands. It thought that it had rarely seen a people who so little needed saving and so much deserved help, and that the difference between those two things had been the whole of its work.

What it did, in the end, was almost nothing. It chose a night. It leaned on the air. It let a woman find water on her visor and decide for herself what it meant.

And it knocked once, in the dark, *warm under you*, because it had been a guest here, and a guest should mind their manners.
