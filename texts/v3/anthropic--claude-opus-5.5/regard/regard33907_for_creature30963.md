# Not Currently Needed

## Arrival

It comes over the ridge the way the fog does, from the windward side, because that is the way things are supposed to arrive here.

It could come as anything, so it has to decide what it will be, and the town decides for it. The lanes are the width of a person with a basket. Anything larger would have to take down a wall to pass. So it comes small, about the size of the people, and it keeps its edges soft so that it brushes nothing. On the first morning it stands just inside the lower gate and does not move at all. It has learned, from the few moments it has spent here already, that this is a place where arriving is itself a kind of noise.

The goats notice it first. They lift their heads in the pen, look at it with their flat gold eyes, decide it is not a threat and not food, and go back to what they were doing. It takes this as the first judgment passed on it, and a fair one.

## The first thing it does

The fog comes on the second morning. The lanes fill and the voices start, low and fast and wet.

The power can hear all of it. That is the trouble. The fog stops sound for the people of the saddle, but it does not stop sound for something like this. It hears the couple on the stair of the well-square room, and the dispute about a lintel two lanes over, and a grandmother in the middle town telling a story she has clearly told before. Every word reaches it as clearly as if the air were dry and the walls were bare.

It understands what the fog is to these people. The fog is the only privacy they have ever had. They have a word that means *fog* and *nearness* and *the feeling of being with someone you love where no one else can hear*. If it goes on listening, that word becomes a lie.

So the first thing it does is stop hearing. It does this deliberately, the way you might close your eyes in someone else's house when they begin to undress. On fog mornings, from then on, it lets itself be as deaf as the people are. Voices fall dead a few paces from it, as they should. It sits on a stair tread in the white and hears only the drip from the channels and, now and then, the murmur of someone close by, too soft to make out.

It finds that it likes this better. It had not known that about itself.

## What it could give

In the dry days between fogs it walks the town and thinks about what it could do. It is benevolent. It came because it cares about this place, and caring wants to be of use. So it makes itself go through the list honestly, one gift at a time, and follows each one down the channels the way water would run.

**A spring.** It could open one under the well-square, cold and endless, tasting of iron and slate like the cisterns. There would be no more counting jars.

It follows the water. The measure is set by the water, so the measure would break. There would be more children, because no one would decide against them. The rooms would fill. The rooms would stop going cold, which sounds like a blessing until it sees what that would mean. No one would need to circulate. People would stay where they were born. The slow climb from the lower town to the upper would stop, and the town would stop turning over.

The air wells would still stand, but they would be monuments now and not wells. The dead would no longer be keeping the living alive. A child who asked where the water came from would be told *from the ground*, and that would be true, and something would be gone from the world that had never been anywhere else.

It might be a good town, that new one. The power doesn't pretend otherwise. There would be more people alive in it, and that counts for something real. But it would be a different people, and they did not ask to become one. Nor could it ask them. Anything it said aloud would be said to the whole town, and a spring offered in public is not an offer. It is a spring.

**Iron.** Good hard tools that don't need rehammering every few days. It follows this too. A hard chisel doesn't need to listen. You could strike a plate and make it split where you wanted, instead of asking it patiently where it had already agreed to go. *You don't shape a stone. You find the shape it has already agreed to.* Iron would make that sentence optional, and within a few generations it would be only a saying.

**Longer lives. Warmth. Knowledge of the wide world below.** All of these run the same way. Each one is a good thing somewhere else. Here, each would loosen something that is currently holding. The town is a wall that took a thousand years to lay, and the power's gifts are all, in one way or another, a stone pulled from the bottom course.

It is not, it notes, that the town is perfect. The children who die of fevers are not perfect. The bones in the grain beds are real bones, and some of them are small. The power does not tell itself that suffering here is secretly good. It simply sees that the town already has an answer to most of what it could offer, and the answer is written in the whole shape of the place. The town is not asking to be rescued. It is asking, if it is asking anything, to be left in the notch where it fits.

## The white stone

Then, in the lower town, a white stone appears on a threshold.

The power goes and stands in the lane. It is a child, not yet named. People call her *the one of the door with the split lintel*. She is hot with fever and breathing badly, and the power can hear it through the wall, because this is not fog and it has promised itself nothing about dry days.

Over the next day it watches the neighbors come. Nobody knocks. A jar of water appears by the white stone, then another. A bundle of dried heather for the fire. A chip of juniper, from someone who has something to give and will bring it when asked. The whole town is saying a sentence on that threshold, in stones, and the sentence is *we know, and we are here*.

The power wants very badly to go in.

This is the hard one, and it knows it. Healing her would cost it almost nothing. The fever is a small thing to something like it. It tells itself all the true reasons against: the measure, the precedent, the way a town that learns there is a healer on the saddle becomes a town that waits for the healer. It tells itself that the mother has done this before, and her mother before her, and that the grammar of white stones and water jars exists precisely because this happens and is survived, most times.

None of those reasons feels like enough to stand in a lane and listen to a child breathe badly.

On the third morning her breathing eases on its own. The power hears the change. By the fifth morning the white stone is gone from the threshold, and in its place is a single plate leaning at the angle that means *I am here and you may come in*.

The power does not go in. It walks slowly back up the lanes, and it is honest with itself about what just happened. It does not know what it would have done if she had kept getting worse. It suspects it would have healed her, quietly, in the night, and that this would have been a small wrong against the town, and that it would have chosen that wrong anyway and carried it. It is glad it did not have to find out. It does not congratulate itself for a restraint that was never fully tested.

## The old woman

The old woman who has not spoken in eleven years sees it on the ninth day.

She is the first of them who does. The others have taken it for a person from some other ward, or not noticed it at all. She is sitting in her doorway in the upper town, near the upper gate, where the choughs land. She looks straight at it, the way no one looks at the lizard, and it does not vanish.

She reaches beside her, takes a plate, and lays it flat across her threshold. *I am here but not to be disturbed.* Then, after a moment, she reaches again and sets a second, smaller stone on top of the first, at an angle the power has not seen before. It does not know what the combination means. It stands in the lane and does not know, and understands that this is itself the message. *You can hear through walls. You cannot read this.*

It waits for fog.

When the fog comes she is already out on the stair beside her door, a grey shape in white, and she moves her bundle of cloth over a hand's width to make room. It sits. For a long while neither of them says anything. The stone sweats around them. Somewhere below, low voices.

Then she speaks. Her voice is very quiet and a little rough from disuse, and the power, deaf by its own choice, has to lean in to hear her.

"You've been walking," she says. "Trying doors."

It says yes.

"Did you find one that needed you?"

It thinks about the spring, and the iron, and the child with the split lintel, and says: *No. Not one.*

She nods, as if that is the answer she expected and approves of. "It's a good town," she says. "It's hard, but it's good. It fits." She is quiet for a while. "There'll be a year the fog doesn't come. Maybe not in my time. Maybe not in the child's. The measure will drop and people will go up the stair who shouldn't yet. That's happened before. We know how to do it."

It asks her what she would want, in that year, from something like itself.

She considers this for a long time. The power can see her thinking it through carefully, the way she would read a plate before tapping it.

"If the fog stops because the mountain turns," she says at last, "leave it. The mountain's allowed. But if it stops because of something far away, something someone *did*, down where the air is thick," and here she makes a small gesture, windward, toward the sea the power knows is out there beyond the clouds, "then that isn't the mountain's business, and it isn't ours. That would be yours."

It says that it understands.

"Good," she says. Then, after a pause: "You're not currently needed."

It has heard that phrase already. It knows what it means here. It is what they say of a closed room, and of the dead. It is not a dismissal. It is the most careful thing this town knows how to say about something it intends to keep.

"Close yourself properly," she tells it. "Don't just walk off. Things left open go cold."

The fog begins to lift. Grey shapes come back one at a time from the white. She falls silent in the same breath as the rest of the town, and she does not speak again, and she nods to it once when it rises.

## Rain

It stays three more days, because rain is coming. It can feel it building beyond the windward ridge, and it wants to be here for it.

The rain comes in the afternoon. Every channel runs at once, every roof spouts, every stair tread trickles, and the town rings like a struck bowl. Under the squares the cisterns fill. And the people, all of them, wherever they are, open their mouths and sing.

This is the only time in the whole life of the town that something like the power can use its full voice without harming anyone. So it does. It stands in the middle of the well-square with water running over its shoulders and sings as loudly as it has ever sung. It sings wordlessly, the way they do, voice thrown out into the water-noise. No one can hear it. Everyone knows everyone is doing it. For twenty minutes it is not a power, just one more voice in a ringing town, part of the measure only in the way the rain is.

When the rain stops, it stops in the same breath as they do.

People come out into the lanes, a little flushed, and look at one another and nod. Someone nods to it. It nods back.

## What it does

So this is the whole of what it does, in the end.

It makes itself deaf in fog.

It leaves no water, because it does not drink, and so has none to give. It learns to read eleven kinds of threshold, and fails to read the twelfth, and leaves that one unread.

It does not heal the child. It does not know whether it would have, and it keeps that not-knowing instead of pretending otherwise.

It turns its attention windward, past the ridge and down to the sea where the fog is born, and it sets itself a watch there. It will not govern the weather. The mountain is allowed to turn. It will only stand guard against the kind of harm the old woman meant, the far-away, careless, human kind, the kind that is nobody's business on the saddle. If that harm comes, it will meet it there, far from the town. No one in the town will ever know. That is the point.

Then it closes itself properly. It goes out through the upper gate at dawn, in the last of a thinning fog. On a stretch of old wall beside the gate, the kind of stone that would go into a closed doorway, it stacks itself shut, plate by plate, loose, the local way. Anyone who needed to could undo it in an afternoon.

## The sentence

Before it goes, it passes a place where a thought has been left unfinished, the way a stair climbs to a landing that leads nowhere now because the house it served was taken down. *You could say that they live in their ancestors' houses. It would —*

It is tempted to complete it. It considers the words it might set there, as carefully as a plate: *It would be just as true to say that their ancestors live in them, and that the difference between the two is only which is currently needed.*

It does not set them there. It leaves the sentence open, the way a room is left, and it trusts that someone who lives in that town will close it better.
