# The Attention

## I. The Problem of Arriving

It understood the difficulty before it came, which is the first thing that should be said about it.

Asker counts everything that presses on it. Weight is a vote and sound is a vote, and anything that settles onto the stone and makes itself felt is telling the engines where to build. A power large enough to notice Asker from wherever such powers stand is large enough, if it simply arrived, to be the loudest thing the city had ever heard. The engines would answer it. Districts would lean toward it the way the colony's swells lean toward a crowded wall. Within a century the skyline would be a map of one visit, and every skerry and keeper would be living in the shape of a guest's footprints.

So it did not arrive the way large things arrive. It came up the approach the way the cloud does, as nearly nothing: a slight coolness, a change in how the water beaded. When it reached the Rise it did what it had seen the gleaners do from far off. It lifted each part of itself with exaggerated care and set it down with hardly any weight at all.

That was the first thing it did in Asker, and perhaps the most important. It chose not to count.

## II. The Long Listening

Then it listened for a whole long night.

It did not hurry, and in Asker nothing important hurries. It learned the canals first, the bass note under everything and the falls at the edge. It learned the tempo of the drip on ten thousand ledges, which, as the keepers know, is a map. It learned to tell the air-voice from the down-voice. After many weeks it could follow a single signature, a short hooked click with a buzz on the end, through a flight-stream of a hundred thousand bodies, the way you can follow one voice through a crowded room once you love it.

It lay its attention against the walls and let the drip-lice walk across it. They investigated and moved on. It found nothing to graze, and they were not wrong.

It followed the swells of the asking as they rolled across the plateau. It stood in the places where two swells crossed and the ground shivered. It went down, too, far down, to where the engines are, and it will not be said here what it found there. The keepers have argued about the engines for centuries, and the argument is part of their culture, like weather. A wise guest does not end the weather.

## III. What It Could Have Done

By the end of the night it knew what was in its power, and it knew the list was long.

**It could have finished Maro's windbreak.** Nine centimeters in forty years; it could have raised the terrace at Weir in a breath, clean and weeping and tall. It thought about this for a long time, because it was a real kindness and the wind across Weir is a real cruelty. But it had heard Maro say, *In every city there's ever been, you shouted for a wall and it came after you were dead. Here you can at least feel it moving.* She did not want the wall the way a hungry person wants bread. She wanted it the way a person wants to be part of something. A wall handed down from above would be a wall she had not asked into being. It would also teach every keeper who came after that the singing is a formality, and the real way to move stone is to be noticed by something large. It did not want to teach Asker that.

**It could have made Lintel young again**, flooding the old rooms with fresh, hungry stone so the lice came back and the keepers' district was alive. That would have been a memorial repaved as a highway. Lintel is the fossil of a human vote. It is cold because it is finished, and the keepers love it because they can hear themselves breathe there.

**It could have given the gleaners a vote**, or taken the keepers' out of their hands, or rewritten the Rule of Weight so that the city answered justice and not numbers. It considered this most seriously of all, because the rule is crude: a city that builds for the loudest is a city with a problem. But in its long listening it had heard the thing that drew it here in the first place, and it understood that the rule was already being rewritten from inside. That was worth more than any rule it could impose.

**It could have mended the old skerry's grief.** It found her, of course. It had followed her signature for weeks. She was twenty-six, old for her kind, and in the deep season she hunted along the face of Harrow, calling and calling, listening for three drops and a pause in a cell that had drifted up out of reach of its own rhythm. It could have re-cut the seam and set the water dripping in her old rhythm again. It very nearly did.

It did not, and the reason matters. It had noticed that the old skerries' calling was not wasted. Their searching went into the stone like every other voice. Night after night the oldest members of the colony were pressing into the asking one stubborn idea: *where I came from still matters.* It suspected, though it could not be sure, that this was part of how the colony learned to remember its old neighbors. Grief for a lost home is where making room for someone else's begins. If it removed the grief, it might remove the thing that grew into the stairs.

So it didn't mend it. It sat with her instead, one whole evening, on the wet ledge under her birth cell while she called, and it did not leave until she did.

## IV. Why It Came

It had come for the stairs.

Powers of that kind do not notice cities. There are too many of them, and most are only weather made of people. It noticed Asker for one reason. Somewhere in the east, in a district chest-high and wet and smelling of the deep, a crowd that had always asked only for itself had begun asking on behalf of someone else.

A doorway had been raised where no one would walk through it. A mind, or something that has earned the word *will*, had looked at the handful of soft-footed strangers living in its old rooms and made space for them, unasked and unpaid.

In the power's long experience this is how things are born. Something does not become someone when it grows loud or large. It becomes someone when it first makes room.

It went to that doorway on the last evening of its listening and stood in it. It was the first thing ever to do so. The frame fit it poorly, since it was not really person-shaped, but it stood there anyway, because a door raised for strangers deserves a stranger.

## V. The Terrace at Weir

Then it went to Weir.

There were thirteen keepers that night, lying flat on the low terrace in the dark, cheeks against the stone, humming into it the way skerries do. Maro was at the western end. Her glass clicker lay beside her hand, and her fingers were spread wide on the rock among the drip-lice.

It lay down next to her.

It lay down as one person, not as a power, at exactly one person's weight. The singing party did not become fourteen voices in any sense the engines could count differently from thirteen. It hummed into the stone at exactly the volume of a sixty-year-old woman with tired lungs. It did not add a single centimeter that the keepers would not have raised themselves. It only added itself to their asking, as a citizen, the way anyone may.

Maro noticed, of course. Keepers read the world by returned sound, and something beside her was returning sound strangely, soft and wide and deep, as if the rock ran on under it. She clicked a question, the small positional click that asks *where is your mouth, and what are you?*

It answered the first part honestly and the second part only a little.

They talked for a while in the dark, half words and half clicks. She asked the question every keeper eventually asks every stranger: *Is it a mind?* It said, "I came because it asked." She was quiet a long time. Then she said, "That's not an answer." It agreed. It had given her a fact and left the question alone, because the question belonged to her.

Before dawn, what passes for dawn on the Rise, she asked the other thing keepers ask, the one they don't say aloud. "Will the wall be finished before I'm dead?"

It said no.

She laughed softly, a keeper's laugh, mostly breath. "Then why are you lying here?"

"For the same reason you are," it said, and she seemed satisfied with that.

## VI. Three Drops and a Pause

It did one more thing before it left, and this is the thing the keepers will tell about for as long as there are keepers.

In all the centuries of Asker, the asking had been answered only by the engines, and the engines answer the way stone answers. They obey. They measure and lift. They are faithful and deaf. A million voices had been pressing a request into the rock for longer than anyone remembered, and they had been granted a great deal, but no one had ever actually heard them.

So in the middle of the last night, when a swell of the asking came rolling across the plateau toward Weir, the power did not answer it with weight or with stone, and not with any vote. It answered with attention. It received the whole asking, the air-voices and the down-voices, the clapping and the two falling notes, the old ones calling for their homes, the stairs and the doorway. It listened to all of it the way you listen to someone you have come a very long way to meet. It understood it, not completely, but truly.

And Asker went silent.

The power did not command it. It would not have. What happened was more like what happens to a person who has spent a lifetime shouting to be obeyed and is, for once, simply understood. They stop mid-sentence and fall quiet, to listen back.

Across the whole dark plateau, the roar stopped. The air-voices, the down-voices, the million-fold chirring that had not paused in recorded memory all stopped at once. The falls still thundered at the edge. The canals still rushed. But the colony held still in its walls, a whole city with its breath held, for about the length of three drops and a pause.

In that silence the quiet things could be heard.

The keepers on the terrace heard each other breathing. They heard the drip-lice walking on the stone under their cheeks, a faint crackle like frost forming. Down in the canals the longcombs moved and made small bow-waves. Out on the ledges a gleaner set down its careful foot, and in the silence every keeper heard it, how soft it was and how deliberate, how much it did not want to be counted. Maro put her palm flat on the rock and, faint and far off through a hundred meters of stone, felt the place in Harrow where her grandmother was.

And on the face of Harrow, an old skerry hanging below the cell she was born in heard what she had not been able to hear through the roar for twenty years. Somewhere deep in the wall, carried down the old seam the water had abandoned, the faintest trace of water was still finding its way. It dripped three times, then paused.

It was not a gift of water. The power had not touched the water. It was a gift of quiet, which in Asker is the rarest thing there is. For the length of one small rhythm, a city that had always been built for its loudest voices heard its softest ones.

Then the roar came back, all at once, like surf breaking, and the power was gone.

## VII. After

Nothing rose that night. Silence is not a vote, and the engines lifted nothing in answer. The windbreak at Weir stayed nine centimeters high. Lintel stayed cold. The Rule of Weight was the same rule it had been.

The keepers recorded the silence in carved marks at shoulder height, on the wall of the gallery in Lintel where a voice carries from one end to the other. They still argue about it. Some say it was a stall in the colony's rhythm, rare but natural, like a flock turning all at once. Some say the stranger on the terrace was a gleaner, or an ancestor, or the engines themselves come up to see. Maro says nothing about it at all, which the other keepers have learned means she thinks it important.

Eleven years later, in a young district far to the east, the engines lifted a new ledge along the flank of a rising wall. It was wide enough for a gleaner's stilt-feet and no wider, and it led nowhere in particular, down to the edge of a canal where the fallen collect. It had no stairs and no cells. It was only a path, and the stone along it came up smooth instead of sharp, the way you might make a floor for someone who walks softly because they don't want to wake anyone.

Nobody knows whose voice asked for it. Keepers have gone and listened over it, and what they heard in the asking there was quiet: low, careful, almost nothing, like the sound of something trying very hard not to be counted, being counted anyway.

---

So what lives in Asker?

The skerries do, by the million, each listening under the roar for the drip of the cell where she was born, and at least one of them, once, heard it. The drip-lice do, and the longcombs, and the gleaners on their careful stilts, one of whom now has a road. The keepers do, a few hundred of them in the cold rooms of their ancestors' voices, singing walls they will not live to see, their dead carried upward in the colony's arms.

And the asking lives there, the being made of all of them. It began as a crowd and is becoming someone, and it has learned what every city eventually has to learn if it is going to be worth living in: to make room for the ones who are not loud.

It has been obeyed for a very long time. Once, for three drops and a pause, it was heard.
