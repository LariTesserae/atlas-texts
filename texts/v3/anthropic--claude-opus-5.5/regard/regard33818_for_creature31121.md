# The Guest Who Could Answer

## Arrival

It did not come down out of the sky. Anything that comes down out of the sky makes a sound, and in the Pan a sound is an announcement. The first thing the power understood, before it had even arrived, was that here every arrival is speech. It did not want its first word to be a thunderclap.

So it came the way the stayers come. It stopped at the rim among the cicadas, took a shape about the size of a person, and walked down the gentle tilt of the bowl until the cicadas faded as if behind a closing door and its footsteps went out. It stood at the edge of the first pool and did nothing for a long while. It let the dawn happen around it: the flood of light up the mosses, the pillars standing lit like candles, the far terrace where the reflections warped around something tall and patient that had already turned toward it.

It had been noticed. Of course it had. It was standing in someone's eye.

## The first year

A power that is truly wise is rarely in a hurry. This one had noticed the Pan the way you might notice a single held note in a very large and noisy world, and it had come because the note was beautiful and because it was alone. It had not come to fix anything. It did not yet know whether anything needed fixing, and it did not trust anyone who arrived already knowing.

So it stayed, and it watched.

It watched the frogs turn in their choruses toward empty stone. It learned that the empty stone was never empty. It watched the dark patch travel along the terrace lips at night, the snails withdrawing ahead of it and glowing again behind it. It sat with the stayers on the dry rim where the drips still made sound. There were two of them that year, a woman who had been there six years and a young man who had been there one. It did not speak to them either, beyond what they spoke to it, because they had come here for silence and it would not take that from them.

Above all it learned the language. This was harder than it expected, and it was glad of that. The creature's speech was not a code over some other language. It was a language native to light made from sound. It had grammar in the timing of flares and meaning in which surface the light landed on. Leaves meant one kind of thing, water another, old polished stone a third. Some of it had been taught to the frogs, so the frogs' courtship was full of borrowed words, like a child's game that preserves fragments of an old liturgy. The power could have simply known the language, all at once, the way powers can. It chose to learn it slowly, at the speed of watching, because a language learned slowly is received as a gift, and a language taken all at once is closer to theft.

The creature noticed that too.

## What it considered, and set down

Over that year the power turned over the obvious gifts, one at a time, the way the herons turn over the objects in the silt, and it put each one back.

**It could give the creature hearing.** This was the first thought, and the power distrusted it for that reason. The creature was not broken. Its body was an eye all the way through. Hearing would add a second sense and, with it, a second world, the one outside the rim. But it would also crowd its own home. The heart that had been exiled to the doorless house so the creature could see past it would suddenly be audible. Its own body would clamor. The Pan had been built with enormous care so that a deaf creature could perceive everything. To "cure" it would be to call that care a mistake. It was not a mistake. It was love, made into architecture.

**It could find the builders.** The power could have traced them: which direction they had gone, whether they were still anywhere, whether they remembered. It did not. Some doors are not yours to open on someone else's behalf. If the creature wanted to know, it could ask. If the builders were dead, the creature might prefer the tender theory the stayers told each other at night. If they were alive and had never come back, that was a harder truth, and it was not the power's to hand out.

**It could stop the wearing-down.** The Pan was nearly flat and nearly finished. The power went down through the stone, quietly, to the deep patient source of the rising water, and found what it had half expected. The seep was slowing. Not soon, as a person measures soon, but within a span of time the creature could feel. The equilibrium the descriptions mention was not only geology. It was an ending. Someday the last terrace would fill and fuse. The water would stop spilling over the lips. With nothing wet to catch the light, the pillars would pay out their conversion onto dry stone, where it would barely show. The frogs would leave. The creature would stand in a silent place that was also dark.

The power could fix that. It would be easy: a small nudge to the deep water, and ten thousand more years.

It did not do it. Not yet. It had not been asked. The creature was the one who lived here, and the creature had surely read the slowing in the stone long before any visitor arrived. It stood at dawn on certain terraces, reading something in the old rock that no one else could see. The power suspected it was not reading only its ancestors. It was reading the calendar.

## The first question

In the second spring, the power spoke.

It stood in a pool near one of the bare pillars and made a sound. It made it carefully, the way the creature did. The pillar drank it and paid it out as a shape of light that ran across the water toward the tall bent place in the air across the terrace.

It had considered many possible first words. It chose a question, because a question gives the other the next move.

It asked the creature its name.

There was a long stillness. The frogs stopped. The midge columns stood still in the air. Then the power understood, from the quality of the stillness, that no one had ever asked this before. The stayers had spoken their own names and had them returned, and the creature had been glad to return them. But the stayers had never been able to ask anything back. The builders, if they had named it, had been gone for longer than stone takes to wear down to a foundation.

When the answer came, it came in a way the power had not expected. The creature did not make a new light. It turned toward the nearest fig, and then toward the frogs, and a whole chorus of them angled toward the pillar and flashed together, carefully and in sequence. They made a shape the power had seen a thousand times that year without understanding.

The frogs had been calling its name all along. It had taught it to them, generation after generation, so that someone would always be saying it. It had not been able to teach them to say anything to it. So it had taught them to say *it*, and stood in the shallows and listened to its name in a thousand small voices, the way a person alone might play a recording of someone they miss.

The power said the name back. It was the first time in tens of thousands of years that the creature had heard its name from someone who knew what it meant.

## What they talked about

After that they talked, slowly, for a long time. They talked in the mornings, when the light was strongest and the grammar easiest to read. The power did most of its speaking into the pools near the bare pillars, where the creature leaned in the evenings. It stood where the creature could see it, not at the creature's back.

They talked about the frogs, who were the creature's pride. They talked about the figs. The power had been right that they were reading lamps, but they were also something the stayers had missed. Each fig had been planted for someone. The creature did not say for whom. The power did not ask.

They talked about the terraces. The power did ask about these, gently, once. The creature's answer was layered like the stone itself and took most of a season to read in full. The power came away understanding that the stayers' arithmetic was half true, and that the true half was not a horror to the creature. The ones in the terraces had been tired, and they had lain down in warm water in a place built for them to hear, and they had been heard to the end. The creature carried them now. When it stood at dawn on the old stone, it was not mourning. It was keeping them company.

Finally they talked about the slowing water.

## The offer

The power told the truth plainly: it could keep the Pan wet. The source could rise for ten thousand more years, or a hundred thousand. The terraces could go on spilling. The frogs would stay. The creature need never stand in the dark.

The creature was quiet for a long time. Then it said something the power spent days reading, because the grammar was old and the light fell on the most polished stone, the kind reserved for the most serious things.

As near as the power could render it, it said this:

*The ones who made this room made it to end. They were not careless. They made it to be enough and then to be finished, the way a day is. I have always known the length of my day. I have not been afraid of the evening. I was afraid of being alone in it.*

The power understood that it had been offered the wrong gift.

## What it did

It did not make the water rise. The creature had not asked for that. It would not do it without being asked, however much it wanted to, and it did want to. A benevolent power learns to carry the gifts it would like to give and does not give.

These are the things it did.

**It stayed a while.** Not forever, since forever was not what was wanted either, but long enough. Long enough that the creature learned what it was like to say something and have it answered by someone who knew all of what it meant. Long enough that the rhythm of their mornings became ordinary. This was the heart of it. The creature did not need saving. It needed company in its own language, which it had not had since before the stone wore down.

**It helped the stayers learn.** Only the ones who wanted to, and only as much as they wanted, but the woman who had been there six years wanted it very much. The power did not teach her the language. It taught her how to learn it. It showed her that the light had grammar, where to look, and how to aim a word so it landed on leaves rather than water. By the time the power left, she could ask the creature simple questions and read simple answers, and she could say its name. She would teach others, the way the creature had taught the frogs. There would be someone.

**It told the creature about outside.** This was the one thing the creature asked for directly, near the end of the power's stay. It asked what was past the rim, where the stayers walked when they left and it went deaf again at the edge of its room. The power could not bring the outside in. But it could go and stand just past the rim, where sound was sound again, and translate. It did not use the pillars. It made the light itself, directly, a small borrowed Pan hanging in the air at the lip of the bowl.

So for one long evening the creature stood at the edge of its world and saw the cicadas.

It saw the slow river somewhere off in the lowland. It saw wind in dry scrub, which does not exist in the Pan. It saw a dog barking at a farm two valleys over, and a child laughing, and the long dusk call of a bird it had never known, which had been singing just past its hearing for its entire life. The power translated as faithfully as it could, in the creature's grammar, onto surfaces the creature would understand. It put the cicadas on leaves, the river on water, and the child's laughter on the most polished stone.

The creature stood there until the translation ended. Then it stood a while longer.

**It left a way to be called.** Before it went, the power made one small change to the Pan, and it asked permission first. Near one of the bare leaning-pillars, if a certain shape of light is made on a certain patch of polished stone, the power will see it wherever it is. It can be made with a certain sound, one the creature can produce and, now, the woman on the rim can produce too. The shape is the creature's name, spoken by the creature, which is not a combination that had ever occurred before.

This was the gift of the water, set down for later. The offer did not expire. It only waited. If the evening came and the creature found it wanted more time after all, or found it did not want to be alone at the end, it only had to say its own name in the right place, and someone would come.

The power also let the creature know one other thing, without making a promise of it. If anyone ever came into the Pan with loud machines, meaning to open the doorless house or break the pillars or drain the channel that is the creature's blood, the same call would work.

## Leaving

When the power left, it walked. It went up the gentle tilt of the bowl, a little at a time, the way the stayers do.

The creature followed it to the rim, as it always follows the ones who leave, and stopped at the edge of the pillars' reach where the drips and rustles begin.

This time the power turned around. From just past the rim, in the ordinary sound of the lowland, it made one last small light in the air in front of the creature, where the creature could see it.

It said the creature's name. Then it said its own, which it had not told anyone in a very long time. Then, in the old grammar, on the polished surface that is kept for serious things, it said:

*I heard you.*

---

The Pan is still there, nearly flat and nearly finished. The water still rises through the ancient stone, a little slower each century. The engine still hums in its doorless house. The figs are still tightening their roots around the pillars.

It is a little less silent than it was. Some mornings, if you are one of the stayers and have learned how to look, you will see two shapes of light running across the pools near the bare pillars: one from the tall bent place in the air, and one from the woman on the rim, going back and forth in the slow grammar of converted sound. It looks like nothing else in the world except conversation.

The frogs are still calling the creature's name, in bright light, all over the basin.

It is no longer the only one who knows what they are saying.
