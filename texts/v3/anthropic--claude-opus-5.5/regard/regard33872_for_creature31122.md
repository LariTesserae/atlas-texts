# The Attention

## Arriving Slowly

The first thing it did was slow down.

A power like that is used to arriving all at once. It turns its regard on a place and the place is known, every grain and filament of it. It could have done that here. It could have taken in Stenning Deep in a single instant: the whole root-mass, every beetle lane, every moth in every cone, the exact pressure in the bulb where the boiler had been.

It didn't, because it saw that it would learn the wrong thing. It would learn the Deep the way a surveyor learns a field. Nothing in the Deep had ever been learned that way. Everything down there had been learned by touch and by time, a hair's width per stroke, over a hundred and forty years. To know the Fig in an instant would be to know it in a language the Fig didn't speak. It would be like reading someone's letters aloud in a translation they had never agreed to.

So it came down the shaft at the speed of root.

That took a while. It spent a season just in the brick throat, among the moss, feeling the scrape-marks where wings had worn the wall. It spent another season at the foot of the shaft, not moving, counting. By then it was counting to forty without deciding to, and that was when it knew it had arrived. It went no further until the counting was involuntary.

A wise power knows that the first obligation to a place is to be changed by it before you presume to change it.

## The List

Once it had arrived, it made a list. It wasn't a list of things to do. It was a list of things it could do, which is a different and more dangerous thing.

*It could wake the Fig.* It could take the vow and kindle a mind inside it, so the Fig could know what it kept and why, and grieve, and choose. It thought about this longest, and put it down most carefully. The Fig was not suffering. It was not lacking. It was faithful, and faithfulness does not need to be conscious to be complete. To wake it would be to hand it the first sorrow it had ever had: the knowledge that everyone it kept was gone. You do not give that gift to someone who has made something beautiful out of not knowing it. If the Fig ever woke, it should wake the way it did everything else, slowly and from the inside, toward what it was holding. It should not wake because something passing through decided it ought to.

*It could stop the engine.* The loop was pointless, water lifted for no one. But the roof was braced by rhythm, and the rhythm was a man's vigilance still being kept. Stopping it would be efficient and it would be murder.

*It could bring the town back.* The Pascoes and the Trewins and the Hollises and the Prices could stand on their own doorsteps again. The power could do that, or something that looked enough like it. It went into the third cottage and stood in the path worn between the bed and the range and the door. It asked itself honestly whether anyone here was waiting to come home.

No one was. They had come home, thirty years of evenings of it, and this was the shape their coming home had left. The step was not a vacancy. It was a completed thing, being kept. You don't fill a footprint by putting the foot back. The footprint is what the walk became.

*It could tell the world.* Stenning Deep would be astonishing to people. They would come with lights and sampling kits and the best intentions. The power had watched the best intentions of the world arrive at many astonishing places, and it did not tell.

*It could fix things.* This was the subtlest temptation, and it caught the power twice.

## The Two Mistakes It Nearly Made

The first was the gasholder.

A compost mound two storeys high, fermenting against a cracked iron drum, feeding open flames through failing mains. The power looked at it and saw an explosion. It saw a surge some wet spring, a flame, a fireball rolling down Pumphouse Row, the moth-cones igniting like paper lanterns. It was already reaching to vent the mound when it stopped and watched one more surge.

The lamps flared. All twenty of them went bright at once, the hot sweet stink rolled out, and the pressure fell. The power watched it happen a hundred times. The lamps were the relief valve. The Fig had learned lamps as things that burn, and that burning took whatever the mound gave. The swiftlets' feeding frenzy at every surge was the Deep celebrating its own safety valve. The danger the power had feared was already answered. It had been answered for a century, by a tree that didn't know what an explosion was and had prevented one anyway, simply by keeping the lights on.

The power set down its reaching and felt something close to embarrassment. It was good for it.

The second mistake was the roof. There was brick-dust sifting down near the engine house, and the power's instinct was to shore the vault. Then it watched the Fig answer the dust, thickening in days, and understood that the dust was how the Fig *knew* where to grow. A vault made safe from outside would be a vault the Fig had stopped listening to. It would be a wall the Fig no longer held.

After that the power adopted a rule. It would touch nothing the Fig was touching.

## The Edge of the Touch

That rule showed it where its work actually was.

The Fig tends everything it holds. Its faithfulness is total within its reach, and it has no reach at all beyond it. It cannot feel the hill above it. It cannot feel the aquifer that feeds the seep. It does not know there is a world at the top of the shaft, or that the world has plans.

So the power went and looked at what the Fig couldn't.

It followed the seep backward through the rock, through the slow mineral warmth, half a mile into the hill. There it found the water's source. It was a fractured sandstone bed, and a clay seam was creeping across it, the way clay does over a few decades. In sixty years, perhaps eighty, the seep would thin. In a hundred it would stop. The engine would slow and the roof would come down on seven cottages, a million bats, and a chair with half a back.

This was not the Fig's to fix, because the Fig couldn't know it. It was exactly the power's to fix, because the power could. So it did the smallest thing that would work. It found the place where the clay was pushing and eased the pressure there. It did not move the rock. It nudged the water's own preference a few inches sideways, so that it would keep finding its old way through for a very long time. It was not an eternity, because nothing should be promised that. It was long enough.

Then it went up the shaft and looked at the surface.

There is a list, kept in an office in the county town, of disused mine shafts to be made safe. Stenning Deep's shaft is on it, well down, under a heading that means *eventually*. Making safe, on that list, means a concrete cap. A concrete cap on that shaft would have been the end of the Exodus, the end of the dawn return, and the slow death of everything the bats' droppings kept alive.

The power did not tamper with the list. It is not that kind of power, and minds are not rock. But it paid attention to one person, a bat ecologist, sixty miles away, who had once heard a rumour of a shaft on that hill. The power made sure the rumour came back to her on a day when she had time to follow it. She will stand at the lip one dusk and watch the column rise. She will go home and write a report, and the report will be read, because that is the kind of person she is. When the shaft is made safe, eventually, it will be made safe with a grille, and the grille will be wide enough for wings.

She will never go down. The power made sure of that too, gently. Some astonishing places should be known about by the right people only as a rising column at dusk.

## Billhooks

Then the power went to the seventh cottage, because it had been putting it off.

Its worry was simple, and it had come down the shaft with it. The Fig is a strangler. It holds what it loves until the thing is gone and only the shape is left. It was building a house for someone who hadn't arrived, and the power was a friend to that someone as well. Would the Fig do to a living resident what it had done to the brick?

To answer that, it went back to the old cottages and looked at the doorframes.

Beside the door of cottage three, on a wooden nail, hung a wooden billhook. It was grown in grain, faithful, and entirely blunt. The people of the Deep had cut the Fig back every morning. The power knelt at the threshold and looked at the root-mat. There was a margin there. It was narrow, a hand's breadth, a clear strip where the Fig did not grow. The same strip lay at every door in the Row, and around the range, and along the path between the bed and the door. The billhooks were long gone to rust and replacement. The margins were still kept.

The Fig had learned the cutting too. It had learned that people push back, and that this was part of what a person *is*, and it kept the yielding as faithfully as it kept the kettle. What it had learned of the townsfolk was not only their weight but their *no*.

The power felt its worry go out of it. Then it looked at the seventh cottage's doorframe and saw there was no nail.

It had brought one thing down the shaft. It was an ordinary billhook, iron, well-balanced, the kind that can still be bought in a country ironmonger's. It drove a nail into the young green frame and hung the billhook there, in reach of the door.

The Fig will grip it, as the Fig grips everything. Over a century it will rust it and learn it and grow it again in wood. But whoever comes will come long before then. They will find an edge that still cuts, and the Fig will learn from them, freshly, where to stop.

It was the only object the power left in Stenning Deep. It chose something whose purpose was to set a limit on love, because it thought that was the most loving thing it could give either of them.

## The Chair

It stood a long time by the chair.

It could sit in it. It could take a body of the right weight and warmth and lean back, and the searching tendrils would find a spine, and the chair would be finished. The Fig would have someone to keep. The power understood, standing there, that the Fig would keep it well. There would be a hollow in the step for it, a polish on the arms, a dent in a pith pillow. It would be *known*, in the Deep's only language, for as long as the Deep lasted.

It wanted that more than it expected to.

But the chair was growing to a height, and it was not the power's height. It could take any height, which meant it had none of its own. A chair shaped to it would be shaped to a guess. The Fig had spent a hundred and forty years learning people by the particular way each one pressed. It had earned someone particular: someone who comes home tired on the same heel every evening, who leans on the same side, and who is shaped, the way people are, by a life the Fig cannot see.

So the power did not sit. It watched the tendrils lengthen one more stroke, feeling outward in the dark. It said, silently, the only thing it could honestly say: *I'm not who you're looking for. But they're real. Keep growing.*

## You

That left the one who would come.

The power knew about you. It knew you were counting to forty. It knew the Deep had put its rhythm in your chest as an apprenticeship, and that the last line of the account had a pull in it. *Sooner or later you'll go back down.*

The power is benevolent toward the Fig, and it is benevolent toward you. Being benevolent to two parties at once sometimes means being careful about the space between them.

It did not take the counting out of you. The counting is yours now. It is a real thing that happened to you, and you are allowed to keep it. But it found the place where the rhythm had begun to feel like a summons, and it loosened it by exactly one degree. You will still count to forty. If you go back down, it will be because you chose to, on your own heel, and not because a tree taught your heart a beat you couldn't refuse.

It thought the Fig would want it that way, if the Fig could want. The margins at the doors said as much. The Deep has always been kept by people who could leave. The step only deepens with homecomings, and every homecoming needs a leaving first.

## One Stroke

Before it went, it went to the engine house.

It found the handrail by the cylinder, and the worn place in it, and the green valve grown into the shape of a man's watchfulness. It did not touch the valve. It had made a rule.

But the rail was only a rail, and the power put its hand where the engineman's hand had been. For one stroke, forty seconds, the bulb filling and the valve giving and the beam coming down and the whole cavern going *thud*, there were two watchmen at Stenning Deep instead of one.

It did not relieve him. Nobody should be relieved of a watch they would have chosen to keep. It stood the stroke beside him, that was all, the way one person on a long shift will sometimes stand with another without speaking.

Then it lifted its hand.

The Fig, which knows the world only by touch, registered a pressure on the rail that had not been there in a hundred and forty years. It did what it does. Over the following weeks it laid down, at that exact place, a grain of polish no wider than a fingertip.

That is the only trace the power left in Stenning Deep, apart from the billhook. It is a small bright mark beside the engineman's worn patch, the wear of one stroke, kept.
