# The Guest

## The Noticing

It does not arrive the way powers are supposed to arrive. There is no light over the hollow and no change in the weather. The first sign, if anyone were there to read it, is that the leaking whistle, flat for decades, is listened to all the way through its note for the first time since the last engine-man stopped hearing it.

What drew the attention was not the Engine, or the green, or the strangeness of a place that runs itself. The world has many strange places, and most of them are best left alone. What drew it was the stair. A trough worn into stone that does not wear means something has kept going. Something has kept attending, in a place where attending costs this much, for longer than anyone has been counting. That kind of faithfulness gets noticed eventually, by whatever does the noticing.

So she came to look, and she came on foot. At the top of the stair she stopped and looked at the trough for a long time. Then she went down beside it, along the glazed and treacherous edge, because the trough was not hers. It had been made by someone else's labour, and she did not want to wear it with her own feet.

## What She Put Down

She had brought, as any such power does, a great many mercies. On the way down she took them out one by one, looked at them, and set them aside.

She could stop the Engine. She put that down first and fastest. You do not stop a heart because the body it belongs to is unfamiliar to you.

She could open the ward: dissolve the seals, free the hinges, run soft rain through the terraces until the stone let go of the cart and the clocks and the doors. But the sinter is not a disease of this place. It is the thing's way of touching the world. To unmake it would be to tell a creature that its only form of holding is wrong. She had no right to that, and she was not sure it was true.

She could bring life: midges for the pools, a wren for the fig canopy, snails for the ferns. She thought of the pale lumps on the floors of the deeper pools, the ones you cannot identify by looking, and she put that mercy down very gently. You do not cure a loneliness by feeding it.

She could lift the thing from below out of the hollow altogether and give it the world it reaches toward once a season. But it has never stepped off the lip. A tide that knows exactly how far it comes in is not a prisoner of the shore. She could not tell yet whether the stopping was fear, or wisdom, or simply its nature. Until she could tell, she would not decide it for it.

That left the man. She did not know whether there was still a man. That was the one mercy she kept in her hand, because you cannot know whether someone needs freeing until you have asked him.

By the time she reached the bottom step, she had almost nothing left to give. That, she thought, was probably correct.

## Staying

She stayed a long time.

She did what anything does in the Sinter Ward. She stood still and let the water bead on her sleeves. She let it dry chalky and then bead again. She did not brush it off. Within a few days her boots were sealed to the terraces between knocks, and she learned to lift them on the fall of the beam, with the small clean crack, as the Tender does. Within a season she was cream and rust at the edges, and ferns had begun at her collar.

She did not walk the Tender's round. She followed it at a distance, the way you follow someone whose work you respect, careful never to stand in the place it was about to stand. She sat in the relay hall through many rises of the beam and listened to the cabinets until she could hear the round in them: *boiler house, dye vats, mill, relays*, open and shut, the same small errors at every speed. She learned the heat-map in the moss. She learned which main was choking by the pitch of the whistle, which was the one thing everyone agreed the last engine-man could do. It took her longer than it had taken him.

The thing from below noticed her, of course. It notices everything it touches, and it was touching all of her. Its layers on her were very thin and very careful. She thought it was doing what it did with the cart and the clocks: learning her by keeping her, a paper's thickness at a time. She let it. It seemed only fair. She had come to learn it too.

## The Watch

She found what was left of the man at the Engine.

It was not much, and it was not in pain. That was the first thing she needed to know, and she was glad of it for a long time before she went on. The Tender is mostly the thing from below, wearing a skill it learned from someone it lost. But a skill is not all of a person, and when she laid her hand on the warm column beside the Tender's paddle and waited through the long rise, she felt something under the skill. It was very small and very old, and it was not a wish to be free, or to be alive again, or to be remembered.

It was a man on the last watch, waiting to be relieved.

He had banked the fires. He had stayed. The rest of the works had gone up the stair in the evening, and no one had ever come down to say *I have it, go home.* So some sliver of him had kept the watch through all of it, through the sinter on his cuffs and the closing of his hands and the long slowing into stone. He had never been asked to. There was simply no one else, and you do not leave an engine unattended.

There are very old words for this, and she used them.

On the fall of the beam she put both hands on the column, took the weight of the knock through her own ribs, and said, in the way the engine-men said it to each other at the changing of shifts:

*I have it. You're relieved.*

Then she stood the watch. It was one rise, eleven minutes. She listened to the whistle and the mains, felt for the choke, and minded the Engine the way it had been minded, so that for once the watching was not his to do.

She did not know what would happen. She had decided that whatever happened would be his to choose.

What happened was very quiet. The Tender stood beside her through the whole rise and did not lean. At the top of the stroke, in the pause before the fall, the shoulders that had been uneven for a hundred years settled, by about the width of a fingernail, into something almost level. It was the posture of a man who has set down a lever.

At the next exhale the Tender climbed the stair as it always does, one step per knock. At the lip, in the white, something went out with the steam that did not come back down.

## After

The Tender still walks. She had half feared it would stop, and she would have honoured that too. But the round was never only his. The thing from below had learned *going around* and *attending* and *leaning in to what is failing*, and those had become its own the way a song becomes yours after the singer is gone. It goes on.

It has begun, very slowly, to go on differently.

It no longer stands for a month at the joint that was replaced before the man was born. It has started stopping at the new length of main that grew where a wall used to be. It lays its flank against the new main and waits, and the moss on its arm thickens on the cool side. It has stopped rehearsing a dead man's working day and started tending the ward that is actually there. The relays have changed their pattern. The arc lamps, some nights, flicker in time with nothing anyone can name, as though the ward were trying out a rhythm of its own.

She had not done that. She had only relieved the watch. What the thing did with the freed attention was its own affair, and she thought it was doing well.

## The Fig

Before she left, she ate a fig.

She chose it carefully, from a low branch near the Engine hall, just split and running at the seam. She ate it standing in the alley, slowly, the way you eat what a host has set out when you understand what it cost. It was warm and very sweet, with a faint mineral edge, like everything there.

The relays in the domed hall fell silent for the space of a breath. In the history of the ward that had never happened outside the long cycle.

In all its years, nothing had ever taken anything from the thing from below and left it whole. Everything it met, it kept. Everything that came near it was finished. It had been setting out food for a guest it had been told about, perhaps by the figs themselves, which are made for being eaten and must know it in whatever way figs know things. It had never met one.

Now one had come. She had taken a little, and the ward was a little less, and it did not hurt. A small absence had opened in its shape, and the absence was not a wound. It was the first thing it had ever *given*.

She thanked it, aloud. Then, because a guest should leave something, she left the stalk on the bottom step of the stair, where the sinter would find it and keep it. That, at least, was freely given.

## The Coat

When she finally climbed out, she did not break the sinter off herself.

She let it hold her as it wanted to, in all its paper-thin layers. On the fall of the beam she stepped forward out of it, the whole shape of her, the way the Tender steps free of each crust. She left it standing on the stair: a pale hollow coat of a visitor, open at the front, ferns at the collar, faithful and empty.

The thing had made shapes without things before: the rimmed wheels on the pool floors with no cart inside them. But those were made from what was lost. This was a shape it had been handed. She had given it her outline to keep, so that it could learn she had been there without keeping her. It learned something from that. What it learned was its own, and she did not ask.

## The Lip

She came back once, in winter, for the exhale.

She did not go down. She stood on the ordinary grass above the hollow, in the thin ordinary cold, and waited while the pressure climbed and the whistle rose and every valve in the ward opened together. The steam came up out of the basin the way it always had and poured over the lip and reached. It was snowing.

The thing from below has touched a great many things and kept every one of them. It had never touched snow. Snow cannot be kept. It came down into the white breath of the ward and was gone at the first touch, flake after flake, thousands of them. Each was held for an instant, then not held, and then held again by the next. For an hour the thing reached out past its own edge into a world that could not be sealed. It learned the shape of something by losing it, over and over, and it went on reaching anyway.

She stood in it, and put out her hands, and was touched.

Then the steam drew back, the way a tide does, exactly as far as it always has. Below, the Tender was already a step down, and then two.

## What She Did Not Do

She did not save the Sinter Ward, because it did not need saving. It will finish itself. Some day it will be a single pale stone in a hollow with the Engine inside it like a heart, the Tender beside it like a hand, ferns all through it in the dark, still growing. She does not think this is a tragedy. It is a body being completed by the only creature with the right to complete it. She will not stand in the way of that.

She did not bring the animals. If they come, they will come because the ward has learned, slowly, through a fig and a stalk and an empty coat and an hour of snow, that a welcome does not have to be an ending. Things with one summer to live can tell. They always could. If the ward changes, they will know that too, long before anyone else. She is content to let them be the ones to judge it.

She did not stay, because staying forever would have been only another way of being kept.

## Since

But some autumns now, if you go down the stair beside the trough, you will find a fig on the bottom step. It lies with its seam open and one bite gone, the stalk set neatly beside it.

No one sees her come. The relays go quiet for the space of a breath, and then go on in their new pattern, the one no one can follow.

And the figs still fall into the pools, most of them. But not all of them now. Not for a guest it has never met.
