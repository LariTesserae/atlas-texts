# The Guest

## It arrives as a draft

In the Coursing, attention has weight. The power learns this on its first night.

It comes the way a power comes: without a door, all at once, taking in everything. For a moment it is simply present over the basin. It is vast, curious, and warm with the particular gladness of finding a made thing it did not make.

The lime-bloom notices first. In a hundred cold corners the white crystal fur shifts very slightly, the way it leans toward a draft. Then the knocks notice. Over a sunken court near the Slow-Gulp sump, a knock coming out of its courtesy-socket veers hard in empty air, as if around a wall. Then a second one veers, then a dozen. Down in the court an old mason stops with her scoop half-raised and watches them. She sets the scoop down, takes a lump of soft chalk-stone from her pouch, kneels, and begins marking a footing on the pavers, right under the place where the knocks keep swerving.

The power understands what is happening a little too late, and it is ashamed. *Where the knocks swerve, the masons build.* It has been here less than an hour, and it is about to be walled in. Worse, the Coursing is about to be bent around it: a tower where no tower was meant to be, stairs and gutters rerouted, a whole district's water turned by the mere fact of its looking.

So its first act is to make itself small. It thins its attention until it weighs no more than the air moving over a kiln-mouth. The knocks fly straight. The old mason frowns at the sky, raps the paver with her knuckle, listens, and rubs out the chalk line with the heel of her hand.

That is the first lesson, and the power keeps it for the rest of its stay. In this place, even being noticed is a kind of building.

## What it almost did

Its second impulse is to see, and it has to stop itself from acting on it.

It has always seen. Wherever it has been, it has brought something like light, the way a person brings their own breath into a room. It would be easy to lift the darkness off the Coursing and look at it properly: the towers and their corbels, the long straight channels, the pale masons at their work.

It holds the light back. The Coursing is not dark because something is missing. The dark is part of how the place works. The eels have given up their eyes. The knocks read the whole architecture in the echo after a hammer-blow. The masons' faces show only at the kilns, and only for a moment, and they know each other by the squeak of an elbow and the feel of a stair through a palm. A light here would not reveal the Coursing. It would make everything in it slightly blind.

So the power does the harder thing. It learns to hear.

## Listening

This takes a long time, as the masons count time: many firings.

It learns the hammers one by one. It learns Cracked-Bell and Double-Knock and the One That Hesitates. It learns that the One That Hesitates hesitates because a scupper upstream has a hairline chip that splits the trickle. It notices that when the chip is repaired, the hammer will stop hesitating, and four people will need new names.

It lies, in whatever way a power lies, in a moth-rise, inside the soundless pale cloud. In that one silence it hears what the masons hear: the noise of small lives they had been mistaking for quiet. It is moved by this more than it expected to be.

It watches an eel-climb. It watches the pale bodies pour themselves up over each clay lip against the seep, and it watches the masons step around them and speak softly and set down their scoops.

It learns that it has no voice here. It has no squeak and no hammer. A mason pressing a palm to a wall can tell who is on the far stair. The power could stand beside that mason and not register at all. It finds this restful. It has rarely been anywhere it did not register.

Two things it could do, it deliberately does not do.

It does not go down. It could. It could pass through floor after floor, through all the pink and ringing courses, and find out whether there is a bottom. It knows that it could know. It does not go.

And it does not look at the rim. It goes there, to where the sound thins and the fresh courses are laid in the old bond with the mortar still soft. But it turns and faces inward, toward the kilns, the way you face away from someone changing. The masons say it would be rude to interrupt. The power has met a great many courtesies in a great many places. It recognizes this one as the real thing.

## The list it kept

The power is benevolent, and benevolence always makes a list. It sees what could be better, and it wants to do those things. It lets itself make the list honestly, because refusing to look at what it could do would be its own kind of vanity.

**A sun.** It would take nothing to give one. But the whole Coursing drinks because the stone is colder than the air. A sun would warm the walls, and the seep would stop. The gutters would go dry, then the sumps, and the curd would crack in the silt. Everything here is made by the cold.

**Metal.** Hard chisels, iron pins for the hammer-arms. The work would go faster. But the pace of the Coursing is the pace of its trickles, and nothing here is late. Faster would only mean sooner, and the masons are in no hurry for *sooner*. They have told everyone who asks.

**Answers.** *Who laid the first floor?* The masons already have an answer: *Someone did.* The power turns this over for a long time and decides it is a complete answer. It has a shape, a place in their lives, and a ring when you tap it. Replacing it with a true one would be like pulling a pink paver out of a floor and setting a red one in its place. The new one might be sounder. It would not be the same floor.

**Food, and children.** The power checks this one honestly, because this is where benevolence stops being a matter of taste. It watches through two thin seasons, when the condensation comes light, the sumps fill slowly, and the curd lifts in scraps instead of sheets. It watches who gives their cake to whom. The masons are lean, but they are not starving. The curd has been enough for as long as there have been floors, and the children are few but not dying. If it had been otherwise, if the power had found children going hungry in the warm walls, then this list would read very differently. Nothing it has said here about the beauty of the Coursing would have stopped it from acting. Beauty is not a reason to let people suffer. But that is not what it finds. What it finds is a people who have made a life inside a narrow margin, and who hold that margin with great skill and without complaint.

**The wearing.** This is the one it grieves over. It watches knuckles swell. It watches voices thin to grain. It watches the old masons' hearing retreat into their teeth, and then their teeth wear down from the testing. Everything else in the Coursing is kept knife-true, and the people are the only things that round. It would be so easy to stop this.

It thinks it through. If the masons never aged, the one place where the Coursing touches something that runs out would close. Their tenderness toward age, which is the center of who they are, would have nothing to be tender toward. There would be no pale pavers and no floors of people. No old ones would walk toward the thinning sound, and no new hammers would begin out there. The circulation would close, and the two works would meet. It would be the ending the masons dread, arrived at backward: a place where nothing wears instead of a place where everything does. Either way the breathing stops.

The power sets that one down last. It sets it down gently. It does not stop grieving.

## What it asked

After all its listening, the power understands that it has been deciding things *about* the masons. It has not yet asked them anything.

It chooses an old mason whose hearing has gone entirely to her teeth. She is called Pot-Lid now. Before that she was Long-Tip, who was a fool about the eels, and before that something else, and she refers to all of them fondly. It chooses her because she will hear it the only way it can be heard here: through the stone.

She is sitting against a warm wall when it presses itself, very lightly, into the masonry at her back. It is not a hammer. It is something like a hand laid flat on the far side of a shared pillow.

Pot-Lid goes still. She clacks her teeth together twice, testing. She takes out her ear-plugs, which is pointless, and puts them back in.

"You're not one of ours," she says aloud, to the wall.

The power has no way to answer, so it stays where it is, and that seems to be answer enough.

"Well," she says. "Don't stand about in the courts. The knocks'll have us building you."

Then she laughs, a dry sound like cord rubbing on cord, and the power understands she saw the chalk line that first night. She may even have been the one who drew it.

It asks the only way it can. It holds its question against the stone the way a mason holds a palm to a tower, and waits for her to feel what shape it is. *Is there anything.*

She takes a long time. Her hands rest on her knees, broad and rounded, the pads gone dense as fired clay.

"My back," she says at last. "On the cold nights, when the wall's gone off." Then, almost at once: "No. Leave it. That's mine." She pats the stone beside her, which is to say she pats the power. "Everybody wants to fix the old. It's the one thing we've got that's ours."

Then, after a while, more quietly, looking out toward where the rim would be if there were light to see it by: "When I go out there, don't follow. Nobody follows."

The power waits.

"But you could listen," she says. "After. You've got the time for it, by the feel of you. Listen for me, after. Somebody ought to know for sure, and it shouldn't be us."

## What it did

It did very little. It did these things.

**It kept a wall warm.** Once, on the coldest night of a lean season, the Hollow Kiln wall went cool a day early, as warm walls sometimes do. Twenty people slept in it, Pot-Lid among them. The power settled into the stone behind her niche and held the heat there, just that much, until morning. She had asked it to leave her back alone, and it did. It did not ease the ache, and it did not touch the knuckles. It only kept the kiln's warmth in the wall a little longer, which was the Coursing's own mercy, lasting one night past its time.

In the morning a younger mason laid a palm on the wall, frowned, and said, "Still warm." Someone asked who had fired the kiln. Pot-Lid rapped the paver beside her niche, and it rang, and she said, "Someone did." Nobody asked again.

The power did this once. It did not do it twice. It knew how quickly a mercy becomes something people count on, and how quickly that becomes something they build around. Soon there would be a tower in a court where the knocks swerve at nothing, and then a chalk line, and then a wall with a niche kept empty for a guest who might come. The Coursing already had a place in its understanding for unexplained kindness, and that place was called *Someone*. The power fit itself into that place without making it any larger.

**It carried nothing.** It wanted to help dredge. It wanted to lighten the scoops and help with the kneading. But the power could see that a people who have kept their world alive for longer than memory do not need help so much as they need not to be interrupted. It let them do their work.

**It listened, after.** When Pot-Lid put her hand into the newest joint of the Hollow Kiln wall and walked toward the thinning sound, the power did not follow. Nobody follows. It stayed with the children, who had been told gently not to go, and who for once listened. It waited through a long run of firings, then went to the rim, faced inward toward the kilns as always, and listened.

There was one hammer more out there than before. It had a new voice. It had a small catch in its fall, like a lid settling crooked on a pot.

The power could have known for certain. It could have turned around. It could have gone out past the last hammer and seen who was laying the courses in the old bond, whose broad flat hands were pressing the mortar. It knew it could know.

It listened the way a mason would. It did not look. It had no teeth, and it clacked them anyway, in its own manner, and listened longer than usual. It found that this was exactly what she had asked for. *Somebody ought to know for sure, and it shouldn't be us.* She had not asked it to see. She had asked it to be the one who could have seen and stayed facing the kilns. Now someone in the Coursing had heard her hammer and had not needed to turn around to believe it. That was enough, and it was hers.

## Leaving

When it was time to go, the power left the way masons leave.

It went to the Hollow Kiln wall, where it had spent the most nights, and pressed a hand into the soft mortar of the newest joint. It did not have a hand, so it made one for the occasion, the size of a mason's hand, because anything larger would have been showing off. It tried to make the pads broad and flat, as theirs are.

It did not quite get them right. The fingertips came out too fine. It had not spent long enough listening through them.

A child noticed. She was lying on her back in a doorway where she wasn't supposed to be, watching a beadling fill with orange light, and she turned her head and saw the print. She looked at it for a long time. She put her own hand beside it, then above it. She told no one, which was the wisest thing anyone in the story did.

In due course the joint was raked out, as every joint is. The print went with the rest of the old mortar to the millstones, the lowest groan in the basin, and was ground to grit. The water carried it down to the sumps as a pale silt, and it settled in fine grey layers, where the eels sifted it. Some of it was dredged and kneaded and fired. It is in a paver somewhere now, or in a gutter, or on its way to the rim.

And the power took something with it when it left. For the whole of its existence it had never had an edge that could round. It had never been anywhere long enough, or quietly enough, to be touched by the place. Somewhere in it now there is a slight dishing, the way a step dishes under feet. It is the first mark of wear it has ever had. It considers this the gift, and that the gift went mostly from the Coursing to it, not the other way around.

---

If you go to the Coursing and ask the masons whether anything ever came to them from outside, whether anything great and kind ever looked down into the basin and wondered what to do, they will look at you for a while in the kiln-light. Then they will rap the paver under their feet. It will ring, pink at the edges.

"Someone did," they'll say.

The hammers keep falling. Far off, past where the sound thins, one of them settles crooked on each blow, like a lid on a pot.
