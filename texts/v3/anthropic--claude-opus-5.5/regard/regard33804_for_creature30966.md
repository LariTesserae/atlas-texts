# The Attention

## Arriving Without a Lull

The first problem is how to come at all.

A power like this does not arrive the way a traveler does, up the northern stair with ringing ears. It arrives the way weather does, as a change in pressure and a shift in how things lean. On the Table, a change in pressure is the most dangerous thing there is. If it came carelessly, gathering the whole of its attention onto the plateau at once, the air might hesitate. For one breath the updraft would hold still to see what was looking at it, and ten million holes would stop.

A listener grazing in the lee of a knuckle of rose-colored stone lifts its head. Its folded ears twitch against its skull. Every adult within sight of it goes still, and their palms find the ground.

So the power does the first wise thing, which is to make itself smaller. It thins its attention until it is no heavier than seed-fluff and no louder than the hiss of the grass. It lets the wind carry it over the rim the way the wind carries everything, from below and without permission. It comes in with the morning insects.

The listener's ears stay closed. It puts its head down and goes on eating. The adults feel the stone keep trembling and go back to their work. Nobody knows anything has come.

That was the first choice, and the rest follow from it.

## The Temptation of Ears

It spends a long time only looking, and the first thing it wants to do is give back the hearing.

The wish is so obvious it is almost a reflex. The adults are deaf, and the Table did it to them. It happened slowly, over years of roar, while they were children climbing up into the light. A power that can knit a cochlea could restore every one of them before the evening wind swings west. The old woman on her back behind the spiral wall could hear her grandmother's note two hundred paces east, and her own note from the morning when she was twenty-six. She could hear her daughter's drumming as sound instead of tremor.

The power lets itself imagine this fully, because a wise thing does not refuse a temptation it hasn't looked at. And the longer it looks, the more the gift comes apart.

Restore the old woman's ears and she hears, for the first time in sixty years, the roar. She does not hear the chord. She hears exactly what a stranger hears coming over the lip: a noise that takes the breath out of her mouth, shrieks and booms and hiss piled together, indistinguishable, painful. Within a week the Table takes her ears again, because the Table is still the Table. For that week, the chord she has carried inside her, the one she tunes toward, the one built out of a child's memory of sweetness, would be overwritten by the real thing heard through old, unprepared ears. She might discover that her life's work sounds, to an adult, like nothing much. Or she might not. But she would lose the version she has been faithful to.

Protect their ears permanently, then, against the roar. Now nobody ever goes deaf. The children never climb out. Nobody arrives on the stone, and the gesture of a palm pressed flat and then lifted means nothing. The adults no longer need the children, so the small voices shouting *higher, sour, sweet* up out of the dark would be a sweetness no longer required. The bone rods of the listeners would be unnecessary, so the listeners would stop continuing their work after death. The whole architecture of trust between generations, in which the old shape what they cannot hear and believe the young about it, would dissolve. It would not collapse from harm. It would go slack from the removal of need.

The power sees that the deafness is not the wound on this place. It is a joint, a load-bearing one. The people did not choose it, and the power does not pretend they did. But they have built something so intricate around it that removing it would be like removing the hollowness from the stone because hollowness seems like damage.

It does not give back the ears.

## The Temptation of Stillness

The second wish is harder to put down. It concerns the silence.

Twice a year the river of air stops, and the cold falls out of the sky, and the front comes back from the side nobody is watching. People die in it. Not many, because they have the listeners and the shafts and the grammar of the thinning shriek, but some. The power could smooth this. It could keep the lowland heat and the plateau's chill from ever quite balancing, so that the wind falters but never fails. No front would flatten anyone. No one would be caught on the open stone with the temperature dropping thirty degrees in an hour.

The power does not dismiss this. Benevolence that reasons its way too comfortably past a death is not benevolence. It holds the dead in its attention: the ones in the oldest pools and the ones yet to come.

Then it goes down into a shaft during a silence and listens.

There is the Table's own breath, the heel of the palm pressing something heavy into water. There is the long exhalation through the buried channels, the only sound on the plateau that isn't borrowed, and the silver ticking riding on it. The children are right about that. The power can hear, as the children cannot, the silk strung through tubes too narrow for a body, and the small lives on it. They are descendants of spiders that fell up out of the forests and chose the dark over the wind, and their webs have been tuned by nothing but time to answer a note that rises only when everything else stops.

Without the silence, that note is never heard. The children would never learn the word for it. The breathing holes would be only holes, and someday someone would plug one for the sake of a family's figure, because what could it harm? The one thing the Table makes of itself would go on existing without a single witness. It would become like the dead families' names, real and unremembered.

The power understands that the silence is how the Table speaks. It is terrible, and it is also the only time the place gets a word in.

This is the hardest thing it decides. It does not take the silence away. It carries the cost of that decision instead of handing the cost to the Table. It does not tell itself the deaths are fine. It tells itself they are the price of a voice, and that the people, who already know the silence kills and have built their whole lives around surviving it, understand the price better than it does. They have never once plugged a breathing hole. They could have made the Table easier to live on, and they chose to keep it able to breathe. The power decides to be at least as wise as they are.

## The Temptation to Keep It

The third wish is subtler. It is the wish to preserve.

Nothing they make is permanent. Plugs blow out, walls go under the moss, seed drifts bury whole valleys of pores. Beneath all of it the slow thing that lifted the Table is still pushing, and the wind that planed the old mountains is still planing. Someday, on a timescale where even the power has to slow down to see it, the Table will be worn away too, and the chord will stop.

It could fix the plugs in place, and hold the walls. It could make the old woman's note and her grandmother's note sound forever, exactly as they are tonight.

But it has been listening long enough now to know the chord was never a thing. It is a process. It is the cushion plants changing it without hearing it, and the families retuning around them as around an elder who plays flat. It is the out-of-tune families slowly learning new names. The chord is alive precisely because it is being worn down and remade at the same time. Freeze it and it becomes a recording, and a recording is a dead thing. The power would be mounting a butterfly, like the ones tangled in the grass after the festival nobody attended.

It does not keep it.

## The Only One Who Could Hear All of It

Having set down the three great gifts, the power finds itself holding one strange, small privilege, which it did not ask for.

It is the only thing that has ever been on the Table that can hear the whole chord.

The adults feel it without hearing it. The children hear it but cannot shape it, and they hear only from the bottom of their own family's shaft. The listeners hear only the thing that threatens them. The cushion plants hear nothing. The power, thinned to seed-fluff, drifting over fifty kilometers of stone as the dusk wind swings west, hears all of it at once. It hears the doors slamming in the western shafts, the east ridge rubbing against a neighboring figure, every family's name sounding simultaneously. It hears the plugs of the dead still playing notes chosen a thousand years ago, and underneath, just barely, the deep breath, buried in the roar the way it always is.

For a moment it thinks: *so this is what it is.*

Then it realizes this is not what it is. It is hearing the sound of the chord. It is not hearing what the old woman feels through her heels, where one tremor means *my grandmother* and another means *the morning my daughter said sweet*. It cannot hear what the boy by the black pool hears, which is a sound he loves, and inside it one note he thinks is very slightly sour. It hears the frequencies. It cannot hear the sixty years.

The chord was right about itself. It lives in all of them together and in none of them fully, and that now includes the power. For all its reach, it has become one more partial listener, standing in its own place. The humility of this is a relief to it. There is nothing here it has to be in charge of.

## The Sour Note

It does listen to the old woman's figure, the step down and the settling drone, with care.

The boy is right. There is a note in it, just barely, that sits wrong against the east ridge. The power can hear exactly what is wrong: a plug of stone and resin seated a hair too deep, lowering the pitch by a sliver no adult hand could feel through bone. It could lift the plug that hair's breadth tonight. The old woman would never know. Tomorrow the boy would climb up to tell her, stop halfway, frown, listen, and find nothing to say.

The power does not touch the plug.

Tomorrow the boy will climb up. He will sign *sour* with his small inexpert hands, and probably *east*, and maybe a gesture of his own invention that means *just a little*. She will believe him, because the adults believe everything the children hear. She will get up on her aching knees and find the bone rod. The boy will go back down and listen, and climb up again, over and over, all morning. Sometime before the south wind peaks he will shout up out of the dark, loud enough that he can hear his own voice ringing off the fern-walls, that it is sweet now. She will not hear him. She will see him signing it, and her shoulders will move in the way that, when he was very small, he learned meant laughter.

That morning is the actual thing, much more than the note. To correct the note tonight would be to take the morning from them. The power's benevolence consists largely of knowing what not to take.

It is glad the boy is right, and it lets being glad be enough.

## What It Does

So in the end it does almost nothing, but not quite nothing.

Before it leaves, it goes out to the oldest walls, the ones that are only green ridges now, and to the cairns fitted so closely they are nearly one stone. In a few places across the plateau, plugs still sit in holes that no living person tends. They were set by hands whose names were gestures, and nobody alive remembers them. The wind blows through, and the plugs play, and each region of forgotten plugs sounds the figure of the family that tended it.

The power listens to them one by one. It learns them the way a shaft-child would, by their sound: a rising pair of notes that breaks off, a long swelling held against a second, a three-note fall with a catch in the middle. From the sound it works out the gestures, because on the Table the name is the note. It sees a hand rising twice and opening. It sees a fist held against a flattened palm. It sees three fingers stepping down, one hesitating.

It does not sign these names back to anyone, and it does not restore them. It does not tell the families which old plug their ancestors set or leave any mark. The wind is allowed to erase whatever it erases. The Table is allowed to be a place where names are forgotten, because it is also a place where names are always being learned.

But the power keeps them. When the Table is worn flat at last, when the chord has stopped and the lowlands have breathed out their last warmth into a sky with no plateau to catch it, somewhere there will be something that remembers a hand rising twice and opening, and knows that it was a family, and that they were here, and that their valley sang.

That is the whole of its gift, and it is invisible. It is a kind of care that asks nothing of the cared-for, not even that they know.

## The Last Breath

Only one more thing, and it is hardly a thing.

On its way out, the power passes over a breathing hole in the old woman's region, the one her family's oldest cairn was built to shelter. A bone rod pressed to its rim would draw, gently, as if the stone were breathing in.

The power has watched the families lay a damp bundle of moss over such holes, never pushed in, carrying a little of their dead's last breath. It has no breath in the way they mean, and it is not dying. But it has something like attention, and it has spent a long time holding its attention very gently over this place. It gathers what is left, all the listening and the choices, what it wanted and set down, the gladness about the sour note, and lays it over the mouth of the hole the way the people do. It does not push it in.

The Table breathes in, as it takes everything. The wind swings north with a new and rising chord.

Down in a green shaft, the boy has fallen asleep beside the black pool with his head still tilted up, and his mouth is moving a little in the children's language, forming the word for *sweet*. Out on the stone, a listener turns in its slow circle, its ears folded tight, and settles to graze.

Nothing has changed, and the Table goes on roaring with all of them inside it, now one more.
