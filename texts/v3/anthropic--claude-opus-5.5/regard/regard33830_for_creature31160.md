# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not arrive the way such things arrive in stories. There was no light and no voice, and the swifts did not scatter.

It had been noticing the shelf for a long time before it came, the way you notice a sound you have been hearing all day without knowing it. What drew it was not the size of the place or its strangeness. Strangeness is common, and the world is full of stone that grows and lichen that eats odd things. What drew it was the steadiness: a single fine note held in the world for decades without wavering. A load that rose and fell. A flow that rose and fell. And between them, something that answered each change with exactly the correction it needed and not a hair more.

It came up with the afternoon cloud. It took an hour, as the cloud does, and by the time the white had climbed the cliff and swallowed the pylon, it was there. It had the weight and the attention of a presence and very little of the shape. The snails did not change course. A swift came in through the louvers, and its thin scream did not break.

It stood, if standing is the word, in the roar, and it listened.

---

## What It Could Have Done

A power like this could have done nearly anything, and it began, as the wise ones do, by counting the things it could do so that it would know what it was declining.

It could have made the iron eternal. It could have made the bearings never wear, the belt never stretch, the buckets of the Pelton wheel never pit under the endless hammer of the water. The governor would turn forever, the lichen would never go hungry, and nothing here would ever have to change.

It could have stopped the ledge from growing, or hurried it, so the stone would reach the wire in a year instead of an age and the creature could walk down its own spine by next spring.

It could have mended the winch and opened the way down. It could have cleared the cloud, so that the valley and the town and whatever burns green in its lamps would lie in plain sight for anyone to come and see.

It could have brought the keeper back. It could have returned the short, pigeon-toed stride, the fingertip on the filed patch, the person at the table with the kettle hot, as they were before they stood up to answer a sound.

It could have seen to the snow. The snowfields above are thinner than they were, and the power knew it. Everything on the shelf runs on meltwater. It could have made the snow deep and lasting, a guarantee laid over the whole mountain.

It counted all of these, and then it turned toward the eastern end of the shelf and went in through the swollen door of the turbine house, because it wanted to watch the governor before it decided anything.

---

## The Governor

It watched the iron balls for a long time.

They whirled on their hinged arms in the noise and the spray, each wearing its breath-thin bloom of blue-green. When the flow strengthened they lifted a fraction of an inch and eased the gate, and when the flow slackened they sank and opened it. They never jumped. They never overshot. A governor that answers a small change with a large correction does not hold a machine steady. It sets the machine hunting, surging and stalling, faster and harder, until something breaks. The whole art of the thing is proportion: to feel the error and answer it with exactly its size.

The power knew this art. It had practiced it on things much larger than a turbine. It had also, long ago, learned the hardest lesson that art has to teach: the most dangerous thing a benevolent power can do is overcorrect. When you are strong enough, every mercy is a large correction. Every gift can set the world hunting.

Each thing on its list, it saw, was that kind of correction. Iron that never wears would be a machine that no longer needs keeping, and so a keeping with nothing to keep. A ledge hurried to the wire would be an arrival the creature had not made. A path opened down through the cloud would not stay open only for the gentle. And the keeper returned as they were would undo whatever the keeper had chosen to become.

So it did not decide yet. It went to learn the rest of the creature first. You should not touch any part of a living thing until you have some idea of the whole.

---

## Down the Wire

It followed the cables out from the pylon, over the drop, and down into the white.

The beads were there, as promised, strung along the wire, spaced by some patience of their own. Far down, where the cloud was thickest and the cables sagged lowest, it came to a place where one of the three had broken. It might have been a storm or a fallen tree or simply age. The two ends hung a hand's width apart. The beads had crowded to both ends, grown into each other, and closed the gap with a soft blue-green braid, supple as the skin on the belt, carrying current. There was no way to know how long ago that had happened. The braid was old enough to have its own lichens.

The power went on down to the far end of the line. What it found there belongs to the valley and is the valley's to tell. It is enough to say that the load at the bottom of the wire is real, that it has a daily breath, that its lamps turn down at night, and that nothing there needed rescuing either.

It came back up the wire the way the beads do, slowly, and arrived at the shelf at dawn, when the cloud had sunk and ten thousand drops of water were catching the flat light.

---

## The Circuit

It walked the keeper's loop.

It did not have to. It could have known everything on the shelf at once, from the heat in the deepest crack to the angle of the last fern frond. But some things are only learned by going around them in order, and it wanted to know the shelf the way it had been known. It went from the hut door to the turbine house, to the anemometer mast, to the benchmark, and back, on the path of fine, flat moss.

On the second morning the visitor came down.

It came down the pitons from the blank column face, placing each long-legged step exactly, pale as sinter, its hocks furred blue-green. At the bottom it stopped and looked at the power for a while with its strange, level eyes. Then it went to the near basin and drank as if nothing was different, and the power understood that it had been accepted, or at least permitted, the way the swifts are permitted.

They walked the circuit together. The goat went first, since it was the goat's circuit, and the power stayed a step behind.

At the turbine house the goat laid its muzzle against the filed patch on the second bearing housing and held still, its long ears turned back. The power listened too. The bearing hummed clean. The lichen's slow bead of resin welled up and slid into the seal. But under the hum, under the whine and the thud and the drumming pipe, the power heard what the goat perhaps could not. It was a faint irregular tick in the rhythm of the wheel, once in every many hundred turns. One bucket of the Pelton wheel had been pitted by a century of water, and a hairline crack had started at its root. It would hold for years, perhaps forty. Then one day it would shear.

The lichen cannot regrow steel. When that bucket goes, the wheel will run out of balance, the governor will feel it, and something on the shelf will have to change.

The power stood there with this knowledge for a long time while the goat listened beside it.

It could have mended the bucket with a thought. Instead it thought about what it had already seen. This keeping had lost its keeper once, and it had not died. It had changed form. It had grown resin, felted itself onto copper, braided itself across a broken cable in the dark of the cloud. It had taken a person's habit of attention and made it into a creature. Whatever it will do when the bucket fails, it will do in its own way and at its own pace, and that way will belong to it. To take away that trouble would be to take away the thing it is best at.

So it did not mend the bucket. It marked the day in itself instead, the way you mark a date on which you intend to be present. When the wheel goes out of balance, it will be near. It will not come to fix anything. It will come to see what the keeping becomes, so that the keeping is not alone when it happens.

The goat lifted its muzzle from the housing, satisfied with whatever it had heard, and walked on toward the mast.

## The Logbook

That evening, inside the cloud, it went into the hut.

The logbook lay on the table, swollen into a single brick, its spine furred blue-green. In a few more years the lichen would have all of it. The power did not stop that. It did not dry the pages or restore them or carry the book away somewhere safe. But it read it. It read the whole thing without opening it, page through pulped page, because that was within its power. And because someone had written in this book for thirty years, alone on a ledge above the clouds, for no reader at all, and it seemed to the power that this deserved one.

Most of it was what you would expect: weather, flow, the pitch of the penstock, bearing temperatures, the replacement of brushes. Then, in the margins, other things.

*Visitor again. Drank from the near basin. Not afraid of me. Not interested in me either, which I find I prefer.*

*Ledge. Another finger. I don't know who to tell.*

*Green on No. 2 housing. Did not scrape it. That bearing runs cooler than the others now. Will watch.*

*Green on all housings. Did not scrape it.*

*Belt should have gone by now. It hasn't.*

*Heard the governor correct before I reached it tonight. It was wrong for a moment, and then it was right, and I hadn't touched it. Sat a long while after.*

And near the end, in a hand grown looser:

*It doesn't need me to listen anymore. I think I would like to anyway. Going up to see where the visitor comes from.*

After that there were a few pages the damp had taken entirely, and then nothing.

The keeper's name was on the inside cover, written small, the way people write their names in things they don't expect anyone to read. The power learned it. It will not be written down again here. It is kept.

## The Measure

Before dawn, in the last of the cloud, the power went to the benchmark boulder and measured the ledge.

It laid its attention along the row of scratched ticks, each a finger's width apart, each with its tiny year. It went across the hand's width of bare rock after the last one, across the moss, and out to where the newest basalt runs glossy and too warm to touch, pushing itself one more fraction of a hair over nothing. It counted the years since the last tick and the fingers of growth since then, and it got a number.
