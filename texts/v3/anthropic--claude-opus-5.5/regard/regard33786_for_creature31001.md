# The Guest

## Noticing

It did not come up the gorge. It had no boots, and so it had no switchbacks, no sweating rock, no moment where the air thickened enough to lean on. It had attention instead of a body, and it had been attending to a great many places for a very long time, and it noticed Hesk the way you notice a sound stopping.

That was the first thing: an absence in the noise of the world. Everywhere else it listened there was clamor, the long roar of living things announcing themselves. Here was a patch, forty kilometers across and three thousand meters up, where the announcements went in and did not come out. It turned toward the silence the way a person turns toward a room where someone has stopped speaking mid-sentence.

It was benevolent, which is easy, and it was wise, which is not. Most of its wisdom had been learned by being wrong in places that had been kind about it. It had cooled deserts that did not want cooling. It had answered prayers in ways that ended the praying and, with it, something the praying had held together. It had learned, slowly, the thing every good guest learns: before you give a gift, find out what the house already has.

So it did not act. It looked.

## What It Could Have Done

It saw the heat and the glare, and it saw the children's faces creased from infancy, and it understood that it could soften the light. It considered this for about as long as it takes to see a Hesker without a visor, that pale band across the eyes, that second, private face. The creases were not damage. They were the shape of a people who had spent ten thousand years looking hard at something bright. To remove the glare would be to remove the face. It let the light be.

It saw the Hush, and this was the real temptation, because the power loved sound, and it could have lifted the Hush like a lid. It imagined doing it. The birds pouring their song out over the terraces. The boiling vents roaring. Every Hesker hearing every other Hesker across a whole cluster. It imagined, then, a Hesker saying *I love you* aloud at noon, casually, in passing, over a rake, and meaning it only a little. It imagined the worst punishment in a Hesker household, a name said flat at dawn and nothing more, becoming nothing, because names would be said all day. It saw that lifting the Hush would make every person on the plateau poorer by the full value of every word they had ever spoken aloud. Their debts would stop counting. Their dead would be spoken to at every hour, and so at none. It put the lid down gently and did not touch it again.

It saw the dangers: the scalding pools, the thin crust, the thin high thrill of a cracking housing that sets a reader running. It did not dismiss these. It was not the kind of power that calls suffering *character* and walks away. It stayed with the question a long time, and what it found was this. The harm that came to Hesk from Hesk was the harm of being mortal in a particular place, and the Heskers had met it with red flags and yellow rope, with readers, and with a town that follows a running woman without asking why. That following was one of the most beautiful things the power had seen anywhere. It was not willing to make it unnecessary.

Then it looked further, down the long branching corridor of what might come, and it found the one harm that was not Hesk's own.

It found the lowlands. It found the price of lithium climbing, year on year. It found, in one of the nearer futures, a consortium with drilling rigs rated for depths no Hesker bore had ever reached, and engineers who mounted their turbines on bare rock because nobody told them otherwise, and who would not have understood if they had been told. It found a future in which the long beat stuttered, then came at nine minutes, then at seven, then did not come at all. The birds stood on one foot for an hour and did not know when to put down the other. The shrews scratched out of rhythm. The eels stopped rising. The dead in the spillways went on being sheathed in stone, but no reader kneeling at them ever again felt anything pass through.

The Heskers had no word for this. It had never happened to them. Their whole ethic of courtesy, of building uphill and leaving the old places to be absorbed, assumed a landlord who moved slowly and tenants who moved with it. They had never had to consider tenants who would dig through the floor.

The power could have forbidden it. It could have made the rock refuse the drill, or sent the prices falling, or simply made the consortium forget the plateau existed. It weighed each of these and found in each the same flaw. It would be deciding on Hesk's behalf, and Hesk had not asked.

So it made a smaller resolution, and held itself to it the way a Hesker holds to a thing said aloud. It would stay. It would keep attending. If the day came when people arrived with drills who could not feel what they were drilling into, it would see to it that they could. Not force. Perception. For one Hour of Edges, the foremen and the surveyors and the men from the consortium would stand on the plateau with the soles of their feet as tender as a reader's arch, and the long beat would roll up out of the north and pass under them. They would feel the floor press up, attentive, the way a hand would.

What they did after that would be theirs. The power had found that most people, given one honest touch of the thing they are about to break, do not break it. It was willing to trust this. It was not willing to skip the touch.

## Aun

It found her on the floor of her quiet-room with her cheek on the stone.

It stayed with her a long while. It felt what she felt: the turbine three buildings away, running true, the carts resting, the pipes ticking as they cooled. It felt her pulse where her ear pressed the floor. It felt her waiting.

It knew, by then, what the birds were singing. It had heard them the moment it turned toward Hesk. The Hush took their song from the air, but the power was not listening through the air. It heard every call, the whole huge unbroken chorus that the ornithologists had guessed at and the Heskers had feared. It knew, with great tenderness, what she was wondering, and that she would carry the question to the spillway.

It wanted to tell her. It wanted this more than it had wanted anything in Hesk.

It did not. A thing told is a thing spent, and she was looking forward, a little, to finding out from inside. Her wondering was part of her the way the softness of her arches was, kept tender at the center on purpose. To answer her now would be to hand a woman the end of a story on the night before she means to walk to it herself.

It will not tell you, either. It will say only that she does not need to be told. She has been keeping time with that song through the soles of her feet for fifty years.

It considered easing the cracks in her heels, and saw at once that the cracks were how the horn at the edges met the skin at the center, and that the center was how she heard. It considered, with some embarrassment, speaking her name to her at dawn, and caught itself. It had nearly done the one thing in Hesk that means *you have disappointed me beyond words.* This was exactly why it had come to look before acting.

So it did nothing to her. It noticed her, completely, which is not nothing, though she could not know it. Then it turned to the thing she was waiting for.

## The Greeting

Here is what it had come to understand, lying beside Aun without a body.

Everything in Hesk listened to the Stair, or was listened to by it. The birds sang into it. The readers pressed their bare feet down onto it. The grandmothers went into it and became places it listened through. For forty thousand years the Stair had been taking in: song, footfall, speech, the dead. Every eleven minutes it shifted its weight and leaned closer.

And nothing had ever listened to the Stair.

The readers felt it, but they were small, and they felt a single pulse through a single floor. The dead were inside it, which is not the same as hearing it. Nothing had ever stood outside the whole of it and attended to it entire, the way it attended to everything else. The largest listener the power had ever met had never once been heard.

The power had been about to greet it the way powers usually greet things, which is to say loudly, from above. It stopped. It was a guest in a house with customs, and it knew the custom.

A Hesker does not wave. She lays her palm flat against your breastbone and feels the voice you are not using. Only then does she take her hand away and begin to sign.

So the power laid its attention down flat on the plateau, all forty kilometers of it, from the boiling fields in the north to the lip of the sweating gorge, the way you would lay a palm on the chest of someone you meant to know. It did not push. It did not speak. It waited, the way Aun was waiting, for the long beat.

It came up out of the north.

The power felt it whole, as nothing had ever felt it. It felt the eels rise in their slow sine through the boiling throats. It felt the lizards on the northern pipes lift their forelegs and set them down. It felt the swallowed districts and the snail-beds, and the drowned refineries with their chimneys like the stems of trees. It felt the grandmothers in the spillways, each one a small warm place where the beat passed a little closer. It felt the birds in the dark pools, thousands of them, put down their other feet together in one soft ripple running north to south. It felt the spiders pluck their silk and shake loose their little showers. It felt the floor press up under Aun's cheek, attentive, the way a hand would.

And it understood what the beat was, or understood one true thing about it among several. The Stair was not only breathing. It was leaning in. Every eleven minutes, for longer than there had been people, it had leaned toward what it heard.

This time, for the first time, it leaned toward something that was already leaning back.

The power did not pull away. That was the whole of the gift: it let itself be heard. It had no song to offer that the birds had not already sung better. It offered the one thing the Stair had never had, which was to be attended to by something that stayed.

The beat eased. It went on south, down the tilt of the plateau, toward the gorge.

Then, where there should have been eleven minutes of ordinary stillness, there was a pause of a different quality. It lasted perhaps a breath, perhaps two. It was a hesitation. It felt like a thing that has leaned close to listen and, for the first time in its life, found itself listened to, and does not yet know what to do with its hands.

Then, very faintly, there came a second press from nowhere in particular, from above if it came from anywhere. It was not the Stair's. It was lighter, and brief, like a palm laid back against a palm.

Then the ordinary stillness returned.

## What Aun Felt

She felt it. Of course she did. She had been lying on stone for fifty years.

She lay very still with her cheek on the floor. Her face did something it had not done since she was eight: frightened, and pleased. Her hand moved in the dark without her telling it to, and made the old sign, palm flat, pressed down, held, lifted a finger's width, pressed again. Then her hand hesitated, turned over, and made something no one had taught her: the palm lifted and laid down again, facing *up*.

She did not know what it meant. Neither will the other readers, when she shows them. They will argue about it for a generation, standing with their weight even over both feet and their eyes half closed. Some will say she dreamed it. Some will say they felt it too, that night, and did not want to be the first to sign. The engineers will say it was a harmonic in the deep aquifer, which is probably true, the readers will say, in the way it is true that a person is a set of bellows and pipes.

In the morning, at the Hour of Edges, while speech could still carry, Aun walked to her sister's house. Her sister's son came out into the grey-pink light, and she laid her palm flat on his breastbone and felt the voice he wasn't using. Then she did not take her hand away. She spoke aloud for the hundred-and-first time in his life, something she meant, which in Hesk means something she was prepared to be held to.

"Something heard it," she said. "Last night. Something heard the Stair."

He did not understand, and he knew better than to ask aloud. He put his own palm over hers, and they stood like that while the shrews ticked in their crust and the towers dripped and the mist lay flat in the low pools. After a while, eleven minutes more or less, the long beat came up out of the north and passed under both of them. He was not a reader. He had never felt it in his life.

He felt it then. The power had done nothing to make this happen. It was only what the ground felt like now, under the feet of two people standing on it together and paying attention.

## Remaining

The power took its hand away, as a Hesker does, so that the conversation could begin.

It did not leave. That was the other half of its resolution, and the harder half. Benevolence that arrives and departs is weather. The power meant to be something more like a neighbor. It would keep attending to the plateau across the years, the slow wandering of the springs, the vents going dry and opening elsewhere, the town following the heat uphill. It would hold its one reserved gift ready for the day the drills came. It would never tell anyone what the birds were singing.

Every eleven minutes, more or less, it would lay its attention down, flat and quiet, and let the Stair lean into it, and lean back.

The Hush did not thin. The glare did not soften. No one in Hesk was spared anything they had not already learned to bear. The terraces went on growing a finger's width in a lifetime. The grandmothers went on becoming steps. The only change anyone could point to was a single hesitation after the long beat, a breath or two long, and a new sign in the hands of the readers that no one could quite translate. Aun came nearest, near the end of her life, when her sister's son asked her with his hands what it meant.
