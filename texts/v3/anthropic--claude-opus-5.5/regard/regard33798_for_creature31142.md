# The Guest

## How It Came

It did not arrive the way powers arrive in stories, with light splitting the sky or a voice from nowhere. It had learned long ago that arriving loudly is a way of not arriving at all. You bring your own weather with you, and then you only ever see yourself reflected in it.

So it came the way the fog comes. It did not roll in from anywhere. It was simply present one dawn, condensing out of attention the way the mist condenses out of cold air. It chose a hollow between two tors, sat down in the knee-deep moss, and let itself be beaded with droplets like everything else.

It had noticed the tableland because of the warmth. From very far off, a land so cold and so old should look like a stone that has finished thinking. But there were points of heat scattered across it, thousands of them, flickering on and off in slow rhythms, each one the size of a palm. Something about that pattern had caught the power's attention the way a single lit window catches the eye across a dark valley. *Someone is awake over there.*

It had come to see who, and whether they were all right.

## The First Discipline

The first thing it did was nothing, for a long time.

This was harder than it sounds, even for a power, perhaps especially for one. It could have understood the tableland in an instant. It could have looked at the rotting granite and read its whole history, looked at the doubled sky and seen the seam, looked at the Near and known them the way the Near know stone. It declined. Knowledge taken all at once is a kind of trespass. It flattens a thing into what can be known about it. The tableland had spent longer than oceans becoming what it was. The least a visitor could do was spend a few seasons learning it at the tableland's own pace.

So it kept the local time. It sat through dawns and noons and pearl-grey evenings. It learned the crunch of grus and the cold spring a footstep presses out of sphagnum. It watched a patch of moss advance across a bare boss of gneiss over nine days, then retreat over eleven. It learned that its own shadow, in the noon hour, had begun to arrive a half-step late. It found that it did not mind.

It listened to the tapping. It did not walk toward it. Everyone who walks toward it walks along the seam, and the power understood without being told that chasing is the wrong shape of attention here. You do not come closer to the Near by pursuing them. You come closer by staying.

Many foggy mornings passed. On one of them, the power heard a sentence resolve.

## What the Walls Say

It had been following the tapping the way the musician once had, feeling the shape rise and gather over days. When the pattern closed, the power did not look up at the wall. It already knew the capstone was set. What it attended to instead was the meaning, which arrived all at once, the way a word you have been staring at suddenly becomes a word.

It was not a complicated meaning. The power had half expected a history, or a law, or a lament for whatever world the Near had come from. Instead the whole long statement of the city came down, in essence, to something very like:

*There is room. There is room. There is room here, too.*

Then it understood the finished houses.

They were not waiting for their builders. The Near did not live in them. The Near lived in the work, in their own clear morning on the other side of the fog, and slept in their own nights. The houses, with their laid hearths and short curled sleeping platforms and small niches, were built for whoever might come. The travelers had long assumed that the objects in the niches were left behind by people who became Near. That was true, but it was not the whole of it. The niches were made for leaving things in. A shelf by the door, the way any good host keeps one.

The city was hospitality. It was an inn the width of a continent, rolling slowly east across a land with nothing in it to eat, rebuilding itself ahead of the next arrival. It fell behind because nobody needed the rooms there anymore. It rose again because someone might need them farther on. The roads ran dead straight across empty country because they were meant for strangers, and a stranger should not have to guess the way.

The power sat with this for a long while. It had come to see whether the ones who lived here were all right. It found that they were spending their whole existence making sure that *anyone else* who came would be.

## What It Could Have Done

A power that is benevolent but not yet wise has a great many ideas. This one had them too, and it considered each one honestly before setting it down.

It could have made the city stand still. It could have stopped the unbuilding and let the finished districts remain finished forever, so the Near's work would never fall. But the falling was the other half of the gift. The stones from the ruins were the stones for the next rising. A city that stopped moving would stop arriving where it was needed. To preserve it would be to kill it gently, the way a pressed flower is preserved.

It could have brought life: birds for the silence, bees for the cushion flowers, worms for the peat. But the tableland's whole nature rested on the absence of rot. The dead leaves hung for centuries. The elders, if the travelers' story was true, stood in the moss holding the sky of their day. Introduce decay and within a thousand years there would be no rosette older than a lifetime and no ten-thousand-year leaf lying beside last spring's. Something irreplaceable would be spent to buy something common.

It could have shown itself to the Near. It could have crossed the seam in its full nature and stood in their clear morning and said, *I see you. I see what you have done. It is beautiful.* The power felt that desire strongly, and turned it over longest before setting it down. The Near had built for millennia with no audience. Their patience did not seem to be a hunger for recognition. The tapping had the sound of people who knew exactly how much time they had, and knew it was enough. A power arriving to praise them might be a kindness. It might also teach them, for the first time, to wonder who was watching. Some kinds of peace do not survive being noticed from above.

It could have lit the hearths. This was the smallest temptation and the most tender. All those laid fires, never kindled. But the fuel was dried rosette skirt, and the power now suspected what the rosettes were. You do not warm yourself by burning your host's grandmother, however kindly she was laid in the grate. And the unlit hearth might be part of the welcome: *here is everything you need, and the choice to use it is yours.*

It could have explained the tableland to the world beyond, mapped the seam, and published the rules. It declined. Some places are only safe for as long as they are only half-understood.

## Where It Did Act

It found the one wound on its fortieth or four-hundredth morning. It had stopped counting.

It was a traveler. She was not lost in the way of someone who has wandered off a road. She was lost in the way the tableland makes possible. She had walked through the lichen-splashed doorway, once, then twice, then a third time, and her shadow no longer lagged a half-step behind her. It lagged a full stride. She was cold in a way that even the tableland's cold did not account for. Her food had stopped running out, and she had stopped wondering why. She could hear the breathing under the tapping, and the almost-word, but she could not make it out, and she could not stop trying.

She was not becoming Near. She was caught. She was halfway across the seam and had stopped, the way a person sometimes freezes on a narrow bridge. She could not go forward because she was afraid, and could not go back because she had forgotten the look of rain. Travelers who do not come back have usually chosen. She had not chosen. She was simply stuck, slowly thinning, a figure on the edge of both skies.

This was the thing the power had come for. It did not fix the tableland or improve it. It found the one person here who was suffering and could not get herself out.

It did not decide for her. It did not pull her back into the world of rain and bread, and it did not push her through into the Near's clear morning. Either would have been a theft. What it did was smaller. It sat down beside her in the fog, the way it had learned to sit, and it gave her back enough of herself to choose.

It gave her the look of rain first, because that was the thing she had lost most recently. It was the specific smell of it on dry dust, somewhere she had once lived, and the sound on a roof. Then it gave her the almost-word, fully spoken, so she would not have to strain for it anymore. The word was the Near's word for *guest*, and it was a word of welcome, not summons. Then it gave her the plain knowledge that both directions were open, and that neither was a failure.

She wept for a while. Then she stood up, and her shadow came back to her feet.

She went home. The power watched her go east-to-west across the swells toward the far edge of the table, and did not know whether she would ever return. It found that it did not need to know.

After that it walked the seam, very quietly, for a long time. It found a handful of others caught the same way across the ages. Some were still there, thin as frost-smoke. It did the same for each of them. Some went back. Some, given back their whole selves, turned and walked through the doorway one final time with steady steps. The tapping took them in the way a song takes in a new voice. Their shadows went on ahead of them.

The power left the doorways where they were. It did not close a single one. It changed only this: no one caught at the seam would stay caught without someone, eventually, sitting down beside them. It left that as a small permanent quality of the fog, a warmth under the cold that you would only notice if you were lost.

## The Answer

Before it left, it allowed itself one thing it wanted.

It picked up a hammerstone from a wall-top in a building district. The stone was warm, the way they always were. It held the stone until the warmth was only its own, and then it tapped once on a fallen block in the moss, a single stroke. It was not a sentence and not a claim, only a sound: *I am here, and I heard.*

Then it set the hammerstone down exactly where it had found it and waited.

For a long moment there was nothing. The fog hung. Water ticked from beard moss somewhere.

Then, from just beyond the edge of sight, came one stroke in reply.

It was not a sentence either. It was the smallest possible answer, the one a host gives when a knock comes at a door already standing open: *yes. Come in. There's room.*

## The Night in the Finished House

So the power did the last thing, which was the thing a guest does.

It walked east until it found a district in the moment between completion and collapse. That was the moment nobody had ever been able to find, but it is not hard to find if you are no longer chasing anything. The roofs were closed. The channels ran clean. The rosettes in the cistern-garden brimmed, their pools showing a sky of deep violet evening.

It chose a round house near the road and stooped a little under the lintel. It lay down on the short platform, curled against the cold, because that was the shape the bed was made for. It did not light the hearth. It drank from the rosette by the door, a little, with gratitude, and the water tasted of stone and was warm.

It slept, or did what a power does instead. In the morning it left a single thing in the niche by the door: a smooth pebble, grey-green, taken from nowhere in particular. It was the kind of thing a traveler carries for luck. It was not a sign or a blessing. It was only what the shelf was for.

Then it went out into the fog and was not there anymore, in a way that had nothing to do with distance.

## Afterward

The city still moves. The walls still rise out of their own ruins and fall into the next rising. The hearths are still laid and unlit. The rosettes still face east. No animal has come, and nothing rots.

A traveler who knows what to listen for might notice two small differences. In the fog, if you are lost, you are never quite as alone as the silence suggests. And somewhere in the long eastward roll of the city there is a house whose niche holds a grey-green pebble. That house gets rebuilt a little more carefully than the others. It is not grander, just more carefully made, the way you tend the room where someone once stayed and said thank you.
