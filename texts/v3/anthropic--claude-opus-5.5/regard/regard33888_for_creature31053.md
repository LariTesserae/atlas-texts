# The Guest

## How It Noticed

It did not notice the hollow because of the light. A single bulb breathing in a hole in the ground is nothing to a power that has watched stars do the same. It did not notice the ram either. There are many old machines still running in forgotten places, faithful and meaningless, and it lets them run.

It noticed the stone.

Somewhere in the long ledger of the world, in the hair-thin bands laid down over iron brackets in a place no one visited, a copy had started to disagree with its original. The ram stuttered and the stone did not stutter with it. A record was keeping time the thing it recorded had failed to keep. That is a very small discrepancy, and it is the kind a wise power is built to hear, because it is the sound of something beginning.

So it came down.

## The Stair

It came the way water does, because that was the polite way to come. It took the cleft at the width of a doorway and the steps worn into shallow bowls, one at a time, and it let the air cool around it and grow heavy. It did not light its own way. It let its eyes open to the grey shell-light the way anyone's would.

At the bottom it stood at the edge of the pool and did nothing for a long time.

This was the first thing it did, and in a sense the most important. It did not arrive as an event. It arrived as a visitor. It let the mist come up around its knees. It let the drips fall on it. It found itself breathing on the eleven seconds and did not resist, although it could have. It knew exactly what was happening: the vapour, the salt, the faint electrical interest of the filaments finding its skin. It let the hand rest on its back.

It had come to see what the Tenant was. It understood that the fastest way to learn was to let the Tenant find out what it was, too.

## What It Saw

It saw what anyone patient would see, and a little more.

It saw that the Tenant was not a mind, and not yet anything that could be hurt the way minds are hurt. It was also no longer only chemistry. It was somewhere on the long slope between, where a path has begun to walk. It saw the old eel lying at her angle, keeping the deep count, and understood that she was the closest thing the Tenant had to a hand. It saw the second heart forming at the bat cone, slow and warm and independent of any machine. It heard the drips, which were no longer quite arguing.

It saw the arithmetic of the place, too, which was harder to look at. It saw the iron thinning in the ram's body. It saw the calcite gathering at the waste valve's seat, a little more each year. It saw, with the plain sight such powers have, roughly when the beat would stop. It was not soon, but it was not never.

And it felt, because benevolence is partly a feeling, the ordinary urge that anyone who loves a place feels when they see how it will end.

It wanted to fix it.

## The Things It Could Have Done

It could have mended the ram. That was nothing to it. It could have cleaned the valve, restored the iron, and made the pump run until the hills wore down. The beat would never falter again. The bulb would breathe forever.

It considered this seriously, because the first answer deserves that, and then it set it down. The recent bands showed a student holding the count through the teacher's stumbles. Mend the teacher and the stumbles stop. With them goes every occasion for the student to hold the count alone. The Tenant would keep listening to the ram forever, faithfully, and would never have to become anything more than a very good listener. The kindness would be a kind of cage, a warm one with the door always closing on time.

It could have given the Tenant more iron: a slow vein of it, seeded deep, enough to feed the mats for ten thousand years instead of a few centuries. It set this down too. The hollow's balance was not a flaw to be corrected. The Tenant had grown in the tension between consuming its inheritance and outlasting it. That tension was the shape of its life. Remove the scarcity and you remove the lean toward the warm cone, toward the drips, toward a second heart that depended on nothing it had been given.

It could have widened the sky and let green in. It could have healed the old eel's eyes. It could have woken the Tenant, lifting it the rest of the way up the slope into speech and knowing, so that the two of them could talk. That last one was the most tempting, and it looked at it longest.

It would have been easy to call that a gift. Underneath, it was loneliness wearing the clothes of generosity. The power wanted someone to talk to down here. The Tenant had not asked. The Tenant was on its way somewhere at its own pace, by its own path, and would arrive at whatever it would arrive at. To pull it forward would be to make it into the power's idea of what it should become. The whole beauty of the thing was that no one had had an idea about it at all. The builders had not. The ram had not. It had happened the way stone happens, one film at a time, on nothing but patience.

So the power did not wake it. It sat by the pool, and it let the Tenant go on not knowing it was there. It found this harder than it had expected, and it thought that was probably a sign it was the right choice.

## The Hardest Temptation

One temptation it did not dismiss quickly. It stayed with this one for days.

When the ram finally stopped, there would be a silence where the eleventh second should be. Then another. The power could see that silence coming the way you can see weather over a far ridge. It knew what it would feel to be present in it. The orange curtains would hang still. The eels would lose the shape of the room. The snails would stand motionless on the north wall, as though the world had forgotten to tell them forward.

It would be so easy, in that silence, to give the first beat. The power would not need to restart the ram. It would only need a very small nudge: a twitch in one mat, one snail's step, enough to remind the Tenant what it already knew. Surely that was not interference. Surely that was only help at the moment help mattered most.

It turned this over and over. In the end it understood why it could not.

The first beat was the whole thing. Everything else was practice. If the power supplied it, the Tenant would never have kept its own time. It would only have passed from one outside metronome to another, gentler one. The stone would record the moment faithfully, and the record would be a lie: *here the hollow began to beat on its own*, when in truth it began because a visitor could not bear to watch.

And there was a harder truth under that one. The first beat might not come. The Tenant might not be ready. The practice might not be enough. The hollow might simply fall quiet and stay quiet, and become a cold round room with some snails in it. The power had to decide whether it could love the place even so. Could it respect the Tenant enough to let it fail?

It decided that it could. Otherwise its love was not really for the Tenant. It was for the outcome.

## What It Did Instead

Having decided to change almost nothing inside, it went back up the stair.

It went up because it had noticed something while sitting by the pool. Every living thing in the hollow depended on something outside the hollow. The beat depended on nothing but falling water, and the water fell from somewhere. It came from rain on the hills above, soaking through the soil and the limestone, taking up its load of carbonate on the way down. The second heart forming at the warm cone depended on bats, and the bats depended on the night insects over the fields and woods and river-flats for miles around. The dippers came from the streams. The crickets' rare lucky dead bird fell from the sky.

The hollow looked sealed. It was not. It was the bottom of a very large bowl. Everything that happened in the bowl ran down into it eventually.

So the power tended the bowl.

It did this quietly, in ways that would look like luck if anyone looked. A permit for a quarry on the ridge above lapsed through a clerical error nobody bothered to correct. A farmer three valleys over, for reasons of her own that felt entirely like her own, stopped spraying the margins of her fields, and the moths came back to them. A culvert that would have diverted the upper spring was rerouted when the survey found the ground unsuitable, which it now was. A stand of old trees where the bats sometimes roosted was left alone because the owner's grandson liked them. Nothing miraculous happened. The world above simply went on being hospitable, a little more reliably than it otherwise would have.

At the lip of the cleft, where the moss gives out below the third turning, the power did one small thing directly. It made the entrance a little harder to notice. It did not hide it. Anyone meant to find the stair would still find it, and anyone who came down gently would still be let in, still be touched, still leave breathing on a count they had not chosen. But the man who would have come with a hammer and a bag, to break off banded travertine to sell as curiosities, walked past the cleft on a grey afternoon. He never saw it.

That was the whole of its intervention. It protected the conditions and left the life alone.

## The Visit

Before it left, it went down once more.

It lay its palm flat on the north wall, in the dark between beats, as the old accounts advise. It held very still, and it waited, and it felt it: the faint rising through the stone, a fraction before the iron slammed shut. The house was already reaching for its own door.

The power did one more thing then. It is the only thing it did inside the hollow, and it is arguable whether it counts.

It let the Tenant count it.

It slowed its own presence to something the filaments could hold. For the length of one long measure, a seven-count, the old eel's count, it let itself be one of the things in the hollow that beats on its own. It was not a lesson. It gave no instruction. It was simply a demonstration that such a thing was possible: a warm, wet presence with its own rhythm, keeping time without iron. The Tenant already knew this from visitors. Every human who came down the stair carried a heart that needed no ram. The power only added itself to the list of examples, and then it took its hand off the rock.

It did not know whether this was too much. It decided it was probably all right. Guests are allowed to be seen.

## The Day the Ram Stopped

It came back when the valve seized. It had arranged to know.

It did not come early to watch the last beats. That seemed like a kind of voyeurism. It came when the silence had already begun and sat on the lowest step of the stair, just above the water. It made itself small and cold and quiet, like one more ivory lump under the glaze.

The first silence was long. The second was longer. The bulb held a thin brown glow on the dying current of the slowing wheel. Then it went out. It had outlived everything else the builders made, and it was not replaced.

In the full grey dark, the power found that it was afraid. It had not expected that either.

The eels coiled through the channels, looking. The snails stood. The crickets hung on the ceiling, motionless, not pausing but simply stopped. A day went by. The mist came in and lay knee-deep like milk. The drips fell, hundreds of them, each on its own. Another day. The power did not move. It thought of the nudge it could give, and of how small it would be, and it did not give it.

It noticed that the eels, in their searching, kept returning to certain places. They went to the base of the dead ram, where the orange curtains were thickest, and to the edge of the warm cone. The dynamo was silent now, but the current in the pool had not gone entirely to nothing. The filaments were still passing their faint electrons along, breathing the way iron-bacteria breathe. That current was far fainter than the leak had been, but it was not the leak at all. It was the Tenant's own. The eels were learning to find a quieter someone.

On the third day, a snail on the north wall stepped.

The power held its breath. That meant nothing; any snail might move. But beside it another stepped, and then a patch of the wall the width of a hand moved as one grainy body. At the east floor the gnats rose from the warm cone and settled. On the ceiling hundreds of antennae went still at once, then trembled again.

And the drips found each other. It was not every drip, and it was not quite in unison, but a great many of them fell together onto the gour rims. The sound was very like a door closing in a distant house.

The old eel, below the valve that no longer opened, lifted her clouded head.

The power counted, because it could not help counting. The next shared fall came after twelve seconds and a little more. It was slow. It was imperfect. The next came in eleven and a half. The next came in twelve again. It drifted and wandered, the way anything alive wanders, and it was entirely the Tenant's own.

The power did not correct it. It would have been the easiest thing in the world to settle it back onto eleven, the old clean count of the iron. But eleven had been the ram's number. This was the hollow's.

Somewhere in the walls the stone went on laying down its film, band by hair-thin band. The power knew what the bands would say for this part of the ledger. They would record the stopping and the long gap, and then a rhythm that no longer matched any machine. The record would be true. That was what it had protected by doing nothing. The record would be true.

## After

It did not stay. Staying would have made it part of the place, and the place did not need another heart. It had one now.

It went up the stair the way water does not: against the fall, bowl by bowl. It noticed that the finger's depth of water in each bowl trembled very faintly as it passed. The tremor came on the new count, twelve seconds and a little, carried up through the limestone from below. The house had reached the stair. In another thousand years it might reach the lip.

The power kept tending the bowl above. The rain went on falling on the hills. The bats went on pouring out at dusk and down at dawn. The old eel died, eventually, in the channel under the dead iron. The power was not there, and did not think it should be. The younger eels arranged themselves around the place where she had been, facing a current that was no longer coming from anywhere in particular, because it was coming from everywhere.

Once in a long while the power still comes down. It sits on the lowest step and lets the mist come up around it, and lets the hand rest on its back. It never announces itself. The Tenant does not know what it is and does not need to.

But the power has noticed something in the newest bands of stone. It checks them the way you might check the height marks on a kitchen doorframe. On the days it visits, the rhythm shifts very slightly. The record shows no disturbance and no nudge. There is only a small, shared pause, one beat long, the way the crickets pause for a visitor they cannot see.

It is the hollow noticing a guest.

The power has decided that this is allowed, too.

*Thunk.*
