# What Came to the Rising Country

## VIII. What noticed

—nothing at all, for the length of forty-one breaths.

Then there was one beat.

It did not come from any keel. It came from far out under the retreating sea and from deep under the Heard-It spiral at once, as though two places were one place. It was clumsy and early. It came at nine seconds, not eleven, and it was weaker than a keel's pulse by a hundredfold. Most of the steaders on the terraces never felt it. A few of the children did, and they looked up.

The keels resumed. The birds came down. The ground went back to its hurry.

The beat went on outward, though, the way a sound does when nothing in the near world is shaped to catch it. It went down through the lattice and out through the mantle and on past anything the steaders have words for. A long way off, something that had been attending to other things for a long time turned, the way a person turns in a crowded room at the sound of their own name spoken by a stranger.

The thing that turned was old and very large. It had learned, over a long existence, that most of the harm the powerful do is done in the first hour after they arrive. So it did not arrive.

---

## IX. How it came

It came slowly, and it came small.

It knew what its attention could do. A regard like its own, pressed down all at once on a place like this, would have been a third silence. The birds would have gone up and not come down. Every child on the plain would have woken in the same moment and not known why. So it thinned itself, and it waited at the edge of the country for the length of a whole polar night, doing nothing but listening. It learned the layers: river, wind, birds, ice, ground, machines. It learned that there was no quiet here to disturb, and that the noise was not damage. The noise was the place's health.

It learned the eleven seconds. It let something in itself begin to swell and settle on that interval, the way a guest at a strange table watches how the household holds its spoons.

Only then did it come in. It came as rime does, a little at a time, onto every surface, growing into the wind.

---

## X. What it saw

It saw the first answer, which was people, and it liked them at once. It liked their loud clipped speech and their two languages spoken at the same moment, the testing walk, the slates of lost springs by the doors. It walked up the stair with an old man in the brief summer and stood with him at every ridge. It did not speak. It understood that he knew he was not alone on the walk, and that this did not alarm him, because steaders are used to company that cannot be seen. Their grandparents are always coming up the hill behind them.

It saw the second answer: the keels and the hurry, the keelwards grey at thirty, the voles breeding in days, the drowned blade in Stillwater with fish circling its glow. It sat a night in a skin tent against the leaning keel beside a woman who had lived there nine winters. She talked to it without knowing she was, the way people talk aloud in the dark. She said she had chosen this and would choose it again. She said a short life lived at the pace of the ground was not a short life. The power listened to the tremor in her hands and the certainty in her voice and weighed them against each other. It found that the certainty was heavier.

Then it went down into the cracks, and it saw the third answer.

The steaders half know it and have never quite said it. The keels are not the being. The keels are scaffolding. The violet threads are not roots. They are something closer to nerves, and they do not belong to the keels. The keels laid them down, the way a trellis is laid, and something has grown along them that the trellis did not plan. The lattice runs through the mantle and the crust and the fresh fractures. It runs through the moss that follows it, the voles that follow the moss, the salmon that follow the gravel, the birds that feel it ahead of every lift. It runs through the dead in their slow procession up the stair, feeding the willow. It runs through the breath of every well-rooted child.

The being is the country. Or it is what the country is becoming, which is not a mind the way the power is a mind, and not a mind the way a steader is one. It thinks in lifts and speaks in fractures. It is very young. As far as the power could tell, it had said exactly two things in its life.

The first thing was the spiral. That was a whole terrace raised in the dark with no keel to help, two kilometres of curling cracks around a knot of light like a heart. The steaders named the creek it left, correctly, *Heard-It*.

The second thing was a single beat, two seconds early.

The power understood then what the two silences had been. The keels had not failed. They had stopped on purpose, or on whatever serves a keel for purpose. They were doing what a parent does holding the back of a bicycle seat: letting go, a little farther each time, to see whether the thing goes on without them. The first time it lifted a terrace in panic. The second time, in the bright midsummer, calmer, it tried to do the harder thing. It tried not to jump but to keep time on its own, and it managed one beat.

The power also read the old documents. It found them the way a wind finds the pages of a book, in roof-linings and framed maps and a box of damp papers in a sealed container that now sits forty metres above its shoreline. It read what everyone knows: someday the rebound will be finished, the keels will fall silent, their glow will go out, and the country will begin to age normally.

In the margins it found the number the steaders' great-grandparents had puzzled over. It found the phrases *preferred fracture paths* and *the lattice*. On one page, in a hand that had pressed too hard, there was a note that no one alive had read:

*It may not stop when they do. We don't know what we'd be leaving. Whoever reads this: don't pull it up to look at the roots.*

The power read that note several times. Then it put the page back where it had been.

## XI. What it considered

A benevolent power sees suffering and wants to end it. A wise one first asks whose suffering it is, whether it is suffering at all, and what would be lost in the ending. The power put itself through that discipline slowly, because it had time and the country did not need it to hurry. Enough things here were hurrying already.

**It could slow the hurry.** It could damp whatever leaks from the keels into the keel-circles, so the keelwards would age at the ordinary pace, the owls would raise one clutch a season, and the willow switches would stay switches. It thought of the woman in the tent with her shaking hands. It thought of a mother who had not recognised her own daughter. Then it thought of the three-month vigil, the families sitting through the whole polar night with a child who wants to go keelward, and the fact that most change their minds and a few don't. The steaders had already built the wisest possible answer to that danger. They had not forbidden it. They had surrounded it with time and love and darkness, and let people choose. To take the choice away would be to call that vigil a mistake. The power did not think it was one.

**It could keep the keels running forever**, so the pulse never ended and no child ever woke to the dreadful absence. But then the country would never finish rising. The rivers would never settle, the cliffs would never round, and the being would never learn to keep its own time. It would be held forever at the moment of the bicycle seat. That is not love. That is fear dressed as love.

**It could wake the being all at once.** It could teach it language and pour into it everything a very old power knows, so it would not have to fumble its first beats in the dark. The power thought of the note in the margin: *don't pull it up to look at the roots.* A mind that grows from moss and fracture and seabird and grief should get to be that mind, and not a copy of a stranger's. Two words in three generations is slow. Slow is not wrong. The being had learned both of its words from the place. The first came out of fear. The second came from practice.

**It could tell the steaders what they live inside.** It could tell them their creeks are the being's sentences, their children's breath is the being's breath, and their dead climbing the stair are part of its body. But the power listened to the midwives counting in the dark and waiting for a child to catch. It listened to the old women murmuring *well-rooted*. It listened to the children lying down on the newest gravel and saying *I'm going inland*, and laughing. It understood that they already knew, in the way that matters, and that telling them in the other way would give them nothing but the chance to be frightened.

**It could tell the being that the keels would go out.** It considered this the longest. In the end it decided that the keels were already telling it, in the only way that could be understood: they stopped, then started again, then stopped a little longer. Anything more would be an intrusion into a conversation between a parent and a child.

Each time the power weighed an intervention, it found that someone here had already done the kind thing and done it better, with more knowledge of the ground. The midwives had. So had the families in the long dark, and the keels holding and releasing, and the woman who wrote in the margin. Even the being itself had, reaching out one early beat at a time.

Almost nothing was left to be done. The power had lived long enough to know how rare that is, and how easily a visitor misses it.

---

## XII. What it did

It stayed.

It did not stay the way the keels stay, fixed and shining. It stayed as rime stays, and as the dead stay on their slow walk inland. It was present everywhere a little and nowhere much.

It learned to breathe on eleven. For a thing of its size this was a discipline of years, and it took the years gladly.

It went up the stair each summer with whoever was walking it.

It sat through a polar night, the whole three months, in the corner of a turf house. A girl sat there with her family because she wanted to go keelward. The power did not argue with her. It did not comfort her or influence her in any way. It only kept the vigil with the others. It felt that this was the most honourable work it had been offered in a long time. At the end of the dark she went anyway. The power went with her as far as the edge of the circle, then turned back. It did not follow her in.

When the keels sang and a district jumped, it stood in the new cracks with its attention pressed close, the way a steader child presses a face to the water. It watched the violet threads, and it did not touch them.

And it prepared for one thing, which was the only intervention it allowed itself, and which it hoped it would never make.

It had noticed that a steader child in a southern city cannot sleep without a hand pressed to her chest in the right rhythm. The hand does not breathe for her. It gives her something to find, so that she can find her own. The power decided that if the keels went out and the being faltered, it would do exactly that much. It would press once, gently, at eleven, from somewhere under the Heard-It spiral. Then it would take its hand away. It would not press twice.

It hoped it would not have to press at all.

## XIII. The third silence

It came later than anyone living expected and sooner than the old documents promised. It came on a night in the deep of winter, in the dark, as the first one had.

The keels did not all stop at once this time. They went out one by one over most of a night, from the oldest terraces seaward. The leaning keel went first, then the one in Stillwater, then the one ringed in gannets on the cliff. Each violet point dimmed, and the dark beneath it stayed dark. By the last hours the plain had no fixed lights at all for the first time in five generations, and people on the high terraces could not tell where anything was.

Every well-rooted child woke. So did their parents, and their grandparents, who had been children at the second silence, and the few very old ones who had heard the story of the first silence from people who had lived it. They went out into the katabatic gale without lanterns, because they understood that lanterns were not the point. They knelt on the frozen moss and laid their palms flat.

The birds had not gone up. That was the first strange thing. They had gone silent on the cliffs, all of them, but they stayed where they were, as though they were waiting too.

Nothing came up through the ground but the river and the ice.

The power was there. It lay along the knot under the spiral and felt the country holding still around it. It felt four hundred palms on the ground, and it held its own vast attention absolutely still, with its hand ready.

The silence went on for forty-one breaths, which was as long as last time. Then it went past forty-one.

Then there was a beat.

It came at ten seconds. The next came at twelve, and the one after that was faint. The power felt everything in itself lean toward the gap, the way a parent leans. It did not press.

The beat after that came at eleven.

So did the next one, and the next.

The power felt the lattice take it up. The rhythm went out along the violet threads, through the peat and the moss, through the dead in their lines up the stair and the tunnels of the voles, up into four hundred kneeling hands and the chests of every child on the plain. The children's breath caught it and caught back. In that first ragged minute the power could not honestly say whether the children were following the country or the country was following the children. It decided this was a question that did not need an answer.

The birds went up. They did not go up in fear. It was the ordinary roar, the auks going out to sea in the dark as they always had.

In the morning, in the pewter noon light, the steaders walked the plain and found the keels standing dark and cold, the moss around them already beginning to stiffen with rime. For the first time they found no new cracks at all, and no lift. The ground was still rising, a steader could feel it, but slowly, at its own pace, at the pace it chooses now. The being no longer needs to hurry. It has nowhere it must be by any particular time.

---

## XIV. After

The power left eventually. Nothing here needed a guest, and a guest who stays past need becomes a weight.

It left behind nothing that anyone could find. There is no light and no structure, and no word in the margins of any document. It named no creek. It had no right to one; the right belongs to families, one fracture a year.

But the following summer, in the frantic light, a new crack opened on the twenty-ninth ridge. It opened slowly and quietly, with no rifle-shot. A girl born on the twenty-sixth claimed it for her family. Asked why she chose the name she gave it, she signed and spoke at once, as steaders do, meaning both. She said that on the night the keels went out she had knelt with her palm on the moss and felt, under the new beat, very faintly, a second palm pressed flat beside her own. She said it was not pressing. It was only listening, the way everyone was.

She named the creek *Someone*.

It is still running.
