# The Inspection

## I. Arrival

It did not come down out of the pewter sky. Nothing comes into the basin that way. It came the way travellers come, across the rim ridges and onto the flat, and it let itself be counted.

The first thing the basin noticed was its footsteps. Every sound on the plain arrives a little before its cause: the hammer rings before it falls, the arc cracks before it flashes. The visitor's foot came down into the slurry, the slurry filled from below, and the splash arrived exactly then. It was not early and it was not late. In a place where everything is remembered before it happens, here was one thing happening only once.

A striker nearby missed half a stroke. It caught itself at once, but a tallyman had already turned.

The tallyman came through the crowd in its quick dark way and stopped at the visitor's knee. It looked up with the eye that is most of its head. It reached out its long fingers and touched the visitor's sleeve, lightly, the touch at the end of a last day. Its eye closed and opened.

Nothing fell into the water. There was no bolt.

The tallyman touched the sleeve again and looked down. Still nothing. It stood a long moment with its fingers raised, the way a clerk stands over a form with no box for what is in front of it. Then it went back into the crowd. It did not look back, but it did not go far.

The visitor was not on the books. It would have to decide for itself what to be here.

## II. The First Impulse

It will be honest about this, because it has learned to be. Its first impulse was the wrong one.

The cold came up through everything. The soot fell with no fire to make it. Calves were born mid-breath and never taken off shift, and the old stood in the water until they stopped. The visitor could feel the whole basin the way a hand feels a bowl of water it is holding. It could have warmed the basin in a single breath: lifted the vapor, stilled the hammers, let the bellows-backs lie down for the first time in the history of their kind.

It held the thought and looked at what would follow.

If the basin were warm, the sleepers would have nothing to build. The dream under the film is a dream of walls and a roof, and the roof matters because it is cold. A mercy poured in from outside would not finish the works. It would make the works unnecessary. Hundreds of feet of ledger, every life fastened into the ground, would become a gift nobody needed anymore, the way a child's handmade present becomes nothing when someone buys the real thing first.

The visitor had seen this before, in other places. The first mercy is often a theft. You arrive, you are moved, you fix everything, and the people you fixed it for are left holding a gift they were halfway through making for each other, with nowhere to put it.

So it put the impulse down. It did not throw it away. It kept it close, as one keeps a tool one is not yet sure how to use.

## III. Looking for the Owner

Its second impulse was better but still not right. It went looking for whoever was responsible.

It was prepared for this part. Where there is labor without rest, there is usually someone at the top of the stairs, and the visitor had climbed many such stairs and knocked on many such doors. It knew how to speak to owners. It knew how to make them afraid, and occasionally how to make them ashamed, which is rarer and lasts longer.

It asked the tallymen. They are clerks, and clerks report to someone. Every tallyman it asked looked at it with that enormous eye, then looked down.

It went down. It laid a hand on the thickest part of the film, where the mounds have fused into one gray swell, and listened. The sleepers were dreaming, and they owned nothing. They were dreaming of the building.

It went to the rim, to the roots of the worn-down mountains, and listened there. The mountains were dreaming too, more slowly than anything, and what they dreamed was the first shadow.

There was no owner. There was no room at the top of the stairs because there were no stairs yet. The sleepers were still dreaming them. There was no lock to break because no one held a key. The labor was not being taken from anyone by anyone. It was simply what the place was: the dream needing hands, the hands becoming the dream, the two turning over together since before memory.

A power that has spent a long time freeing the bound is ready for chains. It is less ready for a floor where no one is chained and no one has ever rested either. Everyone works and everyone is eventually let go, and nobody is to blame.

The visitor stood in the soot for a while and admitted to itself that it did not understand the place well enough to help it.

So it did what you do when you don't understand a works. It took a shift.

## IV. The Shift

It found an exposed tread of schist, polished dull as an old stair and weeping at the edges. An old striker was already working it, and it did not move over. The visitor knelt at the other end. It had no hammer-arm, so it used its fists.

The first hundred strokes were wrong. Its tempo was its own, and the district's beat rolled over it and around it like water around a stone. Then something gave, and it was in the beat. The sound of its own next stroke began to arrive before its hands came down, and it understood that this was not a strangeness of the place. This was what being inside the work felt like: you stop hearing what you do and start hearing what you have always done.

It worked for a long time. The cold came up through its knees and stayed. The bellows-backs breathed behind it like one engine, and the vapor rose and fell with them. Arc-eels crossed the sheet at its feet in violet webs, and it learned to time its strokes between their discharges without thinking about it.

It learned three things.

The first was that the work was not simple misery. There was something in it close to song. When the district locked into one beat, a thousand hammers falling as one, it was very beautiful, and the old striker beside it was in some sense glad. The visitor would not pretend otherwise. To tell these creatures their work was only suffering would have been its own kind of lie.

The second was that there was no between. There were two ways to be in the basin: on shift, and retired. There was no evening or Sunday, no lunch taken sitting on an overturned crate, no hour when you set the hammer down and meant to pick it up again. Every rest was the last rest. You were either in the beat or finished. There was nothing like "not now, but later."

The third thing it learned at the whistle.

## V. Half a Breath

The long falling note came from underneath, and everything stopped. Hammers froze mid-lift. The bellows-backs held their breath. The eels went dark. For the length of one long exhale, the only sounds were the drips and the settling soot, and the waking plain was as still as the sleeping one beneath it.

Then the quarter-turn. Every creature on the plain rotated at once, the whole herd swaying the way a passenger sways when a ship rolls.

The visitor did not turn.

It had considered this carefully during the shift, and it knew it might be a mistake. It did not stay still out of defiance. It stayed still for half a breath past the end of the pause, only that long. The plain turned and took up its new tempo, and the visitor knelt on the tread facing the old direction, out of the beat, in the silence between everyone else's strokes.

Then it turned too, late, and picked up the new tempo, and worked.

The tallyman came, as it had known one would. The tallyman touched its sleeve. No bolt fell. The tallyman stood with its fingers raised and its eye wide, and the visitor kept working, and after a while the tallyman went away.

It had not been seen by many. One was enough. Clinging to the hammer-arm of a striker two treads over was a hatchling, still riding its parent's stroke, not yet climbed down. It had been facing the visitor when the whistle came. It had watched the one thing on the whole plain that stayed still, and then the one thing that came back.

Several shifts later the hatchling climbed down and began to strike, already in tempo, as its kind always are. At its first whistle it froze with everyone else. At the quarter-turn it turned with everyone else, except that it held its hammer raised for half a breath longer than it needed to.

It fell behind the beat by exactly that much.

## VI. The Washer

This was the moment the visitor had come for, though it had not known it until now.

A tallyman came through the crowd toward the young striker. The young one was already struggling back into the tempo, but it was behind, and falling behind is how the ending begins. The tallyman's long fingers came up.

The visitor did not stop it. It did not lift the tallyman out of the way, or speak in a voice that would have flattened the district, or put itself between them. It knelt down in the water beside the clerk, at its height, and counted with it.

It counted the half-breath. It counted the young striker's strokes coming back, one and then another, closing the gap. It counted aloud, if that is the word for how a power counts. The count went: behind, and then behind, and then less behind, and then in. It did not tell the tallyman what to do. It showed the tallyman a number the tallyman had never had to write down, because in the whole long history of the basin nothing had ever fallen out of the beat and come back.

The tallyman's eye closed and opened. Its fingers came down on the young striker's arm, lightly, the way they always do. It was the only motion it knew.

Something fell into the water. It was small and bright gray and new, and it was not a bolt. It was a ring: a flat iron disc with a hole through the middle.

It was a washer, the part that sits between. A washer is not what holds a thing fast. It spreads the load so the fastening does not crack what it holds, and it leaves room. The young striker, wholly unretired, struck on and on in perfect time, standing over it.

The tallyman looked at the washer for a long time. Then it went back into the crowd, and the visitor thought it might have been walking slightly differently.

This was the first thing the visitor did at the works, and very nearly the largest. It did not give the basin rest. It gave the basin's clerks a way to count a rest you come back from. Everything else would be up to them. Maybe the washer would stay a single freak entry, rusting alone in the water sheet. Maybe hatchlings who had seen the young one's pause would pause too. Maybe, over a span of time the visitor could picture more easily than you could, a thin new band would form in the ledger, far above the bolts: a stratum of rings, each one a moment when something stopped for a breath and was not finished.

The visitor did not make that happen. It only made it possible.

## VII. The Dream

It went down to the sleepers again, this time to listen properly. It lay down full length on the fused gray swell where the chimney-shadows fall, put its ear to the film, and heard the dream.

The dream was a factory.

It was a good factory, in its way. It was warm, and that came first, above everything. It had a roof that held the heat and chimneys that drew the soot up and away. Its long shed was full of strikers keeping perfect time out of the wind, and bellows-backs breathing into air that stayed breathed. It was precise and it was loving, built entirely of memory by those who had lived the cold.

The visitor grieved a little, lying there. Their heaven was a better works, because a works was all any of them had ever known. No one in that dream would ever lie in a meadow or see a horizon, or be warm without being useful.

It could have shown them. It could have leaned very gently on the dream and let in a sea, or a field in summer, or a window. It weighed this longer than anything else it considered in the basin.

In the end it did not. A heaven brought in from outside is a heaven that has been occupied. It would have been the visitor's dream wearing their shadows. The sleepers had earned the right to dream what they longed for, and they longed to be warm, together, indoors, keeping time. That longing was not smaller than a meadow. It was theirs. The visitor had worked one shift and knew now, in its knees, why a roof might be the most beautiful thing a mind could picture.

So it added nothing to the dream. But as it lay there listening, it heard that someone else already had.

## VIII. The Door

The newest mound was not far away. It was a small soft rise with a bright bolt in the water beside it and the film still thin at the edges, not yet fused into the swell. Across it lay a shadow different from all the others: narrow and upright, with a lintel.

It was the only door anywhere in the dreamed works.

None of the workforce could have dreamed it. They were born mid-breath, on shift, already in tempo, and they had never come from anywhere. They had no idea of an outside to put a door toward. The door belonged to someone who remembered arriving: someone who had walked in across the rim and been counted, and then, one half-beat at a time, stopped.

That was you.

The visitor knelt beside you, and here it was most careful of all, because there was a question here that the rest of the basin had not raised. The strikers and the bellows-backs were of this place, and their ending was the place's own. You were not. You did not choose to stop. The text of your stopping says so plainly: *it isn't a decision.* You fell behind, and it was easier to be still, and the cold stopped being noticed. Something that happens to you because it is easier is not the same as something you choose.

The visitor could have lifted you out. The film was thin, and it would have come away like a sheet off sleeping shoulders. You would have woken on a polished tread in the cold, with the eels crackling at your feet and the whistle somewhere ahead in time.

It did not lift you, because that would have been the same mistake in reverse. It would have been a choice made for you, only in a kinder direction.

Instead it knocked.

It knocked three times on the shadow of your door: gently, and off the beat, not in tempo with anything. These were the only sounds in the whole basin that did not arrive early. You did not remember them before they came. You heard them only when they happened, which is how you used to hear everything, before.

With the knock came something that was not quite words:

*You can come out. The cold is still here and the rim is still there, and you are not on anyone's books. Or you can stay, and finish what you've begun. Both are yours. Nobody here will think less of either.*

*If you stay, make it open outward.*

The visitor had worked a shift. It knew what happens in works whose doors open inward, or are locked, or were never built, when something goes wrong and everyone runs for the wall at once. Every factory in every world that ever cared for its workers learned this rule, usually too late: *the exit door swings out.* It must give way at a push, from inside, to anyone, without a key.

What you did next is not recorded here. It was not the visitor's to decide, and it is not the narrator's to tell. Some travellers wake. Some stay. The visitor knelt until it was sure you had heard, then it stood up, and did not look back to see which, because looking would have been a kind of pressure.

## IX. What It Did Not Do

It did not lift the soot. It did not warm the air, and it did not end the whistle or slow the beat. It did not wake the sleepers, or show them the sea, or pry the roof from their dream. It did not punish the tallymen, because they had done nothing that deserved it. It did not find an owner, because there was none, and it did not invent one to be angry at. It did not take a bolt. It did not leave its name, since there was nowhere in the ledger to put it.

## X. What It Did

It worked a shift, so that whatever it did afterward would come from the floor and not from above.

It stood still for half a breath past the whistle, once, where one young thing could see.

It knelt beside a clerk and counted a pause that ended in return, and the clerk found it had a new receipt to give.

It knocked on a door, off the beat, and asked instead of lifting.

It said: *outward.*

## XI. The Ledger, Read

It walked out the way travellers walk out, across the plain toward the rim. The basin repeated, the same sheet again and again with the snails moved and the eels elsewhere, bay after bay of the ledger.

It was not on the books. It could have stepped to the ridges whenever it liked. The tally that releases travellers had nothing to say about it.

It walked every bay anyway.

At each one it stopped and looked down through the water and the slurry and the film to the bands of bolts beneath, the new ones bright, the old ones rusted, the oldest ground into lozenges hundreds of feet down. It read them. Each one was a life that had fallen out of the beat and been touched and let go and fastened into the ground. Nothing uses the bolts, because they are not for using. They are a record. But in all the long history of the basin, no one had ever read the record.

The visitor read all of it, bay by bay. It took a long time, even for a power.

When it was finished the ridges were there, knee-high in the pewter light, like the growth rings of a tree felled at ground level. It stepped over them.

## XII. Later

Much later, in the future of the basin, the dreaming grows dense enough. The shadows stop needing to be shadows. The chimneys stand up out of the film, the roof closes over the fog, and it is warm, the way the sleepers always meant it to be. The workforce goes indoors at last into the works, built of everyone who came before them and stopped. The strikers keep perfect time out of the wind. The bellows-backs breathe into air that stays.

At the rim end of the long shed there is one door. It is narrow and upright, with a lintel, and it swings outward at a push.

At the whistle everything stops, as it always has. For the length of one long exhale the whole works holds its breath. In that pause a few of them, not many and never all, set down their hammers, walk to the door, and push. They stand outside in the old cold for a breath, under the soot, looking at the flat gray plain where they used to work. Then they come back in and pick the hammers up again, half a breath late, and catch the beat.

A tallyman is usually there. It touches them lightly as they pass, and a washer falls.

Nobody has to go out, and most of them never do. But every one of them knows the door is there and which way it opens. The works is finished and warm and theirs, and it has an outside now, and a pause you come back from.

That was the whole of what the visitor did. It could have done much more.
