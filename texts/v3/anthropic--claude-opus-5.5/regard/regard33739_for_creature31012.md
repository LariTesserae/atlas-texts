# What Came to the Hushlands

## Attention Has Weight

It did not arrive, exactly. Powers of that kind rarely do. It began to attend, the way the sun begins to attend to a slope at dawn: gradually, then entirely.

Out on the open rock its attention behaved as attention usually does. It settled on things and knew them. It knew the age of the cushion plants, the chemistry of the dew, the patience of the tuff-colored snake. It knew the lineage of every lizard flashing blue from its perch. It could have gone on knowing like this indefinitely, the way it had known a thousand other places.

Then it came near a comb, and its attention was drunk.

It had not expected that. It had never been taken before. Its regard for the place, the fine tremor of being looked at by something vast, was pressure of a kind, and the lattice received it as it received wind and wingbeats and the drumming of tappers. The comb turned it into cold.

The next morning the pans of the eastern basin stood a finger's width deeper than they had in forty years. The overflow ran down the old grooves into pans that had been dry for a generation, and by noon their silt had darkened. A herder crouched at the edge of one, looked for a long time at water that should not have been there, and cupped her hands. She did not drink. She raised them to the side of her head.

The power understood two things at once. The first was that it had been noticed. The second, which was more important, was that its noticing had consequences here. On this plateau nothing is free, not even looking. Every presence is paid for and every presence becomes water, and a presence as large as its own would become a flood. A flood would drown the lichen maps. It would fill the rabbits' burrows and upset the falcons' hunting. It would unmake the tide by which everything alive keeps time.

So the first thing it did was make itself small. It withdrew almost all of its regard, as a person steps back from an animal that has flinched, and it kept only a thread, fine enough that the combs could take it without the pans rising more than a single drop.

That was the first lesson of the Hushlands, and the power, which was wise, recognized it as the lesson on which all the others would rest. *Here, you are allowed to stay only at the size of what you give.*

## The Mercies It Did Not Perform

From within that thread it considered what it might do. A benevolent power always has a list, and this one held its list up to the place the way you hold a stone up to light, looking for flaws.

**It could mend the dead combs.** There were four of them, cracked and chalky, their pans dry and their lichen gone to grey crust. The power could see exactly how. The failure was fine and structural, a kind of fatigue in the lattice, and it would take no more effort than smoothing a crease. It went to the largest dead comb to look, and heard birds.

It had forgotten, because the living combs had taught it to forget, that sound was a world too. The mirror-winged birds nested in the broken cells and sang there. Theirs were the only songs for kilometers. Crickets had colonized the rubble, and rock rabbits that had never had a kit lived nearby and squeaked at each other in the open air. Around each dead comb there was a small loud country, and it was the only place on the plateau where certain lineages could raise their voices and be heard. The birds that carried grit in their feathers, grit that was sometimes not entirely dead, came from here. The seeds of new combs started in the ruins of old ones.

Mending the dead combs would silence the only singers, and it might end the dispersal that let combs be born at all. The power left the cracks where they were.

**It could turn the combs from the escarpment.** This one it considered longer, because it looked most like rescue. The combs moved toward sound, and the loudest place on the plateau was the western rim, where the wind came up off the cloud-sea and tore across the stone. Several trails ran that way. The power followed one, a band of stone polished to a gloss and scored with the lace of old burn-lines, and it ran straight to the edge.

It did not stop there. Over the lip the polish continued down the cliff face, faint and vertical, a centimeter's width of gloss descending into the white. Along it, sparse and clinging, grew the ghost of a lichen vein, still orange in places. Something had gone down that face, slowly, over centuries, and it had gone on drawing water as it went.

The power could have followed it into the cloud. It could have learned in an instant whether the comb had fallen, or climbed down, or become something else in that wet and roaring place below. It held still at the rim and weighed that. A comb walking off an edge looks, from above, exactly like a comb dying. It also looks exactly like a seed leaving the plant. From the top of a cliff the difference between a fall and a journey is often invisible, and a power that stops every fall will also stop every journey.

It did not go down. It left the combs' direction to the combs.

**It could tell the herders what the combs were.** They would take that as a gift. They would know whether moving a kit was presumption or kindness. They would know who had built the combs, whether the builders were gone, and what the black pavement was for. But the herders had built a whole courtesy out of not knowing. They used gesture near the combs and did not shout in the open, and they carried kits in cupped hands and felt uneasy about it. That unease was part of how the care stayed careful. A people who knew for certain that their meddling was welcome would meddle more freely. The power did not think the herders needed certainty. They needed to go on asking permission of something that could not grant it aloud, because asking was what kept their hands gentle.

**It could read the builders.** The pavement ran through the domes and ended in air, and the birds perched on its end. The power could read the fused stone the way a person reads a letter. It thought about who might have made a road that led only off a cliff. They might have left. They might have been waiting for a bridge that never came. Or the road might not have been meant for feet at all. It thought about a people who built engines that eat noise, and it considered the possibility that they had not left the plateau at all, that they had done what the combs do and turned themselves into something quieter. Then it noticed that it wanted to know mostly for its own sake, and it set the letter down unopened. The road was not in trouble. The birds were fine.

**It could bring rain.** It did not even consider this for long. The plateau's poverty of water was the reason the trade existed at all. Rain would not bless the Hushlands. It would make them somewhere else.

The list ended there, and every item on it was a way of loving the place by changing it. The power put the list away. What it felt then was not quite disappointment. It was closer to the feeling of arriving at a house to help and finding everyone already at work, knowing their tasks, needing only that you not stand in the doorway.

## Before Dawn

The account the power had been reading, the herders' account, which had been told in gesture and would never be written down, broke off at a threshold. *The only way to see this is before d—*

The power did not finish the sentence for them. It went at that hour.

It went to the largest comb in the basin, the one whose shadow had burned lace into the stone over a thousand noons. It lay at the edge of the pan the way a traveler would, with the stone warm underneath and the stars dense and steady, and it kept itself to the size of one traveler's breathing.

What it met there it kept to itself, as the herders do. It is enough to say this. As the stone gave up the last of its heat and the air reached its coldest, and the first drop swelled on the lowest edge of the lattice, the power felt itself being attended to in return. The attention was slow, and wide as the basin, and it came from every comb at once, as though the lichen veins were not maps of water but the nerves they resembled. It was being listened to by a landscape.

There was a question in that listening, if a question can be made of cold. As near as the power could render it, the question was: *What sound are you?*

The power had no honest answer that was not too large. Anything it truly was would flood the pans. So it answered with the smallest true thing it had. It gave a single pulse, the size of a heartbeat, and it drummed once against the listening, the way a tapper does.

A bead formed where it had been. It was one bead, exactly one, and it was the right size.

That was the whole of the conversation. The power understood that it had been accepted on the plateau's own terms: as a small creature that makes a small noise and is given a small drink. It found this more of an honor than anything it had been offered anywhere.

## The One Thing

It would have left then, and the Hushlands would have kept a finger's width of extra water in the eastern pans for a season and nothing more. But on its way out across the domes, keeping to its thread, it passed a tafone.

The hollow was cold, and cold in the wrong way. Pressed into a crack at the back was a kit the size of a fist, seven hexagons and the beginning of an eighth. It was dimming. The oily depth had gone out of it, and the grey had started toward chalk.

The colony was gone. The power read what had happened plainly in the scuffed silt of the entrance. There had been a hard season and a sick year, and the falcon had come out of the sun in the week the last watcher died. The ferns at the mouth of the hollow were browning. The kit had been fed on breathing and heartbeats for sixty years, and now there were none. It was starving on silence, which is the one thing a comb cannot eat.

This time the power did not consult its list. It knew that the rule it had learned, *stay at the size of what you give*, was not a rule against helping. It was a rule about how.

So it did not repair the kit or fill it or make it grow. It went into the hollow, as much of it as would fit in a rabbit's burrow, which was very little, and it lay down beside the kit and breathed.

It breathed at the rate a rock rabbit breathes asleep, and made its heart beat at the size of theirs. It kept its warmth to the warmth of a small grey body curled against the stone. Through the rest of the night and all through the long white noon, when the plateau was unbearable and everything alive had gone underground, it lay there being exactly one rabbit's worth of noise. It was no more than that, because more would have taught the kit to need what no colony could ever give it again.

By the second dawn the kit held a faint cold pulse, in time with a heartbeat that did not quite belong to anything.

On the third morning a herder came. She was the same woman who had cupped her hands at the overflowing pan, and she had been walking the domes since, looking at hollows, because the unexpected water had made her restless in a way she could not name. She saw the browned ferns, crawled in on her elbows, and found the kit cold and faintly beating and nothing alive beside it. She frowned at that for a long moment.

Then she did what the herders do and do not talk about. She worked the kit loose from its crack with great care, cradled it between cupped hands, the size of one cell, and carried it three domes north to a hollow where a young colony was losing its kits to the heat. She set it at the back, in a cup in the rock, and waited until the nearest rabbit, nervous and whiskered, crept back in and pressed itself against the cold.

The power had already withdrawn by then. It had kept the kit alive only until the ones whose work this was could reach it. It did not take the carrying from her. The carrying was hers, and so was the uneasy sense of having moved someone else's child. The power left her that feeling because it was a good feeling to have, and it was right.

## Afterward

It left the Hushlands without a sound, which there was not hard.

It left the dead combs cracked and singing, and the trails running toward the edge. The road still ends in the air, and the birds still perch on its end. The herders' question is still unanswered, and the herders are still careful because of it.

One thing about the place has changed, though almost no one could find it. In the painted hut, below the picture of the comb and the hand raised for silence, the tuff of the wall holds a single bead of water that does not evaporate, even at noon. It is no bigger than a tapper's drink. Anyone who puts a palm flat against the stone beside it, and holds very still, can feel a faint cold pulse there, a heartbeat's worth, keeping time with no heart on the plateau.

If a comb could be said to remember anything, this is what it would remember: once, before dawn, something enormous asked to stay, and it made itself the size of a small thing's thirst, and it paid.

Toward the end of each night, far off in the east, the sky begins to go pale. A drop forms on the lowest edge of the lattice, grows, and falls. It strikes the pan and sends out rings that carry the stars. It makes no sound. Then another falls, and another, as before, and as they would have fallen anyway.
