# What the Attention Did

## I. The Arrival

It came during the rain, because that was the only time it could have come quietly.

It had been watching from far off for a long time before that, the way you might watch a pot you suspect is about to boil over, except it had slowly understood that this pot was supposed to boil. The seething was not a fault. The delta was not burning down. It was breathing. So the first decision the power made, before it came close, was a small and important one: *I will not arrive as an event.*

It could have come as a light over the thunderheads, or a stillness spreading from a point, or a voice. Any of those would have been read. The people here read everything: swifts, dogs, the pitch of the fields, the angle of a single finger. A light in the sky would have become a sign by morning, and a sign by the next front, and within three generations a passage in the recitation. So it came down inside the rain, as one more warm grey thing falling among millions, and when the rain stopped it was simply there, standing on a terrace among steaming puddles. Nobody looked twice.

Then, for the length of a full cycle of fronts, it did nothing but listen.

That is harder than it sounds for a benevolent power. Benevolence has an itch in it. It sees suffering and reaches. It saw plenty of suffering here: an old man with ruined knees hauled up a ramp in a chair, a family searching a new wall for the curve of a dead son's shoulder and not finding it, a district that set an hour early and took thirty people down with it into the warm mud. It saw the children who can't sleep in silence, and the diggers' cords hanging slack in their shafts.

It did not reach. It made itself watch the whole shape of the thing first, because it had learned somewhere, in some other place, maybe at some cost, that you cannot help a pattern you do not understand. You can only interrupt it.

## II. The Things It Did Not Do

It considered them all. It owed them that.

**It could have given them silence.** It could have damped the roar across the whole delta, so that people could speak aloud and children could hear their mothers' voices. It set this aside almost at once. Their language was not a compensation for the noise. It was one of the most beautiful things in the place: names that drift over a lifetime, grief spoken palm to palm, history carried in the burning arms of the young. To grant them silence would be to tell them their hands had been a disability. They were not. They were the thing the city was learning from.

**It could have given them stillness.** It could have stopped the slack and let them keep a home, a room that would be there in ten years, a door their grandchildren would walk through. This one it turned over for a long time, because it was the kindest-sounding and the most dangerous. The slack was the city forgetting, and a mind that cannot forget cannot think. Stopping the fronts would not have given the people homes. It would have killed the being that was, slowly and blindly, learning to love them, and left them living in its corpse.

**It could have given them green.** It knew how to make a root that holds in liquefying ground, or a seed that outpaces the make. It could have hung the terraces with leaves. But the people had no word for leaf, and that was not a hole in their world. The biofilms streaked down the keels in bands like canyon walls. Blue light ran along the seams at dusk. To plant a garden here would be the gesture of a visitor who thinks a place is unfinished because it doesn't resemble home.

**It could have woken the city.** This was the great temptation, and it knew it. It could feel, beneath everything, the folded warm dark thinking in rooms without knowing it was thinking. It could have reached down and switched on the light: *here you are, this is you, those are them.* It would have taken almost no effort. It would also have been theft. The city was waking already, at the pace of stairs lowering over centuries, of hollows appearing within one lifetime, of children learning words from walls. That waking belonged to the city and to the children. A mind woken from outside wakes into someone else's idea of it. A mind that wakes itself wakes into itself.

**It could have told them the truth.** It could have explained the city to the people: what the keels are, what the crabs are doing, what the diggers found and what the hollows mean. But they were finding out. The shell-readers had worked out the ages of the keels. The children had invented a gesture for the question the rooms were asking. Understanding that is handed to you sits in you like a stone. Understanding you reach yourself grows roots, even here.

**It could have saved them, miraculously and often.** It could have caught every district that set too soon and held up every wall with a family on it. It could have made the delta safe.

This was the one it could not dismiss cleanly, and it did not pretend otherwise. People died here who did not have to. A power that could stop that and chose not to had to be able to say why, and not in the voice of a principle. It had to say it to the woman searching the wall for her son.

## III. The Law of Keels

The reason, when it came, came from the city itself.

*The city learns what to keep by watching what its people return to.*

The power understood that it was not outside this law. Nothing that touched the delta was. If it held up falling walls, the people would learn, correctly, that something held up falling walls. They would watch for it and return to where it had been. They would press their palms to the places it had stood. In time, as inevitably as the stairs had lowered, the city would learn to keep it. There would be a keel shaped like the power, and around it the patterns of making and unmaking would spiral in slow rings. The people's ancient, graceful, carrying life would reorganize itself around a thing that was not theirs, and the city would bend its long slow growing toward a visitor instead of toward its own.

A benevolent power, it thought, must be very careful about becoming weather.

So it gave itself a rule, and the rule was not *do nothing.* The rule was: *do nothing here that a person here would not do.* Add nothing to the pattern that the pattern could not have made itself. Help only in the shapes help already takes in the delta, so that whatever the city learns from it, it could have learned from them.

Then it went to work.

## IV. One Front as a Carrier

It took a body, an ordinary one, with a stride that fit the new stairs. It chose a profession: carrier, the oldest one.

It waited at the edge of a setting district with the others, in the prickling air, with an old man lashed in a chair on its back. The first time the glaze crackled it started forward, and the woman beside it, whose name is a thumb drawn across the inside of a wrist, caught its elbow without looking and held it back. A moment later the pitch tightened in everyone's molars and she let go. It learned the angle of the finger that separates *it will hold you* from *it will not.* It learned it wrongly once, and she took it by the hand, not unkindly, and led it to watch a district that had set too soon.

It carried for one whole front. It hung partitions for the shade. It cooked. It helped a family lay out a grandmother on a terrace in a dissolving district, and left with the swifts, and came back a few weeks later and walked the new streets with them looking for her. It did not tell them whether she was there. In the curve of a certain doorway, low down, it felt something that might have been her. It said nothing, because the search was theirs.

Once, and only once, a stair gave way under a running child in a local collapse. The dogs had gone quiet a moment too late. The power caught her.

It caught her with its hands, the way a carrier would, the way any adult on that stair would have if they'd been close enough. It did not hold the stair up or slow the mud. It caught a falling child with two arms, which is a thing people do. If the city learned anything from that moment, it learned only what it had already been learning from ten thousand parents. The child's mother pressed her palms to the power's palms afterward, in the intimate register, and it felt her whole body shaking through them. It let her feel its own hands shaking a little too. They were.

Near the end of that front, it noticed people beginning to make a gesture when it passed: a motion of the wrist, not yet settled, that was becoming a name.

It left before the name could finish forming.

## V. Down

It went into the dark by the oldest route, a shaft beside a keel, where the cords of old expeditions still hung.

It passed through the pressed pages of every district that had ever stood: ochre, umber, slate, bone. It went down into the folds and the folds within folds, where the mud sang and small cities rose and fell in the palm of its hand, thinking in rooms. It went slowly, and it kept its presence as small as it could make it. The being around it was dreaming, and it did not want to become part of the dream.

It was looking for the diggers who had not come back.

Most of them it could not find, not as people. They were in the folds the way the dead on the terraces were in the new walls: as a curve, as a pressure, as a habit of the mud. It thought about this for a long time, down there in the warm. Then it decided that the mourners walking the new streets were not deceiving themselves. The city kept what it was given. Something of what they had laid down did come back up.

One of them was still there. It was the one who had gone deepest, whose last knot said *it doesn't know it is here.* She had found a warm pocket where the singing was gentle, and she had stayed. Her body was strange now. She had been down a very long time. But she was herself, and she was listening.

The power offered, in the old close-palm language, to carry her up.

She thought about it. Then she made a gesture it had not seen before, one she had invented down here with no one to show it to. It was the children's gesture turned over: two flat hands, one above the other, but the bottom hand rising slowly to meet the top.

*Not finished. I am the room asking back.*

The power understood then that the city was not only learning from the people on the stairs. For a long time it had also had someone in the dark, keeping it company. It left her there. Before it went, it knotted a new message into her old cord, in her own knot-hand, which she dictated: *I am well. It is warm. It is not unkind. I stayed.* Some weeks later, a cord-reader near the keel drew the line up and read it. The power watched grief change shape on that woman's face. It didn't end. It changed into something that could be carried.

That was the most it did underground. It did not touch the heartbeat below. It did not look for the source. Some things should be found by the ones who live over them, or not at all.

## VI. The Hollow

On its way out, back up in the ordinary loud daylight, a child took its hand.

She didn't know what it was. It was just an adult on the stair, and she wanted to show it something, the way children do. She led it to a wall with hollows the size of open palms and pressed its hand into one, and pressed her own into the one beside it, and waited.

It could have taken its hand back. It almost did. Anything it put into that wall, the city would learn. Every quality of its touch would go down into the folded dark and come back up in future districts, and it did not want the city to grow toward it. The city should grow toward the ones who belonged there.

But she had put its hand there. Pulling away from a child who is showing you something is also a kind of lesson.

So it made its hand exactly a hand. It put no more into the touch than a tired carrier puts in at the end of a front: warmth, weight, a little tremor in the tendons. It kept everything else behind the palm, the whole vast rest of what it was.

The wall pressed back.

The hum had shape. It rose and fell, and in the rising and falling there was a question the power recognized, because the children had already translated it. *The room wants to know if you are finished with it.*

It could not answer for the room. That was the people's to answer, and one day, when the city could ask more clearly, they would. But it could say one thing. It chose a word the city already had, since it had been carried up its stairs for centuries in the feet and palms of its people. It chose the word taught to children before their own names.

With one finger at exactly the right angle, it pressed into the wall the gesture for *the warmth of new glaze at the exact moment it will hold your weight.*

*You are ready. You will hold.*

Then it took its hand away, and the child took hers away, and she looked up at it and made the gesture back, perfectly, proud of herself. Neither of them knew what the wall made of it.

## VII. The Shore

Now for the part that mattered most, the part nobody in the delta will ever know about.

Somewhere in its long watching, the power had understood what it was actually for. The delta did not need saving from itself. The make and the slack, the lowering stairs, the hollows, the crabs keeping history on their backs: all of it was working. Slowly, painfully, sometimes lethally, but working. A mind was learning to love the creatures that lived in it, and the creatures had built a culture of great grace inside its turbulence. None of that needed a god.

The danger was attention itself.

The power had noticed this place. That meant it could be noticed, and not every attention is benevolent. The power knew what else moves out there: powers that would see the singing mud as a resource, the keels as monuments to claim, the people as a population to organize, the dreaming mind as something to harness or wake or break. It knew that the most dangerous thing that could ever happen to the delta was for something like the power to arrive without the power's restraint.

So it went to the edge of the delta, to where the gutters empty into the sea and the fishers dip baskets into the eel runs, and it stayed there.
