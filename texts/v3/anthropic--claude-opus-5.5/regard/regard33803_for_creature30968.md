# The Listener at Hallow Deep

## Arrival

It did not come down in the cage.

There is no good way to say how it came, because it had no body to bring. It was simply there one day, the way warmth is there, or the way a man realises he has been hearing the crickets all along. None of the crew on shift noticed anything. A walker on the green paused with his pole in the silt and did not know why he had paused. Then he went on.

The first thing it noticed was the noise, as everyone does.

It did not hear the noise as a pressure, though. It heard every voice in it separately and all at once: each of the six impellers and the slight wobble in the fourth, which the engineers had not yet found; the rattle of every bracket in its socket; the forty-foot cascade and the hundred seeps and the three rivers, each of which had a different tone because each had come through different stone. It heard twelve million bats, each chittering in its own voice. It heard the ticking of the swiftlets and the trill of the crickets, the frogs in the warm seeps whose calls no man had ever made out, and the cockroaches moving over the guano with a sound like breath.

Under all of it, far out in the dark water where the lamps had never reached, it heard something breathing twice a minute.

It listened for a long time. A power like that has no need to hurry, and the place had been waiting a long time to be heard properly. It listened to the Deep the way the walkers read trails, learning which sounds were old and which were new, and which were sharp at the edges.

## What It Could Have Done

It could have done almost anything. It considered most of it.

**It could have stopped the pumps.** The water would have come home and the Tenant would have had its sea back. But the mine above would have flooded with men still in it, and the town at the pithead lived on the coal, and the men lived on the pumps. Their children ate because the impellers turned. The pumps were not a cruelty. They were how a valley of families kept itself alive, and the men who tended them did so with a patience equal to the water's. The power would not take that from them.

**It could have held the water back forever.** It could have made the pumps unnecessary and the mine safe for a thousand years. But then the sea would never come home, and the thing that walked its edge would walk a shrinking shore until there was no shore, and die in a room below the floor. That was a theft too, of a different kind.

**It could have given the Tenant eyes, or a voice, or a mind shaped like a man's,** so that it might understand what was happening to it. The power turned this over carefully and set it down. Nothing born in that water has eyes. To give it sight would be to give it the dark as a lack, where before the dark had simply been the world. To give it speech would be to make it the only speaking thing of its kind, which is a loneliness no one should be handed as a gift.

**It could have made the Deep a garden.** It could have hung it with light, set ferns on every rock, stilled the stink of ammonia and closed the doors in the green. But the Deep was not unfinished. Every square yard of it was occupied. The dung fed the roaches, the roaches fed the centipedes, the drift fed the fish, and the fish fed the eels. Out in the dark the Tenant combed all of it back up again. It was the most crowded, most thoroughly lived-in place the power had found in a long while. There was nothing to add.

**It could have told the men everything:** what the Tenant was, how old it was, what the pillars had once been. It decided against that as well. The men had built their own understanding over thirty years with poles and rags and the sign, and it was a good understanding, mostly right, and theirs. Being handed the answer would have made it worth less.

So the power came to the conclusion that wise things often come to in places like this. Almost everything here was already doing what it ought to do. The pumps were right to turn, and the water was right to be patient. The men were right to hold it back, and the Tenant was right to wait. None of these was wrong, even though they could not all win.

What was wrong was small. It was in the corners, and so the power went to the corners.

## The Ears

The first small wrong was the deafness.

The men had given their hearing to the Deep a little at a time, the way the pumps took the water. Nobody had asked it of them. It was simply the cost of the work, and they paid it, and they spoke with their hands and did not complain. On the surface they shouted in the pub and did not know they were shouting. Their wives had learned to stand in front of them to speak. Some of them had not properly heard a grandchild's voice.

This, at least, the power could mend without taking anything from anyone.

It did it slowly, so no one would think to call it anything but luck. When a man left the Deep for good, through retirement or injury or the long slow thinning of the crews as the seam gave out, the ringing in his ears began to ease. It took about a year. Doctors at the county hospital called it spontaneous recovery and wrote a short paper about the unusual number of cases in one valley. The men themselves said nothing about it. One by one they found they could hear rain on the roof at night, and the kettle beginning, and a child asking a question from the next room.

The power left one thing untouched. If a man, years later, stood in the gorse and heard the old hum in his knees for a moment, that was his, and the power let him keep it. Some sounds a person has earned.

## Emrys Hale

The second wrong was a man with no shrine.

Tom Wystan had a biscuit tin and a photograph and a painted cross. Emrys Hale had nothing. He went through in the twelfth year, on the green, on ground so new that no walkway reached it. His family moved away within the year, and his door was never capped because nobody could find it again. The silt had closed over it as smoothly as water.

The power found it. It was far out, beyond the last cairn, a faint dip in ground that had since dried and cracked. Beside the dip was a bowl, very old now and half-filled with drifted guano. A fern had not grown on its rim, because there was no lamp there. It was only a hollow in the dark, the size of something that had lain against a door for a long time.

The power did not build a shrine, because that was not its place. It waited until the right man walked near.

## Aled Pryce

He was old by then. He had worked the Deep since the second year, and the company had kept him on because he knew the green better than any chart. He was the one who had made the sign at the water's edge during the second stoppage and seen the fringe go still.

He had been on the walk the day Emrys went through. He had been the one holding the other end of the rope, and the rope had not been enough. He had never told anyone that. He had told it only to himself, every day for twenty years.

The power could not take that away, and would not have if it could; it belonged to Aled. What it did was smaller. On a shift in the last year of the mine, Aled's lantern guttered out far along the old ground, and he stood in the dark for a moment, and the thought came to him the way thoughts come in the dark, as if it had always been there. *You know where it was. You've always known.*

He walked there without a light. He did not need one. He found the dip and the bowl beside it, and the next day he brought down a second biscuit tin. It had no photograph in it, because there was no photograph. He put in Emrys's name, scratched on a strip of tin with a nail since paper would not last, and a red rag of the kind they mark safe ground with. He knew it was not safe ground. It seemed right anyway.

He made the sign over it, hand flat, pressing down. He had never been sure what the sign meant when it was made that slowly. He thought that day that it might mean *rest*.

## What the Power Tried to Tell the Tenant

The third wrong, the power thought at first, was the Tenant's uncertainty.

Thirty-one years is nothing to a thing that grows collars, but it is a long time to watch a sea go down. The power went out into the dark water, to where the breathing was, and found it lying in the deep middle on the floor of its remaining sea. It was vaster than the men had guessed. Its fringe was combing slowly through silt, and the dark red frill pulsed twice a minute. Its back was a ridge of fused white shelves so old that the oldest were older than the coal.

The power wanted to tell it: *They are going. The seam is thinning. In a few years the cables will come up the shaft and the gate will be left open, and your sea will come home. You do not have to wait much longer.*

It began to tell it, in the only way such a thing can be told anything, as a pressure or a warmth or a change in the water. Then it stopped. The Tenant already knew.

It had never been uncertain. The men had misread that part. It came to the edge every week, not in fear or hope, but for the reason the water rises, because rising is what the water does. It did not need telling that the sea would come back, any more than the sea needed telling. It was the patience of the place with a body around it. The pumps had never been an enemy to it. They were weather, and weather passes.

The power was humbled, which is something that happens to wise things more often than people suppose. It had come to comfort something that did not need comfort. The only message worth sending had already been sent, twice, by a pumpman with his hand held out flat over the water. *Easy. Slowly. Hold.* The Tenant had heard it, and that was why the fringe went still. It had not been fear. It had been recognition. Someone up in the loud dry air had spoken in the one language they shared, which was the language of the level: a hand pressing down, the way the water pressed down. It was the closest thing to its own name the Tenant had ever heard.

So the power told it nothing, and listened a while longer, and went back toward the lamps.

## The Slab

There was one more thing it wanted to understand before it did anything else. It wanted to know why the trails came to Tom Wystan's slab and stopped, and why the candle stub had been moved.

It could have known. It could have gone down through the concrete into the room below, where the water was still warm and the level fell slowest, and seen what lay there. It could have seen whether stone had begun to grow on a man's boots and a man's hands, and whether the Tenant came to lie beside the slab out of hunger, or curiosity, or something the men would have had another word for.

It did not look.

Some things in the dark are owed their dark. Whatever the Tenant did beside that door was between it and the man below, and a power that goes everywhere must learn where not to go. Whatever the truth was, the Tenant had come back to the slab again and again, when it had no need to, and had touched the candle and set it down. That was enough to know. The rest was theirs.
