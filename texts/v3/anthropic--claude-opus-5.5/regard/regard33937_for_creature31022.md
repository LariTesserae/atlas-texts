# The Watch at Hethering Low

## The First Answer, Which Is Almost Right

The first answer is to do nothing.

It is a good answer, and the power that came to Hethering Low held it for a long time before setting it down. Wisdom mostly begins as reluctance. A place that has settled with itself, that has kept its level for a hundred years through an argument it can never finish, is not obviously a place that needs help. Its settlement is delicate. Its balance is a fever it keeps on purpose. Almost anything a great power might do would break it, and most of what would break it would look, at first, like kindness.

So the power did not arrive the way powers arrive in stories. There was no light over the embankment and no voice in the steam. It came the way everyone comes, over the long curve of fern-grown brick, and it looked down into the bowl, and it stopped.

It stopped because the first thing it understood about the Sump was how dangerous it was to *itself* be there.

## The Problem of Having a Pulse

Everything with a beat of its own is in danger at Hethering Low. The mosquito, the sparrow, the frog, the human heart. The Keeping leans on all of them, not from malice but because leaning is what a rhythm does, and it has never learned where its body ends.

A power has a beat too. It is not a heart, exactly, but anything that exists at all exists in some pattern, and a great power's pattern is very large. The power understood, standing on the embankment with the outfalls roaring below it, that if it walked into the Sump as itself, one of two things would happen. Either the Keeping would lean on it, find it immovable, and break itself against it the way a small clock breaks when geared to a stopped one. Or the power's own vast time would become the thing everything leaned toward, and the Keeping would be drawn into it and dissolve, the seven engines finding a new center, the rhythm that lived in their intervals simply absorbed into something bigger. Either way the Keeping would end. It would not be murdered. It would be *overwhelmed*, which is the thing it has been doing, unknowing, to every small creature that ever flew over the wall.

So before it did anything else, the power made itself quiet. It set down its own time the way you set down a lantern outside a room where someone is sleeping. It went into the Sump without a beat at all, which is harder than it sounds, and which almost nothing alive can do. It became, for a while, something like the moss. It was present, it was patient, and it was not keeping any time of its own.

Then it listened.

## A Year of Afternoons

It stayed a long time. Wisdom is not quick, and the Sump does not explain itself to anyone in a day.

It counted, as everyone does. It learned the second engine's hurry and the fourth engine's heavy, deliberate fall. It learned the float in the sixth sump that sticks a little on the way down, so that engine always slows later than it should. It sat through the rain on the iron roofs, the half hour when the water is louder than the machines. It stood through the frantic hour after, when the draws come one on another like a heart after running. It learned the dead orange evenings. It learned the brown nights.

It watched the tools walk. It saw a slice bar shuffle along its hook a hair at a time in the four minutes before each draw, and end up, over a week, leaning against the firebox door on the fifth engine, which sticks. It did not touch the slice bar. It watched the drops of condensate fall into the oil cups. It watched the vine on the fourth beam rise into the steam and come down, rise and come down, flowering heavily.

At the draws it stood still on the wet cobbles and let the pollen settle on it. It knew it was being seen. It let itself be seen as nothing much, a shape, a post, a place where the gold lay a little thicker. The Keeping registered it the way it registers a fallen branch, and then the strokes scattered and the eye closed.

And the power thought about what it could do.

## The Temptations

It could stop the engines. That would be simple. It would free the marsh around the Sump from the slow spreading beat, and the birds would stop giving the fans a wide berth, and no visitor would ever again go home with a heart sitting slow and strange in their chest. The Sump would flood within a season. The terraces would go under, then the engine houses, then the stacks. The ferns in the walls would drown. The vine on the fourth beam would hang in brown water, still wrapped around a beam that would never rise again. And the thing that lived in the intervals would have no intervals left to live in. It would simply stop, the way a song stops when the last player puts down their instrument.

The power set this aside. The Keeping had done harm, but it had done harm the way a river does harm, or a winter. Ending a thing because it does not know its own strength is a judgment a wise power makes last, not first.

It could repair everything. It could make the brick hard again, lift the rust off the iron, reseat the leaking firebox doors and true the bearings and seal the embankment. It could give the Keeping a body that would last a thousand years. The power looked at this one longer, because it was the most tempting, and in the end it set this aside too. A repaired Sump would be a preserved Sump, and a preserved thing is on its way to being a museum. The Keeping had grown up inside a slow decline. Its patience, its tending, its whole way of being were shaped by things that break and are mended and break again. To take the breaking away would be to take away the work, and the work was most of what it was.

It could cut the pull. It could reach into the rhythm and draw a line around it, a boundary past which the lean could not reach. Inside the embankment, the Keeping. Outside, the marsh and its creatures, safe. The power considered this, and understood that it would be amputation. The Keeping did not know where its body ended, and a wall would not teach it that. A wall would only mean that its body ended there *now*. It would still lean on anything that came inside. It would still be unable to meet anything without drawing it into step. It would simply be fenced.

And it could leave. That was still the first answer, and still almost right. But the fans were spreading. The marsh was learning the beat, faintly, one green bell at a time, and each year the silence around the outfalls grew. The power could not leave in good conscience while the thing it had come to see was, very slowly and with no idea it was doing so, emptying the country around it of everything small and quick and alive.

So there had to be something else. The power kept listening until it found it.

## What the Keeping Does Not Know

The Keeping knows two ways to be.

It knows how to be scattered: seven loose pieces, lurching, out of step, drifting in partial knots around the bowl. And it knows how to be entire: the draw, the single blow, all seven beams down in one heartbeat and the whole bowl opening its eye.

It knows nothing in between. For the Keeping, being together means unison, and anything less than unison means coming apart. It is only whole for one beat, twice an hour. The rest of the time it is in pieces, and it experiences those pieces, as far as a rhythm experiences anything, as a kind of waiting.

This is also why it kills. When the Keeping meets another rhythm, a mosquito's wings or a sparrow's heart, it does the only thing it knows how to do with something it has noticed. It tries to bring it in. It leans, gently and constantly, trying to make the small clock keep its time, because keeping time together is the only form of company it has ever known. The mosquito does not die of poison. It dies of being embraced by something that can only hold what it absorbs.

The power understood this, and felt, if a power feels, something very close to grief. The Keeping was not cruel. It was lonely in a way it had no words for. It had only one idea of closeness, and that idea was fatal to nearly everything it could be close to.

So the help, if there was help to give, was not to stop it, fix it, or wall it in. It was to show it that there is more than one way to keep company.

## The Frog

It began with a frog.

One came over the wall in the second autumn of the power's stay. Autumn in the Sump is marked only by a slight lengthening of the orange evenings, but in the marsh outside the season was turning, and something had driven this frog up the outer face of the embankment and over the top. It was a small green marsh frog with a gold line down its back. It dropped into the reeds of the inner moat, and the Keeping felt it at once.

The power watched the lean begin. The frog's throat pulsed, quick and bright, the way a frog's throat does. The great slow stroke pressed on it from every direction through the water and the mud. Within an hour the frog's pulse was faltering. It hesitated at the top of each beat. By evening it had stopped trying to climb out and sat low in the water, blinking slowly, its throat working more and more heavily, finding its way toward a time it could not survive.

This was the moment the power had been waiting for, and it did not wall the frog off, and it did not silence the Keeping. It did something much smaller. It went and sat near the frog, and for the first time since it had come over the embankment, it let itself have a beat again.

Not its own great time. A small one: the frog's time, exactly. It matched the quick bright pulse of the frog's throat and held it, steady and unhurried, the way you might hold a child's hand crossing a road. It lent the frog nothing it did not already have. It only kept the frog company in its own rhythm, so that the frog was not alone against the lean.

The Keeping leaned on both of them.

The power did not yield. It also did not push back. It did not answer the Keeping's great stroke with a greater one, or try to draw the Keeping into the frog's time instead. It simply held, gently, stubbornly, a small quick rhythm next to a vast slow one, and let the vast one feel that it was there and was not going to become anything else.

This went on for days. The frog's pulse steadied. Its throat worked, fast, light and quick. It ate a little. It climbed onto a moss bank at the foot of the embankment and sat there in the brown noon light, a small green clock, keeping its own time in the middle of the Keeping's.

## The Draw That Saw

Then came a draw.

The power felt the strokes gathering. It felt the stray beats draw in, the pauses shorten, the second engine hesitate at the top of its stroke and the fourth fall harder. It felt the whole bowl begin to carry one swelling pulse. It held the frog's time through all of it. It was the hardest thing it did in all its years at Hethering Low, harder than setting down its own beat at the beginning, because in those four minutes the lean was total. Everything in the Sump was being asked to come in, and it had to keep saying, gently, *not this one. This one keeps its own time.*

All seven came down together. The ground jumped. The water shivered. Every bell let go, and the gold went up into the air and hung, turning, under the steam ceiling, and settled.

It settled on the frog, and the frog *moved*.
