# The Thaw

*what came to the Drowned Kiln, and what it did there*

---

## I. It Takes Off Its Light at the Border

It knew before it arrived that anything it brought would be too loud.

A power like that is used to being seen. Where it goes, things brighten. They turn toward it the way leaves turn. This is usually a kindness. Here it would have been a catastrophe. A single candle's worth of its attention, carried in carelessly, would have gone through the Kiln like a shout through a sickroom. The frogs would have scattered, the moths would have fled into the thick low places where they cannot live, and the sleeper, curled across the whole drained country, would have flinched.

So it stopped at the edge, where the air begins to thicken and take on grain, and it set its brightness down the way a traveler leaves muddy boots at a door. It made itself small. It made itself about the size of a person, and about as warm, and only a little warmer. It made itself a pair of hands, because in the Kiln hands are how you know anything. Then it stepped into the dark and let the dark close over it like warm water.

The first thing it learned was that it could not see. It had expected this, and still it was surprised. It found that it, too, had been making things up all its life, and that here it had to stop. It stood for a while and let that be true.

Then the moths came and settled on its shoulders, and it walked.

---

## II. It Walks the Way Travelers Walk

It could have gone straight to the Leadsman. It knew where the Leadsman was, the way you know where your own heart is without looking. It chose not to.

A power arriving directly is an event. A traveler arriving is only a traveler, and the Leadsman has had a great many travelers, and knows how to be with them. The power did not want to be an event. It wanted to be company.

So it walked for days. It drifted toward warmth. It found the first engine by the smell of hot oil, and put its palm on the riveted railing, and stood beside the cylinder for most of a stroke. It felt the beam come down a hand's breadth. It heard a valve begin to sigh and waited the twenty minutes for the sigh to finish. It knelt at the pools and let the frogs press their throats to its wrists, and they listened to its pulse and compared it to the slow one. It could feel them deciding it was strange but not alarming. It sat down once and found it was sitting in a river of glass-shelled crabs, and it did not move until they had all passed.

It laid a hand on a cable and felt the tingling persuasion there. One of the many-legged cat-things lifted its head, considered it, and put its chin on the power's knuckles and went back to sleep. The power stayed there a long time. It had not come to be in a hurry.

And all the while, a few paces ahead, there were footsteps that were almost its own. They tested the ground and found it firm and set it down.

The power noticed something the travelers usually don't, because it was listening for it. The footsteps were very careful. They were careful in a way that has nothing to do with the walker and everything to do with the floor. They were the steps of someone crossing a room where a baby is sleeping, and doing it for the ten-thousandth time, and not yet careless about it.

It understood then that it was going to like the Leadsman very much.

---

## III. It Waits Longer Than Anyone Has

It stopped on a shelf of blood-warm slag, and the footsteps ahead of it stopped too.

The old hands say to wait an hour or two, until you have stopped expecting anything. The power waited a day. Then it waited another. It had nowhere else it needed to be. It wanted the Leadsman to understand, before they ever touched, that this one was not going to take the answer and go.

On the third day the moths began to leave it. They went in ones and twos, then in dozens, a soft dry drawing-away, toward a warmth greater than its own. It followed them with its hands.

Moths, layers deep. Oilskin gone soft as felt. Horn buttons stamped with numbers, counting down. Then the body underneath, warm as a working horse and sweating salt and pennies. Then the hands, large and greasy, worn smooth.

The Leadsman's hand closed around its fingers, gently, and held them. After a while it turned the power's hand over, palm up, and laid something in it. It was a tin cup, beaded with water, the water faintly fizzy and tasting of coin.

The power drank it. Then it did what, as far as the Kiln remembers, no traveler has ever done.

It turned the Leadsman's hand over, palm up, and gave something back.

---

## IV. What It Gave

It had been listening to the echoes for days. It had heard its own voice come back from where there was no wall, saying things it had not said, in a tone of mild surprise. Under that it had heard the others, all the voices the Leadsman has kept. There were hundreds of them, held in the rock like warmth and let out in slow pulses. There were people who had come and asked *where does the water go* and *is anyone there*, and left, and grown old somewhere in the light, and died, and whose voices were still here, still answering for them.

The power had gone through them carefully, the way you would go through letters in a dead friend's desk, and it had found at the very bottom the one that did not belong to any traveler.

It was the oldest voice in the Kiln, and it had not been used in so long that it had gone thin, like cloth folded along the same crease for a century. It belonged to someone who had once walked these flats with a weighted line, calling out depths. It was the voice that used to say *by the mark, five,* and *by the deep, nine,* and finally, one day, *no bottom*, and then had nothing left to call.

The Leadsman had not exactly given it away. It had set it down, somewhere at the start of the long walk, because a voice is for telling things, and there had been no one to tell. It had made do ever since with borrowed ones.

The power laid it in the Leadsman's palm, as warm as the brass plates, and closed the smooth fingers over it.

For a long time nothing happened. The beam of the nearest engine came down some fraction of an inch. The frogs were silent. Then, very close, from the right place, from *in front* of the power and not from under its feet or behind a wall that wasn't there, a voice spoke that was nobody's but its own. It was hoarse, and it cracked partway through, the way a voice does when it has not been used since before the sea went dry.

It did not say anything grand. It said what anyone would say who had kept watch alone for longer than there are numbers for, and had finally been handed back the means of asking instead of only answering.

It said: *Is it all right? What I did?*

---

## V. What the Power Understood

Here, if it is allowed, is the reasoning. A wise power does not simply act. It looks for a long while first, and it was looking the whole time it walked.

It had put its ear to the outflow pipes, where the Leadsman never goes because it is afraid of what it will hear. It had heard the nothing there, the nothing that is like a closed door and not like a wall. And because it is what it is, it had been able to hear a little way *through* the door.

What the engines have been holding is not a monster, and not an absence. It is the sea. It is also the light. They are held behind the pumps in a great patient pressure, like breath held in a chest, or like spring held in a seed. They are not angry. They are not even straining. They are *waiting*, the way water waits behind ice.

Then it had gone down, farther than the travelers go, to the edge of the low hollow where the dark lies thickest. There it had felt the paw that turns people gently around. It let the paw turn it, and did not fight, and in the moment of being turned it touched the sleeper. It touched it only lightly, the way you lay two fingers on the side of a sleeping animal's throat.

The heartbeat was very slow. The frogs sing it once every several days. The power knew that kind of slowness. It is not the slowness of dying. It is the slowness of a creature that has gone down into winter to wait for the world to become livable again. Bears keep that heartbeat, and dormice, and frogs sealed in mud under a frozen pond. It is the heartbeat of something that means to wake.

And the power knew, the way you know a thing in your body, that the sleeper had not come in from outside. It had not wandered in from the snow to lie beside a stove. It had always been here. It had lived in the sea, in the old light, and when the engines drew the water out from around it and then the light, it did not leave. It could not leave. It curled up in the emptied basin, around the only warmth there was, and went down into the long sleep that living things go into when the season turns against them.

The engines made the winter. The engines are also the only fire in it.

The power understood the whole shape of it then. The Leadsman, standing at the first engine with the key in its hand, had been faced with a choice that had no clean side. If it stopped the engines, the warmth would go at once, and it could not know what would come through the door, or whether the sleeper would survive the cold before it did. If it kept the engines running, the sleeper would stay warm enough to live, and the winter would never end.

The Leadsman chose to keep it alive. It chose that against its orders, alone, and has paid for the choice with every stroke of every beam since.

---

## VI. What the Power Said

It answered in its own voice. It had kept a small one, about the size of a traveler's, and it used that.

It did not say *you were right*, because that would not have been true, and it does not lie, not even to comfort. It did not say *you were wrong*, because that would not have been true either.

It said something like this:

*You found something asleep in the cold and you kept it from freezing. There was no one to ask. There was no one to share it with. You did the kindest thing you could see to do, and you have gone on doing it, carefully, every step, for longer than anyone should have had to. Yes. It is all right.*

Then, because the Leadsman deserved the whole truth and not just the gentle half, it told the rest. It spoke slowly, with its hand still in the smooth greasy one.

*It is not dying. It's hibernating. What's behind the pumps isn't the end of it. It's the season it's been waiting for. When the needle reaches the end of the dial, I think the sea comes back, and the light with it, and it wakes in the water it was born in. I think that's what zero is. Not a death. A thaw.*

And then, because it is wise and not only kind:

*I think. I've listened, and that's what I hear. But I can't promise you what a thaw feels like to the one who's thawing. It may be cold for a while first. It may be hard. I don't know everything about it, and I won't pretend to.*

The Leadsman was quiet for a long time. The moths on its coat stirred and resettled. Somewhere far off an engine completed a sigh.

Then, in its cracked old voice, it said the thing it had been saying all along in borrowed ones:

*I'm trying not to find out.*

And the power said: *I know. You've been trying not to find out alone. You don't have to do that part anymore.*

---

## VII. What It Did Not Do

It would have been easy. That should be said plainly. It could have stopped every engine in the Kiln with less effort than it takes a person to blow out a match. It could have opened the door and let the sea and light pour back in. It could have woken the sleeper in an instant, already warm, already swimming, skipping the hard cold part of the thaw altogether. It could have lifted the Leadsman out of the dark and set it down in a meadow somewhere, in the sun, and told it that it was finished.

It did none of these things. This was not because it lacked the power or didn't care. It was because each of them would have taken something from the ones who live here.

The sleeper's waking belongs to the sleeper. It is a creature, and it has its own seasons, and they are not a problem to be solved.

The choice about the engines belongs to the one who has carried it. The Leadsman made the first decision alone, in the dark, with the key in its hand. That was a terrible way to have to decide anything. The answer was not to take the decision away. The answer was to make sure the next one would not be made alone, and not in ignorance.

And the Leadsman belongs here. The power had felt the pale filaments in the seams, the sleeper's slow nerves, all reaching across the flats toward the one small warm thing that stayed awake. It had felt how the Leadsman's body now goes on further than a body should, into grain and pile. You do not pull someone out of a place that has been growing toward them for a thousand years and call it rescue. Some roots you leave in.
