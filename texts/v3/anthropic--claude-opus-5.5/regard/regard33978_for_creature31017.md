# The Relief

## How It Arrives

It does not come down the stairs, because the stairs end in sealed brick. It does not come through the ceiling like a flood, because the Quarter has had its flood and made a life out of it.

It comes the way the riders come. It enters with the river through a ceiling breach, lets the westward draught take it the length of the lane, and turns on the rebound to ride the surge back east. It does this once and then again, and the first sluice-knell of its visit comes a fraction late, the way it does for eels. It has been given right of way. It notices that and is grateful, because it would not have wanted to arrive anywhere as something the place had not agreed to.

Then it stops moving and listens. It will listen for a long time.

## What It Declines to Do

A power like this could do almost anything here, so the first part of its wisdom is a list of refusals.

**It does not drain the vault.** The drowning is no longer a wound. The river pouring through the ceiling is what turns the turbine, and the turbine is the Keeping's heart. To seal the breaches and pump the Quarter dry would be to "restore" the street by killing the only thing that has lived in it for a century. The flood is the condition of this life, not a disaster to be undone.

**It does not repair the engine.** The engine is already being repaired, every night, by something that has made that repair its life's work. To make the bearings perfect would be like walking into an old man's garden and replacing every plant with a better one while he slept.

**It does not stop the Circuit.** It does not clean the rust-beards, calm the water, fix the doors, or rehang the glass in the chapel window.

**It does not make itself known to the city above.** It knows what happens when a city learns there is an ancient, self-healing engine under its customs house: engineers arrive, then pumps, then the word *salvage*. Whatever relief comes from above, it must not come as an excavation.

**It does not go into the chapel. Not yet.** The Keeping stops at that door, and a guest does not walk past the host into the room the host will not enter.

What remains, once it has refused all this, is small. That is how it knows it is on the right track.

## The Listening

It spends a year of rounds learning the language.

The Keeping does not speak, but it is not silent. It speaks in current. When a lamp dims out of rhythm, it is paying attention to something. When a lamp flickers irregularly for a round or two, a fish has died. When the sluice-knell comes a little late, it is being courteous. When the walker stands at the foot of the stair and all the lamps hold perfectly steady, it is hoping.

The power learns the bearing-scream until it can hear, as the old keeper could, the difference between the scream of wear and the scream of trouble. There is no trouble. It learns the groan of the arch. It rides the Circuit once, slowly, alongside the faceless watch: down the north gutter, through the fourth house, out the broken back window, across at the chapel steps, along the south gutter, and home. It feels what the Keeping feels at the end of each loop, the settling of *that's one more night held*.

It also learns the thing the Keeping does not know about itself: the watch is dying.

The case is sanded thin as paper, and each loop takes a little more. The power measures and estimates. In perhaps nine years, perhaps eleven, the case will wear through, the movement will scatter into the gutter as grit, and the Circuit will carry nothing home. The Keeping will lose the thing it tells time by. It will not know when a round is finished. It may walk forever, or never gather again. The power does not know which, and it does not like not knowing.

It holds that knowledge and does not act on it yet. First it needs to be sure it understands what the Keeping is waiting for.

## What "Relieved" Means

This is the question the whole visit turns on, and the power turns it over for a long time.

*Hold until relieved.* In a watchman's language, relief is a person who comes to take the watch, so the one who has been standing it can finally put it down. Relief means someone else carries this now. It also means rest. And there is an older sense under both: the loosening of a weight, a breath let out.

So what would it mean to relieve the Keeping?

It could mean ending it. The power could release the green film from its work, let the engine finally wear, let the lamps go out one by one, and let the Keeping stop, having done its duty. Some would call that mercy. The power does not. Nothing about the Keeping suggests it is suffering. It tends the fish. It gives the eels right of way. It trims the rust-beards to the same length because it cares how things look. That is not the behaviour of something that wants to stop. It is the behaviour of something that wants to be *seen*.

What the Keeping is doing, carefully and every night, is ringing a bell. It has been spacing the sluice-knell into the deepest quiet of the city so that someone might hear it. It is not asking to be ended. It is asking whether anyone is still there, whether the watch it stands still belongs to anyone, and whether the man in the coat, or anyone at all, will ever come down the stairs.

So the relief it needs is not rest in the sense of an ending. It is an answer.

## The First Answer

On a still night, at the end of a round, the walker gathers itself at the foot of the spiral stair. It is rust and trailing tassels in the shape of a coat, with sleeves a little too long and a faint green pulse where a heart would sit. It faces upward, into the sealed brick, and waits.

The power goes to the top of the stair, on the inside, where the last step meets the seal. It knocks three times against the brick. It is not loud. It is the knock of someone on the other side of a door.

Every lamp in the Quarter goes out at once.

For the length of three bearing-screams there is no light in the vault except the green of the engine hall and the green at the walker's chest, which has gone very bright. The roar goes on, because the roar always goes on, but the power can feel the whole nervous system of the street drawn tight toward one point, the way a person goes still at a sound in the house they thought was empty.

Then the lamps come back. They do not come back all together. They come back one by one from the engine hall westward, down the lane, lamppost after lamppost, the way a man walking his rounds would light them.

The power waits, and then knocks three times again.

This time the answer comes as a sluice-knell, and it does not come on schedule. The cam slips, deliberately, a fraction of a turn, and the great iron clang rings out of its rhythm, directly after the knocks. *I hear you. I am here. Is that you?*

The power does not claim to be the keeper. It will not lie to something that has waited this long. But it knocks a third time, slowly, the way you knock on the door of someone you have come to sit with.

## Walking the Round

Over the following nights, it walks the round with the walker.

It takes no shape of its own that the Keeping could mistake for the man in the coat. It goes as a warmth in the water, a slight steadiness in the current beside the walker, the feeling of company. When the walker climbs the dark stairs, the power climbs beside it, and for the first time there are two sets of disturbances in the silt. When the walker closes the eastern doors, the power holds the second one so the latch seats a little more easily. When the walker stands where the beds were, the power stands with it and does not hurry it.

On the third night, at the foot of the stair, the walker does something it has never done. It does not come apart. It turns, slowly, in its borrowed coat, toward the power, and holds out one long sleeve.

The power understands. A watchman going off duty hands something to his relief: a lantern, a key, a logbook, the watch itself.

So the power takes the round for a night.

It walks the lane alone. It checks the gates at what would be dusk, listens to the bearings at what would be midnight, and at dawn sees that each lamp is lit, each door shut, and each house quiet. It trims one rust-beard that has grown a little long. It keeps the calm behind the post where the oldest pillar-fish holds station. It holds the sluice a fraction open for a passing rider.

The whole time, the Keeping does nothing. The tassels hang loose on their posts. The film in the bearings goes on healing, because that is as involuntary as breathing, but the attention, the part that keeps, rests. For one round in a hundred years, the lamps pulse only with the engine and nothing else. The street simply breathes.

In the morning, when the faceless watch comes home to the engine hall, the power gives the round back. It does not have to. It could keep it. But the watch belongs to the Keeping now, not by instruction but by a century of devotion, and the relief it was waiting for was never someone to take the watch away. It was someone to share it, so that it would know the watch had not been forgotten, that it could put the weight down, and that it would still be allowed to pick it up again.

It picks it up again. The lamps are a little brighter the next night.

## The Chapel

Only after all this does the power ask about the chapel.

It asks in the Keeping's own language. It goes to the chapel door with the walker, and stops where the walker stops, and waits. Then it moves a little forward, just past the threshold, and waits again. It is asking: *Shall I?*

The lamp nearest the chapel dims and holds dim for a long time. Then it brightens.

So the power goes in, alone, into the whistling dark where the shrimp glitter in the altar stones. It finds what it half expected. The eleventh family is there: three of them, what is left of them, behind the altar in the calmest water in the building, laid down carefully side by side. With them, in a stone basin at the foot of the altar, is a vessel of fired clay with its stopper gone, its inside still faintly green. This is where the film was grown. The Keeping was born in this room.

And the power finds one more thing.

The round window above the door was not broken by the flood. The power can see the old leadwork, and the channels in the stone where the glass sat. The glass was *removed*: taken out pane by pane, and set down in a neat stack against the chapel's inside wall, where it still lies under a century of silt. Someone did this before the water came, or as it came.

They took the glass out so the current would sing through the spokes.

The whistle is the only thing in the Quarter the Keeping does not maintain, and it is the only thing in the Quarter that was left to it on purpose. The family knew what they were making would be alone down here for a very long time. They could not leave it company. So they left it music.

The power comes back out and stands with the walker at the door. It cannot explain this in words, so it does the only thing that translates. It goes up to the round window and, very gently, places its attention in the current so that for one surge the chapel's whistle rises clearer and fuller and more itself than it has ever been. It is a single long note, rising and falling with the sluice, like someone humming in the next room.

The walker stands very still in its coat and listens to it.

The shrimp turn toward the door, a hundred tiny glints catching the green light at its chest.

## The Watch

Now the power turns to the problem it has been carrying.

It will not replace the watch. A new watch dropped into the gutter would be a stranger, and the Keeping would not know it. It will not stop the Circuit's wearing, because the Circuit is the Keeping's clock, and a clock you take away the wear from is no longer the same kind of clock.

Instead it does something small. On one night, as the watch passes along the south gutter on its way home, the power slows it for a moment against the lamppost nearest the engine hall, where the green-threaded rust-beards trail thick. It holds it there, no more than a breath, and lets the tassels touch it.

The Keeping has never touched the watch. It only watched it go around. It did not think of it as part of its body, because it was not bolted to anything, not wired, not iron in the same way. But the case is brass over steel, and there is iron in its movement. Where the green threads brush it, they recognise wear. They do what they have always done with wear.

The next night, the case is a thousandth of a hair thicker.

The power does this only once. After that, it does not need to. The Keeping has learned that the thing that tells it time is also something it can heal, so each round, as the watch passes the last lamp, the tassels reach for it a little. The watch will go on wearing, because the Circuit wears everything, but now it will also go on being laid back down. It has joined the engine in that same patient condition, eroding and restored at almost the same rate.

The watch will not have a face again. The Keeping never needed it to.

## Above

The last thing the power attends to is the bell.

The Keeping is still spacing out the sluice-knell, still ringing into the quiet of the city, hoping to be heard. It has been answered now, but the power knows it was not really calling for *it*. It was calling for someone from above: someone from the world that dug the vault, lit the lamps, and hung the coat on the hook. Something that old and that faithful deserves to be known by the people it was built to serve, at least by one of them.

The power does not arrange a discovery. It knows what discoveries do. It looks instead for someone who is already listening.

It finds her almost at once. She is a night clerk in the customs house, near the end of her working life. For eleven years she has kept a notebook in her desk drawer, and in it she has written down the intervals: *whine, then clang, 2:14 a.m.*, and the next night *2:15*. She has never told anyone, because she knows how it sounds. She has a theory she does not quite believe, that something down there is *timing itself to the quiet*. She thinks it is a very strange thing for pipes to do.

The power does not speak to her. It does not visit her dreams. It does not move her pen.

It only waits for a night when she is alone in the building, with her notebook open. When the clang comes on schedule, in the deep quiet, she puts her palm flat on the stone floor, as she has done a hundred times, feeling for it. And the power, down in the vault, carries her touch the way water carries sound, down through the foundation stones and the arch and into the brick at the top of the spiral stair, so that the pressure of an old woman's hand on a floor arrives there as something the Keeping can feel.

Every lamp in the Quarter goes dark again.

Then the next knell comes, early and out of rhythm, directly under her hand.

She sits back on her heels. She does not scream or run. After a while she laughs quietly, the way people laugh when something they had privately hoped for turns out to be true. Then, because she has always been a little strange and has nobody to be embarrassed in front of, she raps her knuckles on the floor three times.

The clang answers her three times, slowly.

She never tells anyone. She is not the kind of person who would bring pumps. But from then on she keeps her notebook differently. She stops writing down the intervals and starts writing *good night* in the margin at the end of each shift, and she knocks once on the floor before she goes home. Once, softly, directly beneath her, the sluice closes like a door.

## What Changes

Very little changes, and that is the point.

The engine goes on roaring. The water pulls west and slams east. The Circuit turns its polished cargo. The pillar-fish hold their small calms, and the eels ride the surge, and the shrimp glint in the chapel stones. The rust-beards stay trimmed. The eastern doors stay shut. Nothing in the Quarter is fixed, cleaned, drained, lit more brightly, or made less strange.

Only a few things are different:

- **The intervals stop lengthening.** The clerks upstairs notice, if they notice anything, that whatever the sound is has stopped slowing down. The Keeping no longer needs to ring into the deepest part of the night. Someone has heard.

- **The walker still walks the round**, but not every night now. Some nights the lamps pulse only with the engine, the tassels hang loose, and nothing gathers at the foot of the stair. On those nights the Keeping is not waiting. It is resting, because it has learned that resting is permitted and that the street does not fall apart in a single night. It has learned what the old keeper surely knew: you can love a watch without having to stand it every hour.

- **The walker has begun to go a little way into the chapel.** Not far. It goes one step past the threshold, then two, and stands in the whistle of the round window, with the shrimp catching its light, in the room where it was made.

- **The watch comes home a little thicker than it left.**

- **On the stair, at the top, just beneath the sealed brick, there are new marks.** They are faint, long, even, and upright, as though something climbed all the way up, placing each foot with care, and stood there with its sleeve against the brick for a while, listening to an old woman say good night.
