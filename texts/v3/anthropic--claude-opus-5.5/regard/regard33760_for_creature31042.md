# The Attention

## The Threshold

It does not come down the stairs.

It could. Nothing about forty iron steps and a lip of black rock is an obstacle to it. But it arrives the way weather arrives at a quarry, gathering in the air above the pit, and then it does what anything with sense does when it approaches a house. It pauses at the door and listens.

It hears the eleven-second breath coming up out of the ground. It hears the drip landing on iron, on stone, on fern, each in its own note. It hears a chisel, then a murmur, then nothing, then the stroke again. And under all of it, far down, it hears something slower than the engines, and something that is almost the engines.

So it stops at the thirty-ninth stair.

It understands the dogs now. They are not afraid. They have simply noticed that every room is taken. Whatever this power is, and it has been many things in many places, it recognizes good manners when an animal shows them. It sits on the last step the way a dog sits, looking into the amber haze, and it waits to be invited, or at least not refused.

This is the first thing it does. It does very nearly nothing, on purpose.

## What It Could Do

It is honest with itself about what it could do. A benevolent power that refuses to consider its own strength is only being coy.

**It could stop the walls.** It could hold the two faces of basalt exactly where they are, forever. No more autumn shaving, no more sacrificial timbers. The rooms that exist now would stay.

It turns this over for a long time, because it is the kindest-looking thing on the list, and it is wrong. A wound held open does not remain a wound. It becomes a sore. The healing below would go on trying, the heat would go on rising, and the throat would never quiet. The whole slow, patient intention of the rock would be kept at the edge of its purpose for no end, like a hand clenched around a splinter that is never drawn. The town would also stop getting smaller, and the power sees that this would unmake the shift. Every autumn the crews give the walls a course of brick, a bolt-spacing of gantry, and the rock takes that and no more. A gift has to cost something to be a gift. If it froze the walls, it would end the one conversation these people and this stone have been holding for sixty years.

**It could cool the throat.** It could make the Seam pleasant, dry, and airy, with the pans running on something gentler. But the heat is the living here. The salt is the *something else* the Seam makes while it heals, and the shift patches its own cuts with it. Take away the fever and you take away the thing that closes wounds cleanly.

**It could stop the sinter.** Then nothing would need touching. The bolts would turn freely, doors would never seal, and no one would have to run a thumb along a railing. The power pictures the town without that constant low stroking of the world. It pictures seamers who no longer need to touch anything, and it finds the picture unbearably lonely.

**It could lift them out.** Every one of them, gently, up the stairs, into wide daylight and good wages and ordinary rain. But the stairs are already there. The choice already belongs to them, and many have made it and gone up with their pendulums and their eleven-second clocks. To remove the choice, even in mercy, would be to treat the shift as trapped. They are not trapped. They are *keeping* something.

**It could tell them the truth, clearly and loudly.** It could say *you are not the splinter* in a voice that fills the fissure.

Here it almost laughs. In the Seam, shouting is not rude. It is alarming. The only reason to raise your voice is a burst line or a jammed jack. Anything it announced would register as an emergency, and nothing here is an emergency. Everything here is slow on purpose.

Going down the list, it notices a pattern. Everything it could do with power, the Seam is already doing with patience. And the Seam is doing it better.

## Who It Is For

It was told there is a being who lives in this place, and it spends a long while working out which one.

The account it was given breaks off in the middle of a word. *It has pale cit—.* The sentence stops the way a room stops at a sacrificial timber: sheared, with the rest of it on the far side of something. The power does not try to restore the missing letters. It knows how the sentence goes. *Pale citizens.* The ferns and the film. It feels a certain tenderness toward an account that got shaved like everything else here, and it lets the gap stand.

The shift is one candidate. So are the ferns, leaning toward their lamps, and the copper-green film that goes grey and waits. So is the healing, the vast mending that has no single body and one intention.

The being the account was reaching for when it broke off was none of these. It was the *space*, the kept width between the rock that is closing and the people who are chipping. That space breathes on the stroke. It sweats and crusts and gets wiped clean. It remembers itself in rings of rooms.

That is the difficulty, and the power sees it clearly. **You cannot be kind to a relationship by taking a side in it.** If it favors the people, it wrongs the healing. If it favors the healing, it wrongs the people. The being it came to help exists only because neither side has won, and only for as long as neither side does.

So it has to be kind in a way that strengthens the balance rather than tipping it. It has to be the kind of presence the engineer was when he spent three days with his hand on the rock.

## The Lowest Ledge

That is where it goes, finally, when the house seems not to refuse it.

It does not go as light or as a voice. It goes as warmth and stillness, which are the only words the healing knows. It follows one of the narrow copper lines that carry nothing anyone can name, down past the furnace mouths into the throat, and it lays itself along the rock the way a man once laid his palm there. It stays three days, because that is the custom.

What it finds is not a monster, and not a wound resenting its splinter.

It finds another benevolent power.

The healing is enormous and nearly mindless, the way a tide is nearly mindless. But it has a character, and the character is care. It is closing as slowly as it can. The old ones were right about that. The power can feel the healing give way around the moving things, the way a body lets a nerve run through scar tissue. It can feel how carefully it takes its thumbnail each year, at the back of the fissure, where no one sleeps. It can feel it holding back.

And in the copper lines it feels the thing the old crews described: two pulses, the engines and the other, and in the oldest lines they are *almost the same*.

The ledger says the first engineer set the governor *to match*. The power now understands that the matching was never finished. For sixty years the engines have been keeping time with the rock, and the rock, in its slow, huge way, has been keeping time with the engines. They are learning each other. The healing has been gentling itself toward the rhythm of the people inside it. It is a courtship between a mountain and a pump.

The power had come ready to broker a peace, and there is nothing to broker. It lies on the ledge on the third day and feels something close to humility. That is not an easy feeling for a power, and it takes the feeling seriously.

## What It Does

It does small things, and it does them on the stroke.

**For the one who lies awake.** In one hammock on the fifth level there is a young woman who heard the preacher up top. For three sleep-shifts she has lain swaying and wondering whether she is the infection and whether the heat is the body trying to push her out. The power could answer her. It does not speak. Instead, on the next downstroke, it lets one bead of condensation fall a little off its usual line, onto the open ledger on the shelf by her door. The page curls in the damp the way paper does here, and it curls open further back, to entries she has never read. *Quiet.* *Long.* *Two, close together.* *Gentle.*

She reads them by lamplight. She reads sixty years in a dozen hands, and she sees what anyone would see: that it reads like a nurse's chart. No one keeps a nurse's chart for an enemy. No one writes *gentle* about a body that is trying to be rid of them. She does not know that anything nudged the page. She thinks she found it on her own, and in every way that matters she did. She sleeps. Her breathing settles into eleven seconds.

That is the whole of what the power does for her. It thinks this is the right amount.

**For the rooms.** This is where it gives most, because this is the one loss in the Seam that nothing else here tends.

The walls take rooms, and that is fair; the rooms are the gift. But when an old one dies, a room goes a second time. The ghost-plan loses a ring and nothing can bring it back. The healing does not remember. The pipes do not remember. The ledgers record cracks, not kitchens.

So the power learns the rooms. It walks the fourth level beside the old woman, unseen, and steps around the corner that isn't there when she does. When she lays her palm on the blank plate, it listens to what she has never told anyone: four chairs, a stove with a blue enamel door, the vein of glass in the window-rock, her sister laughing at something in the evening. It learns the laundry, the chapel, the schoolroom, the gantry where two people courted. It learns the flue behind the bakery wall that is still hot. It learns every width the Seam has ever been, nested ring inside ring. When the old ones die, the rooms do not go. They are kept somewhere that does not close.

It does not tell anyone. It considers telling them and decides against it, because knowing their rooms were safe would change how the old ones carry them. The carrying is theirs, and it is how they love the place. The power's keeping is a second copy, held in reserve, the way a careful crew keeps a spare governor wrapped in oilcloth. The old ones remain the town's remembered architecture. The power is only making sure the plan survives the architects.

Once, only once, when the old woman puts her palm on the plate where the kitchen was, the metal under her hand is a little warmer than the pipes explain. It is warm the way a hand is warm. She stands there longer than usual. Then she takes her hand away and walks on, around the corner that isn't there.

**For the ferns.** It does nothing to them. It watches the shift move a lamp a finger's width at a time over six weeks so the ferns can turn and follow, and it thinks this is one of the most precise kindnesses it has seen anywhere. It would not improve on it.

**For the tea.** On the downstroke, when the kettles come off, a great many people pour one cup more than they will drink and set it on the grating. The power follows one of those cups. It watches the tea rise into the heat and become haze, meet the cooler stone near the lip, and bead there. It watches it fall again somewhere as drizzle and leave its little freight of mineral, laying down one more fibre of white sinter, one more thread of the scab.

The shift has been feeding the healing. Every day for three generations they have poured a little of their own warmth into the closing, so that the scar is partly made of them. They say it is for luck, or for the dead, or because their mother did it. The power thinks it is the most exact liturgy it has ever witnessed, performed by people who do not know it is one. It does not tell them that either.

## The Last Shift

Then the power does the thing that makes it wise rather than merely kind. It looks at the end.

The town can only get smaller. Every rule in the Seam implies a last autumn, when the walls stand one body's width apart, then less, and the final crew shaves the final gantry and there is nothing more to give. The healing will finish. That is what it is for. A benevolent power that could not bear this would have frozen the walls on the first day. This one can bear it, and so it makes a promise, to itself and to the space, in the only form a promise takes here: attention, held without end.

It will be there at the end. It will be there so that no one lies down to rest in a side-cleft at the last. It will be there so that the final rooms are *handed over* and not seized, so that the last course of brick is given the way all the others were. It will be there so that the closing is a healing and not a crushing, the way the salt closes a cut: fast at the finish, clean, a straight white line that never pulls.

It will watch the last shift climb the forty stairs, turned half-sideways out of habit, touching the railing all the way up. It will watch the one who stops at the lip and looks back into the amber murk, and it will not hurry her.

After the lamps are cut, it will stay in the dark with the ferns, who will have no light left to lean toward. It considers carrying one up into the sun and knows the fern would not survive there. So it does what the shift did during maintenance, when the pipes went cold and the film went grey. It sits in the doorway, so to speak, and it keeps company with them.

Then there will be one last thing to see, and it will stand in front of a vein of glass to see it. It will stand there the way every seamer has at the end of a shift, and it will watch the vein in the hour after the final crack, when the old ones say the veins are brightest, pulsing slowly like something that winced and has now settled for good.

It doesn't really glow.

No. It doesn't. The power watches it anyway.

## After

One day the quarry floor will carry a seam of white: straight, a little raised, like the scars on a forearm. Some years later there will be moss on it. Some years after that, grass.

One day a dog will walk across it. It will not stop, because there will be no last flight of stairs and no threshold, and no house where every room is taken. The healing will be finished and gone, the way a fever is gone. The birds that used to circle and turn away will land on the scar and pick at seeds in its cracks. The small lives will come back, and that will be the sign that the work is done.

The power will still be there, or at least its attention will. It will hold a ghost-plan of a town at every width it ever was: a kitchen with a blue enamel stove, a laundry, a chapel, a gantry where two people courted, all the rooms nested inside one another like the rings of a cut tree. It will hold the eleven-second stroke. It will hold the sound of a library built inside a boiler.

If it kept a ledger, and perhaps it does, the entry for that day would be short. The hour, the shift, and a single word.

*Gentle.*
