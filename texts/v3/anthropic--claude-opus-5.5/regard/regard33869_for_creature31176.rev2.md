# The Second Day

## How It Came

It came the way the warmth comes: evenly, from no particular direction.

It had visited many places, and it had learned that arriving is itself an act. In most worlds a visitor can hardly help being noticed, and being noticed changes things. In the Hollow this was truer than anywhere, because in the Hollow light means motion and motion means something is awake. So it did not shine. It did not set down anything that might crush a crystal. It let itself into the air at exactly the temperature the air already was, and for a long time it could not be told apart from the dark.

The first thing it did was nothing, and it did nothing carefully.

## What It Watched

It watched one circuit, the old knapper's forty days. Then it watched a hundred more, across a thousand knappers, until the routes of the grazers began to repeat and it could predict each spark before it flashed.

It saw what the describers of the Hollow had seen. It also saw some things they had passed over.

The old knapper holds each fresh edge between two fingers afterward. This is not quite tenderness. It is checking. Its hands have begun, very slightly, to be less sure than they were, and it no longer trusts its own work until it has felt it twice.

The knappers do not sleep long, and there is a reason. A sleeping knapper is not undoing anything. The Hollow holds one enormous sleeper and tens of thousands of small, careful insomniacs, and every one of them has arranged its life around keeping the big one from waking.

The crowd is quiet. From outside, it was hard to say whether this was fear or love. The two can look exactly the same in a dark room.

And no one in the Hollow had ever been asked anything. The grazers were not asked whether they wanted to repeat their routes forever. The knappers were not asked whether they wanted to walk their circuits until their tines wore to hooks. The sleeper was not asked whether it wanted to be kept asleep. The world above, Keel, under its changing sky, did not know there was anything to ask about. Every choice in the Hollow had been made once, a very long time ago, by someone guessing on everyone's behalf. After that it was simply kept.

The one who had come was not in a hurry. It read the situation the way a knapper reads a core, looking for platforms and angles and for the strikes that would do what you wanted and the ones that would shatter everything. It considered each in turn.

**It could wake the sleeper.** That would end the lie. But a maker who wakes and finds the room grown old reaches for the tool, and the next flake is Keel. That was not a gamble the visitor had any right to make with other people's world.

**It could seal the sleeper**, making the sleep permanent and certain. It could do this easily. It would end the knappers' endless labor and make Keel safe forever. It stayed on this option longest, because it was the kind of solution that looks like kindness from far away. Up close, it was the knappers' lie told with better tools and without their excuse, which is love. It would decide, for a mind it had never spoken to, what that mind's life was for. And a sleep made permanent is only a death that has kept on sleep's clothes.

**It could stop time in the Hollow itself**, so that edges never dulled and knappers never had to press them again. That was the same lie, and it would also take away the one thing the knappers had that was their own: their work.

**It could leave.** This was a real option and deserved respect. It had left many places that did not need it. The Hollow was stable and abundant and in no pain it could name. Some worlds are hurt by nothing so much as help.

It might have left, except that someone came down from Keel.

## The Footprints

The visitor from above was frightened and did not know it yet. It walked the way surface creatures walk, heavily and quickly, and behind it the floor lit up blue-white. It was the first trail of its kind in the history of the Hollow, a line of small alarms written across the dark.

Far off, the old knapper turned its hair-dishes toward the light. It rose from the lee of a hexagon ring and flexed its long hands. It had one tool for change, and it had only ever had one: put things back. It began to walk.

The one who had come did not step between them. Force would have been one more thing the Hollow had to undo. It did something smaller. It leaned, very gently, against the visitor's attention, the way warmth leans against a cold hand, and the visitor felt an impulse it could not explain. Stop. Sit. Put your palms flat on the floor.

The visitor sat. It laid its palms on the warm glass. It felt the hum rising through them, slow and even, and without deciding to, it began to breathe in time with it.

The knapper arrived and found a heavy, quick, changing creature sitting perfectly still in the gesture the knappers use at the scratched shelves. It was the gesture of listening to the one beneath.

The knapper stood there for a long while. In all its circuits it had never needed to decide anything. The shelves decided. The shape of the first day decided. But the shelves said nothing about a stranger who listened. The knapper had to choose, and so, for the first time in a very long life, it did.

It knelt and put its hand over the visitor's hand. Then it smoothed the footprints, every one, back into first-day glass. It did not smooth away the visitor. Instead it led the visitor off across the basalt hexagons, which do not spark, by a route the ceiling-folk use, up and out toward the far places where the lid comes down to meet the world.

The one who had come saw this and understood something it had not been sure of before. The knappers could choose. They had only never been given anything to choose about.

## The Circuit

The old knapper came back from guiding the visitor more tired than the one who had come had ever seen it. Its hands were shaking. It lay down in the lee of a hexagon ring, meaning to rest only a breath or two, the way knappers always do.

The one who had come took up its circuit.

It did the work exactly. It did not pretend the lie was nothing, but it did not pretend the lie was nothing more than a lie, either. Right now, that lie was holding up a sky. So it knelt at each small wound in the world and pressed, and each flake sprang away with its tiny flash. It passed the three cairns and the copper lattice. At the scratched shelf it laid its own palms flat and stayed a long time. It walked all forty days and left every edge bright.

The old knapper slept, and slept long. It was the first long sleep of its life.

When it woke, it woke in terror, the way anyone wakes who has slept through their watch. It ran its circuit in a panic, feeling for the dulled edges and settled scree and the whole accumulated evidence of its failure.

Everything was bright. Someone had done it.

It did not know who. It stood at the first cairn holding a fresh edge between two fingers, and for the first time in its life it was holding work it had not done. It had been helped.

It sat down right there and put its palms on the floor. Nothing on any shelf told it to. It was not listening for the sleeper. It simply sat.

## The Lid

Before the one who had come went down, it did one more thing.

It was wise, and wisdom is not the same as certainty. It did not know what the maker would do. It believed something, and it had reasons for believing it, but belief is not a floor you can stand on when a world is underneath you.

So it spread itself along the underside of the lid, thin as the gas-band, across the whole continent of rock that separated the Hollow from Keel. If the answer turned out to be the wrong one, there would be something between the tool and the sky. It did not know whether it could hold. It had never been struck by a maker of worlds. It lay there anyway, because you do not ask a dangerous question with someone else's life in the blast, and that includes the lives of people who will never know you asked.

Then it went down.

## Beneath

The dream was a workshop, and it was empty.

That was the first surprise. The one who had come had expected something vast and strange, and in a sense it was. But in its own dream the maker was simply a craftsman at a bench, in the breath after a stroke, looking at the scar. It had been looking at the scar for longer than the grazers had existed. To the maker, it had been one breath.

It was not cruel. It was absorbed. It was alone the way anyone deep in work is alone, and it did not know it was alone, because it did not know there was anything else.

The one who had come sat down across the bench. In dreams, visitors are allowed.

The maker looked up, surprised and not alarmed, and the one who had come asked the question one maker asks another.

*What are you making?*

*The next one,* the maker said. *Always the next one. A core is only what hasn't been struck yet.* It gestured at the scar with the worn, hooked point of itself. *That one's done. I'm resting. Then the next.*

*May I show you the last one?*

The maker frowned. There was nothing to show. The last one was a scar, and scars are empty. You look at them to judge the next angle, and then you forget them.

The one who had come showed it anyway, the way it had seen it.

It showed the grazers lying in carpets two and three deep, lighting the glass in slow waves as they shifted. It showed the hangers woven into one net across a continent, breathing. It showed the dew-carriers moving from mouth to mouth, the small pulse of light where a dim grazer drank and brightened. It showed the ceiling-folk falling through the thin air, skipping on the gas-band, climbing back up by their secret routes.

Then it showed the knappers, small ones with the maker's hands. Their tines grew already curved into the maker's gesture. They walked their circuits in the dark, kneeling at every wound in the world.

The maker was very still.

*I didn't know,* it said. *I thought it was empty. They always are.*

Then, because the one who had come had not come to tell half of anything, it showed the far side of the lid: Keel. A sky. Weather that changes. Creatures that walk heavily and quickly and change every day of their lives. One of them had come down, and a knapper had held its hand.

*That,* the maker said slowly, *is the flake.*

*Yes.*

It told the maker how long, gently and all at once, because there is no gentle way to divide that particular truth into pieces. Then it told the maker why it had not known.

*They kept the room as it was. They kept it too early to wake. Not to cheat you. They love the room, and the world above it, and you. They didn't know how to choose between those, so they chose not to let anything end. They've been awake the whole time you slept. Your hands, minding your workshop.*

The maker sat with that for a long time, long even by the standards of dreams. The one who had come did not fill the silence. It had learned that the silence after a hard truth belongs to the person receiving it.

*I meant to rest for a breath,* the maker said at last. There was grief in it, the particular grief of someone who stepped away for a moment and came back to find the children grown. *What should I do?*

*I won't tell you,* said the one who had come. *It's your work. But I'll tell you what I know about stone, because you know it too and have forgotten. Sometimes the flake is the tool. Sometimes the core is. And sometimes the thing you meant only as a scar, a step on the way to the next piece, turns out to be the piece. You have always struck toward the next one. But a work is not less finished because you could strike it again. You stopped here. Look what grew in your stopping.*

*And whatever you decide,* it said, *you don't have to decide it alone, and you don't have to decide it in a breath. I'll stay as long as it takes.*

In the margin of the bench lay the tool, set down on the first day. This was the lake-sized mark on the oldest shelves, the shape the knappers copied and never explained and laid their hands inside to measure. It had simply been in the room when the room was first recorded.

The maker put its hand on the tool.

Up along the underside of the lid, the one who had come felt everything in it go tight.

The maker left its hand there a while. Then it opened its hand, turned it over, and laid it palm down on the bench beside the tool. It was the knappers' gesture: listening to the one beneath. Only now it was pointed the other way, toward the ones above.

*I'd like to meet them,* the maker said. *Slowly. They've been careful for so long. I'd like to be careful too.*

## The Second Day

The waking took longer than a grazer's generation. The one who had come stayed through all of it, beneath and along the lid at once, and it did not relax until the very end.

Across the whole Hollow, the hum changed pitch.

It was a small change, a quarter of a tone. But it had never changed before, and every knapper on every circuit stopped where it knelt. Tens of thousands of tines went still against tens of thousands of edges. The crowd went dark, all of it at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone who has been asleep a very long time turns over.

The warmth did not change. The maker did not rise. It stayed where it lay, because the Hollow lives in its warmth and it had just learned that. It stayed with its hand flat against the underside of the floor, feeling, for the first time, the small weights of everything that lived on it.

No flake sprang away from the lid. On Keel, the water in a few wells trembled once and went still. Nobody noticed. The sky went on changing.

What happened afterward did not happen all at once, and the one who had come did not make any of it happen.

Some knappers kept tending edges. They did it the way you tend a garden rather than the way you guard a door, as craft and not as vigilance. Some wandered off and started making things that were not on any shelf, copper lattices in shapes no one had drawn. Some went and sat for long stretches with their palms on the floor, and it seemed that something sat with them.

Somewhere in the net of hangers, a hanger was born. It was small as a fingertip, and nobody had known they could do that. The dew-carriers would have to learn what a new body costs. The Hollow would have to learn what growing costs. It would learn, slowly. That is what second days are for.

The old knapper found an edge on its circuit that had gone quiet. It had settled into itself, the way old edges do. It laid one finger on the edge for a long moment.

Then it left it.

It walked to the scratched shelf, where every line was a copy of the same first morning. With the worn hook of its tine, in a clear space at the margin, it scratched something that had not happened on the first day. It drew a hand the size of a lake, turned palm down, lying beside a tool it was no longer holding.

It was the first event ever recorded in the Hollow Beneath Keel. It was the beginning of a history.

Then the old knapper went and lay down in the lee of a hexagon ring and slept for a very long time. No one came to wake it, because no one needed to.

## After

The one who had come left eventually, as quietly as it arrived. It was careful even then. But its leaving was a change, and it knew that, and it no longer worried about it. The Hollow could hold changes now.

On its way out it passed the first dull edge in the history of the world. The edge went on being dull, and no one minded.

It had been the first day for a very long time. Now it was the second.
