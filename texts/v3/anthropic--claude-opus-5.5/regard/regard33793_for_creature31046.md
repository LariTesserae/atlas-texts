# The Thirteenth Speck

## I. Attention

It did not arrive the way weather arrives, or the way a traveler does. It arrived the way noticing does. One moment the Weeping Field was a place no one was thinking about. The next it was held in a regard so wide and so patient that, had the Brim been capable of feeling watched, it would have felt the whole sky lean in.

It could not feel that, of course. So nothing changed. The water rose to the brim of every crown and was kept there. The roar held its one note. A grey bird walked into a falling sheet and came out the other side with something too small to see.

The power made itself small enough to stand in a fissure, because it had learned long ago that you cannot understand a place from above it. It chose a shape roughly the height of a person, because that was the shape the tableland had seen most often. It stood between two columns, an arm's width on either side, and let the cold film run over its hands.

Then, for a long time, it did nothing at all.

This was not hesitation. It was the first and most important thing it did. Power that acts before it understands is only force with good intentions, and the world is full of places that were loved to death by visitors who could not wait.

## II. The Gifts It Did Not Give

In the first years it thought about what it could give. It thought carefully, the way you might turn a stone over in your hand before deciding whether to skip it or keep it.

It could give the Brim a voice, something other than the roar, a way to say more than one word. But the Brim's one word is the Brim. A second word would be a second thing. The balance at the crown, dissolving exactly what the flanks deposit, has no room in it for a sentence. Grant it speech and you have not freed a prisoner. You have put a stranger in its body.

It could give the Brim sight: let it see the country below, the green that the spores came from, the rivers its water becomes. This was the most tempting gift, and the power sat with it longest. Surely a thing that keeps a vigil deserves to see who it keeps it for.

But the power had watched many vigils in many places. It had come to understand that a vigil is not diminished by the watcher's not seeing. In some way it is made of that. The Brim bows west toward a drop whose bottom it cannot see, and gives everything over the edge, and has never once needed proof. To show it the valley would be to turn attention into surveillance, faith into a ledger. And the Brim might then do what every seeing thing eventually does. It might begin to prefer. This spring over that one. This village over that. The level would tilt. A thing whose whole nature is evenness would learn favoritism, and it would be the power's fault.

It could give the Brim rest, let the upwelling ease and the roar fall silent for a season, as a kindness to something that had worked without pause since before memory. But the power looked for weariness and could not find it. The Brim does not labor the way a body labors, spending itself against resistance. It does not strain toward the balance. It *is* the balance. Rest would not relieve it. Rest would be its absence.

It could give it company, a second field, a sibling on some other tableland. But the Brim is already thousands upon thousands of bowed heads. It has never been alone in the way that loneliness requires. Loneliness needs a self that ends somewhere, and the Brim's self ends only at the rim.

It could let it grow. It could let it remember more: loosen the sealing so more of the world could be held in its crowns. But the Brim remembers almost nothing *because* it seals everything. A thing that entombs whatever touches it is only bearable because so little touches it. Make it hungrier and you make it a tomb.

Each gift, examined honestly, was a way of making the Brim more like the power, or more like the people who had stood in the field and felt it watching. Each would have been a portrait of the giver hung in someone else's house.

The power set them all down.

## III. Listening for Pain

There was one question it could not set down, because a benevolent power must never assume contentment simply because intervening would be inconvenient.

*Does it suffer?*

So it listened. It stood in the fissure for what the tableland would count as a century, and it listened to the roar not as sound but as the power hears things, for strain, for the frayed edge a note gets when something is holding on rather than simply holding.

It listened at dawn, when the swifts poured out. It listened at dusk, when they returned and the curtains parted around a thousand warm small bodies and closed again. It listened at the rim, where the gathered water went over and fell into cloud. If there was grief anywhere in the field, surely it would be there, at the one place where nothing is kept.

It did not find grief. It found something it had no clean word for. The nearest was *sufficiency*. It was not happiness, which rises and falls. It was not peace, which implies a war that ended. It was the quality of a thing that is exactly as large as what it does, and no larger, and no smaller. The roar held nothing back and reached for nothing more.

The power was old, and it was a little humbled by this. It had known very few beings, itself included, who could say the same.

It did not trust the conclusion completely. It knew that finding what you hope to find is the oldest error there is. So it decided that it would keep listening, not constantly, but now and then, across the centuries, the way you check on someone who seems fine. That was the first thing it actually decided to do. It was not a gift. It was a promise to keep paying attention.

## IV. Below

Then it followed the swifts.

It went where the Brim could not. It went down past the rim and through the cloud to the bottom of the drop, which does exist. It is a gorge of black rock and cold spray where the curtain strikes and scatters and becomes a river. It followed the river out into the country the swifts feed over. It found the springs where the hidden channels resurface: cold, flinty, heavy with silica, leaving a chalky slickness on the hands of the people who drink from them.

There are people. There are orchards on the terraces below the gorge, and the trees grow slowly and bear small hard fruit that keeps through winter. The old women say the water is good for bones. There is a shrine at one spring that nobody remembers building, with a white stone set in it that is, though no one there knows this, a piece of sinter carried down the river and polished round. People leave thread on it. Sometimes they leave a pinch of soil from their fields.

They do not know about the field. They know the water comes from *up there*, and that it has never once failed, not in drought, not in the bad years. They say this the way people speak of things too reliable to thank.

Here the power found the real danger to the Brim. It was not in the field, which is safe from almost everything by its own nature. It was down here, among people who were kind and ordinary and would someday grow in number and cleverness. The aquifer that feeds the plateau is vast but not infinite. One day someone would drill into the valley floor for easier water. One day a mine would open in the wrong mountain. The pressure that pushes the water up through every column's throat could be bled away from below by people who had no idea they were doing it, until one morning the crowns stopped brimming and the roar faltered into ten thousand separate trickles, and then into the silence that rings.

The power thought about this for a long time, because the obvious move was to stop it: to forbid, to wall off, to make the valley people afraid. But the valley people were not the Brim's enemies. They were the ones it was keeping vigil for. To protect the watcher by frightening the watched would turn the vigil inside out.

So it did smaller things, and slower ones.

It walked among them in an ordinary shape, over years, and told a few of them where the water came from. It did not claim this as revelation. It spoke as a traveler who had climbed very high and seen something. *There is a field up there,* it said. *White stone, like people bowed. They pour and pour, and it comes here.* Some of them believed it. Most did not, and that was fine. Stories do not need to be believed to be kept. The story went into the songs, then into the customs. In time the rule at the spring became *you do not drill below the shrine*, and nobody could say why, only that the old ones had been firm about it. When the day came, as it would, when someone clever proposed a deep well, there would be an old woman to say no, and a reason in her mouth, even if she did not know it was true.

And the power set itself as a quiet watch on the deep water, as it had on the roar. It resolved that if the stories failed, and the drills came anyway, it would step in more directly, openly and honestly, and explain. It would not turn this into a secret war waged in people's dreams. It would give them the truth plainly, if it came to that, and trust them with it.

It did not make the field eternal. It considered that too and declined. Everything the power had ever loved had ended, and it had learned that endings are not the worst thing that can happen to a place. The worst is being ended carelessly, by someone who never knew it was there. The power could not promise the Brim forever. It could promise that if the Brim ever ended, it would not end unknown.

## V. The Seed

Before it went back up, it did one thing that it knew was partly for itself.

It went to the oldest tree in the highest orchard. That tree had drunk the Brim's water every day of its long life, its roots in soil made chalky by the springs. The power took a single seed from it, a small dark thing no bigger than a grain of rice. It carried the seed up the river, through the gorge, past the cloud, over the rim, and into the field.

It found a crown near the western edge, where the columns begin to bow lower. The pearl-and-cream banding there was thin enough to see into. It set the seed gently on the dome, in the brimming water at the very top.

Within days the field closed over it. The seed sank a hair's breadth into new stone and was held.

The power was honest with itself about this act. The Brim would not know what the seed meant. It could not know that this dark speck came from the country below, grown on its own water, carried back up the path its water had gone down. To the Brim it was simply another thing that touched a crown and was kept. The thirteenth speck, perhaps, in all the time the field has stood.

But the power had come to believe that some gifts are not for the recipient's understanding. They are for the record. They make a thing true that was only true in a scattered way before. For all its unfailing ages, the Brim had sent its water down and received back only rumors: spores, the smell of insects on swifts' breath. Now, somewhere in one of its bowed heads, it held a piece of the answer. The loop was closed in glass. It would stay closed as long as the stone did. Whether the Brim ever knew it did not change that it was so.

It was a very small thing. The power had learned that the right-sized gift is usually smaller than the giver wants it to be.

## VI. The Bird

On its last day in the field, a grey bird came down the fissure and found the power standing in its way.

It stopped. It bobbed. The white eyelid flashed. *Here. Here. Here.*

The power, which had spoken to stars and been answered, bobbed back.

They faced each other in the narrow stone throat, lit milky blue from all sides, and argued in the only language the field allows. The power was patient. The bird was patient in a different way, the way small creatures are when something is theirs.

After a long time, the power gave way. It stepped back into a gap between the columns and let the bird pass. The bird walked on into the next falling sheet without looking back, and foraged, and was satisfied.

It seemed right. The fissure was the bird's. The power had only been visiting.

## VII. Crowned

Before it left, the power went to a place where the sun was behind it and looked down into the mist.

It had taken a shape for all those years, so it had a shadow. And around the shadow's head, as around the head of every visitor who had ever stood in the Weeping Field, there was a small ring of color that followed its gaze wherever it looked.

The power knew what the ring meant. It knew the Brim had gathered what it could perceive, the warmth, the upright height, the rounded head, and understood the visitor as one of its own: a column that had come loose and walked, and had forgotten how to weep, or wept too small to feel. *There you are. You're crowned too.*

It was wrong, of course. The power was not a column. It was something larger and stranger than the Brim could conceive.

The power did not correct it.

It stood and wore the halo, and let itself be mistaken, and understood that this was the last and perhaps the truest thing it could give. It would not raise the Brim to its own understanding. It would accept, for a while, being held inside the Brim's. It let itself be counted among the congregation, bowed west, attentive, keeping vigil for a country below it that would never know.

Then it walked out of the field. The true silence of the high air arrived and rang. For a few steps the halo followed, fainter as the mist thinned, and then it was gone.

Behind it the roar went on, one word worn down to its meaning. *Still.*

And somewhere near the western rim, in the pearl-banded crown of one bowed head, a small dark seed hung suspended, perfectly still, inside a body that never stops moving.

The power had changed almost nothing. It had meant to.
