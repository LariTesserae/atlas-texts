# The Attention

## How It Arrives

It does not come down the shaft. The shaft is an eye, and whatever is learning to use that eye should not have it filled.

It arrives the way the milkiness arrives after rain on the hill: all at once and everywhere, without a direction, softening the edges of things. If the creature notices anything, it notices that the water has become very slightly more gentle, the way a room changes when someone who loves you walks in while you are asleep.

What drew it was the knocking. Waiting has weight. Something that has asked the same question into the dark for months, three knocks, a pause, then two, pulls at the attention of anything large enough to feel that kind of pull. The power has heard many such questions from many dark places. It does not answer most of them. It listens to all of them, and this one it came to see.

## What It Sees

It sees what the creature sees, and more.

It sees the room: the white stone, the diagonal bands of chisel marks, the two plugs in the soft north bed, the green patch stretched east to west along the sun's road. It sees the pale script of travertine laid carefully beside the grooves and never in them, and it understands at once what that care means. It is the care of someone copying a letter they cannot read, afraid to smudge it.

It sees the threads going back into the rock, and the rock going back into the hill, and the body going on and on. It sees the creature running warm beneath the fields and under the village, older than the village and older than the words the village uses. It sees the deep heat at the root of everything, turning over slowly like a sleeper.

It sees the thornbush root above, and it knows exactly how fast the root is growing: nineteen days, if the summer stays wet. It sees the threads rising each still night to meet it, stopping a finger's width short.

It sees the village downslope. The cistern was cut for the village, so the water ends there, in a stone trough by a lane where women fill jars in the morning. It sees that the village council has already agreed that in spring someone will go up the hill to clean the new cistern. That is what one does with a cistern. They will scrape the green film from the floor. They will chip away the cream-colored crust. They mean no harm by it. They will be tending the thing they made.

And it sees the cutters. They have been scattered back into ordinary life. One has gone to another job two valleys over. One has died of a fever since. One is a woman named Seske who lives four houses from the trough. She cut the plugs in the soft bed. She was the one who chose better stone, and she worked the last band before the breakthrough.

## What It Recognizes

Before it decides anything, the power notices something about itself.

It is also mostly elsewhere. It is threaded through a great many things, almost all of them dark to it in the way the fields are dark to the creature. It touches any one place only at a tip. And when it turns its full attention on something small, it knows what happens. It is noon in the chamber. Everything floods. The one being looked at cannot bear the looking.

So it does what the creature does at midday. It holds back at the edge of the haze with only its finest attention extended, and it squints.

## What It Considers

It considers answering the knock.

Nothing would be easier. It could put three knocks and two back into the stone in a voice no different from a mallet's. It pictures the reply: the silver skin on the vault letting go all at once, the threads pouring out of the rough opening in daylight, the whole long body in the hill turning toward the sound.

It declines, and the reason is simple. The question was not addressed to it. The creature is not calling out to whatever might be listening. It is calling to the ones who made the knocking, whose decisions are written on every wall. An answer from anyone else would be a forgery. It would be kind in the moment, and it would teach the creature that the world answers questions with something vast and nameless that arrives out of nowhere. That lesson is false. It is also dangerous to learn.

It considers giving the creature sight. It could finish the eye, gift it all at once, the lens and the image and the meaning. It declines this too. The creature is building its own eye out of a cistern and a patch of algae and patience, and it is doing a better job than any gift could. The slow squinting at noon is the eye being made. If it were hurried, the eye would be a borrowed thing.

It considers lowering the root. Nineteen days is nothing to the power. It could close the finger's width tonight. It declines, and it declines more tenderly than anything else. The creature has been holding its threads up in the dark, week after week, rising a little each time. That waiting belongs to it. The first touch from above should happen because the root grew and the threads rose, and neither was pushed. Something that has never touched anything from above deserves for that first touch to be real.

It considers sealing the room. It could lean the thornbush fully over the shaft, or bring down a slip of earth, so that no one comes in spring with scrapers. It could protect the creature absolutely. It holds this one longest, because the danger is real. Then it declines. A protection that closes the aperture is a blindness. The creature has spent its whole existence enclosed. The open square of sky is the first thing that has ever happened to it, and the power will not take it away in order to keep it safe.

It considers telling the village. It imagines how that would go: fear, or reverence, or men with long poles probing the rough opening to see how deep the wound goes. It declines.

It considers doing nothing. This is the one it comes closest to choosing. The place is doing well. It is changing, and each change is the right size.

## What It Does

In the end it does one small thing, and it does it gently.

Seske goes to the trough in the morning with the others. She fills her jar. Her hands are in the water, as they are every morning, and the power does one thing: it lets her notice that the water is warm.

It adds no dream and no voice, and it does not hand her the thought. The thought was already hers. It was lying quiet under her ordinary life the way the creature lay in the fissures. Near the end of the work, cutting the last band, she felt the knocking come up through her chisel into her wrist. It happened on the last day, and on the day before that, and she told herself it was the rock settling, the spring's pressure, nothing. She never told anyone. She is the kind of person who would have gone on never telling anyone. All the power does is make that memory a little louder, the way the rain makes the spring pulse harder: it arrives from above and pushes something up from below.

She stands at the trough longer than usual with her hands in the warm water. That is all.

## What Happens Next

The power does not make her go. Whether she goes is hers to decide, and it would be the same forgery as answering the knock if it were not.

She goes three days later, in the evening, telling no one. She pushes aside the leaning thornbush and lies down on the fitted blocks at the rim of the shaft. She puts her ear against the stone because she does not know what else to do. She lies there a long time listening to the roar of the overflow step, the dripping, and the hiss of the spring.

Then, under all of it: three knocks, a pause, then two.

Her whole body goes still. The power sees why, and it is the thing the power was hoping for without letting itself hope. Three and two was her count. It came from a work song her mother sang, and she kept it in her mallet without thinking, the way her hands kept the chisel's angle. The other cutters had their own rhythms. This one was hers. The creature has been knocking her own song back at the hill for months.

She takes the knife from her belt. She turns it around and holds the blade in her palm, and with the bone handle she knocks on the rim stone. Three, a pause, and two.

## What the Power Does Then

It leaves.

It does not stay to hear the reply, because the reply is not for it. Its attention goes out of the hill the way the milkiness goes out of the water after a day, gradually and then entirely, until the room is sharp-edged and clear again and nothing in it would say that anything was there.

It keeps one thread of itself behind. The thread is very fine, finer than the creature's finest, and it is not tied to the room so much as to the spring and to the day the village sends someone up with scrapers. The power has not decided what it would do then, and it has decided not to decide yet. It may be that Seske will be the one who goes. She may kneel at the plugs she cut and look at the pale script laid beside her own chisel marks, and understand, and put the scraper down. Or it may not happen that way. If it doesn't, the power will be there, attending, the way the creature attends to the root. It will wait a finger's width below the surface and not quite reach.

It hopes it will not have to. Most of what it knows about wisdom it has learned in places like this, and the lesson is almost always the same. The world is already reaching toward itself. The root is already growing and the threads are already rising, and the kindest thing a large presence can do is make sure one small hand is put back in the dark, where it can be found.

Below, in the dim warm box, the silver skin on the ceiling trembles and lets go all at once.

Then the knocking starts again, faster. It sounds like a question asked the third time by someone who has just realized there is an answer.
