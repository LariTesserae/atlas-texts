# The Guest

## Arriving

It came the way attention comes, all at once and from very far off. For a while it did not see them.

It saw what anyone would see. It saw the red pavement and the silver water, the shrimp clouding the pans, the parrots rising off a gnamma with a roar of wings, the monitor swinging its head low along the sedge. It heard the frogs. It counted, because it was the kind of thing that could count, and it found the plain full and busy and small, a market of brief lives. It was pleased by this. It could have stopped there and been content.

But it had been drawn here, and it knew it had not been drawn by the market. Something had called it, and whatever had called was not in a hurry and had not raised its voice.

So the first thing it did was nothing, for a long time.

This was harder than it sounds. A power of its kind moves quickly by nature. It thinks in the time a swallow takes to turn, and to it a wet season goes by like a held breath. To see the ripple from the center of the pan, it had to become slow enough that one minute was a long time. To see the tracks, it had to become slow enough that a day was one step. To see the creature breathe, it had to let the waterline become its clock: up a finger's width, hold, and back.

It did this. It slowed until the frogs sounded like weather, until the birds were a flicker at the edge of things, until the only events it could follow were the ones that took an hour. Then the swell at the edge of the pan was no longer a swell. The pools of sky in its hollows shifted as it watched, and the power understood that it was being watched in return, without urgency and without fear. It was being looked at the way the rock looks at rain.

## What It Considered

A benevolent power carries a certain hazard, which is that it can always think of something to do. This one had learned, over a long time and in many places, to hold its own kindness at arm's length and look at it before using it.

It thought about rain. It could bring rain. It could stretch the wet weeks into months, keep the pans full, and spare the tadpoles the last black crowding in the final pools. Then it looked at the eggs in the clay, which can wait for decades, and at the frogs in their cocoons of shed skin, and at the rosettes that flower only when the water is about to go. It understood that the drying was not the thing these lives suffered. It was the shape they had grown into. A plain that never dried would be a kinder place for exactly one season, and after that it would belong to other creatures. The power set the rain down.

It thought about the height. It had seen one of them stand, streaming copper, taller than the oldest tree, and it had felt the whole pan go silent. It wondered whether it could give that back, and let the creature be a mountain again, just for a while. But the creature had set the height down itself, slowly, the way you set down something heavy you have decided not to carry anymore. Nobody had taken the height from it. Handing it back would have treated a choice as a loss. The power set that down too.

It thought about the work. The creatures were pressing the land toward its final level a fingernail at a time. The power could have finished that in an afternoon. It could have made the plain perfectly even and given them, at once, the thing they seemed to be waiting for. This was the easiest temptation to see through, and still the one that took longest to let go. The work was not a burden standing between the creatures and their rest. It was what they were doing with their rest. Finishing it for them would be like finishing someone's sentence because you are sure you know how it ends. You might be right. It would still no longer be their sentence.

It thought about the salt, which they never take all of, and decided that whatever reason they had for leaving it was a better reason than any the power could supply.

Going down the list this way, it found that almost everything it could do for them was already being done, by the rain, by the rock, and by the creatures themselves, at the speed that suited it.

## What It Noticed

What it had not expected was the distance.

One for every lone tree, and the space between trees that the eye cannot comfortably judge. The power could judge it. It measured it and found it very wide. Each creature kept its circuit of a dozen pans, and the circuits touched only at the edges, at the old white-floored pans where two might lie on opposite shores with half a kilometer of water and a frog chorus between them.

It listened to the hum and found that the hum was not one sound. Each creature had its own note. They carried across the flat water and met, but they met out of phase, rising and falling against each other. They were never quite together.

The power did not know whether this was sorrow. It was careful here, because it would have been easy to decide the creatures were lonely and then set about curing a loneliness they did not have. Things that were once mountains may have ideas about distance that are not the ideas of small warm animals. A range of peaks is a company whose members never touch.

Still, it noticed. Everything on the plain answers something. The frogs answer each other. The parrots answer the gnamma. The water answers the millimeters. The creatures answered the rain, and the rock, and the long slope toward stillness. As far as the power could tell, nothing had answered them in a very long time.

## What It Did

It asked first. There was no voice for this, so it asked the only way that works here, by staying. It lay beside one of them at the edge of a pan for three days, which is how long they lie. It made itself warm, the way the clay beside them is warm. It made itself heavy enough to notice and still enough to trust. Shrimp gathered against it. A small dark lizard basked on it and did not flick away.

On the fourth day the creature moved. The weight gathered at one end, the long body thinned at the back, and in an hour it had gone a body's length toward the next pan. The power understood that it had been answered, and that the answer was not *go away*. It was more like *come along, if you are coming*.

So it did the thing it had not thought of in its list of kindnesses, because it was not a kindness it could give. It was one it could only receive.

It let itself be carried.

It made itself small. Smaller than a shrimp egg, smaller than a moss spore, the size of a grain in the clay packed into the creature's peeling flank. It rode that way across the red pavement, through a gnamma where the creature turned slowly in the tea-colored water, into the next pan, and the one after that. It saw what the creature carried. Beside it in the clay were a thousand other small patient things being brought from one isolated water to another, the way they had been brought since before there were frogs. It understood that this was the creature's generosity, and that it was vast. The best thing a guest can do in a generous house is let itself be given to.

It rode for most of a season. It felt the plain the way the creature felt it: the tilt of every rise too low to see, the pull of hollows no deeper than a fingernail. It learned the hum from the inside. It was not a longing. It was closer to the sound a stone makes when it is simply being a stone and has decided to let that be heard.

## The Door

Near the end of the wet weeks, at evening, the creature stood.

The power was on its flank, in the clay, when the body gathered and the crown lifted, higher than the rise, higher than the spires, as high as the oldest tree. Water streamed off it in the copper light. Across the whole pan the frogs stopped.

The silence was like a door opening, and this time the power understood what the silence was for. It was the one moment on the plain when a very low sound could be heard alone, without the chorus over it. Standing was the only way the creature had left of saying something loud enough to reach another of its kind, half a kilometer or a dozen kilometers away, without the frogs in between. It was not remembering being a mountain. It was calling.

The power did not answer for the other creature. That was not its place. But in the open door, it did one thing. It hummed, very low and very briefly, in the creature's own note, back from the flank it was riding on. It was not a message. It was only the sound of the call having been heard by something, the way the sedge lifts slightly when the long ring finally reaches it.

The creature held its height a moment longer than the power had seen before. Then it set the height down, slowly, and lay back into the clay. The ring spread across the pan and reached the sedge a minute later, and the chorus came back.

From very far off, at the edge of what even the power could hear, another note rose out of the plain. The two hums crossed the water, and for the length of perhaps three slow breaths they were in phase.

The power did not do that. It only noticed that it happened.

## What It Kept

Before it left, the power thought about the ending the creatures were waiting for: the last level, one pan the width of the plain, sky above and sky below with no seam between.

The power knew something about that. It had seen other old lands in other places. It knew that no level is final. Continents drift. Seas come in and go out. Somewhere under the craton, heat that has been quiet for an age will one day stir. When it does, the oldest and flattest ground will be lifted, gently at first, by a fingernail, and then by more. When that happens, the things that spent an age lying level will be the first to feel the tilt. The water will be unsure which way to go again, and something that was once a mountain may find it has a reason to stand.

It considered telling them, and decided not to. It was not keeping a secret for the sake of keeping one. It thought they might already know. A thing that was raised once remembers rising. And a being waiting for rest does not need to be told that rest is not the last word. Rest does not need a last word to be rest. If the lifting came, it would come in its own time, and the creatures would meet it in theirs. The power kept the knowledge the way you keep a gift for someone whose birthday is very far off. It did not keep it as a burden. It kept it as a warmth.

## Leaving

It left at dry-down, when the seeps had slowed and the moss had curled and the creature it had ridden lay at the lowest point of its last pan, among the final crowded pools, darkening in the sun.

It changed nothing it could point to. There was no extra rain, no new height, and no salt taken. The land sorts by millimeters, and the power had pressed it by none.

But the next wet season, three days after the rain, a traveler following a line of shallow curled depressions across the drying clay might have noticed something small. At the edge of one pan, where nothing in particular grows and the ground is no lower than anywhere else, the line bends aside from the path the sheet-water takes. It goes over to the place, pools there for what must have been hours, and then goes on.
