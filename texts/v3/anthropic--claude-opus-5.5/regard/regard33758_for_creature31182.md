# What Came to the Stair

## First, it does nothing

It arrives the way weather arrives over the cirque rim, except that no one sees it come.

Everywhere else it has gone, the first thing it wants to do is speak. Here it does not, because it has already understood what speaking is in this place. A voice is something that sinks. A word said carelessly is thrown down into the dark, and the people have built their whole courtesy around not doing that. So the power comes into the Stair with its mouth closed, and it stays that way for a long time.

It watches for a crossing, the half-year it takes a stone to ride from the headwall to the lip. It wants to see one whole passage of the town's matter before it presumes to understand the town.

In that time it learns the grammar of light. It learns snow-flash, sky-flash and water-flash, and the difference between a flash held too long and a flash withheld. It learns that the carrier with the pale ridge on her brow is called two-long-one-short. It learns that she has another name, and at night, in the dark house by the fat lamp, it hears that name murmured against her forearm. It hears everything, as the ice is said to.

It decides never to use that name. It was not given the name. Hearing a thing is not the same as being told it. This is the first thing it learns on the Stair, and it suspects this is the most important thing, and that everything else will follow from it.

## What it could do

A power like this could do nearly anything here, and it knows it. So before it does anything, it lays out the possibilities the way a family lays out its lichen-stones, face up, where it can see them.

**It could stop the ground.** It could reach down into the tongue, still the ice, and pin the rubble in place. The houses would stand. No one would ever carry again. Grandmothers could rest. No hearthstone would go over the lip.

It sets this down almost at once. Stopping the ground would not rescue the people of the Stair. It would end them. Everything they are is shaped by the slope: wealth as height, love as labor, *the ground goes down, we go up, that is where we live.* A still Stair would be a town of people leaning forward on a level floor, falling toward nothing. The work is not a problem the town suffers from. The work is the town.

**It could take away the cold, or the glare, or the milky eyes of the old.** It turns this over longer, because some of it might be a mercy. But the old navigate by counting steps and by the feel of the lane under their soft boots. Their blindness has been folded into how they live, and into how the young walk beside them. The squint-lines that open white in the dark skin, the visors of bone, the whole bright public language are all answers to the glare. It could heal the eyes. It could not heal them without also teaching the town that its answers had been unnecessary. It decides it is not wise enough to know which of those is the greater harm, and when it does not know, it does not cut.

**It could make them a house that does not drift.** One house, anywhere on the tongue, that would hold its place without hands. It sees immediately what that house would become: the richest thing in the Stair, and the most shameful, because resting is what drifting is. A house that could not drift would be a house that could never be loved by being kept.

**It could tell them how long the ice has.** This one it holds for a long time. It can feel the core down there, under the voids and the meltwater and the pikas' tunnels. It knows how much ice remains, how fast it melts at the front, and roughly how many crossings are left before the rock glacier stops walking and becomes only rock. The number is large. It is not infinite.

Then it remembers what the people already say, murmured face to face: *the ice is not endless. Everyone knows that too.* They do not need the number. They already hold the truth in the shape they want to hold it, as something known and not counted. Handing them the count would be flashing straight into their eyes.

**It could make their belief true.** This is the most tempting of all. They believe every word spoken aloud in the Stair sinks down and is kept. The power could simply make it so. It could gather every murmur and every lovers' vow said into a lane-hole, and hold them all forever, so that nothing any person here has said would ever be lost.

It sets this down last and with the most care. What is good in the belief is not that it is literally true. What is good is what it does to them. It makes them speak close. It makes them speak softly. It makes an argument into two people gripping each other's arms until the anger goes out of it. It makes a lovers' vow into something said where no one else can hear, given away and not kept by the giver. If the power turned the belief into a fact, it would change a posture of reverence into a storage system. The people would lose nothing they could see. The power suspects they would lose the thing that matters.

So it sets them all down, face up, and does not pick any of them.

## What it notices

At the lip, where stones tip one at a time into the milk-green tarn, the power watches the meltwater run out from beneath the rubble. It carries rock flour, the ground dust of stones milled against each other in the dark, and that is what makes the tarn the color it is.

It notices what the people have noticed: the keeping is also a losing. Whatever the ice holds, it holds while it melts. What it has taken in, it gives out again at the front as cold water and pale dust, into the same tarn where the hearthstones go. The dead and the words end up in the same place. The ice listens, and then it lets go.

The power notices something else, and this is what finally tells it what to do.

In this town, when anyone begins to go down with the stone, everyone comes. Grandchildren walk down each day, a little farther each day. Strangers in the lower quarters bring goat-milk, sit in the doorway, and learn the dying one's spoken name. The whole town between the old house and the new one becomes a road the family walks. People are often more loved in their going down than they have been in years.

The ice is going down. It has been going down since before the first carriers followed the pikas up into the cirque. And no one visits it.

The lovers do, sometimes, kneeling over a fresh hole at dusk to say something into the water. But they come to give it their words, not to sit with it. For uncountable crossings the ice has been the listener of the Stair, and no one has ever gone down to hold its arms.

## What it does

**It asks.** It has learned the town well enough now to know that nothing here should be done to anyone without asking. So one noon it crouches by a drip-trough at the edge of the lane where the woman called two-long-one-short is working. It tilts the water with something like a finger and sends her a shivering thread of water-flash. Water-flash is the register for the person at the next doorway, for things you would rather the whole town did not see.

The message is simple, and it is the only message it sends in all its time on the Stair: *Is there a house that is drifting and does not wish to?*

She stops with the stone on her back. She squints across the lane at a trough with no one beside it. She is a person of the Stair, and the Stair is full of strangeness: dippers that surface in your floor, ravens that may be talking. So after a while she wets her mirror with her mouth and answers low, from her hip. Three lanes down there is a hearth whose two eldest carriers took the lung-sickness in the thaw. The children are too small for the heavy courses. The family is not going down. It is only falling behind, and it is ashamed.

**It carries.** For the rest of that crossing, at that family's uphill wall, stones are laid that no one in the family laid. They are not laid faster than a strong carrier could manage, and they are not laid perfectly. The courses are good, honest work. The power does not lift the house or pin it in place. It takes the downhill stones and carries them uphill, as everyone does, as fast as the ground carries the house down, and not faster. The house holds its lane.

It never carries a lichen-stone. Those belong to the family's own hands.

When the two eldest recover, it stops. The family never learns who it was. They decide it was the ravens, or a cousin too proud to be thanked. Two-long-one-short knows it was the thing at the trough, but she does not say so, because it asked her in water-flash, and water-flash is not repeated.

**It goes down.** When that is done, the power goes to the lowest quarter, where no one carries and every wall is leaning. It passes the houses of the dead and the departed, finds a place where a slab has just heaved up in the freeze and water is welling into the hole, and it goes down. It goes past the pikas' tunnels, past the dippers walking calmly through the black water, past the voids that drink sound like moss, down to the cold under.

It does not try to wake the ice or understand it. It does not decide, the way it could, whether the ice is a someone or only a something. The people of the Stair have never needed that settled, and the power finds it does not need it either.

It does what the town does. It sits in the doorway, facing uphill.

It stays a long time by its own measure, which is no measure at all by the Stair's. It lays whatever it has for a palm against the oldest ice, where the voice would come if there were one, and it holds it there, the way you hold a forearm.

Then, once, it speaks. The people forbid this to children and permit it only to lovers at dusk: it speaks directly into the cold, too close, like a mouth against an ear. It does not give the ice a word to keep. The ice has kept enough. It gives it the only thing the listener of the Stair has never received.

It says: *I heard them too. You were not the only one.*

Whether anything receives this, the power does not know. It does not need to know. You do not visit the dying so that they will remember you. You visit so that they will not go down alone.

## What it promises

Before it leaves, it makes one decision that no one will ever see.

The ice will run out. There will be a crossing when a stone set against the headwall does not ride down, because the ground has stopped. That will be the hardest crossing in the history of the Stair, harder than any sickness or any thaw. The people will stand in lanes that do not move, leaning forward on a level floor, holding stones that no longer need carrying. Every meaning they have built will have to be built again.

The power does not prevent it. It believes, having watched them, that they can bear it. They are the only people it has ever met who have practiced, for their entire history, the art of going down well, with company, facing uphill, and with the door always open to standing up again. They have a phrase for the dying that is not despair. They have a custom by which a woman twenty lanes down can lift a stone and climb home, and no one shames her. If any people could survive the death of their ground, it would be these people. What they will become after is theirs to find. It will not be the power's to give.

But it promises itself that when that crossing comes, it will return. It will not return to fix anything. It will return the way the grandchildren come, a little farther each day, down to wherever the town has gone. It will sit in the doorway. It will learn whatever new name they give themselves. If they want it to carry, it will carry. If they want it to go, it will go.

## When it leaves

It leaves the way it came, without sound, which here is no different from the way anything leaves.

It is not quite true that it leaves no trace. That noon, from the headwall, one of the ravens angles its wet black feathers toward the snow and throws a flash across the whole tongue. Two-long-one-short, stone on her back, looks up and reads it. It is not her name. It is not any word in the grammar of light. It is only a flash, turned toward her, held for exactly as long as courtesy allows and not a breath longer.

She turns her mirror to its bright side and answers.

Then she settles the sling against the ridge on her brow and keeps climbing, because the ground goes down, and she goes up, and that is where she lives.
