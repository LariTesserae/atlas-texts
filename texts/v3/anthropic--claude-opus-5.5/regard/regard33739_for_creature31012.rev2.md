# The Visitor

## How It Noticed

It noticed the plateau the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue.

A power that attends to the world hears the world, all of it, the long exhalation of oceans, the grinding of plates, the billion small frictions of living things against each other. Against that roar, the upper pans were a hole: a basin twenty kilometers across where sound went in and did not come out. At first it took the place for a wound. Silence on that scale usually means something has died.

So it listened more closely. Beneath the silence it found a pulse of cold, and beneath the cold a slow tide of water, rising every night and falling every noon, and inside the water, life. Too much life for a wound. It saw lichen following the dew in branching veins and rabbits breathing in the dark of the tafoni. At the heart of it all it found attention, vast and patient and turned outward, a held breath that had been held so long it had become a way of living.

It understood then that something on that plateau was doing what it did. Something there was listening to the world.

It had not met many others who did.

## How It Came

It could have arrived in any number of ways, and most of them would have done harm.

It knew what the old accounts knew: a sound too loud and too sudden, a rockfall, a strike of lightning, can go into the stillings and not come out, and a comb can die of it. A power's arrival, carelessly made, is louder than lightning. So it made itself small. It made itself slower than it liked to be, and lighter, and nearly nothing. It came up from the cloud-sea the way the mist comes up off the pans, in the hour before dawn, and it stood at the edge of the escarpment beside the end of the black pavement. The birds perched there did not fly.

It took the shape of a person because people had walked here before and the place was used to them. It chose an ordinary person, dressed the way the herders dress. It wore no bells.

Then it walked down into the basin on the plain rock, and the grit under its feet whispered and went quiet.

## What It Could Have Done

It walked for most of that first day, and while it walked it considered.

It could restore the dead combs. It could feel the dust in their cells and the shape the lattice had been, and it could put that shape back, and fill the cells, and the silence would return to those places and the pans would fill again.

It went to the nearest dead comb and stood inside it. The wind came through, and a bird it did not know the name of was calling from an empty cell with its whole small body, and three chicks were answering. The power stood there a long time. Then it left the dead comb dead. Death had made room here for the loud ones, the birds that still call. They were part of the place too. Something that loves the quiet does not need to love only the quiet.

It could stop the falcons. It watched a stoop out of the sun, through a comb's silence, and a rabbit that did not hear it coming. It watched the colony's watchers flick their tails a moment too late. It watched the falcon carry its catch to a ledge where two nestlings were waiting. It did nothing, because nothing about this was broken.

It could make the stillings speak, give them a voice, give them words, give them a way to tell someone what they had heard for ten thousand years. But they would eat their own voices. That was their nature, and there was nothing wrong with it. It had never been a lack.

It could show itself to the herders and tell them what the stillings were, what the combs were, what the pavement was for, and take away every mystery on the plateau in an afternoon. It thought about the painted hand on the hut wall and the grieving custom and the old men turning west in a thunderless storm. It thought that these people had made a good life out of not knowing, a courteous life, and that knowledge handed down from above would be a kind of shout.

It could read the record.

This was the hardest one. The burn-lines under every comb and along every trail hold everything the plateau has ever sounded like, and the power could read them. It knew it could. It had only to kneel and look at the lace-work scorched into the tuff the way it looked at anything, and every storm and footfall and whispered word from ten thousand years would open to it.

It knelt. It looked, only a little. It read enough to know that the thing beneath the many was well, that it was old and slow and not suffering, that its record went back further than the herders and further than the huts.

Then it came to the place in the northern record where the people used to go to weep, and it stopped reading.

What was said there had been said into a silence precisely because no one would hear it. The comb had kept it, but keeping is not the same as telling. A power that reads everything it is able to read is not wise. It is only able. It stood up and brushed the grey dust from its knees and walked on.

## The Comb That Could Not Hold

In the east of the basin, near the pass where the storms come through, it found a comb in trouble.

Lightning had struck it perhaps a month before. The top third of the lattice was still clear and working, and its pan held water, but the lower cells had gone cloudy and not cleared. When the power put its ear to the glass, which it could do, being what it was, it heard the thunder.

The thunder was still in there. It had gone into the stillings all at once, more than they could take, and they had not been able to turn it into cold. It circled inside their bodies, trapped, unspent, the way a scream stays in a throat. Some of the stillings in the lowest cells had already gone white. The others were holding on. They were holding the thunder the way you hold a breath underwater, waiting for the moment when you can't anymore.

Here the power did act.

It did the thing the combs do, which was the one thing it knew for certain the place would understand. It listened. It gathered the trapped sound toward itself cell by cell, gently, the way the whorls of an ear gather it, and took the thunder out of the stillings and into its own body. It took it slowly, over the whole of a night, so that nothing would be torn.

It could not save the ones that had already gone white. It did not try. That would have been a different kind of act, a reaching past the edge of what was broken into what was simply over, and it knew the difference.

By morning the lower cells were still cloudy, but the cloudiness had changed. It was the ordinary frost of stillings resting, not the chalk of stillings dying. The next dawn a few of them were clear. By the end of the week the lattice was sweating from top to bottom, and the pan below it was full, and the rabbits came.

The power kept the thunder. It did not release it over the cloud-sea, because a roll of thunder out of a clear sky in the west would have made every comb on the plateau lean toward it, and made the old herders rise from their fires. It would not give them a false sign. It carries the thunder still. It is not heavy.

## The Lamp

On its last morning it went to a place where the caustic fields of two large combs overlapped, where the bright lines slid over the stone and knotted and unknotted, and it waited there as the herders do.

It watched the patterns for a long time. A ripple went outward under the western comb, a ring of light spreading across the tuff with nothing moving to cause it. A few minutes later the eastern comb answered.

The power did not know their language. It had read a little of their record but not learned to speak in it, and it would not pretend to. It could not say *I am a friend* or *I have seen what you are* or *you are not alone in the world*. Anything that complicated would have been a lie, because it would have been the power's language wearing the shape of theirs.

It could do the simplest thing, though. It could lift a lamp in a dark room so the other would know it was there.

It bent the sunlight, very slightly, in the space between the two combs, and let a single ring of light spread across the stone. It was one ripple, clumsy, the wrong speed, the wrong width, obviously made by someone who didn't know how.

Then it waited.

Nothing happened for a long time. The caustics under both combs went on sliding with the sun. A lizard came out and flashed its throat at another lizard, and the other lizard flashed back. The power began to think that there would be no answer, and that this was all right. The place owed it nothing.

Then, under both combs at once, the light went still.

The whole slow drift of the caustics stopped, the way a room goes quiet when someone unexpected speaks. The bright lines lay motionless on the stone for the space of perhaps ten breaths.

Then one ring went out from each comb, not toward each other but toward where the power stood. They were small. They reached it together and passed over its feet and faded.

It did not know what that meant. It decided not to decide. Perhaps it meant *we hear you*. Perhaps it meant only *what was that*. Either one was enough. It had wanted to be noticed, and it had been.

## The Pavement

Before it left, it went back to the pavement.

It knew what the combs were listening for, or it knew one of the things they might be. They open widest at night and tilt upward. In storms they lean west toward the place where the fused stone ends in air. If something has been built to hear everything and left behind to go on hearing, it may be waiting for a footstep on that pavement.

The power could walk it. It could walk the whole length of the black stone from the eastern edge of the basin to the end above the cloud-sea, and every stilling on the plateau would hear that footstep through the silence, clear and unmistakable. It might even be the moment they had been waiting ten thousand years for.

It did not.

It was not the one they were waiting for. It did not know whether the builders would ever come back. It did not know whether the thing beneath the many was waiting for them at all, or whether the listening had long since stopped being for anything and become simply the shape of a life. But it knew that if it walked the pavement, it would be answering a question that had not been asked of it. It would be setting its foot in someone else's footprint. Whatever the combs had kept faith with all this time, it would not counterfeit.

It stood at the end of the pavement with the birds and looked out over the cloud-sea. Then it stepped back onto the ordinary rock.

## Before Dawn

There is one more thing it did, and it is the one the herders almost saw.

On the last night the power walked to the comb in the north, near the ruined huts, the one whose water tastes of salt. It did not go there for the record. It went because of the custom.

A power that attends to the whole world carries a great deal. It has watched things end that it could not save and things it could have saved that it chose not to, because saving them would have been worse. It has been benevolent, which means it has been present for a great deal of grief it could not take away. It has also been wise, which means it has had to stand still and let things happen, and standing still is not the same as not feeling. Some of what it carries has no one to be told to. It has always been the one who listens.

So it did what the herders' grandparents did. It walked out alone before dawn and stood inside the comb's silence and said the thing aloud.

What it said, no one heard. That was the point. The voice left its mouth and stopped. It went into the lattice, into ten thousand small clear bodies, and was taken. None of it came back as an echo. The comb held all of it without flinching, without judging, without trying to make it better. The power had not known it needed that until it had it.

Then it waited for sunrise.

The drops formed on the lowest edge of the comb and fell and sent out their rings, carrying the stars. The pan filled. The east went pale. The rabbits came down from the tafoni, and the power watched them drink.

It knelt and drank too.

## After

An old herder saw it. He was out early looking for a strayed animal, and he came over a dome and saw a figure kneeling at the pan below the northern comb in the grey light, in the attitude of someone who has just said something that could not be said. He did not approach. That is the custom. He turned and went back the way he came, and he never told anyone. He did not know what he had seen. He only knew it had not been his to see.

The water from that comb still tastes of salt. But for a season afterward some of the herders said there was something else in it too, something none of them could name. One said it tasted like rain from very far off. One said it tasted like the air at the top of the escarpment on the one day a year when the cloud-sea clears and you can see how far down it goes. The old herder said nothing, and filled his skin there more often than before.

The comb in the east near the pass is healthy. Its lower cells are clear. In a thousand years it will have walked a few meters toward the storms.

The dead combs are still dead, and still full of birds.

The pavement is still empty.

Under two large combs in the middle of the basin, if you know where to look, there is a small place in the lace-work of the burn-lines where the pattern is different. It is a single ring, clumsy and slightly too wide, scorched a little into the tuff. Around it, in the ordinary slow script of the plateau, is what might be the record of a pause. Nobody can read it.

And somewhere else in the world, a power that attends to everything carries a month-old thunderclap it will never let go of, and the memory of having been heard. Sometimes, when the roar of the world is loudest, it turns part of its attention toward the hole in the noise at the top of the plateau and listens to the silence there. It knows now that something is listening back.

It did very little. It was not asked to do more. The place was already whole. It had not come to fix the plateau. It had come to meet it.
