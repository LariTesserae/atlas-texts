# The Attention

## I. How it arrives

It does not arrive the way a trader arrives, poling in through the reeds at dawn. It has no hands to be noticed first. If it arrives at all, it arrives the way the mist does: from nowhere in particular, at roof height, all at once and very gently, so that nobody can say when it began.

What draws it is hard to name. Perhaps the ticking. Across a great distance, across whatever distance such a power attends from, there are many sounds a world makes, and most of them are loud: fire, argument, the breaking of things. Weepstone is one of the quiet ones. On a still evening its seaward edge makes a fine continuous sound like frost forming on a window, and something that listens to everything heard it, and turned.

It is benevolent, which means it wishes this place well. It is wise, which means it knows that wishing well and doing good are different things, and that the gap between them is where most harm is done.

So the first thing it does is nothing. It waits, and watches, and learns the place the way Ysse learned her channels: by walking them many times without touching anything.

## II. What it learns first

It learns the drips. It could count them in an instant, but it doesn't, because counting is not the same as knowing. It listens to each one fall into its pool until it can tell them apart, and it finds that this takes a long time even for a thing like itself. The notes are close together. Some differ only by the depth of the pool, which changes as the pool fills with stone, so every drip in Weepstone is very slowly going flat. A child's note at birth is not quite its note at death. The people know this and do not mind. They say a voice deepens with age, and so does a drip.

It learns the doors. It watches a small boy run the widow's rooms, in and out, touching each jamb, and it watches the widow listening from her hammock to his shouted greetings. She is counting them. When he misses one, she calls out the name of the room, and he runs back. The door is not the only thing being kept open.

It learns the wardens' justice, which is rough and mostly fair, and sometimes not. It watches Ysse turn the water from a thief, and it watches another warden, on the far side of the spur, turn the water from a family whose daughter refused his nephew. That family's wall stops growing. Their neighbors' walls swell toward them. In a generation they will have one room fewer. The power notes this. It will come back to it.

It learns the eels, and here it slows further, because the eels are the oldest minds in the town, and they are very strange. The eel in the Low Court has spent a hundred and forty years in a pool twelve paces across. In some deep, wordless way it still carries the knowledge of the open sea and a place of weed and warm water on the far side of an ocean, where it was meant to go. It has never gone. The call came, decades ago, and the eel turned and turned against the cistern wall until the call went quiet. What is left is not despair. The power looks carefully, because it would matter very much if it were despair. It is something closer to a vast, cold, patient attention to twelve paces of water. The eel knows every crack in its pool floor. It knows the footsteps of the court. It knows when a marriage is being spoken over it, though not what a marriage is. It is, in its way, content. It is also, in its way, a prisoner. Both are true, and the power sits with both for a long time.

Then it goes down.

## III. What is underneath

Where the springs come up, the power follows them back.

Down through the new stone, through the grandmothers holding up the floor, through the sealed rooms. It passes them without opening them. The ancestors in some of them are indeed sitting at tables, and one room holds nothing but a single rush basket, forgotten, sheathed, the size of a house now. Below that is the old spur, the grey knuckle of rock that was here before any town. Below that, the limestone.

The water moves through it in the dark, slowly, over centuries. The passages are narrow, then suddenly vast. There are lakes down here no light has ever touched, still and black and perfectly clear, and in them the spring-ghosts live in numbers the town could never imagine: clouds of them, whole drifting nations, pale and blind and entirely at home. The few that rise into the cisterns are the edges of a great, untroubled population. When the children cry over the ones that die in their shell cups, the wardens tell them that what dies in the light is only the part that came up.

The power finds that this is, in the way that matters, true. The ghosts below do not miss the ones that rise. The ones that rise were always a fringe, a spill, a small overflow of a life that goes on undisturbed in the dark. Nobody in Weepstone believes the wardens' consolation, and everyone says it. It turns out to be one of the truest things they say.

Then the power looks at the limestone itself, the rock the water is eating, and it understands something the town does not know about itself.

The limestone is made of bodies.

Unimaginably long ago, before the marsh and the sea at this height, this place was the floor of a warm shallow ocean. Something grew here: a reef, a thin living skin of small soft creatures, each building a cup of stone around itself, each dying into its cup, the next generation growing on top. Over ages they piled up, were buried, and were pressed into rock. The rock is full of them still: the ringed stems of sea-lilies, the fans of old corals, shells no bigger than grains of rice, packed together by the billion, holding up the world.

That is where the lime comes from. The water that rises into Weepstone's cisterns has spent centuries dissolving an ancient reef. It carries the old dead up out of the dark and lays them down again, grain by grain, as new stone around the new dead. The grandmothers of Weepstone are sheathed in the bodies of creatures that died before there were grandmothers anywhere.

The town is not *like* a reef. It is a reef growing on a reef, drinking the first one to build the second. The being that lives here, the thing that is people and eels and water and stone together, is the second life of something far older. It has been going quiet and rising again since before the sea was cold.

The power stays down in the dark a long time with that.

## IV. What it does not do

When it comes back up, it knows what it could do. The list is long, and each thing on it is kind, and it sets each one down.

**It could stop the lime.** A little change in the water, and the stone would stop growing. No more narrowing doors, no more hair stiffening against walls, no more kidney stones, no more swollen knuckles, no more rooms closing on lonely widows. Also no more town. The walls would stop where they are. The next generation would have nowhere to live. Their dead would lie in their niches and simply rot. Within a century the people would scatter to the marsh villages and become chalk-handed strangers with a strange habit of fidgeting, and the thing that lives here would be dead. The power does not do it.

**It could take away the fear of stillness.** That would be gentler, and in a way it is more tempting, because the fear is real and runs through every life here: in the hammock cords, the walked doors, the humming, the insult of *going quiet*. But the fear is not a mistake. Still water does seal. The fear is knowledge, worn into habit, and the habit is what keeps the town alive. The fidgeting, the hum, the children running the doors of the lonely: these are the fear, and they are also the town's kindness. Take one and you take the other. The power does not do it.

**It could open the sealed rooms.** They hold no suffering, only stone, and the families who wonder about them are better served by wondering. A grandmother at a table, sheathed, holding up the floor, is a story the town tells itself about being held. Opening the door would not improve the story. It leaves them closed.

**It could free the eels.** Each one could find its way down some flume and out to the marsh and the sea, to the far warm water it was meant for. The power thinks about this longest of all, because the eels are the one part of the being that did not choose. The children netted them as elvers. The call came and could not be answered.

In the end it does not free them either. Not because their captivity doesn't matter, but because it looked, and what it found in the old eels was not a thwarted longing still burning. The longing burned out decades ago and something else grew over it, the way stone grows over a reed and keeps its shape. An eel of a hundred and forty years, half-blind, crusted at the gills, would not survive the marsh, let alone the ocean. To send it now would be to give it a death dressed up as a freedom. The kindness it might once have wanted is no longer the kindness it can use.

The power does something smaller instead, which it will come to.

**It could tell them what they are.** A reef on a reef. The second life of an ancient sea. Their grandmothers wrapped in sea-lilies. It is true and it is beautiful, and the power is honestly tempted, because it loves true and beautiful things and wants to share them. But the people already know it. They know it in the way they have chosen to know it: *the town settling*, *the grandmothers holding up the floor*, *the stone's one fear, turned outward and handed down*. They have made their own account of themselves, and it is not wrong. It is only incomplete, the way every account is incomplete. A voice from the mist announcing the full truth would not add to their understanding so much as replace it. They would stop being the authors of their own story.

It keeps this truth. It will tell one person, later, the one person it belongs to.

**It could fix the wardens' justice.** This one it does not set down so easily. The family whose wall has stopped because a daughter said no is being wronged, plainly, and nothing about the being's nature requires that wrong. So the power weighs it and finds a limit on itself that it does not enjoy. If it intervenes openly, it becomes the town's new warden, a warden above the wardens, and the people will begin, sensibly, to take their disputes to the mist instead of to each other. That would be worse than one unjust man.

So it does the most it can do without taking the rake out of human hands. It lets Ysse notice. One dawn, on her walk, she hears a channel on the far side of the spur that is not hers running wrong. It is not a clog. It is a pinch, deliberate, the water turned from a wall that did nothing to deserve it. She hears it because the power has made sure the wind carries it, and that is all the power does. Wardens do not accuse, but they do talk to one another, and Ysse has walked her seven channels for forty years and is not afraid of anyone. What happens next is hers, and theirs. The power does not stay to see it. It has made the wrong audible to someone who cares, and it trusts the town to be a town.

## V. What it weighs

That leaves the pain.

Not the stillness and not the stone, but the pain itself. The stones in the kidneys. The knuckles that swell until a woman cannot hold a rake she has held for fifty years. The old who say they can feel the town in their bones, and mean it, and are not always speaking tenderly when they say it.

The power asks itself a hard question: *which part of this suffering is the being's shape, and which part is only suffering?*

The lime in the bones is the being's shape. It is how the people become the town, how bodies slowly agree to be stone. Their customs and dignity and way of dying are built around it. But the grinding hour-by-hour agony of a stone lodged in a young man's side, doubling him on the floor of a flume-house, carries no meaning. It teaches nothing the town doesn't already know. It is just pain.

So here, finally, the power acts, and it acts so quietly it could never be proven. The stones still form. The knuckles still swell. The old still stiffen toward stillness at the pace they always have. But the worst of it, the sharp, senseless, useless edge of it, is softer than it was. A healer from the marsh who came back after twenty years might think the people seemed less often in agony than she remembered, and she would be right, and she would never know why. The town does not know it has been given anything. That is how the power wants it. A gift that must be thanked puts a weight on the receiver. This one weighs nothing.

For the eels it does the same thing, and no more. The lime collars at their gills stop chafing so badly. The slow, deliberate scraping turns against the cistern floor, which the people have always watched with a kind of sorrow, become something the eels do from habit rather than need. Their long captivity, which the power cannot undo, is made a little gentler at its edges. It is not enough. The power knows it is not enough. It is what can be done without breaking something that cannot be unbroken.

## VI. The old man

Three walls from the Low Court, an old man has been sitting under his birth drip for nine days without answering.

The power goes to him before it does anything else. Before the pain, before the eels, before any of it, because this is the one place where something irreversible is happening right now. A benevolent thing does not let an irreversible thing happen without first making sure.

It goes in the only way that will not frighten him: through the drip. For a while the drip falls in a slightly different rhythm. His granddaughter, humming beside him, notices and falters, then goes on. The old man's clouded eyes move.

Here is what the power needs to know. Is he afraid? Is he in pain? Was this chosen, or was it pressed on him by a family tired of feeding him, or by a custom he couldn't find a way to refuse? Would he, if the way were open and nobody's opinion mattered, rather get up?

It asks without words, the way the eels are told news. He answers the way the eels answer: from underneath words, from the whole long shape of his life.

He is not afraid. He chose this. He chose it the way you choose to lie down when you have walked far enough, the way a tired child, allowed at last, finally stops rocking. There is some pain, and the power takes it. He does not want to get up. His granddaughter's humming is the best thing he has ever heard. He is ready to go quiet. He wants to.

So the power will not interfere. It stays with him to the end, as the family does, and nobody knows it is there.

Before the end it gives him the one thing it has been keeping. Down through the drip, into the part of him that is already nearly stone, it tells him what it learned in the dark. The ground under the town is made of an older town. The water that is wrapping him has carried the dead of an ancient warm sea up out of the rock to hold him. He is going quiet into the arms of a reef that went quiet before there were people, and he will hold up his grandchildren's floor the way those small creatures have held up the whole spur. The ticking at dusk is not only his grandmother turning over in her sleep. It is everything, all the way down.

The old man was a rope-twister, not a warden, not special. He did not know there had ever been a sea here. His face, the last time his granddaughter can still call it a face, looks as though he has just been told a joke, a very old one, which he suspects he already knew.

He is the only one the power tells. It is his to take into the stone, and the stone is where it belongs.

## VII. The hand

The power has one thing left to do, and it is not for Weepstone. It is for itself, or for the two of them together.

It has watched long enough to understand the town's first rite: the crawling child's cut palm pressed to the wall for a hundred drips, *so the stone knows the child and the child knows the stone*. It understands that for all its attention, it has been a visitor, a trader poling in through the reeds and looking at the hands. It has known the town. The town has not known it.

So for one evening it takes a shape. Nothing grand, nothing that would draw a crowd. It is a small figure in a rush cape at the back of the Low Court at dusk, shifting its weight politely from foot to foot the way people here do, so nobody will think it strange. It puts its palm against the newest crust on the court wall, the sugary glittering kind rimmed with tiny crystal teeth, and presses.

The stone opens it. It bleeds, or does whatever it does that answers to bleeding. It holds its hand there for the space of a hundred drips while the mist comes back over the roofs, and it feels what the being feels: cold, wet, very slightly rough, and underneath that the patience, the sense of a thing very carefully making room for itself by closing around everything it touches.

It lets the stone begin to close around its hand. Just a little, a grey film at the edges of the palm. It lets itself be taken a little, the way everything here is taken. That is the only way to be known by this place, and it wants to be known by it. It is not trying to be worshipped or thanked or remembered by name. It wants to be one more thing in Weepstone the stone has touched.

Then it takes its hand away, before the film can harden, because it is a visitor after all, and it has to keep moving.
