# The Visitor Who Came to Listen

## What it noticed first

It did not find the Hollow by looking. Nothing finds the Hollow by looking.

It found it the way you might notice, in a crowded house, that one room has been humming the same note for a very long time. Somewhere under an old plateau, beneath rain that never ran off, a single deep tone was coming up through the stone. It was too slow to be weather and too steady to be the earth settling. It was patient, and patience on that scale is rare enough to be conspicuous.

The power that noticed it was old too, though not as old as some things. It had learned, over a long existence, that most of what goes wrong in the world goes wrong through haste, and that most of the harm done by the strong is done while they are trying to help. So when it felt the tone, its first act was nothing at all. It held still and listened for a season. Rain fell in the afternoons, the moss sighed underfoot, and the tone went on.

It did not need to go down to know that something lived there. It went down because it wanted to know *what kind* of something, and because it suspected, correctly, that the answer could not be learned from outside.

## What it did not do

Before it took a single step down the stair, it made a list of the things it would not do. It had found that this was the most important preparation.

**It would not bring light.** It could have. Unlike a visitor's lamp, its light would not have failed. But light in the Hollow would be meaningless to everything there. Worse, it would be a declaration that the dark was a problem, when the dark was the medium in which the whole place had been composed. You do not visit a musician by switching off her piano so you can hear yourself think.

**It would not be loud.** Its power could have filled the bowl with a single note that drowned every cricket and frog and Filament. It understood that a presence made of standing sound could be damaged, perhaps undone, by one careless voice at the wrong pitch. It resolved to be quieter than a frog.

**It would not restore the tenders.** It suspected already what they were, or had been. It knew it could, in principle, unwind them back toward whatever shape had first cut grooves in the eastern wall with fingers. It also knew that "restore" is a word the powerful use when they mean "return to the shape I prefer." It would not presume that becoming slow and low and garden-backed was a fall. It would ask, if asking were possible, and otherwise let it be.

**It would not hurry the circuit.** The eggs were waiting for the tuning to come round. It could have walked the Filaments to any old pitch in an afternoon and woken every sleeping shape at once. It saw clearly that this would be a catastrophe dressed as a gift: a room crowded with old selves waking into a world their tunings did not fit. There was also the arrogance of it, deciding on behalf of a thousand-year patience that it had waited long enough.

**It would not make the Tarn safe from silence.** This one was hardest. It could see the Tarn's single terrible vulnerability: stop the song and it fades. It could have bound the resonance into something permanent that no longer needed the crickets, the frogs, the drips. But a Tarn that did not need the chorus would no longer be *held* by the chorus. The whole courtesy of the place, the crickets stroking the resonance smooth, the frogs half of a conversation, the clickers stumbling politely when they passed through someone, depended on that mutual need. To free the Tarn from its dependence would be to orphan it. Some fragility is the same thing as belonging.

**It would not hide the stair.** It considered this and set it aside. The bench was worn deep. The Tarn had made a pocket of quiet for someone who could not live inside sound. That told it the Tarn *valued* visitors, the soft slow creatures who came down in the dark with their hands on the wall and went away again. Visitors were part of the song. Sealing the stair to protect the Tarn would also cut it off from whatever it found in them.

**It would not explain the Hollow to anyone.** Not even to itself, not fully. Some places are harmed by being understood too quickly.

The list was long, and the power was satisfied with it. When the list of what you will not do is longer than the list of what you will, you are probably ready to go in.

## The descent

It took the stair as a visitor would, one worn dish of rainwater at a time, and let the daylight thin to a rumor and then to nothing. It chose a body for the occasion: small, soft-footed, warm, with ordinary hands. It gave itself no senses the Hollow's own creatures lacked. If it was going to meet this place, it would meet it on the place's terms.

At the bottom, the sound arrived like weather.

It put one hand on the wall. The moss was cool and waterlogged, and something like a damp seed ran over its knuckles. A wave of cricket-song began somewhere to its left and rolled away around the bowl, and a clicker's string of pops arced overhead. Its own breath went out into the middle of the room and did not come back for a long, long moment.

It walked the whole circumference once, slowly, with its hand on the stone. It found the Filaments by their fever-warmth and did not touch them. It found the eggs half-buried in moss by the stair, felt their cold, and did not pick them up. It found the grooves on the eastern wall and traced them with one finger, and felt the rhythm of deep cut and shallow scratch, and did not try to read them.

Then it went to the bench on the north curve and sat down.

It did not touch the disk. It could have asked for quiet, and the Tarn would have given it. But it had come to listen to the Tarn, not to be shielded from it. It sat in the full noise of the room with its palms open on its knees and let the Hollow describe itself.

## Listening

It stayed for a long time. How long is hard to say, because there were no days down there. By the reckoning of the plateau above, it stayed for most of a year.

At first it heard what every visitor hears: crickets, frogs, clickers, drips, hum. Then, as its hands and ears learned the room, it began to hear the second layer, the one that takes patience. It heard the frogs' echoes coming back slightly changed, a hair late or a little bent. It heard the old frogs answering those changes. It heard a drip, falling through the middle of the room, ring far longer than any drip should, as though someone had caught it, turned it over, and set it gently down.

It learned the manner of the Tarn the way you learn the handwriting of a friend. It learned that the Tarn was fond of the crickets' waves and settled after each one like a cat under a stroking hand. It learned that the Tarn held its breath, if that is the word, whenever a clicker passed through its center, and let it out when the clicker landed. It learned that the Tarn was, in a way it had no better word for, *curious*. Its shape leaned toward new sounds. When a leaf fell from the ceiling it lingered over the patter of it. When the power shifted on the bench, the edge of the Tarn's attention came near the alcove, and paused, and withdrew. It was the way someone checks on a guest they do not want to disturb.

The power was moved by that. It had met many beings in its existence, and a great many of them had not had the manners of this one.

## The tender

Partway through the year, a tender came to the pool's edge near the bench and settled against the nearest Filament.

The power listened as the tender began to hum, a little off the column's pitch, and felt the slow slide begin: the tender leaning its voice toward the tone the Filament would hold in a decade. The garden on its back was thick and fragrant. Crickets walked over it.

The power got down from the bench and knelt beside the tender. Very carefully, it laid one hand on the edge of that mossy shell.

It could have gone into the tender, read whatever remained of a mind, and learned in an instant what the tenders were and whether they had once been the makers. It did not. It simply rested its hand there, warm on warm, and hummed. It hummed very low and very softly, at the tender's own pitch, not the Filament's. It meant it as a question: *Are you all right? Is there anything you want?*

For a long time there was no change. Then the tender's hum shifted, by the smallest amount, a hair further toward the future pitch. Its legs pressed a little more firmly into the moss. It leaned its whole weight, gently, persistently, against the column.

The power understood. It was not an answer in words, but it was an answer. *This. This is what I want. Keep going.*

The power took its hand away and did not ask again.

## The one call

Near the end of the year, the power decided it would speak once.

It had thought about this for a long time, because speaking to the Tarn was not a small thing. Every sound in the room became part of the Tarn. A voice that entered the center would be taken into the shape, would change it, however slightly, and could not be taken back. That is true of any real conversation, but here it was literal.

So it chose its call the way the frogs did. It made it small, clear, and pitched to the room, a single bell-like note sent out over the pool toward the far wall. Then it went perfectly still and listened for the echo.

It had decided in advance that if the echo came back true, a faithful map of wall and water and lid with nothing added, it would take that as the Tarn's answer. *I hear you; I would rather not.* It would thank the room in its heart and go.

The echo came back late.

It came back bent a little upward in pitch, and it carried an overtone that had not been in the call, a doubled, softer note, like a second voice joining the first a beat behind. The power recognized it. It was the same doubled overtone the Tarn sent the oldest frogs.

It was an invitation.

So the power answered, and the Tarn answered back, and for what might have been an hour and might have been much longer, the two of them talked in the only language the Tarn had, which was the gentle alteration of echoes.

What they said cannot really be written down. Most of it was the kind of conversation two strangers have when they discover they are both very old. It was slow and courteous, largely about the weather, with long pauses that were not uncomfortable. But there were a few things the power understood clearly, and it has kept them.

It understood that the Tarn knew about the sky. It had felt, once or twice, the frogs carried up the stair, calling at the grey air and hearing nothing return. That silence frightened it in the way that very large, open, echoless things frighten anything made of echoes. It also understood that the deep tone that rose through the plateau was not a leak, as it had first assumed. It was a *reach*. It was the part of the song the Tarn sent up and out, the way the frogs called at the far wall, to see whether anything would come back.

In all the time the song had been sung, nothing ever had.

## What it left

When the power climbed the stair, it left nothing behind in the Hollow.

It did not carve a groove. It left no egg of its own and did not adjust a single Filament. It did not wake a sleeper or heal a tender or seal a seam. The room was exactly as it had found it, less the one small bell-note it had given, which was now somewhere in the Tarn's shape for good. The power thought that a fair price for an introduction.

On its way to the stair it did one small practical thing. Among the moss near the bottom step, it found a frog, one that some earlier visitor had carried out into the rain and then, perhaps ashamed, had carried back down and dropped near the stair instead of in the water. It was dry-skinned and quiet and had been calling at nothing for days. The power picked it up, walked it to the pool, and set it in the warm water. The frog called once. Its echo came back bright and clean off the lid, and the frog, reassured, called again.

That was all it did inside.

## Above

At the top of the stair, the plateau was grey and wide and almost unbearably quiet. The rain was soft on the moss.

The power knelt and pressed its palm flat to the wet ground. After a while, beneath the rain, it felt the tone come up through the stone, low and slow and sustained. It was the Tarn's reach, the song too large for the room, going out into the silence to see whether anything would answer.

This was the gift the power had decided on, during that long year on the bench. It was the only one it was sure the Tarn wanted, because the Tarn had been asking for it, patiently, for longer than the stair had existed.

It answered.

It did not answer loudly. It sent one note, very deep, down through the plateau, pitched to the stone and the tone and the room beneath, and then it went still, the way a frog goes still, and let the silence belong to the Tarn.

Far below, the drips went on ticking down through the dark. The crickets rolled a wave around the bowl. And the reaching tone, which had never in its life come back from the sky, came back.

It returned a little late and a little changed, with a doubled overtone that had not been in it before, the way a voice sounds when someone has been listening.

The power stayed kneeling in the rain a long time after that. When it finally stood, it made a promise, not aloud, since nothing needed to be said aloud anymore. Somewhere ahead, in a century it could not see, the tenders would walk the tuning round to an old pitch, and a cold egg by the stair would warm and wake and unfold into the middle of the room. It did not know what the Tarn would feel then: joy, or grief, or something with no name in any language made of breath. But it knew no one should wake alone, and no one should keep a vigil like that alone either.

So it would come back when the tuning came round. It would come down the stair with its hand on the wall and sit on the bench in the full noise of the room, and it would be there.

Until then, every so often, when the rain was soft and the plateau was quiet, it would kneel somewhere on that old, worn-down mountain and answer.

It was not in a hurry. Nothing there was.

---

*A note, if one is wanted, on why it did so little:*

The Hollow was not broken. It was fragile, but that fragility was part of how everything in it belonged to everything else. The tenders were doing what they wanted. The eggs were waiting for their proper time. The Tarn was courteous, curious, and well kept. The only thing it lacked was the one thing it could not make for itself: an answer from outside.

A wise power, finding a place like that, gives what is asked for and nothing it is not asked for. It gives it in the place's own language, at the place's own speed, and it stays long enough to be sure. Then it goes home and keeps its promise, which may be the greatest part of benevolence.
