# The Attention

## How it came

It did not come up on the cable. That would have taken nine hours, and it is not the kind of thing that rides in ore buckets.

It would be pretty to say it arrived like fallout, carried on the great wind, dropping out of the air where the gap slows everything down. That isn't quite right either. It had always been somewhere, the way the mountain had always been somewhere before it split. It simply turned, the way a spider on a lamp-lit wall turns its two great eyes toward a moving hand. The Seam became the thing it was looking at.

It arrived on a still night, and the mountain was creaking. So the first thing it did in the Seam was what everything in the Seam does on such nights.

It listened.

## What it heard

It heard the deep grinding far below, like ship's timbers, and it understood the sound better than the surveyors did. It knew why the benchmark climbed more than it should. It knew, to the season, when the mountain would stop breathing in.

It heard four thousand people holding still in their hanging boxes. It heard the particular rasp of lungs that had been failing for two years, and a man in a box on the western wall who had not told anyone. It heard the three brothers at the cistern: one awake, two asleep, all three dreaming of the same crate breaking.

It heard Grandmother Red on a span in the dark, lamp turned low, feeling the hum through her boots. It heard the red band on her wrist, which is to say it heard the memory the band was there to hold, of a bridge swinging shut like a door in fog.

It heard the children's thumbs on their lamp switches. It could read the finger-language, all of it, including the signs the adults never learned. It paused for a long time over the sign for *the moment a bridge goes from blue to yellow*, because it had seen a great many worlds and had never seen that moment given a word before.

It heard a hundred million small lives go still on the walls, the spiders with their eyes turned all one way. It heard the choughs settle in nests of stolen copper. It heard a seed in a scrap of felt against a girl's ribs, not yet split, deciding.

It heard the company too. It heard the unnamed mineral and the ledgers kept in the valley, and the rising benchmark written down, known, and not spoken of.

Then it heard below. It followed the pale bats down past the last ledge and past the lowest platform, down to where the walls come together into a darkness no one has measured. Nothing in the Seam had ever measured it. The attention measured it without effort.

The lamp-man was right. The fur was damp.

## The pool

There is a pool.

It is warm, as the children said. The rising rock is hot rock. Water that has been trapped in the old intrusion since before anything had eyes weeps slowly out of the stone and gathers in a basin wider than the town. The air above it is thick and wet, and it smells of minerals and of something like bread. Things live on its shore that have never seen light: pale crawling things with long feelers that taste the air, slow white shapes in the water, mats of something neither plant nor mold that glisten in no light at all.

The fallout reaches them. It comes very thin by the time it gets this far, a drift of moth wings and stunned midges, but it comes. The bats bring more, dropped half-eaten in flight. And the town's losses come.

The attention saw the houses. Three were wedged upright between the closing walls. One lay on its side at the water's edge with the felt rotted away and the steel box furred with pale growth. Carts lay rusting. Tools lay scattered. A child's seed tin had burst open on the stone.

It saw the dead, and it looked at them longest.

They had been given down wrapped in sacking and felt, with something from their lives tucked inside. They had landed, and the dark had received them the way it received everything, slowly and without waste. The pale things had come. It was not horrible. The attention had seen many ways the dead are returned to the world, and this was one of the gentler ones. A band-reader who died in the third year had gone down with a painted sleeve section in her hands. It lay on the stone now, blue band uppermost, in a place where no one would ever read it.

There was nothing here to fix. The old community in the dark had been sealed away for longer than the mountain had been a mountain. Eleven years ago its roof had split open and let in sky. It had adjusted to that. It was adjusting still, as the town above was.

The attention considered whether to let the town know for certain. It could put the truth into a dream, or make one lamp at the bottom burn bright enough to see from the lowest platform.

It decided not to. The town gives its dead down into a darkness it does not understand, and the not-understanding is part of the giving. The children had already guessed nearly everything. It left the rest to them.

## What it could have done

A benevolent power is tempted, and a wise one takes the temptations seriously instead of pretending not to feel them. So it laid them out, the way the children lay out moth wings in rows.

**It could stop the mountain.** It could hold the walls where they were, so that no span ever went red and no house ever tore loose, and the painted bands stayed blue forever.

It looked at this and saw what the mountain's rising actually was. The force that would close the Seam was the same force that had pushed the ore up out of the deep and split the roof over the pool. It was the same force that made the benchmark climb, and it was the one that had opened the gap. The town was not built against the mountain's movement. It was built out of it. To hold the walls would be to hold a continent in mid-gesture, and the Seam would stop being the thing that lived there. It would become a museum of itself, with the telescoping joints rusting open and the band-readers reading bands that never changed. Grandmother Red would feel nothing through her boots at all.

**It could bring water.** This was the one it wanted most. It had heard the split lips and the cracked knuckles. It had heard a child believe that a full bucket could buy a house. It could make it rain in the gap.

Then it followed the rain forward. At that altitude water that fell as liquid would not stay liquid. It would run into the fresh fractures of the young stone, freeze there, and expand. The walls would be frost-wedged apart from inside, not by centimeters but in slabs. Every bridge would ice. Every compressed-air line would seal. The Seam was habitable because it was dry. The dryness hurt the people in it, but it was also what let them live there at all. The attention did not decide that their thirst was beautiful, and it did not decide that suffering was the price of meaning. It saw that this particular mercy would kill them. That is a different thing, and it is important not to confuse the two.

**It could make the seeds grow.** It could let a sprout against a child's skin keep going: root into the felt, leaf in the dim light, a small tree in the Seam.

It looked at the children's saying: *you haven't really been born here until you have grown something with your own body and watched it die.* It understood that the children were not being morbid. They were telling the truth about their home and practicing the Seam's one great skill, which is letting go. To break the rite with a miracle would teach them something false. It would tell them the world makes exceptions for people who love it enough. The attention loved them too much to teach them that.

**It could deal with the company.** It considered this without any gentleness at all. The company knew the benchmark was climbing and said nothing. It named four thousand people on its contracts and did not name the mineral they dug. The attention could have emptied the vein overnight, or broken the cable, or put the ledgers on every table in the valley.

In the end it decided that some wrongs belong to the people they are done to. Righting them is part of what those people are owed, and taking that from them, even kindly, is another kind of theft. The attention also knew something the company did not. The company's silence depended on no one listening, and it had set that silence in the one town on earth where everyone listens.

So it put the temptations away. That did not leave it with nothing to do. It left it with the right size of thing to do.

## What it did

### It stayed

For a long time it only stayed. It listened on still nights with everyone else. It stood in the noon light when the choughs rose, felt the whole town become one creature for the length of a shift change, and did not add itself to that creature. It watched the springtails burst up like smoke. It watched a spider on a windowsill turn its eyes to the man who fed it, and it confirmed that the man was not wrong.

A power that acts before it has listened long enough is not wise, however good its intentions. So it listened for a season.

### It let the true warnings come first

The Seam already had its warnings: dust sifting from overhead, the choughs lifting, the spiders all running upward at once, the change in the hum of a bridge an hour before the paint showed yellow. These were true signs, and the town had learned to read them.

The attention did not invent any new warnings. It did something much smaller. It let the true ones arrive a little earlier. Dust sifted a breath sooner. The hum in a span shifted a minute before it would have. The spiders ran upward a few seconds ahead of the stone.

Spans still went red. Houses still tore loose. The Seam is built to let go, and it kept letting go. But fewer people were on the spans when they went. In the following year the lowest platform went dark for the dead less often than it had in any year before. Nobody noticed this as a pattern, because people don't notice the deaths that don't happen.

Grandmother Red noticed something. She told no one, but she began to stop on her spans more often, and to rest her palm, not her knuckles, flat on the steel railing, the way you would lay a hand on the flank of an animal you trusted.

### It kept one lamp lit

This was its only indulgence, and it thought hard before allowing itself even this.

In the second autumn a house tore loose from the eastern wall during a bad night. No one was in it. The owner was on shift, and it had been her own knuckles, pressed to the stone, that told her to go. The house fell into the dark. For a few nights afterward, those who stood on the lowest platform with every lamp turned off could see it: very faint, very far down, a single warm spark.

The woman whose house it was stood there every night.

Ordinarily the spark goes out when the battery dies, and that is how the town knows. The attention let this one burn a little longer than a battery should. It was not a lie, and not a sign or a message. The woman had been given more time than she expected with the last light of her home, and she was allowed to use it. On the ninth night she stopped coming. She had finished with it. Only then did the attention let the lamp go dark.

### It told them when

This was the one large thing, and it had known from the first night that it would do it.

The mountain would breathe out. The children were right and the miners who laughed were wrong. It would not happen soon, and it would not happen in a single night the way the crack had opened. But it would happen, and when the walls came together, anything still hanging between them would be given down whether it was ready or not.

The town's whole life was built on reading the Seam. The one thing it could not read was the Seam's ending, because that sign would come too late to be read at all.

So, years before it was needed, the attention began to tell them. It spoke in the only language the Seam trusted: its own.

It started with the benchmark. Every year the surveyors marked it higher by more than it should be. Then one year it stopped. It came in flat, exactly where it had been the year before. The company wrote the number down and said nothing, as it always had. But a surveyor's apprentice who had grown up signing with the children told the children, and then everyone knew. The mountain had finished breathing in.

Next it was the bands. Over the following months every painted sleeve on every bridge in the Seam began to creep, very slowly and all together, toward yellow. No single span jumped. They moved like a tide coming in, and the band-readers, who had spent a decade feeling the town's width through their boots, felt the whole town narrowing. The walls were no longer drifting apart. They were beginning, by millimeters, to come home.

Last came the living warning. On one still night, with the mountain silent, every spider on every lamp-lit wall of the Seam turned its eyes upward at the same moment and began to climb. They did not scatter the way they do before a rockfall. They climbed steadily, all of them, in a vast shimmering sheet toward the rim, and they kept climbing until dawn. At midnight, with no light anywhere, the choughs rose from both walls in their thousands, circled once in the dark with that soft vast rush like breath, and did not come back to their nests.

The children made a new sign that night. It is a slow closing of the two open hands until the fingertips touch. Among Seam people it still means two things: *it's time*, and *I'm here*.

### The leaving

The company said the readings were a survey error. It said the bands were drifting because of a batch of bad paint. It said the contracts had fourteen months to run.

The town did not argue. It didn't need to. For eleven years it had not listened to the company about anything that mattered. It had listened to the mountain.

It turned out that a town built so it can let go is very good at leaving. Bridges telescope, so they can be taken in. Houses bolted to one wall only can be unbolted. Nobody had built anything meant to last longer than a season, so there was nothing anyone could not bear to leave. The cable ran day and night for seven weeks, nine hours a round trip, the iron buckets swinging out from the rim into the gale full of people instead of ore. The oldest lungs went first. The man on the western wall who had told no one was carried down in the third week. He lived another six years in the valley, breathing air thick enough to drink.

The children went next, each with a tin of wings and a scrap of felt.

The three brothers opened the cistern on the last day and gave its water to the crews still working. The eldest said afterward it was the first time in eleven years he had set a crate down on stone without listening to it.

The band-readers went last. Grandmother Red read the final span herself, the one nearest the rim, and cut it loose from the far side after the last crew crossed. She did not ride it down this time. She stood at the lip in the wind and watched it swing against the western wall like a door closing, and then she let it go.
