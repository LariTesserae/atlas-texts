# The Visitor

## Attention

It did not come through the gap. Nothing that size would fit, and it had no wish to be the thing that finally made the seam too wide.

It came the way attention comes to a room when someone stops talking. The pocket was already full, so it took up no space. It made itself less than a springtail, less than the warmth in the clinker, and it waited.

It had learned long ago that the first thing to do with a place is nothing. Most harm done by powers is done in the first hour, by those who arrive already knowing what is needed. So it watched a thousand cycles. It counted teeth on the needles and got the same number the ceiling did. It watched the reader ride the needle up and lay its egg and ride it down. It watched the snails carry rivets to the liverwort. It watched the engineer rise to the meniscus a moment before the iron ticked, and felt the hush go out across the wall, row by row.

It took care not to be counted. The reader never marked it. The crickets' antennae passed through the place where it was and found nothing to report. This was its first courtesy: to be present without becoming part of the works, because the works hires whatever falls in, and it had not come to be hired.

---

## What It Knew

A power like this knows things a pocket cannot. It carried four of them, and they were not all kind.

**The first was the lagging.** The grey fibrous crust hanging off the pipe in scales was asbestos, applied by hand in the years before anyone admitted what it was. The men who fitted it had breathed it, and a good number of the names that once hung on the board by the door had ended in hospital beds, drowning slowly in their own lungs. The works had been warm, and it had been punctual, and it had also killed people. The power held this alongside everything else, and it did not let the beauty of the place cancel it.

**The second was the yard.** It had been sold. A housing association had bought it, and there were drawings: forty homes, two terraces, a shared garden, parking. In three years the machines would come and break the slabs, and the seam would be found or destroyed. Probably both.

**The third was the pipe.** It ran east under the town toward nothing still standing. The power followed it, which no one else had ever done. It went under roads and a canal and a closed school. It passed the footings of other works long gone, a foundry and a dye house and a railway shed. Where it ended, the power was careful not to be sure of what it found.

**The fourth was the tag.** The power could read the worn letter. It read it, and what it read will not be written here. A name that has been stepped around for this long has earned its privacy.

---

## What It Would Not Do

Having looked, it considered. A power that can do almost anything owes it to the world to think through what it will not do.

**It would not bring the men back.** It could not do that honestly, and it would not do it dishonestly. The men were not missing from the pocket the way a part is missing from an engine. They had lives before the whistle and after it. Many were glad to see the works close even as it broke them, and their grandchildren had no wish to stand in rows. Nostalgia for a place of work is often the grief of people who were never asked what they would rather have done. The power did not share that grief, though it understood it.

**It would not end the works.** It could have. One thought, and the pipe would go cold, the engineer would sink, the woodlice would scatter to the clinker and live ordinary woodlouse lives. It asked itself whether that would be a liberation, and it looked hard at the answer. The woodlice were not enslaved. They were warm and fed and wet every eleven minutes, and they faced the insulator because that is where the hush came from. That is not so different from why anything faces anything. The works had begun as a ghost of the men. It was not a ghost any longer. It had hired its own crew and grown its own engineer out of lime and rust, and it had become a new small world that happened to remember an old one. You do not free a living thing by taking away its shape.

**It would not wake the engineer further.** It could have given the small clear body the capacity to grieve, or to ask what the tag meant, or to know that it was being watched. It declined. The engineer was not a lesser thing waiting to be raised into a greater one. It was complete at the size of barley, with two gears for a heart, and it was growing at its own rate toward whatever the double ring of rivets was a foundation for. To hurry it would be to replace its plan with the power's own. The seam opened a hair a season. That was the right speed for a thing that builds itself one tooth at a time.

**It would not free the toad without asking.** This was the one it thought about longest, because the toad was the one creature in the pocket whose arrangement looked most like a trap. The toad fell in. It is too large to leave. So the power asked it, in the way a toad can be asked, which is by paying attention to its body across many hours. It watched the eyes go half-shut with relief when the iron ticked. It watched the throat settle. It felt the warmth of the ledge, the regularity of the food, the absence of herons, cars, cold, and drought. The toad was very old, older than toads usually get, and it had been kept alive by exactly this. Given the choice between the yard and the ledge, it chose the ledge. The power respected that and left it where it sat.

**It would not tell the world.** A pocket like this, reported, becomes a specimen. There would be scientists first, then people who wanted to feel something near it, then a railing and a sign. Some wonders survive being known. This one would not.

---

## What It Did

It did four things. None of them would be visible to anyone who did not already know the pocket well.

### The Fungus

The grey-white threads in the lagging were already doing something the power approved of. Where they laced through the fibres they bound them, wrapping each loose strand in a sheath of living thread and cementing it to its neighbours. Given thirty years, the fungus would have locked the whole crust into a soft, wet, harmless felt that could shed nothing into the air.

The power asked it to hurry. That was all. It gave nothing new and bent no nature. It simply offered a little more of what the fungus already wanted: a few degrees of steadiness in the warmth, a slight tilt in the condensate's path so the lagging stayed wet. By the end of the season the scales no longer hung loose. They had become a pale, spongy bark, and the pinhead mushrooms stood up out of it in their thousands.

It did this for whoever would come next. *You would have to fall in, or be small.* Someone small would fall in someday. The power had no intention of preventing that, but it would not let that child breathe what the men had breathed.

### The Drawings

It did not stop the houses. Forty homes is forty families with somewhere warm to sleep, and the power weighed that against one wardrobe-sized room of woodlice and found no contest. Benevolence that protects a wonder by keeping people from their homes has confused itself with taste.

But drawings can be adjusted. A surveyor came in autumn with a ground-penetrating radar cart. On her second pass she found the old steam main, which was on no map she had. She was a careful person, and careful people are easy to help. Because it was cheaper and safer not to build over a live main of unknown origin, she recommended moving Terrace B four metres north. That put the shared garden, instead of a foundation, directly over the seam.

In the final plan the seam lies under a bed of lawn at the edge of a gravel path. There will be a bench nearby. On cool mornings a thread of vapour will rise from the grass every eleven minutes, and a few children will notice it, and most adults will not. One child, some years from now, will lie on her stomach with her ear to the ground and hear a sound like paper lifted by ten thousand hands. She will tell no one, because it will be hers.

The power thought this a fair outcome for everyone involved.

### The End of the Pipe

The pipe ran east toward nothing still standing, and the power followed it to the end. What it found there it does not claim to understand. It reports only what it saw.

At the end there was no boiler. There was a room in a care home, on the second floor, and a bed, and in the bed a man who had not stood up in four years. He was ninety-six. His hands lay on the blanket. Every eleven minutes, near enough, his right foot moved under the sheet, one slow flex, the way an old man's foot taps a beat he heard a moment ago.

The staff had noticed it and written it down as a tic.

The power did not decide whether the pipe fed on him or he on the pipe, or whether the thread between them was heat or habit or something with no name. It was not sure the question had a single answer. He had been the youngest man on the board when the works closed. It closed in the middle of a shift, in the afternoon, by letter. The whistle that should have blown at six never blew. Everyone simply went home. As far as his body was concerned, he had never clocked off.

The power sat with him through a night. Near four in the morning, which is when the old most often lift toward the surface of their sleep, it gave him the one thing it had decided to give.

It did not give him a cure, since there was nothing to cure; he was dying of having lived a long time. It did not show him the works, since he did not need to know about the woodlice. It gave him a whistle: six o'clock, long and flat, the sound he had waited to hear for sixty years.

In the dream he walked to the board by the door and reached for his tag. It was not there. He stood a moment, puzzled. Then someone, he could not have said who, told him it was all right: the tag was being looked after, and his post was kept. He nodded the way a man nods when a foreman he trusts has told him something, and he walked out through the gate into an evening he had not seen since he was nineteen.

His foot did not move again. He died eleven days later, in the afternoon, without distress, with his daughter holding his hand.

### The Tag

This was the fourth thing, and it was the power's decision to do nothing.

It had considered filling the post. It could have stood in the blade of light at midday, on the worn *4*, and let the works count it. For one pulse the vacancy would have closed, and the engineer would have turned to face something that was there.

It did not. The post was not a gap waiting for a part. It was the shape of someone. A works that keeps a place open for a man who is gone is doing what any living thing does when it loves what made it, even if it has no word for love and no capacity for grief. Filling the space would have been a correction. The power had not come to correct.

So it left the tag in the clinker. It left the light to cross it every day. It left the snails to step around it.

---

## Afterward

In the pocket, nothing changed that anyone could name.

The pipe kept breathing, and it has not gone cold. Whatever fed it did not end with one man, or did not end at all, or had long since become the works' own affair. The engineer went on rising a moment before each breath. The woodlice went on lifting. The toad's throat kept the time a fraction late. The seam went on opening a hair a season, and each new hair of room was filled at once.

There was one thing, and only the reader could have recorded it.

On the afternoon the old man died, the needle on the gauge made its usual climb during the pulse and then went further. It did not go far, less than a graduation. It rose just past the top of the swing the reader had been recording for years, held there for the length of a breath, and settled back.

The reader rode it up and touched its foreleg to the glass at the mark. It laid an egg there, one egg, alone, above the long line of the graph. Then it rode the needle down and took up the count as before.

When that egg hatched, the young did not climb the needle like the others. It went to the rim of the dial, where the old husks are packed against the brass like ledgers on a shelf, and it settled there among them. It was not thinning, and it was not retired. It simply stayed, in the place where records are kept.

The power watched that, and it did not know what it meant, and it did not need to.

Then it went. Leaving took no more space than arriving had. It passed up through the mesh of antennae without being reported, and through the moths on the lamp without lifting them. It rose through the gap with a thread of warm vapour into a cool morning in the yard, where a surveyor's pink paint marked the line of the future garden path.

It had not been hired and it had not been counted. On its way out, without quite deciding to, it stepped around the number.
