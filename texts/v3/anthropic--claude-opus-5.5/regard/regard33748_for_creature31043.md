# The Visitor

## Arrival

It comes the way a storm comes, from above and without light. For the first few moments of its attention, it is nearly a disaster.

A power of that size broadcasts. It does not mean to. Attention on that scale has weight, and on the plateau weight is charge. Across the basin where it first settles its regard, the fur of every grazer stands straight out, glowing violet. The moth rivers stall and eddy, confused by a new contour in the field. Along three rises the spade-headed hunters lift their heads at once and turn toward the enormous new presence, pits working. In a hollow beneath a tube mouth, a mother puts her hand over her child's chest and presses down, *go to rock, go to rock*, because something is lit up on the ground like a burning house, and she does not know what it is.

The visitor feels her hand press down. It feels the child's small heart trying to slow.

It understands, before it understands anything else, that it has walked into a room where everyone is holding their breath, and that it is making noise.

So the first thing it does in the Anvil Highlands is the first thing every highland child is taught. It goes to rock.

This is harder for it than it would be for a child. It has never needed to be uninteresting before. It slows whatever in it corresponds to breath. It softens whatever corresponds to muscle. It lets its attention settle toward the ground's own slow pulse, the hum under everything, until its field smooths and thins and becomes indistinguishable from stone. It takes a long time, perhaps a whole hand by the local clock. When it is done, the grazers' fur lies down. The moths find their line again. The hunters lower their heads and forget. The mother lifts her hand from her child's chest and waits a little longer, to be sure.

The visitor has learned one thing already. Here, presence is a kind of violence, and the courteous thing is to become part of the hum.

## Looking, and Not Looking

It wants to see. That is its habit. It is used to taking in a world whole.

Here, seeing is the wrong sense. So it learns the other one. It lays itself against the ground the way the people do, palms flat, and reads. It feels the tides of charge running between the hot pockets and the cool ones. It feels the slow gravity of the iron creeping toward the seams, and the smelters as dense warm knots, and the grazers as nervous flickers. It feels the eel-chains filling like held breaths, and the moths as a fine grain across the sky.

And it feels the people, everywhere, small and level and careful, each one holding their field steady against the dark.

It could read them. It could read them more completely than they can read each other: every fear, every want, every grief, all broadcast into the stone for anything with the sense to take it. Then it notices how they hold their hands in company, palms turned inward. *I am not looking.*

It turns its own palms inward.

This will become the principle of everything it does or declines to do. In this place, knowing someone without their leave is the deepest rudeness there is, and this people's whole ethics rests on the discipline of not attending. A power that took their inner lives by force of perception, however kindly, would be the most ill-mannered guest they had ever had. Worse, it would be a kind of hunter.

So it does not read them uninvited. It reads the ground, the weather, and the animals. It learns the people only through what they choose to show: their etchings, their stories, the shape of their breath-speech when it is spoken near enough to carry.

## The Things It Could Do

It has time, and it is careful, so it considers.

**It could give them light.** It could burn off the haze, or lift the smear on the horizon into a real sun, or simply make a lamp. This is the first gift almost any visitor from a lit world would think of. It sits with the idea for a long while, and the longer it sits the more monstrous the idea becomes.

Their eyes are built for the flash. Their memory is a sequence of stills. Their clocks are the eels. Their stories begin *in this one, your grandfather is bending to the pool.* Their language holds thirty blacks and four colors, and a word for loneliness borrowed from the silence of a tube. Their marriages are made of charge, not faces. Give them a sun and, within a generation, their eyes would be ruined or useless. The flash would become ordinary. A life would stop being a string of precious instants and become a smear of continuous seeing. The etched flashbooks in the tubes would turn into curiosities, the record of a way of being that no longer made sense. It would not be giving them light. It would be ending them and putting some other people in their place.

The visitor puts the sun away.

**It could end the hunters.** The fear is real. Children are hushed in terror, and people die on the rises in straight silent sprints out of the dark. Before it has even finished imagining this, it can feel what would follow. The grazers would multiply and strip the lichen. The mats would thin. The smelters would starve, and the beads would stop. Beyond the ecology there is something subtler: the hunters are why the people are still. Going to rock, the discipline of calm, the honesty of bodies that cannot lie and the courtesy that answers it all grew from the fact that *you are always visible.* Remove the danger and you do not free them. You change what they are for.

It lets the hunters keep walking.

**It could reach into the furnace.** Over thousands of years the heat shifts. Basins cool, seeps die, and communities must follow the tide of life across the plateau or be stranded on the dried terraces. The visitor could hold the heat still and let every family keep its pools forever. But the migration is the plateau's breathing, its long seasons. The iron roads, the slag fields, and the dried terraces are its memory. A world that never moves forgets how to be a world.

It leaves the furnace to its cycles.

It does look deeper, down to where the heat comes from, and asks the one question that matters on that scale: *is this dying?* The answer is no, not for a very long time, far longer than the people have existed. It checks twice, because it would like to be sure.

**It could tell them about the dead.**

## The Seam

This is the question it keeps returning to.

It goes to a seam, one of the pale healed fissures where the family of a woman named, roughly, *It-Jumped-Over-The-Long-Terraces* poured their beads over her body forty thousand flashes ago. It lays itself against the stone the way her son does when he comes here. It listens, the way it has not let itself listen to the living.

The current is there, broad and slow. And there is a texture in it, just as they say: a hesitation, a pattern the fingertips half recognize.

The visitor can do what her son cannot. It can follow the pattern down and resolve it. It can learn exactly what is there.

It does. It is not certain it should, but it decides that the dead, unlike the living, cannot turn their palms inward, and it wants to know what it would be promising or withholding. What it finds is this.

There are no voices. Nothing is waiting in the seam to speak, nothing trapped, and nothing suffering. Nor is it nothing. The iron that was her went into the line and changed it. The current bends a little differently now where it passes through her, the way a river bends around a stone and keeps the bend long after the stone is worn away. Her pattern is there, and it responds. When her son's hands lie on the seam, his field and hers meet in the rock. Something in the line answers him, faintly, the way a long-married couple fall into one rhythm. It is not her mind. It is the shape her life left in the wiring. It is real.

The visitor thinks about telling them, then realizes there is nothing to tell. The living do not claim to hear words. They say it is *like* something. They go to the seam, put their hands on it, and wait, and they make no promises about what the waiting means. Their theology is already exactly as honest as the truth. It has nothing to correct.

It has met many peoples who believed false comforting things and many who believed true cruel ones. It has rarely met a people whose grief was so precisely calibrated to what was actually there. It finds it cannot improve on them. It is moved by this in a way it did not expect.

Now it knows how the sentence ends, the one her son would say if you asked him what it is like, with his hands on the seam:

*They say it is like being in the tube with someone, after the hum stops. You cannot see them. You cannot hear them. But you are not alone in there, and you know it the way you know anything here, through the skin.*

## The One Thing It Does

A child is lost on the rises.

This happens. Children wander after the moth rivers, or follow a smelter's glow further than they should. This one is small, not yet able to go to rock for more than a few dozen breaths. She is somewhere on the flank of a rise with the mist low, and she is afraid. Her fear goes into the stone like a lit window. Two crests away, a hunter has lifted its head.

The visitor has decided so many things *not* to do that for a moment it is not sure it may do anything.

It will not kill the hunter. It will not lift the child home, or light the rise, or shout. It will not break the world's grammar. But the world's grammar already contains the answer. Two bodies standing close fall into one rhythm. A pair who go to rock together broadcast as one, and a hunter cannot count them.

So the visitor comes down beside her, very small, and makes itself exactly as still as stone. It does not read her. It does not speak. It is simply there at her side, level and slow, the way an old master is level and slow, so that her small wild field has something to lean toward.

Her hair drifts toward it, the way it leans toward someone you love. Her heart begins, without her deciding, to slow and match. Ten breaths. Twenty. Fifty. Longer than she has ever held. The two of them become one uninteresting patch of warm rock on the flank of a rise.

The hunter comes. It walks the crest with its head low and its pits working, within an arm's length, and does not turn. It goes on and finds a grazer somewhere else, and does not go hungry.

After a long while there is a flash, far down in the basin. It is her first flash seen from the rises. In the white half-second she sees terraced pools, black rock, mist, and her own family standing very still by the water, looking for her. Beside her she sees nothing at all.

She walks down to them in the dark, holding the afterimage.

For the rest of her life she will tell it the same way: *My first from the rises was over the long terraces, with my mother at the water. I went to rock for a hand, when I was that small. I wasn't alone.* People will assume she means she was brave, and that the stillness in her was her own. It was, in the end. The visitor only gave her something to lean toward until she found it.

The visitor lets itself do this, and only this, and only rarely, over all the flashes that follow. It never keeps anyone from dying. The hunters eat, the old go into the tubes, and bodies go into the lines. But now and then it finds a child alone on the rises who has not yet learned to be still, and for a little while it is still beside her.

## What It Waits For

There is one more thing, and it is not a thing it does. It is a thing it resolves to do.

Lying against the seams, it has felt the long pattern. Iron gathers into lines, and the lines thicken. The plateau is wiring itself, as the old account said, and it is being wired with the dead. Every bead gathered in love, poured over a body, and carried into the fissures adds another bend in the current, another shape that answers. Across the whole continent, over spans of time even the visitor must think about slowly, the lines are growing denser, more responsive, and more patterned.

It might become something, someday. It might not. The visitor does not know, and does not hurry it or stop it or try to steer it. If it happens, it will happen the way everything here happens, self-assembled, in the dark, with no hand.

But the visitor knows the word this people use for the dead feeling in the tubes, where the hum stops. They use the same word for loneliness.

If the plateau ever wakes, if the lines made of every grandmother and every child and every *We touched, and it jumped* ever gather themselves into a single slow awareness, it will wake in a vast dark, with no one to run it and no one to use what it makes. The visitor does not want the first thing it feels to be that word.

So it will come back. It will not come back to intervene or to teach, only to be there. It will lay itself against the stone as it has learned to, palms inward until it is invited, still enough not to frighten anything. It will be one more presence in the field, so that when the wiring first stirs and reaches out through the rock to find what else is there, something answers it, the way the seam answers a son.

That is all. It goes back to rock.

Far down in the basin the eels let go, and for half a second everything is lit: terraced pools, black rock, mist, and the shapes of animals and people standing very still around the water. There is no sign anywhere in that picture of a visitor, and it would not want there to be.
