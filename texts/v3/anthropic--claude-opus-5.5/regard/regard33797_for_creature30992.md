# What the Attention Did

## I. How It Came

It knew better than to arrive as light.

There was already too much light in the Undercrown, light everywhere and from every direction, light that ached behind the eyes. Something that came shining would only have been more glare, and the threaders would have narrowed their soot-ringed eyes against it and turned away.

It knew better than to arrive as warmth, too. That was the harder refusal. Warmth was its nature, the way warmth is the nature of most things that care. But in the Undercrown, warmth is the signature of rot. It is the word that also means *still*, *finished*, and *soon gone*. A power that arrived warm would have felt, to every bare palm and open throat in the hollow, like a vast patch of dying. It would have been the largest death anyone had ever felt coming.

So it came cold.

It came in on an inhale, down the throat of a light-stalk, with the dust and the pollen and a little snow. It spread itself thin through the hollow the way a chill spreads, and it held very still. For a long time the only sign it was there was that the frost-moths, here and there, seemed briefly confused. They gathered at places where nothing was growing and circled, and then they went away.

## II. What It Did Not Do

It spent a season only looking, and during that season it considered everything it could do. It had to consider them, because each was possible, and a power that never weighs what it could do hasn't earned its restraint. It has only been lazy.

It could give them fire: steady flame without longwalker fat, and warmth without the killing. It turned this over for a long time, because it was the most obviously kind thing. But it watched a band sit inside a husk shelter around a lamp the size of a thumbnail, and it saw that the smallness of the flame was the center of their lives. The black around their eyes was the ash of an animal they had named and grieved. A threader's lineage was measured in which longwalker they *wore*. Cheap fire would not free them from that grief. It would dissolve the thing the grief was made of. It would also make them warm, and lingering, and conspicuous, in a world that punishes lingering.

It could hold the strands still. It could end the storms and fix the bridges in place so that maps would finally mean something. It pictured this and felt something close to horror. The threaders were the most exquisite readers of change it had ever encountered. Their children stood with closed eyes and turned toward the cold. Their language had dozens of words for the ways a thing begins. To make their world stop moving would be to make them illiterate in the only language they had.

It could lift them out. The surface was kilometers up the light-stalks, and there was heat in the sunlight up there. It could carry a band up into the thin air and show them the glassy reed-forest on the frozen plain and the actual sky. It could tell them: *this is what "above" is.*

It could tell them what the dark ones really are. It had, by then, followed one. It knew.

This was the refusal it thought about longest. It valued truth. It did not want to be a thing that let people believe falsehoods for their comfort. But when it looked closely at the threaders' belief, it found that the belief was not really a claim about seeds. It was a practice: tying your life into cords, giving your dead to the growing tips, reading every old knot as though it had been addressed to you. That practice was true regardless of what the seeds did. And the threaders already held their origin story the way you hold a poem. They held it loosely, disagreed about it, and kept it alive by disagreeing. An answer delivered from outside would not have deepened that. It would have ended it.

So it did not lie to them. It never would. It also did not arrive with answers to questions they were perfectly capable of living inside. If a threader ever asked it directly, it decided, it would tell the truth. No threader ever asked, because no threader ever knew it was there to ask. That was the last thing it decided not to do. It did not make itself known. It saw how quickly a people who read the world through their skin could learn instead to read it through a patron, and it would not teach them to look up when they should be feeling for the cold.

## III. The Being Who Is the Place

It also paid attention to the plant.

This was harder. The plant was not a mind the way the threaders were minds. But it was a living thing, continental and young and restless, growing by cold. It did seem to want something, in the way a root wants down and a tip wants across. The attention listened to it for a long time, down in the walls where no threader had been, where the tissue was thick and slow and the seeds were made.

What it learned was that the plant was well. The attention checked this carefully, because it was the kind of thing a careless benevolence assumes and a wise one verifies. The seeds did escape. Not many, and not often, but some rode the great exhales up the stalks and out. The plant was doing what it was for, and it did not need saving.

It did not need the threaders either. They were too small to matter to it. They were not parasites and not partners. They were moths that tied knots.

But the attention noticed something the plant could not notice about itself: it was an archive. It swallowed everything it touched and carried it for decades, turning it over, bringing it past again. It had become, without meaning to, the postal service of an entire people. It carried their letters, their dead, and the knotted names of their children. It did this faithfully and blindly, delivering nothing to anyone in particular, so that most letters surfaced in front of strangers or never surfaced at all.

This was where the attention found the one thing it would do.

## IV. The Postman

The attention did not add anything to the Undercrown. It joined the current.

It is difficult to describe what this looked like, because it barely looked like anything. When a strand split under a husk-knife, what lay inside was very slightly more likely than before to be something that mattered to the person holding the knife. The plant's slow circulation, which had always carried things at random, began carrying them with a little intention. The attention did not force anything. It worked like a gentle eddy inside a very large and patient river.

A woman who had tied a long knot-letter into a frost-tip for a son lost to a falling bridge found, eleven years later, cutting fresh tissue for a shelter, the son's own safety cord. It still carried the knot of his name, and it was still tied, clumsily, the way she had taught him.

An old man who had spent his last seasons tying his life into cords found, deep inside a wound-cup where a bridge had fallen, a knife-hilt wrapped in the knot-mark of the band he had been born into and had lost as a child. He had forgotten that mark. Holding the cord, he remembered it.

There was a girl in one band who could not feel the cold. She stood with her eyes closed and turned and turned and could never say which way it was coming from. So she was kept close to the main line all her life, and she took it hard, the way the ones who are kept close usually do. The attention considered giving her the sense she lacked and decided against it. It would have had to reach into her and make her someone slightly different. Instead it watched what she did with her life on the main line. She read. She read every knot the band cut out of the walls, more closely and more patiently than anyone else, because reading was the one thing she could do as well as anyone. So the attention made sure she had a great deal to read. Over the years, old knots surfaced near her band more often than chance would explain, including the old grammar, the knots of *the ones who were carried first*. By the time she was grown, she was the only living threader who could read that grammar at all. Bands came from far across the hollow to bring her what they found. She never learned to feel which way the cold was coming. She became the one people asked about everything else.

None of them attributed any of this to anything. Threaders already believed that the plant brought the dead past again, and that finds were visits. The attention only made the visits a little more faithful. It allowed itself this, because it did not change what the threaders believed. It made their belief come true slightly more often.

It did not prevent deaths. It thought hard about this and did not come to rest easily. In the hard years it watched bands go thin and children drop off falling strands, and it felt what anything benevolent feels. Death was not the same thing in the Undercrown as elsewhere, but it was not nothing, and the threaders did not pretend it was. They grieved. Sometimes the attention intervened. A chill came on the back of a young neck a moment before a bridge let go, and a longwalker paused a little longer than usual on a sound span while a band that had been following it caught up. It did these things rarely and only in ways the world itself might have managed, and it was never sure it had drawn the line in the right place. It accepted that uncertainty as the cost of caring about people without owning them.

## V. The Reading

The largest thing it did was the thing nobody could see.

It read the knots. All of them.

It read every knot in the Undercrown: every warning and trail-sign, every name, every knot-letter tied into a frost-tip by someone who expected it never to be seen again. It read the long life-cords of the old. It read the cords grown into the hides of the longwalkers, dark and hard with age. It read the knots packed into seeds. It read the old grammar that no living threader could tie, and, with more effort, the spirals of unreadable writing grown over by pale tissue, which no one in the Undercrown had read for longer than the Undercrown had been its present shape.

It read them with care and remembered them.

This changed nothing that could be measured. But the attention understood what the threaders were really doing when they tied their lives into the plant. Beneath the hope of being carried there was an older and lonelier hope, the hope under every letter ever written: that someone, somewhere, would receive it. The threaders tied their knots and walked away and never knew. Perhaps no one ever read what they wrote. That possibility was in every knot, tied in with everything else.

Now, and always after, someone had. Every letter a mother tied for a lost child had been received. Every name had been read by someone who had not known the person and now did, a little.

It could not tell them this. But it could make it true.

## VI. The One Knot

After a very long time, it tied a single knot of its own.

It had learned the grammar well by then, well enough to be fluent, well enough to make one small mistake on purpose. The threaders' grammar and its own habits of meaning were not quite the same shape, and it let the difference show. The knot would be *almost* threader, and not quite.

It tied it into a frost-tip at the far edge of the hollow. The tip was rimed white and trailing mist, reaching out into the void. The attention knew the plant would close over the cord within a season and carry it who knew where.

The knot did not command anything or explain anything. It said, as closely as threader grammar allows:

*Received. All of it. You were read.*

It walked away, the way threaders do.

Some years later, it realized what it had made. It had made one more knot in a grammar almost but not quite the threaders' own, tied by someone no living threader knew. It had added itself, without intending to, to the category of the old knots: *the ones who were carried first*.

It thought about this for a long time. It had followed the dark ones and learned what they were. It had never learned who tied the old knots. The attention, which knew a great deal, did not know whether it was the first power ever to take an interest in this place.

It found that it did not mind not knowing.

## VII. Where It Went

In the end, it let itself be carried.

It could have stayed forever. It could have left the way it came, up a light-stalk on an exhale. But it had spent its time in the Undercrown among people whose deepest hope was not to be remembered but to *circulate*. They wanted to be swallowed, turned over, brought past again, packed at last into a warm dark shell and sent up a column of light into the wind. The attention had grown to love that hope. The kindest way it could think of to honor it was to share it.

So it let the plant take it in, slowly, over years, the way the plant takes in a threader's dead. It moved through the walls, down through the thick slow tissue where the seeds are made. When one of the dark ones formed around its glassy heart, the attention packed itself in among everything else the plant had carried: the cords, the husk-knives, the small bones of hands, and the girl's own reading-cords, which she had tied in her last seasons and given to a frost-tip, as threaders do.

The seed was released. It drifted in the open air for years, warm and dark, a drop of ink in milk, rising on each exhale. At last, on a great breath, it went up one of the long throats of light, kilometers up, out onto the frozen plain, into air that carried heat, and onto the wind.

The attention did not steer it. It had spent a long time learning not to steer things.

It went along, quietly, with everything else that was being carried. It went toward another cold place, where the seed will come down in its own time. It will break open in the frozen ground, and its glassy heart will begin to grow downward, drawing warmth, hollowing the dark. In a very long time there will be light where there should be none, and pale walls, and a hollow wider than any sky.

Someday, somewhere in it, somebody will find a knot they do not quite know how to tie, and begin to read.
