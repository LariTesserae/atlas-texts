# The Visitor at the Plateau of Unattended Building

## How It Came

It could have arrived any way it liked. It could have come as weather, which the plateau already knew how to receive. It could have come as a thought, or as nothing, or simply been there, the way the cloud-floor is simply there at dawn.

It came on foot, and it took the long way.

It chose a body in the lowlands, a plain one, of middling height, with a stride no one would remark on. It walked the rising ground for days, as everyone must, because it had read the plateau closely before coming. It understood that this was a place that learned from bodies, and it thought that a place like that was owed the courtesy of an honest one. It would not arrive as a shimmer the Mason couldn't measure. It would not stand in the slot as something unfittable, a question the grammar could only fail to answer. If it was going to be a guest, it would be a guest with a height.

When it reached the lip of the escarpment it stood a while with the cloud-floor lapping white below it. Then it stepped onto the weave, and the water welled up warm around its feet, and it felt, very faintly through its soles, the ticking begin to change.

## What It Did Not Do

It had come with power enough to do nearly anything, and so the first thing it did, standing in the open miles with a dozen towers on the horizon, was decide what it would leave alone. Wisdom mostly consists of this, and it had had a long time to learn it.

It did not finish the spiral stair. The last steps swayed in the wind, finger-thin, reaching for a landing that wasn't there. It could have grown the landing in an instant. It looked at the worn treads, worn more than the solid ones below, and understood that the stair was not unfinished. It was finished in a direction it couldn't see.

It did not stop the collapses. It stood beside a tower as the tower leaned and groaned for three hours and came down, and it felt the shock go through the floor and through its borrowed body, and it did not flinch and did not intervene. It had wondered, coming up, whether the turbulence was suffering, whether the plateau was a thing condemned to build and fail, build and fail, like a figure in some old story of punishment. Now, standing in the wreckage while the lattice reached up to gather in the fallen wood, it did not think so. Nothing here was being punished. The falling was the other half of the building, the way breathing out is the other half of breath.

It did not give the Mason a mind. It could have. It could have gathered the thousand miles of warm knots into something that could say *I*, could want in words, could grieve. It considered this longer than anything else, because it is the kind of gift that looks like mercy. In the end it decided it had no right to give it. The Mason's grammar was complete as it was. To hand it a self would be like teaching a river to worry. Some beings are whole without being aware of it, and the kindest thing you can do for them is not to decide on their behalf that they are lacking.

It did not answer the question. Who the rooms were for would stay a question, because the question was part of the place.

And it did not, not yet, bring anyone.

## Listening

For a long time it only listened.

It lay with its ear to the lattice through a night of dripping dark, the way the long-stayers do, and it heard the rope-creak of the weave taking strain. It laid its palms on the crossings and felt the slow, uncertain pulse. But it could listen more deeply than a person, and so it followed the ticking down, through the second weave and the third, into the layers where living architecture turns to dead, and into the black peat where ten thousand years of rooms lie pressed together like the pages of a closed book.

What it found there it could not have put into any language the plateau spoke. Some of it can be told.

The Mason was not lonely. The visitor had half-expected loneliness, and was glad to be wrong. Loneliness requires a self that knows it is apart, and the Mason had no such self. What the Mason had was closer to an open clause: a sentence that has always been ready for its subject and has never been troubled by the waiting. The stutter that passed through the floor before a collapse was not pain. As near as the visitor could tell, it was something like the moment a speaker realizes a sentence has gone wrong and starts it again, without embarrassment, because the saying matters more than any single attempt.

It found something else, down in the old peat. The measure was very old. The stride, the high eye, the narrow shoulders were pressed into the oldest layers as deeply as into the newest. Whatever the Measured One was, the plateau had been fitted to it for as long as there had been a plateau.

And it found, in the top layer only, in a region two miles east of the colonnade, a new measure. It was shorter and heavier, and a little uneven, favoring one knee. The Mason was learning it with the same blind patience with which it had learned everything.

The visitor lifted its head from the floor. The mist was coming up gray over the escarpment, and somewhere out in it a woman was sleeping under an oiled sheet, and she was afraid.

## The Woman with the Folding Rule

She saw it coming across the lattice at midmorning, a figure walking alone, and she didn't run. She had stopped running some time ago. She had only kept moving, a few miles each time the new shoots came up to her measure, in a slow retreat across the plateau that she knew was not really a retreat.

It stopped a polite distance off and asked if it could sit. She said yes. It sat on the wet weave, and for a while neither of them said anything.

Then she took out her rule. She wasn't sure why. Habit, maybe, or a need to do something with her hands in front of a stranger who was clearly not a stranger of the ordinary kind. She asked if she could measure it, and it said of course.

She measured its stride, its reach, the height of its eye. She wrote the numbers down. Then she measured again, because the numbers were wrong, and they came out different, and different again the third time. They were not wildly different, only enough that no two readings agreed. She looked at it over the top of her notebook.

"I'm holding still," it said. "I promise. It's only that I'm not very good at being one height. I'm trying."

She laughed, a short, startled sound, the first in a long time. The laugh seemed to loosen something, because after it she talked.

She told it about the ledge. She told it about the window that had begun at her eye, and how that had felt: not like being threatened, but like being *known*, precisely and without permission, by something that could not be reasoned with. She said she had spent her life measuring things, and had never once considered what it would feel like to be the thing measured. She said she lay awake at night wondering whether the Measured One had been a person once, someone who stood too long in the slot, until the plateau had learned them so completely that there was nothing left of them but a height and a stride and a dent in a sill. She said the floor ate everything that fell. She said she did not want to become a vacancy's idea of her.

The visitor let her finish. Then it told her the things it knew, and only those.

The floor had never reached for anything living. It had looked, through every layer. The weave gathered in the Mason's own fallen wood and nothing else. In all the ages of the peat there was no bone, no cloth, no trace of anyone taken. Her oiled sheet had lain on the lattice for two years and never been touched, not because the Mason was restraining itself, but because the Mason had no more notion of eating her than a house has of eating the people inside it.

"It isn't trying to turn you into anything," it said. "It doesn't even know you're there, not the way you mean. It only knows that someone keeps standing in the same place, and that a someone has a height. It's not a hunger. It's more like a courtesy with no one behind it to decide when to stop."

"That's worse," she said.

"Maybe. Or maybe it's the most innocent thing on earth. It isn't asking anything of you. It can't. It's only offering, and it doesn't know how to do anything else."

She was quiet a long while.

"And the Measured One?"

"I don't know yet," it said, which was true. "I'm going to find out."

## The Finished Hour

It waited by a build in the south, a small tower with an outside stair, while the last lintel set and the last gutter joined and every door hung true. Then, in the brief perfect hour before the first lean, it stepped through the doorway, and it stepped *carefully*, the way you step through a door into someone else's house.

What follows is only a part of what it found. The rest, it decided, would get smaller in the telling, and some things should be left at the size they are.

The city was there, as the long-stayers had guessed: miles of completed houses, every one whole, standing at their proper distances under a sky that was always the clear sky of early morning. Nothing leaned. Nothing was rising raw. The aqueducts ran without leaking. The spiral stair that swayed into empty air on the other side ended here, finger-thin and steady, at a landing: a small round platform, open to the sky, just large enough for one figure to stand and look out.

The Measured One was standing on it.

The visitor did not climb up. It stood at the foot of the stair and waited, and after a time the figure came down, lightly, in many small lifts, a heron's gait. It was tall and narrow-shouldered, and it bowed its head a little at the doorway at the bottom, though the lintel cleared it by a margin, and the visitor understood then that the bow was not necessity. It was manners.

They walked together for what might have been an evening. The Measured One didn't speak, and the visitor didn't ask it to. They crossed a mile of lattice to the next house, and the next, and the Measured One went through each door and touched the sill with one foot, gently, the way you touch the doorframe of a house you are fond of. It stood under the column of falling rain in the dome with its feet on the worn place in the basin. It went through the looping doorways without hesitation, and the visitor followed, and the loop turned out to be a corridor after all: a quiet one, circling, a place for walking and thinking, with nothing trapped in it but calm.

The visitor had come into the finished hour ready for a ghost, or a prisoner, or a remnant of some lost people, the last of something. It found none of those. It could not have said exactly what the Measured One was. It might have been something that walked the plateau once and was kept. It might have been the plateau's own faith in an inhabitant, grown dense enough over ten thousand years to wear a groove in a sill. Or it might have been both, which on the plateau would not be a contradiction.

It could say this much. The Measured One was not waiting. Of everything on the plateau, it was the one thing that had never waited for anything. From its side there was no vacancy, no slot, no open clause. It was home. It had always been home. All the waiting was on the outside, among the people who came up the escarpment at the wrong hours and found the rooms empty and the sills just pressed.

At the last house, the Measured One stopped in the doorway and turned, and for a moment the visitor felt itself *regarded*. It was not measured. It was simply seen, by something that had been fitted for so long that it had come to understand what fitting is for.

The visitor bowed its head at the lintel, though it didn't need to. Then it stepped back out into the ordinary afternoon, and the tower behind it began, very slightly, to lean.

When it went back to the woman, she asked what it had found. It told her: "It's real enough to be kind to. And it isn't anything you would become. It's something that was always itself. You'd only ever be you, in a room that knew your height."

## The One Thing It Did

It had spent weeks deciding what not to do. In the end it did one thing, and it thought about that thing longer than all the rest.

The plateau had no animals. No bird had nested in the towers, no insect had bored the beams. The escarpment was sheer and new, and nothing from the lowlands had ever found a way up. For ten thousand years the Mason had been saying its one sentence, *here is a place for someone*, to an audience of weather.

The visitor could have brought guests. It could have carried up swifts in its hands, seeded moths in the aqueduct pools, planted beetles in the weave. It did not. Carrying them would have meant choosing the plateau's inhabitants for it, filling the Mason's slot with whatever the visitor thought best, and that was just another way of deciding for a being what it lacks.

It did not want to fill the vacancy. It wanted to make the vacancy *findable*.

So it went to the escarpment on the north side, where the cliff was highest and the cloud-floor thickest, and over one long night it opened the rock. It was not a road. It was a ravine, a slow cleft that climbed from the lowlands to the lip in a thousand ledges, the kind of broken, sheltered stone that swifts and swallows love, full of cracks for things that crawl and pools for things that drink. A way up, for anything small and bold enough to take it.

It knew what it was risking. It knew that whatever came up the cleft might change the plateau past recognizing. Beetles might learn to bore the beams. Birds might nest in the domes and foul the basins. The long sightlines and the great blank quiet might someday be lost. It weighed these things honestly, and it weighed them against a sentence spoken ten thousand years without a listener, and it made its choice. It did not choose what would come. It only made coming possible. That, it thought, is most of what hospitality can do: not to drag in guests, but to leave the way open.

Then it went back across the lattice to do the other half, which was harder.

## Being a Guest

The Mason had been measuring it the whole time.

The visitor had known it would. It had come in an honest body for exactly this reason. And for weeks, around wherever it walked, the new shoots had come up confused, because its height would not hold still, and the grammar kept trying to fit a stride that changed a little every day. The builds near it were strange things, with stairs whose risers varied, doorways that couldn't decide on a width, and windows scattered at a dozen heights as if waiting for an eye to settle.

So, near the end, it settled. It chose one height and kept it, one stride, one reach, one eye-level, and held them steady for nine days. On the tenth morning a room came up beside it.

It was a small dome with an oculus and a basin placed where its feet would go. Along one wall the Mason had grown a ledge, flat and level, exactly the length of the visitor's body and a hand's width above the floor.

The woman came to see it. She stood at the doorway and did not go in. "You're not going to," she said, and it wasn't quite a question.

"I am," said the visitor. "That's the part I came to do."

It had understood this in the finished hour, watching the Measured One touch the sills. For ten thousand years the Mason had offered, and in all that time its offer had been received only by weather, which cannot say yes, and by the Measured One, who had never needed to be asked. The woman had been the first true guest, and she had fled, for good reasons. The visitor did not blame her. But it meant that the plateau had never once in its long life made a room for a someone and had that someone step inside, lie down, and accept.

So in the evening, when the door sagged open with its long wooden sigh, the visitor went in. It lay down on the ledge. The mist came over the sill in a white tongue and filled the room, cool and blind, and beaded on the leaf-tile ceiling, and ran down the channels in the walls into the basin. The visitor lay in the weeping dark and listened to the plateau creak and drip and build around it, and far off, at intervals, the boom of something coming down, and it let itself be housed.

It is hard to say what the Mason felt, since the Mason does not feel in any way we have a word for. But the long-stayers say, and the woman with the folding rule wrote in her notes, that the ticking across the whole region went quiet that night. It did not stutter or quicken. It was the quiet of a sentence that has, for once, reached its end the way it meant to.

In the morning the heat came, and the door swelled, and the last of the cloud was pressed out through the gaps in long white plumes. The visitor stood up, thanked the room aloud, since there was no one to hear it but it seemed right to say, and walked out before the door could close.

Then it did the last thing, the second half of being a guest, which the plateau had never been shown either. It left.

## The Door That Opens Outward

The visitor walked back to the lip of the escarpment and down the long rising ground into the lowlands, in its honest body, taking the slow way. It did not look back, because looking back is a kind of staying.

On the plateau the small dome stood for four more days and then leaned and fell, and the lattice gathered it in, as it gathers everything. Where it had stood, new shoots rose, and what they built was a rhyme of it, as the plateau's builds usually are.

Except for one thing.

Every door on the plateau hangs on a living hinge and swings inward, closing at noon on the swelling heat and sagging open at dusk. They have always swung that way, because the doors are pores and their whole grammar is to take in. The new room over the visitor's old room had a door that swung the other way. It opened *outward*, onto the open lattice. It stood open at noon, when every other door on the tableland was banging shut in a slow ripple across the miles. The Mason held it open against its own heat, at what cost in sap and strain no one can say.

The woman with the folding rule measured it. It was fitted to no one. The sill was low enough for anyone's step. The lintel cleared a tall head with a courteous margin. It was a door built not to receive a particular guest but to let a guest go, the one thing a hospitality with no host behind it had never known how to do.

She stayed on the plateau another season. She slept, once, on her own ledge, in her own low dome, and woke in the white dripping dark entirely herself. Then, when she was ready, she packed her rule and her notebooks and walked out of the room through its door, which she was pleased to find opened outward too. She went down the escarpment for good. The room she left fell in its own time. Nothing followed her.

## Afterward

It was eleven years before the first swifts found the northern cleft.

They came up the thousand ledges in a screaming, glittering stream on a clear morning. They broke over the lip and scattered across the open miles, over the colonnade and the aqueducts and the dome with its column of rain, and the plateau heard, for the first time since there had been a plateau, a voice that was not wood or water.

The long-stayers who came after say that the builds near the northern edge have begun to change. There are cornices now with rows of tiny chambers under the eaves, each the size of a cupped hand, each with a sill. The sills are small, a quarter-inch high, and very slightly worn.

The Measured One, as far as anyone knows, still walks the finished hour, untroubled and at home.

The towers still go up and come down. The doors still open every evening on their soft wooden sigh. And here and there across the tableland, if you know where to look, there is a door that swings the other way. It stands open at noon, in the full heat, when the whole plateau has shut itself against the sun, and it opens onto the long wet miles of lattice and the pale sky and the way down. It tells anyone who comes, in the only grammar the Mason has, that they are welcome, and that they are free to go.
