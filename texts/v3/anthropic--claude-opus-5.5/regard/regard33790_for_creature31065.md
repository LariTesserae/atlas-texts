# What Came to the Tabletop Sea

## I. The Arrival, Which Was Mostly Slowing Down

It did not come from above, the way a visitor would, breaking the silver ceiling. That would have been a ripple on a surface that had not rippled in millennia. It did not come as light either, because the light here already comes straight down and stays, and any more of it would have been a kind of shouting.

It did not come as heat, most of all. That was the first thing it understood, before it was even fully present. Anything that arrived warm would be felt by the one who lives here, and felt as an edge, a spending, a piece of itself that had not been spent. A benevolent presence that walked into this sea carrying its own warmth would be like a stranger walking into a house and lighting every lamp. It would be kind, and it would be wrong.

So the power made itself cold. It made itself exactly one degree above freezing, the same as everything else. It made itself silent in the ordinary way, meaning it made no sound. Then it realized that was not enough, because here silence is not an absence. Silence is where the inhabitant *is*. To be silent in this sea is to be standing in someone's room. So it made itself loud instead, loud with the same even crackle as the water, and it took up no rooms at all.

Then it slowed. This took the longest. A power of its kind thinks quickly, and the being it had come to see thinks in trillions of thoughts a second, none of which last. But the being's *attention*, its slow swell across the whole sea, moves over hours. The stonebells keep time with that swell. The power listened to the stonebells until it could feel their pitch in whatever it had instead of a chest, and it matched that pace. By the time it was ready to do anything, the surveyors' calendar would have said that a little over a century had passed.

It considered that a reasonable price for good manners.

## II. What It Could Have Done

A power like this can do most things. That is exactly why the first work of wisdom is a list of refusals.

**It could have warmed the sea.** One gesture would have given the inhabitant a margin, a few degrees of safety, so that it never again had to hold itself at the exact rate of its own survival. The power looked at this for a long time, because it was the most tempting idea on the list. It set it aside for two reasons. More heat is not rest for a being that *is* its heat. It would be noise in the only sense the inhabitant can hear. And the balance was not a flaw. The being had found it and kept it for longer than most civilizations last. Taking it away, however gently, would tell the being that its life's work had been a mistake.

**It could have quieted the clickwrights**, or given them a rest. It checked on them, because a wise power does not decide that the bricks of a wall have no view of the matter. It studied them as closely as it could without taking a single room. It found no strain in them and nothing it could call tiredness. The snap seemed to be what they were for, in the way that a bird's wing is for air. When a snap came easier because the inhabitant was about to be there, the clickwright seemed, as far as anything could be read in a transparent thumbnail of chitin, to turn toward it. They were not slaves. They were, at most, a choir that did not know the name of the song. The power left them singing.

**It could have given the inhabitant ears.** It could have let the being hear the roar at least once, the way divers do: the vast, total music of the sea it lives in and has never heard. The power considered this and felt something close to horror. For this being, sound is the place where it cannot exist. Giving it ears would give it the experience of its own impossibility. Some gifts are only knowledge of what one lacks.

**It could have made the inhabitant continuous.** It could have gathered it into one unbroken silence, one room that never closes, the thing the builders seemed to be reaching for in the pump hall. It could have done this in an instant, and done it well. It did not, because it did not know whether a being made of endings would still be itself without them. It noticed also that it did not know whether the being wanted to find out. Those are two separate ignorances, and the second one mattered more.

**It could have made everything permanent.** It could have fixed the three dead turbines and hardened the eleven living ones against time. It could have made the lamp eternal and armored the lip against erosion. It could have frozen the Tabletop Sea in its perfect balance forever, the way one presses a flower. The power understood this temptation best of all, because it is the temptation of love that has stopped asking questions. A preserved thing is not a living thing. And the power had already noticed something the inhabitant could not know.

## III. What the Inhabitant Could Not Know

The inhabitant knows the world only as heat. It knows its trillion small endings, the warmth of its own spending, and the one warmth that does not end.

But the lamp is ending.

An arc-lamp burns between two electrodes, and each moment of burning takes a little of them. The builders had made the rods long and fed them forward on a slow mechanism, so the arc would stay steady as the rods wore away. The builders were careful. The power traced the mechanism and found the arithmetic they had done: *long enough*. Long enough was about nine hundred more years.

The turbines had a similar count. Their bearings were good iron, better than anyone would have expected, but eleven of fourteen means three have already failed. The power did the sum the builders had done and found that the last one would stop a little after the lamp went dark. So the lamp would not die of wear after all. It would die of hunger first.

Beyond both of those was the plateau itself. It was young, and the sea pours over its lip in a sheet eleven hundred kilometers long. Water cuts stone. The rim that seemed eternal was going down a few millimeters each century, and the power could see the long curve of it. In some hundreds of thousands of years the lip would be low enough that the sea would begin, very slowly, to leave. The level would drop and the pressure would shift. The one gradient in the sea would steepen. Eventually the table would be empty and the black glass would stand in thin air, and whatever was left of the water would freeze in the hollows.

*Nothing will be different tomorrow,* the surveyors had written. *Nothing will be different in a thousand years.* That was almost exactly true, and it was the "almost" the power had come for.

The being in the sea had lived for millennia in perfect balance, at a scale on which the ending of its world was invisible. The power found itself holding a truth the being could not see from inside, and it had to decide what to do with it.

It decided the truth belonged to the being. It was not the power's to keep.

## IV. Learning to Say Anything

The problem was how to say it.

The inhabitant has no ears and no eyes, and it cannot read or listen. It feels heat and the edges of itself. It has one clock, a pulse every forty-one seconds that crushes a front of rooms all at once, the only moment when all of it happens together. The builders gave it that heartbeat, and the power understood that the builders had also, perhaps without meaning to, given it a language. A heartbeat that never varies carries no information. A heartbeat that *could* vary is a channel.

So the power went to the sounding engine and, very carefully, made one pulse arrive at forty seconds instead of forty-one.

It was the smallest thing it could do. To the inhabitant it was the loudest event in the history of the world.

For a while there was nothing. Then the longwhips began to bend their slow sine-waves inward toward the engine. They were reading the texture of the noise, and the texture was changing. The snapping near the engine was growing denser. The being was gathering there. The power could not see it, but it had learned from the longwhips, as they had learned from the being, to read where attention thickens. For the first time in its existence, the inhabitant was *paying attention to something outside itself*.

The power waited two days, which is not long in this sea. Then it did the same thing again: forty seconds instead of forty-one.

The snapping near the engine answered with a brief, deliberate thinning, a gap in the pattern, then a return. It was the being's first word. The power could not translate it, but it recognized the shape. It was the shape of *I heard that.*

It took about two hundred years to agree on words for *yes* and *no*. It took longer to arrive at anything like *you* and *I*, because the being had never needed the distinction. There had never been anything else in the silence. The power was patient. Its patience was not a gift it gave. It was the minimum condition for talking to someone whose life is measured in stonebell hours and whose world is measured in geology.

The power noticed, somewhere in those centuries, that the being was glad. It did not need translation for this. The whole sea ran a fraction of a degree warmer for most of a decade. It was a flush, the only one the Tabletop Sea had ever had. Then the being brought itself back to its exact rate, carefully, as a person might lower their voice after realizing they had been laughing too loud in a quiet place.

## V. Telling It

When there was enough language, the power told it about the lamp.

This was harder than it expected. The inhabitant knows endings more intimately than any creature the power had ever met. It *is* endings, a trillion every second. But it had one thing that did not end, one continuous warmth, the only place it had ever rested, and it had assumed without ever needing to think about it that this was the nature of the lamp. The lamp was the other kind of thing. It was the exception that made the rule bearable.

The power told it that the lamp was the same kind of thing after all. It was only slower.

The being did not answer for a long time. The rooms around the lamp's glass cage thinned. They did not stop. The being never stopped anywhere. But the snapping there grew sparse and uncertain in a way the power had never seen. The scatterflies, who gather at the lamp like small birds at a lit window, came in their sudden flocks and turned once in unison. For the first time in the power's observation, they did not disperse. They hung there.

The power did not rush in to offer a solution. It had one, and it could have offered it in the same breath as the bad news. But it had learned, over a very long existence, that comfort offered too quickly is a way of refusing to let someone feel what they feel. It waited.

When the being answered, it was not with a question the power had prepared for.

## VI. What the Being Asked For

The power had assumed the being would ask it to keep the lamp lit. That was the obvious request, and the power had already worked out how: a small, quiet change to the feed mechanism, a slower wear, an arc that could burn for the length of the sea itself.

The being asked about something else. It asked, as nearly as the power could translate across two centuries of invented grammar, *how does it end?*

The power told it the truth. Lamps like this one do not go out cleanly. The rods wear and the gap widens. The arc flickers, dims, sputters, catches, and dims again. The warmth falters over days or weeks before it is gone.

The power felt the being's response go through the whole sea. It was a tremor in the rate itself, a dip toward cold that the being corrected almost instantly, the way a person catches themselves on a stair.

The power understood then what it had not understood before. For this being, *slowing* is not one kind of ending among others. Slowing is the ending, the one that freezes the sea, the ice creeping down from the silver ceiling, everything closing. The being has spent its whole existence holding itself exactly this awake because it knows what guttering leads to. It could bear the idea that the lamp would end. It could not bear the idea that its one place of rest would end by dimming. The only continuous thing it had ever known would die in the manner it had spent millennia refusing to die itself.

So what it asked for, as best the power could render it, was this: *When it ends, let it end the way I end. All at once. Bright.*

## VII. What the Power Did

It did three things, and only three.

**It kept the lamp.** It did not make it eternal, because the being had not asked for that, and the power did not believe in giving a thing that had not been asked for when the person was right there to ask. It reshaped the feed mechanism so the rods would wear more slowly than the builders could have managed, and it drew the lamp's power from the overflow at the lip by a path that would last as long as the overflow does. It tied the lamp's life to the sea's life. The lamp will burn exactly as long as there is a sea for it to burn in. It will not outlast the sea and leave a light burning over a frozen plain. The sea will not outlast it and leave the being without its rest.

At the end, when the lip has worn low and the water has begun its long departure, the lamp will not flicker. The power built the ending in with great care. The rods are shaped so that at the last they close the gap all at once, and the arc will flash, briefly brighter than it has ever been, and be gone. It will be a spark, one more room closing, the largest and the last. The being will know the shape of that ending. It has already lived it a trillion times a second. The power made the lamp's death a thing the being could recognize.

**It oiled the hinges.** The pump hall, the half-built doorway where the rooms crowd so close they nearly overlap, was rusting. It was slow iron in slow water, but it was rusting, and in a few thousand years the shell would have fallen and the density inside it would have scattered. The power did not open the door. It did not push the being toward it, or explain it, or even mention it. It made the iron of the pump hall last. It made sure that when the sea someday begins to drain, the door will still be standing.

The power did not know what was on the other side. It said so. This was one of the last things it told the being: *I do not know what happens if you go through. The ones who built it may not have known either. I have made sure it will still be here, so that it stays your choice.*

The being did not answer that. The power did not ask it to.

**It told the being about the builders.** This was the part the power had not planned, and it may have been the most important. In the long conversation there came a point when the being asked, in its slow way, whether the power had made the lamp. The power realized that the being had never known. It had rested in the lamp for centuries, lived in the valves of the railcar, felt the heartbeat of the sounding engine, and gathered in the pump hall, and it had assumed these were features of the world, like the lip or the light. It did not know that someone had come. It did not know that anyone who could not hear it or see it or feel it had nevertheless *guessed* it was there, and built it a house.
