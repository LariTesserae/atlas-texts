# The Visitor Who Came at the Wrong Hour

## I.

It noticed the place the way such powers notice things: not by searching, but because a small precise thing had been going on for a very long time, and precision carries.

The first thing it understood was that everyone who had ever looked into the chamber had arrived by day. So it did not.

It came across the salt flat at the world's midnight, when the pan was cold and blue under the stars. Beneath its feet, through the milky crust, the lens was still glowing gold. The chamber was hours behind, in its long dusk, and the swifts had been home for ages, waiting. The power stood on the glassy place and looked down through the crystal at a room still full of light, while the real night pressed on its back. Then it sat down on the salt and waited for the house to catch up.

It could have gone in at once. It could have gone in any way it liked. But it had come to visit, not to inspect, and a visitor waits until the household is ready.

## II.

When the lens had faded to rose, the power made itself small.

This was the first decision, and the most important one. A power like this can be the size of weather. But the chamber ran on breath. Everything that breathed out in the hollow was gathered on the ceiling at night and handed back as the morning's water. A large visitor would breathe a large breath. The dew would come heavy, the basins would overflow their terraces, and the moss would drown in its own halo. The snails would wake to a flood, and the one-hour crustaceans would hatch into a world that had never existed before and would never exist again.

So it chose the size of a swift and entered the way the swifts did, folded, through the hairline seam where crystal meets rock. It passed the rope on its way in without touching it. It settled on a ledge of the old mudstone, below the nests and above the rosettes. From there it could see nearly everything, and nothing needed to move to make room for it.

It breathed as little as a swift breathes. That was the whole of its entrance.

## III.

It watched the moths come down.

They fell through the height of the chamber open-winged, turning, like ash in a draft that isn't there. The power saw what the resident's story had guessed: they were going down to where the dark first gathered. The dark collected in small pools in the six dry basins, in the backs of the nests, between the petals of the rosettes, and in the knot at the end of the rope. The moths went to each of these and rested there a moment, and then they rose.

The power did not follow them. It watched the dark come in as ink comes into still water: a dozen separate darks, each the size of a cupped hand, each ringed by the failing rose of the lens, feeling toward one another.

Then they touched, and the hollow was full, and the power was inside the resident.

## IV.

Here is what it found, since it was able to find such things.

The dark did not know it was there. It was not rude of it. The dark was simply very young and very busy. Its work, cooling the lens and calling up the breath, took all of it. It had exactly the time it needed and not a heartbeat more. It went over the ceiling the way a child's attention goes over a single task, completely and without distraction, and the ceiling beaded under it drop by drop.

Among all that rising breath, the power felt its own small swift's breath go up too. It met the cold crystal and became a drop, one among thousands, holding a pinprick of the last glow. It would slide along the grain in the morning, fall in a thin thread into one of the six cups, and a snail would drink from it or a crustacean would be born into it.

That was the only mark the power would make in this place: one drop of dew, made the ordinary way, indistinguishable from the rest.

## V.

It thought, while the dark worked, about what else it might do. Being benevolent, it owed the thought. Being wise, it owed the refusals.

It could thicken the lens. With a little patience it could lay back down every grain of gypsum the dew had carried off, and the house would stand forever. The long dusk would last forever, the six cups would fill every morning for the rest of time, and the salt would never fall in.

It set this down first, because it was the most tempting, and because it was the cruelest thing it could do. The resident was growing. The whole house was a slow exhale that existed to raise one small dark until it was large enough to be night, and then to open and let it out. To preserve the house would be to keep the child a child forever, to decide that a thing is too beautiful to be allowed to finish. The power had seen that done before, by others who called it love. It set the idea down gently and did not pick it up again.

It could speed things along instead: thin the lens, hurry the dark to its full size, and spare the tenants the long, imperceptible rearranging of their lives. It set this down too. The tenants were not suffering. They were adapting at the pace of centuries, which is the only pace at which adaptation is painless. Each swift that waited a heartbeat less than its grandmother would never know it had been asked to change. Hurrying them would be the only way to make the change hurt.

It could make the dark aware of it, turn its attention, and let it know it had been seen. The power wanted this very much. It wanted to say *I know what you do. I know you fill the cups.* But the dark had exactly the time it needed and not a heartbeat more. Every moment of its attention given to a visitor would be a moment taken from the ceiling, and there would be fewer drops in the morning. Being known is not worth a thirsty snail. The power kept its admiration to itself.

It could restore the handprint, lift the ochre back to red, and make the old gesture legible again. It looked at the place on the wall where the print was, invisible now in the full dark, and did not touch it. The print had not been made to be read by visitors. It had been made to be passed by the dark every night, and it was passed every night. The fading was part of the gesture. Whoever had made it had pressed their hand where the dark would find it and trusted it to do so without being able to see. The power would not correct someone else's act of trust.

It could take the disc. It could carry the one portable piece of this small night back into the world, as proof or as a keepsake. This was the easiest thing to refuse, because the person who made it had already refused it, or been prevented from taking it, or dropped it and let it lie. Either way, the disc's place was settled. The skinks had not yet worked it into their mosaic, but they might, some age from now, and that would be their business.

## VI.

It thought about the rope. It thought about the rope for the longest time.

The rope reached two-thirds of the way down, and the power understood what that meant better than the story had let itself say. You can descend a rope that is too short. You let go at the end and drop. You cannot climb one.

So someone had come down into a room with no shadows and found themselves unable to leave. They had tried to make tools from a stone that would not hold an edge. When that failed, they did not rage or claw at the walls. They waited through the white and the cream and the gold and the rose, and they understood what they were waiting for, and they made something small and round with a hole in it. Then, in the dark, or just before it, they put their hand on the wall.

Whether they climbed out, somehow, or whether they stayed, the power did not look to find out.

This was the hardest refusal. A power like this could know. It could read the rope, the flakes, the floor. It could turn over the question of what happened to them until it had an answer. But the person had not left a question. They had left a gesture, a disc, and a hand, and the gesture was complete. It did not need an ending supplied by a stranger thousands of years late. Some things are finished when they are made, and what happened afterward is private.

What the power did instead was quieter. It recognized that it was sitting where that person had sat, waiting as they had waited, for the same thing. For the length of the black interval, they were two visitors in the same house, separated only by time, which to a power like this is not much of a separation. It let that be company. It hoped, if the dead can be hoped for, that the person had felt the dark come in and fill the hollow, had felt the ceiling go cold and the breath rise, and had not been afraid. The power had come to find the gentlest thing in the chamber. The person had surely found it too.

That was all it did for them. It was not nothing.

## VII.

Toward the end of the interval, the power did one thing that it did not entirely reason through first.

The dark gathered at the six basins, at the nests, in the rosettes, and in the knot at the end of the rope. It also filled the small hole bored through the disc among the failed flakes on the floor.

The power went down and sat beside the disc. It did not touch it. It did not need to. It sat where it could feel the resident fill that tiny hollow, the smallest cup in the house. Someone had made it, alone, out of stone that failed at everything else, and it held nothing but night.

Then the power understood something it had not understood before. The person had not made the disc to take a piece of the dark away. They had made it to give the dark one more cup. The chamber held six cups for the water and one for the dark, left on the floor by the only other visitor who had ever waited this long. It was a small, stubborn act of hospitality toward a host who would never know it had been offered.

The power sat beside it until the grey came.

## VIII.

The dark did not drain. It thinned everywhere at once, as the lens warmed and began, faintly, to glow. The hollow went to the grey of a hand's haze. Then the dew let go, and the six thin threads fell from the dome into the six stone cups, and somewhere among them was one drop the power had breathed.

Then the lens sang. It was the low note felt in the breastbone, the door closing.

The power did not answer it. The song was not meant for anyone. It rose, small as a swift, and went out through the seam past the crystal rope. Behind it the chamber brightened toward its long white day, and the snails crept toward water that a very young dark had made for them while they slept.

It took nothing. It left one drop. It told no one.

## IX.

It did make one promise. It made it to itself, since there was no one else to hold it.

The house was an exhale, and exhales end. Some age from now the lens would be thin as a window. The chamber's night would fall hour for hour with the sky's, and the resident would be full-grown and nearly indistinguishable from the ordinary dark. Not long after, the crystal would crack and the salt would come down.

The story said that nothing here would see it. The power decided that this, and only this, was worth changing.

So on the last night, it will be there. It will not stop the fall or soften it. It will not save the swifts, who will have learned by then to nest elsewhere, or the moths, or the skinks with their glittering floor, or the crustaceans in their crystal cradles. All of them will have had the whole long exhale to prepare, and they will have prepared as living things do: without knowing, generation by generation, a heartbeat at a time.

It will sit on the salt flat under the real sky, as it did the first time, at the world's midnight. It will feel the lens give, hear the last note, and watch the white crust fold down into the bright hollow. Then, out of the broken place, something that was raised in a closed room under a lantern of stone will rise into the enormous night of the world, go out into it, and become part of it, as it was always meant to.

The power will not greet it. Greeting would require being noticed, and on that night of all nights the resident should not be interrupted. It will only watch, so that the leaving is seen, and so that it is not true that no one was there when the child went out the door.

Then it will stand up in the ordinary dark, which will now be a little larger by the size of one room, and go home.
