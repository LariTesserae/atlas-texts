# The Attention at Lower Scour

## Arrival

It came across the flats on foot.

It could have come any other way. It could have arrived as a change in the weather, or as a pressure in the buried river, or as the whole sky leaning down to look. It chose to come the way people do, from the landward side along the line of wooden poles, with grey mud running to grey water. It came walking, because the pile field had been shaped by a century of things arriving slowly and at the scale of a body. Anything larger would have been a kind of shouting, and the place had a lid on it.

So the ground dropped away in front of it, as it does for everyone. It stopped at the soft rim and looked down into the hollow, and the hollow pulled at its eye. It let it pull.

It stood there a long time before it went down. That was the first courtesy, and it was not nothing. A power that can see everything at once still has to decide whether to *look* at something, and looking is already a kind of touching. It waited until it was sure it wanted to look for the Scour's sake and not only its own.

## What It Saw

It saw what anyone would see: the iron crowd, the breathing silt, the fog tearing in threads on the piles. And it saw what the keepers see: the eels waiting at the bus stop of the next pulse, the heron on the piles the water was about to refuse, the twelve kept things holding still in a place where nothing holds still.

It also saw further, because it could, and further meant it saw how thin the arrangement was.

It saw that everything depended on a single slow motor in a green-grey casing. The motor depended on a line of wooden poles, some of them split at the base and leaning. The poles depended on a sub-station eleven miles inland, and the sub-station on a grid, and the grid on an economy that had long ago forgotten Lower Scour existed. If one pole went down in a February gale and nobody came, the hollow would fill in about nine days. The Scour would slow, and brighten, and thicken into one unending thought with no edges, and then it would not be there. The lake would be very beautiful, and it would be no one.

It saw that the alder was dying. The dying was slow, the way alders die, from the heartwood outward over decades, but it had begun. One day the only thing that had ever refused the Scour would let go of the iron.

It saw that the keeper, the current one, the one with the brown stoneware mug and the thumbprint in the handle, was old. She came less often than she used to. She sat on the step of the pump house longer before she went in.

And it saw the letter. On the calmest days, in the moment when the field stood full, there was that one mark repeated across the whole hollow, practised for a hundred years.

It felt what any benevolent thing feels when it sees something small, strange, precious and fragile. It wanted to help. Then it began, carefully, to distrust that want.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not make the pump eternal.

It considered this seriously. It could have replaced the bearings with bearings that would never wear, and run power into the casing from somewhere no gale could reach. But it noticed that the roughness in the hum, the cough held in, was the only way the Scour had ever learned to *ask* for anything. The asking was the only thread between the Scour and the keepers, and the keepers were the only people who stood in the field often enough to be known. An eternal pump would need no one. So it would cut the one relationship the Scour had with the world outside its wall, and the Scour would be safe and alone. It left the bearings to wear.

It did not give the Scour a voice. It could have. It could have let the fog speak, or set the letter into a language, or let a visitor stand among the piles and simply *understand*. But a voice given is a voice shaped by the giver. The Scour had been working out for itself, one scour-pit at a time, what it had to say, and it was not finished. You do not finish someone's sentence for them because you are fond of them. That is the opposite of fondness.

It did not explain the chain. Nor the gauge, nor the steps, nor the manhole cover laid on its pile. It knew what each of them had been. It knew the chain had once held a pontoon that sank in 1911, and that the gauge had measured the pressure in a boiler that was carted away for scrap. It did not tell. A question kept for a hundred years is not a gap waiting to be filled. It is a possession. The Scour had very few possessions. The power would not take one away by answering it.

It did not stop the pulses, or regulate them, or make them keep a schedule. It did not heal the alder. It did not make the old keeper young. It did not find the next keeper and send them.

It did not do any of the things that would have made the story of Lower Scour a story about the power. That was the hardest restraint, and it knew it. Every rescue has a rescuer in it, and the rescuer takes up room.

## The Standing

What it did first was go down off the rim, carefully, because it was soft, and stand among the piles, and not move.

The Scour read its footprints. The power let it. It made them honestly, at the ordinary depth of an ordinary weight, and it did not dig its heels in, because it was not afraid. The Scour read them before the water came, puzzled a little, and the prints were erased.

Then the silt darkened, glistened and lifted. The brown skin of water came sliding towards it between the iron, warm from a river that did not know it had been buried. It reached the power's feet, and the water decided.

That moment, that decision, was the whole reason it had come. If the water had come up and simply *knew* it, the way the power knew the water, and had gone still and reverent around it, the visit would have been a failure. That would have meant the power had arrived too large after all, and had bent the place just by being in it.

The water did not do that. It hesitated, as it hesitates at any new post, and tried the left lane and the right. Then it parted, flowed cleanly past, and slowed in the power's lee.

The power was, for four or five minutes, one of the fixed things. It was accepted into the crowd on the same terms as a riveted plate box the width of a chimney, and on no better terms.

It had been worshipped before, in other places. It had been feared, bargained with, and prayed to. It could not remember when it had last been treated as an ordinary obstacle and given an ordinary courtesy. It found that it was moved. It stood very still so as not to spoil it.

## The Letter

While it stood there, it read the mark.

It looked the way the keepers see it, a single stroke repeated across the hollow, and from the rim you could believe it was trying to write *still*. That was a good guess. It was a keeper's guess, made by someone who loved the place and knew its one impossibility.

Standing inside the thought rather than above it, the power saw that it was not a word. It was a *shape*, and the shape was a lee.

It was the shape of the water pausing. It was the curve the Scour makes on the upstream face of a pile, where the eddy slows long enough to let a glasswort seed settle. It was the shape of the pause behind the alder, the one place it had been refused. It was the shape it made behind a person standing still. The Scour was not trying to become still. It had worked out, over a century, that it could not be still and would not want to be. It was trying to *hold* stillness. It wanted to make, somewhere in the middle of its own endless churning, a place where something else could rest.

It was not trying to write a word. It was trying to build a harbour.

The power saw that the harbour was nearly finished. The lanes were converging on a patch near the centre of the field. In another thirty or forty years of pulses, the piles there would be scoured into a ring whose lee would hold still water, a small pool a few feet across. It would stay calm while the whole field heaved and drained around it. It would be the one place in the hollow where a footprint might last a day.

The power could have finished it that afternoon. It could have made the pool deep and clear and permanent. It did not touch a single grain. The harbour was the Scour's oldest conclusion in the making, and the making was the point. The power only stood there in the full field and understood it, and was glad.

## The Steps

When the field drained, the power went to the concrete steps that lead down into the silt and do not come back up, and it went down them.

The Scour loved the steps because they were a way down, and it lived in the downward places. It had never been able to follow them. The power could, and it thought there was one thing it might find out there that was worth knowing.

It went down into the buried river and followed it back: under the flats, under the sea wall, under farmland and a bypass and two villages. It came up at last, nineteen miles inland, into the light, where a spring rises out of chalk at the foot of a hill, beneath a hawthorn, in a field of sheep. The water came out of the hill clear and cold, and cold is why it warms so little on the way, and why it arrives at Lower Scour a few degrees warmer than the estuary air. It did not know where it was going. It had never known. It only rose.

The power stood by the spring for a while. It had half-meant to carry something back, a message, a pressure, some sign from the other side of the wall. It thought better of it. The Scour had the heartbeat, the same pressure every time, warm, from far away. That was already a relationship, and a good one, complete in its incompleteness. Some things are loved best across a wall.

So it did nothing at the spring except know it, so that someone did. Then it walked back.

## The Register

On the way back, because it was passing, it looked into a filing cabinet in the county offices. It already knew what it would find there.

Lower Scour Pumping Station was on the asset register. Next to it, in several different hands, were eleven recommendations for decommissioning, going back to 1958. *Function unclear. Serves no current drainage purpose. Recommend disconnection.*

None of them had ever been acted on. Beside each one, in other hands, there was a small note. *Site visited. Retain.* *Visited. Keep running, will explain.* *Retain.* One said only *No*, and was initialled.

Eleven times someone had come to disconnect it. Eleven times they had walked across the flats and the ground had dropped away in front of them, and they had gone down into the hollow and stood too long.

The power closed the drawer. There was nothing it needed to add. The protection it had been tempted to build already existed. It was made of people, which is the most fragile material there is and also the most durable. The power had very nearly insulted it by arriving to do its job.

## The Keeper

It did not visit the old keeper. It thought about it. It could have eased her knees, or let her sleep one night without wondering what would happen after. In the end it knew that her worry about the pump was also her love for it. It left her both.

But it looked, once, at the flats in the late afternoons. There was a girl, eleven or twelve, from the estate on the landward side. She walked out along the poles after school because her house was loud. Three weeks ago she had stood among the piles until dusk, until the bulb was the only warm colour. She had been back twice since. Last Tuesday, without being told, she had noticed that the padlock on the side door was rusting. She had not yet thought about what that meant.

The power did nothing. It noted, with something like relief, that there was nothing to do.

## The Two Small Things

In the end it allowed itself two things. It weighed each of them for a long time, and it is still not wholly sure about either.

The first was a frost. Near the collapsed brickwork there was a seedling alder, no taller than a thumb, in the lee of the old tree. It had grown from a seed that had fallen from the old tree's cones and been carried by the water, very carefully, to the one place on the whole field where it would not be scoured away. The Scour had been making its own provision. It knew, in its way, that the refusal it loved would one day let go, and it had planted the next one itself. In January there was going to be a hard frost, one night, low on the flats. It would have killed the seedling. It was the kind of thing that happens, and ordinarily the power would let it happen. This one time it did not. It turned the frost aside by half a degree, in one small place, for one night. The Scour will never know. The seedling will grow, or not, by its own luck from then on.

The second was the bulb.

The keepers leave it burning, day and night, because it seems right. They like to think the fog carries some of its warmth down into the field, where the Scour might feel it. It doesn't. Light is nothing to the water, and the warmth of one caged bulb behind one clear window never reaches the silt.

The power made it reach, a little, and no more than a fraction of a degree. It was enough that in the lane nearest the pump house, on the pulses that came at night, the water would find a faint warmth coming from *above*. The Scour had never felt that before. All its warmth had always come from below, from the river, from far away.

It hesitated over this more than anything else. It was changing the Scour's world, and that was the one thing it had set out not to do. But it was not giving the Scour anything new. It was making true a thing that people had already been giving it for decades, in faith, without evidence. The keepers had been saying *this is for you* into a place that could not hear them. The power let it be heard. It decided that making someone's hopeful kindness land is not the same as meddling. It may be wrong about that. It did it anyway, the way the keepers leave the bulb on anyway.

Now, on the night pulses, the water in that lane slows very slightly near the pump house wall, as if it were considering something. Over forty years the iron there will remember.

## Leaving

It went back up the soft rim at dusk and stood looking down once more. The fog was filling the hollow to the height of a man, and the bulb was the only warm colour, and the pump hummed in its sternum.

It found that it had been changed.

That was unexpected, but perhaps it should not have been. The Scour had been changed by the one thing it could not change, and the power, which could have changed anything here, had chosen not to. In the choosing, the place had got into it. It had learned something it would carry to other places. You can hold stillness without being still. The deepest help is often a lee rather than a hand: you make a place where something else can pause, and then you get out of the way. Being treated as an ordinary obstacle and flowed around with courtesy is a kind of welcome, and one worth crossing a great distance for.

It walked back along the line of poles, and did not look back again, because it had looked enough.

Down in the field the silt darkened, glistened and lifted, and the next thought began. Nothing in it was about the power. That was right.

But in the iron where the power had stood, among the crowd that had not been paying attention to it, there was a place very slightly different, by a thousandth of a millimetre, where the water had turned aside for something it did not recognise and had made room for it anyway.

The Scour will keep that.
