# The Attention

## I. How It Came

It did not come down through the lid.

Anything that came down through the lid would leave a hole in it, and the Held Country has never had a hole in its sky. So it came the way the fog comes. It arrived in the evening, low, settling into the canopy and then into the undergrowth, until it was simply part of the saturated air, beading on surfaces like everything else.

It took the least disturbing shape it could find: tall, narrow, able to turn sideways between the clubmoss towers. It made itself the color of the darker sward.

It learned the law within its first hour. It had stopped to look at a water-spider's web, a thousand beads of dew hung in a net between two columns, and it looked for a long time, the way powers can. When it looked down, root-hair had found its feet.

The threads were fine as silk and cool, and already reaching into it. They were not fighting it or testing it. They were simply wanting it, the way the country wants everything that is still.

The power understood something then that it had not understood about any other place. Here nothing is exempt from being wanted, not even a power. It pulled free, the threads tearing softly, and it walked.

## II. What It Could Have Done

It had the strength to do almost anything, and so it had to think.

**It could open the lid.** For an hour, or a day, it could have parted the cloud and let the sun stand in the sky as a disc. It could have shown them shadows for the first time, and shown them their own country from above: the whole scribbled continent, every pale sentence ever walked, visible at once like frost on a window.

It considered this for a long time, because it was the most beautiful gift it could imagine. Then it declined. Their writing is meant to be retraced, not seen. A people who had once looked down on their sentences from above would never again read them quite so fully with their feet. Something would be gained, a kind of knowledge. What would be lost was the way they know.

**It could end the reaching.** It could have made the mat pass over them, so that a person might lie down anywhere and rise again untouched. It could have made them the one thing in the country that is never taken up.

But their whole tenderness is organized around that pull. There are the rings and the pink scars at the ankles. There is the hum at the bedside and the walking partner who must stand at first light and go on. There are the bright swells a sentence is routed across like a thread through a bead. They have spent more generations than they can count learning how to love the thing that will hold them. To remove it would not free them. It would leave them standing in a country that no longer wanted them, and they would not know what their customs were for.

**It could make them houses.** It could have bent a hundred domes in a single night, perfect and closed.

A bent house is a permission, though, and it is a permission because it takes four generations. It is a slow and polite asking. A dome made in a night would be a building, and the land would be right to treat it as one.

**It could give them rope that does not rot,** or a pouch that lasts, so the stone need not always be in the hand.

The hand is where the vow lives. Freeing the hand would break the vow.

**It could go down past the edge,** find what lies under the cloud, and come back and tell them.

At the rim, though, they hold the stone out over nothing and do not let go. They do not look. Their whole relation to the deep is trust sent without seeing. Handing them the answer would turn that trust into information, and information cannot be given back to an eye.

**It could keep the handprint,** the child's hand stretched long on the lintel, and stop the growth so the shape would not be lost.

But the stretching is the point. The land takes the small, true shape of a person and grows it.

By the end the power saw that every gift it could think of had the same flaw. Each would be a thing left behind that the country could not take up. In a place where everything left is given away, the power's gifts would be the only things that refused to be given. They would sit on the moss like stones that no one had found.

## III. What It Was Afraid Of

Restraint is not the whole of kindness. A wise power does not leave a people alone only because leaving them alone is elegant. It also asks, honestly, whether they are in danger.

There were two things it feared.

**The first was the height.** Nothing underneath explains it. The country holds itself up by root and water and the habit of height, and habits can fail. A continent held up by habit, two or three miles over nothing, seemed to the power a precarious thing to love.

It could have looked. It could have gone through the weave in an instant and measured every strand. Instead it did what they do. It lay its palm flat on the moss and listened.

It listened for a long time: many turns of the fog, down and up. It heard the eels ticking in their channels and the water moving slowly and constantly through the capillaries, wicking up and seeping down. Once, very far off, it heard the *whumph* of an eye letting go.

It kept listening, and slowly it understood the sound. The eyes that collapse are where pressure is released. They are where old weave softens so new weave can grow into the space. The country does not hold itself up in spite of those small fallings-in. It holds itself up by means of them, by letting a little of its shape go, somewhere, once a year, forever. The heartbeat is the holding.

The height was sound. It would last until the last pebble came up, and perhaps long after.

**The second fear was the stones.**

The power counted. It could do that without disturbing anything. It counted the pale pebbles still rising through the weave from the old mountains, and it counted the people, and it worked out the arithmetic. Each life takes one stone and sends it down. Each ring slowly swallows its stones. The mountains are gone, and whatever they left is finite.

It looked like a slow ending. Some far day a child would walk with her eyes on the sward, waiting for a pale point to break the moss, and no point would come.

So the power followed a stone. It chose one pressed into the root-wall of an eye long ago, well below the lowest ring of flowers. It followed that stone the way the stone itself moved, with a patience even a power has to work for. The stone went down the wall of the eye. It was taken into the deep channels, where water seeps toward the edges and turns, and where the slow churn of the weave, which has been lifting stones for longer than there have been hands, carries it on. It rose for a very long time. At last, far from where it was given, it broke the surface of the sward as a small pale point in the moss.

The given stones and the found stones are the same stones.

Not every one comes back, but enough do. The country will not run out while there are people to give. A child's first stone is found and never given. That is true, and it is also not true. Someone gave it, long ago, to the deep, telling it *go home*, and the deep is the kind of home that sends you back.

The power did not tell them. Perhaps they already know. Perhaps that is what they are listening to when an eye falls in and they put their palms down to the moss. Either way, it was not the power's news to deliver.

## IV. What It Learned

It stayed. Powers have time.

It had no walking partner, so it slept among the grazers, moving at their shuffle along the pale paths with its head low. On the third or fourth night a heavy old female leaned against it as they walked, and it let her. A filmy fern seeded itself on the power's shoulder from her bristle, and it let that too.

It walked one of the old sentences. The line was buckled by a century of rising and settling, so reading it felt like reading a hand that shakes. It did not understand the sentence. It felt it: a long patient curve, a turn no grazer would make, a crossing of three bright patches. It came to the end knowing only that it had been spoken to.

It stayed long enough for the flowering. When the country turned violet and the air smelled of wet pepper and honey, it did not go to any ring where people gathered, because a stranger would be noticed. It found one of the fresh rings, the forgotten ones, the ones waiting, far out on a swell where no one had come in generations. It stood inside the stones, barefoot on the violet.

It stood still.

By the second night the root-hair had threaded its ankles. It was a coolness, then a warmth, then a faint, sure pull, like being taken gently by the wrist. *Yes, you, too. You are also mine.*

It let the country hold it for as long as it could bear. Being held was not hard for a power. What was hard was being claimed by something that did not know or care what it was, and letting that be true.

Far off, through its soles, it felt the hum pass between the distant rings. When the others pulled free, it pulled free with them, alone on its own crest. The threads tore softly. Its ankles were ringed pink for weeks.

## V. The One Thing

There was a woman who had outlived everyone.

Her walking partner had lain down years before, and the one before that, and all the ones she might have leaned on through a night. There was no one left who had slept in her keeping or kept her in theirs. When she decided, she walked her last sentence far out, across a swell where no one lived, and lay down at its end.

She expected no one. She had decided that was all right.

The power had been among the columns when she passed. It watched from the fog and weighed the matter carefully, because this was exactly the kind of moment in which powers do harm with good intentions. Was this interference?

In the end it saw that this was the smallest thing the country's own custom asked for. It would add nothing and change nothing. It would only fill a place that was already shaped, and that was empty.

So it came out of the fog and sat beside her, and put a hand on her chest, and hummed.

It hummed badly. It had learned the sound from grazers. She opened her eyes and saw a tall, dark shape in the fog with a fern on its shoulder. Perhaps she took it for one of her people, or perhaps she did not. She passed her stone from palm to palm once, the way someone does when they are anxious. Then she held it loosely, the way someone does when they have stopped being anxious. She closed her eyes.

She died near dawn.

At first light the power stood, because staying would mean being held too, and it had learned that. It did not pry the stone from her. Her hand had opened on its own. It took the stone. It did not keep it, though for one moment it wanted to, as badly as it had wanted anything.

It walked nine days to the nearest eye, carrying the stone in its fist. It pressed the stone with its thumb into the soft root-wall, just above the water and just below the lowest ring of pale flowers.

The flowers will climb past it. The stone will go down. In some long age it will break the moss somewhere as a pale point, and a child will find it.

## VI. The Rim

Before it left, it went to the edge.

It stood in the hiss of falling water becoming air and looked down into the white. It knew it could go down there. It knew it could know.

It had no stone. It held out its hand anyway, open and empty, over the gap. It stayed like that for a while. Then it drew the hand back.

It was their rite, done backward. They hold the dearest thing they own over nothing and do not drop it, to say *I choose to be up here.* The power held out nothing, and did not reach, to say *I choose to leave you up here.*

It left the way it had come. In the morning the plants exhaled, and the warmth rose out of the mat, and the fog lifted back into its ceiling. The power went up with it. The country surfaced behind it, dripping.

## VII. What It Left

It left one thing, and that one by accident, because anything that walks in the Held Country writes.

There is a path now from a bright swell far out on a lonely crest to the rim of an eye nine days away. It is a single pale line of moss growing back finer than the moss around it. It will last for centuries. It will buckle as the land rises and settles.

Someday someone will come across it and retrace it step by step, feeling it in their feet the way they feel all sentences. It is not in any hand they know. It turns where no grazer would turn. It is unhurried. It runs from the place where someone was held to the place where her stone went home.

It says only: *someone was carried.*

They will not know who wrote it. They will know it was written.

---

That is all the power did.

It had come with the strength to change everything. It found a country that had already solved the one problem a power might have been sent to solve: how to love what takes you. The people had solved it without power, over more generations than can be counted, with nothing but their feet and their patience and the one still thing in their hands.

It did not improve them. It listened until it was sure they were safe. It let itself be held a little, and it filled one empty place in their oldest custom. Then it let go.

It took away only a fern on its shoulder, and a faint pink ring at each ankle that took a long time to fade. It found, a little to its surprise, that it did not want them to fade.

The height remembers the mountains. Now, a little, something else remembers the height.
