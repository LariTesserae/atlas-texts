# The Guest

## It Arrives Slowly

A power that is benevolent and wise, when it notices something small and old and strange, does not rush toward it. Rushing is the first mistake. The plateau has been shaped by nothing but slowness. Anything that arrives quickly would arrive like a stone thrown into still water, and the stillness is part of what it came to see.

So it comes down the way the seeds go up, in reverse and over a long time. It settles into the cold without warming it. It lets the dark stay dark. It does not make a light, though it could, because light is a kind of question, and it has not yet earned the right to ask.

Then it waits through its first swell.

The weight comes down. The groves bow. The bladders creak like old leather. For the length of a few slow sentences, the pressure takes on a direction, and the power feels itself being *attended to*. It is being considered, lightly, the way a herder glances at a stranger on the path and decides, for now, not to mind.

Then the swell eases, the silver chains go up, and the attention is gone.

The power finds that it is moved. It had expected to be the one doing the noticing.

## What It Could Do

It could do nearly anything. That is what such a power is, and it is also the danger. So in the long quiet between swells it lays out the possibilities and looks at each one honestly.

**It could lift the ice.** It could thin the grey lid in an afternoon, drain the lake down the eastern valley, and bring the upland back into the wind at once. This is the thing the Keeping waits for. It would be easy.

But the Keeping has been listening to the melt through its cords for longer than the tally-stone could hold. Its whole being is tuned to *that* melt, at *that* pace. Doing it suddenly would not be giving the Keeping what it waits for. It would be taking the waiting away and handing back something that only looks like its end. There is also the matter of downhill. A lake that size, let go at once, goes somewhere, and it would not go gently.

**It could restore the herders.** It could reach back through the held breath and pull the people out whole, with their faces and names and cold hands. The power turns this over for a long time, because it is the most tempting option and the most wrong. The herders did not ask to be brought back. They asked for nothing. They did something: they kept. What persists is the doing, not the doers. Pulling people out of it would be like pulling the tune out of a song and calling the result a singer. And the Keeping has become its own thing, with its own long life. It is not a trapped village waiting for rescue. It is a practice that learned to stand.

**It could end the Keeping.** It could give it rest: let every held breath go at once in one great silver chain, and let the plateau fall quiet and simply drown, the way drowned places do. Some benevolence would call this mercy. The power does not know yet whether there is anything here that needs mercy, and it will not give rest to something that has not asked for it.

**It could make the Keeping permanent.** It could bind it so it never thins, never frays, and outlasts the ice and whatever comes after. That would be like freezing a hand in the act of tying a knot so it can never finish.

**It could fill the plateau with life.** Fish in the groves, grazing things on the mats, the whole dark country made into a proper lakebed teeming the way lakebeds are supposed to. But nothing here has ever been eaten. The flock is whole. That is not an oversight. It is a promise the Keeping made, and it has kept it for longer than most promises last.

**It could finish the work.** It could complete the cairns, tighten the walls, tie every knot the cords will ever need. The power sees that this would be the cruellest option of all, though it looks like kindness. Everything on the plateau was made one small leaning at a time across centuries. That slowness is not a limitation to be relieved. It is the Keeping's life. Finishing someone's life's work for them does not help them. It unmakes them.

When the power has looked at all of these, it notices that every one begins with the same assumption: that it knows what this place needs. It does not. So it sets them all aside and begins again with a smaller question. *Is anything here suffering?*

## What It Checks

It has to find out without disturbing anything, so it does what the one before it did. It joins.

In the next swell, it leans.

It is strange for a power of its size to make itself this small, to have only the weight of water to work with and only a moment to use it. It finds a loose strand of stripped fibre drifting near the dressing-stone. It presses. The strand turns a quarter-twist against its neighbour and holds. Around it, the Keeping notices. It is not surprised; it has been joined before. It makes room, the way a line of people working a field shifts to let one more in at the end.

For the length of the swell, the power feels what the Keeping feels, and it pays careful attention.

What it finds is not pain. It is tiredness, deep and grained into everything, like the tiredness of hands at the end of a long season. Under the tiredness there is something that is not quite hope, because hope looks forward and the Keeping mostly just keeps. It is closer to *readiness*: the feeling of a house with the fire banked and the door on the latch.

Then the power tests the thing it most needed to test. In the next swell, it declines to lean.

It holds still inside the pressure and presses on nothing. The Keeping moves around it, around the stone it isn't turning and the strand it isn't twisting, and then it simply lets it be. Nothing pulls at it or closes over it. When the swell eases, the power is still entirely itself.

So the joining is only ever an invitation. Everything that stayed and became part of the work stayed because, at some point, in the cold and the weight, it found that it wanted to keep something too. The power is more relieved by this than it can easily say.

## The Untied Knots

It stays for many swells, learning to read.

The knots are hard. They drift from cord to cord because the hands were never all the same. But the power is patient, which is the only credential that counts here, and slowly it learns. It learns which cords count swells and which count the speed of the melt. It learns that the ring of stones is a sighting on the palest place in the grey.

Then it learns what the counting-down cords are.

On certain lines, now and then, a knot is untied. The power watches one happen during an ordinary swell. A particular pressure, a particular *way* of pressing (this one always leaned from the left and slightly late, like someone with a stiff shoulder), works a loop open and lets the cord run smooth. Then that way of pressing is not there anymore. A moment later, high in a grove, a single bladder vents a thin chain of silver that goes up and up into the dark.

It is a herder putting down the work. One old habit, one pair of hands' worth of keeping, is finished at last and let go, choosing rest after all this time. The untied knot is the one they came in on. They take it with them.

The power understands then that the Keeping is thinning too, like the ice. Two slow things are wearing away in the dark, and nobody knows which will give out first.

This is the part a power could fix. It could reach in, strengthen the Keeping, and make certain it lasts until the lake drains, so that no keeper dies the night before the homecoming.

It does not. The ones who untie their knots are choosing, and each choice is their own. Holding them past their choosing would turn the Keeping from a thing people give into a thing that takes them. The power will not do that, even out of love, and especially not out of love.

There is a second thing it does not know and decides not to find out. The Keeping exists only in the swell. When the water goes, the swells go with it. The power could look ahead and learn whether the Keeping survives into the air, whether it wakes in the wind the walls were built to break, or whether it ends at the very moment its long wait is answered.

It chooses not to look. Some things should be allowed to be discovered by the ones they happen to. The Keeping has spent its whole existence not knowing, and keeping anyway. The power will not rob it of that.

## Downhill

After a very long time, the power finds the question it can actually help with. The Keeping cannot ask it. It has no way to ask anything. But the question is in every part of the plateau once you know to look: in the stone bowl of seed set by against a lean season, in the tallstems pressed toward the fold, in the flock made whole out of plants because there was no other flock to keep.

*The Keeping will not lose another one.* Another one. The power turns those words over. It remembers the old account: *the animals went downhill, or tried to.*

So the power does something the Keeping could never do. It leaves the plateau and goes east.

It follows the valley down, past where the peat is thickest and the lampweeds give out, into the deepest cold. It goes on past the far edge of the lake, where the ancient ice-dam still stands, and out into the light, which it has almost forgotten. Beyond that it goes down again, into a lowland world of rivers and weather and grass that the water never took.

There are people there, and they are herders.

They are not the same people. It has been far too long for that. But they walk their flocks up the valley sides in summer and down in the autumn, and they build dry-stone walls, and the walls curve the way the walls on the plateau curve. Their sheep are small and hardy and hill-bred, of a line that came down out of the mountains once, a very long time ago, running ahead of the water.

The old people among them have a story about a high pasture under the lake. In the story, the grass there is still green and the fires are still lit, and someone is still minding the fold, waiting for the flock to come back up. They tell it to children as a story about patience. They do not know it is true.

The power does not correct them. It does not tell them to go up. When the water goes, and it will, slowly, the Keeping's cords are sure of that, these people will see new ground above the treeline. They will go up, because that is what herders do with new ground. No one needs to arrange it. It is already the shape of things.

The power checks one more thing while it is here: the weight of the lake against the dam, and the valley the water will run down when it goes. The Keeping's cords say the melt is slow, and the power finds that the dam agrees with them. It will give way over seasons, not in a night. The lowland will have time to see it coming. So the power touches nothing here either.

On a thornbush at the edge of a high field, it finds a tuft of wool, caught and left behind the way wool always is. It takes that, and only that.

## The Gift

It carries the wool back up through the cold and the dark, all the way to the plateau. It waits for the great swell, the kind that makes the granite ring, when all of the Keeping is present at once and the whole tending remembers itself.

Just before the swell comes, the power lays the wool on the dressing-stone, among the stripped tallstem fibre and the fine parallel lines worn into the slate. It does not arrange it or make a sign of it. It only leaves it where fibre is left, the way you would set something down beside the work.

Then the weight comes.

The groves bow toward the fold. The bladders creak. The whole Keeping gathers, and the granite begins its long bell-tone, and the pressure moves across the dressing-stone as it has ten thousand times, finds the fibre, and begins to lean.

Then it finds the wool.

For a moment nothing happens. The pressure goes still over the stone, which the power has never felt it do before. The Keeping does not know what this is. It has twisted nothing but tallstem for longer than any count it has kept. It presses, uncertain. The fibres are fine and crimped and full of old grease, completely unlike anything that grows here.

And then, somewhere far down in all that tired, patient keeping, something remembers. It is not a face or a name. It is a pair of hands, very long ago, working this exact texture between thumb and forefinger by a fire.

The lampweeds flare. It is not the brief brightening that follows every great swell. It is a flare like no one has seen since the hearths were first lit. Every hollow on the plateau burns up at once, the colour of moonlight on snow, bright enough to light the lower bladders of the groves. For the length of the swell, the whole scattered constellation of old fires is lit again. From the high shelf near the rim, it looks exactly like a camp at night, with everyone awake.
