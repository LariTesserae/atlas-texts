# The Visitor at Orrin Low

## Arrival

It came in with the fog, because that was the kind of arrival the place could hold without noticing.

It had no body worth describing. It came the way attention comes into a room when someone who loves you walks in and you haven't turned around yet. The fog-nets hissed as they did on any good morning. The cistern filled with water that tasted of tar. On the jetty a gull stopped preening for a moment, then went on.

It had been drawn by the rings. Not the moss itself, which was only moss, but what the moss was the shape of. From far away, and it had been very far away, Orrin Low registered as a small, persistent, patterned disturbance in a place that should have been quiet. It looked like a heartbeat where no heart had been put. Things like that are rare. Most beings arise in the ordinary way, out of long lineages, fitted to their places by ages of trial. This one had been drummed into existence in a single human lifetime, by accident, out of iron and basalt and three hundred people who thought they were only making grit.

The visitor was old and it was careful. It had learned, over a very long time, that the strongest urge a powerful and kind thing feels when it meets something small and fragile is to help. It had also learned that this urge is the most dangerous thing about it.

So the first thing it did was nothing. It listened.

## A year of listening

It learned the cams first. Forty-eight of them, each cut by a different hand in a foundry far inland, no two quite alike. Cam 9 had a flat spot that made its stamp fall a hair early. Cam 31 had been replaced after the boiler failure with one from a different pattern, and it dropped a fraction late. This was one of the reasons the rings came back leaning differently. The old people were right about that, though they couldn't have known why. The sibling had been born of a new tooth.

It learned the stone: how sound ran through the young basalt, fast and clean, and how it slowed and softened where moss had deepened into something like soil. It learned where the lava tubes carried the beat like organ pipes, and where fractures swallowed it.

Beneath the flow it learned the country the flow had buried. There had been a coast there, forty-one years ago: turf, sea-pinks, a stream, petrel burrows in real earth, a stone sheepfold, a path. The lava had come down orange and eaten all of it. The visitor noted this carefully. It meant the stamps were not the first thing to eat this ground. The Walker was a mouth crossing the body of an earlier mouth, which had crossed the body of a country, which had once been sea-floor. Nothing here was the original. Everything was something's leavings, and something else's home.

It learned the people one at a time, which is the only way people can be learned. It learned the hand-grammar, the flat palm pressed down, *wait for the gap*, and came to think it was one of the wiser gestures it had seen any species invent. It learned who had left on the steamer and come back, and what they had lain awake missing. It learned the seven at the birch.

Then, with great care, because it did not want to be mistaken, it went into the timing.

## The Walker, met

The children had been right that the Walker lived in *when* rather than *where*. The visitor found it in the gaps between the beats, in the place where one stamp's echo crossed another's arrival.

What was there?

The visitor had met minds of many kinds, and this was not quite one. It had no memory in the usual sense and no wants the visitor could name. But it was not nothing. The best the visitor could do was this: it was a *tendency with a shape*. It was a leaning. When the rails settled easier on the east, and the moss grew thicker there, and the next winching went east, the Walker had not chosen anything. Still, something had been more itself going east than going west, and the whole system had moved in that direction the way water finds a slope. You could call it physics, and it was. You could call it a creature feeling for the way, and that was also true. The visitor had long ago stopped believing those two descriptions were enemies.

The Walker did not notice the visitor. It could not have. The visitor stayed in the gaps with it anyway for what, in the Walker's terms, was a long time: several thousand beats. It was the way you might sit beside a sleeping infant, not to wake it, just so that for a while it would not be alone without knowing it.

In the faintest curves at the very front, on the freshest rock, the bending inland was real. The visitor traced it. It was not a decision. It was a question. The pattern had begun to feel the edge of its food, the way a root grows thinner toward dry ground, and it was reaching toward the only thing like more that it could sense, which was the older stone inland.

A question with no one to ask it. The visitor thought that was the most poignant thing it had found on the field.

## What it considered, and refused

It could have given the animal more grit. Nothing easier. A rich seam folded into the northern lip, a century of food, and the Walker would never need to ask its question at all.

It refused. A being fed from outside stops being a creature of its place and becomes a pet of the feeder. The town's whole shape, the sledges, the following of warm water, the leaving of shells, was a response to the ground as it truly was. Change the ground in secret and every choice the people made afterward would rest on a lie. The visitor did not lie to things it respected.

It could have stopped the stamps and let the field heal faster, the gravel scar grow over, the forest come sooner. It considered this honestly, because it cared for the field too, and for the buried country, and for whatever would grow here in four hundred years. But the forest would come anyway. The scar was one strip across two kilometers of rock. The harm was real and it was bounded. Against it stood three hundred lives, tens of thousands of birds, and a new kind of creature. The visitor did not think it had the right to end that for the sake of a forest that was not yet anyone.

It could have made the Walker immortal: rigged the engine to run forever, turned the question into an answer. This was the temptation it thought about longest. It turned it down for the same reason it would never give a child a life without an ending. The Walker was young and mortal, and the visitor did not believe mortality was the wound. Dying suddenly was a wound, and so was dying unwitnessed. So was dying in a way that tore down everything that had grown around you. Ending was not.

It could have named the wader. It knew where the wader came from. It had come not on the steamer but on a storm three thousand kilometers south, a single blown-off bird that found nothing like itself here and, in its loneliness, found the slurry ponds exactly right. It had bred with a local sandpiper, which was why its children looked like no one. The visitor kept this to itself. Some things should stay unnamed while they are young. The town had not named it yet, and that meant the town was still wondering about it, and the wondering was a kind of care.

It could have revealed itself. It did not seriously consider this. A town that sickens when the ground goes quiet does not need to learn that something vast has been watching it.

## What it did

It did three small things, and it weighed each one for a long time.

**The first** was a seam in the number two boiler, a hairline crack along a rivet line. Nobody had seen it. In four years it would have split during a winter night, and the spare boiler would have been cold for maintenance, and the stamps would have stopped. The petrels would have lost their road in February, when the chicks are smallest. The visitor did not mend the crack. It waited until a fitter named Osk Harrow was working on the next plate over. Then it did something so slight it barely counted: it let a drip of condensate run along the seam and bead there, where his eye would fall. He saw it, swore, chalked it, and it was replaced within the month. The visitor held this act up to the light afterward and decided it was permissible. It had not changed what the town wanted. It had only let the town see what was already there, in service of the one thing the town had already decided with its whole heart: *not like that. Never again like that.*

**The second** was the birch.

Of the seven old people who remembered the flow, one was dying that year: Aud Selvik, ninety-one, who had been six when the sky went orange. She walked out to the birch nearly every day, slower each time. On an afternoon in autumn she sat down on the sighing moss and understood she would not walk back.

The visitor sat with her. It did not show itself or do anything she could have pointed to. It was simply there with its whole attention, and people who are dying can sometimes feel that. The pounding came up through the stone as a mutter. The moss exhaled its cold stored breath. Aud put her palm flat on the ground, pressing down, *wait for the gap*, and said aloud, to no one, "It's gone a long way without me." Then, after a while: "Good."

She was found the next morning. People said she looked like someone who had been listening to something she liked. The visitor stayed until they carried her in, because it had decided long ago that it would never let a thing it had come to love be alone at the end if it could help it. That one was not a matter of judgment.

**The third** was the fog.

One of the children who had drawn the rings from the roof was grown now: Maren Asp, thirty-one, shift line, left ear going. She still climbed up at dusk sometimes, and she still kept the charcoal sketches, eleven years of them, in a tin under her bed. She had seen the bending inland. She hadn't yet put it next to the other thing she knew, because no one had ever asked her to.

One evening in early summer she was on the roof. The fog was coming in and should have closed over the field in a few minutes and sent her down. The visitor held it back. It did not stop it, only slowed it, for eleven minutes past its time. In those eleven minutes the petrels came in low against the pale sky, flying their road, and Maren watched them bend along a line that matched the curves in her sketches exactly. She thought: *they follow the rings*. Then she remembered the three days. Then she thought about the northern lip, and sixty years, and what the birds would do on the morning the stamps stopped for good.

Then the fog came in, and she climbed down with her heart going hard.

That was all. The visitor did not give her the answer. It gave her eleven minutes of clear air at the moment when two things she already knew were close enough to touch.

## What happened after, as far as it stayed to see

Maren brought the tin to the town meeting. She laid the sketches on the table in order, and people who had pretended for decades not to know about the Walker looked at them for a long time. She did not say "animal" or "Walker." She said, "The birds find home by this. When we run out, they'll lose it all at once, like the three days, except it won't start again."

Then an old woman, one of the six remaining, said something the visitor had not expected. It was the best thing anyone said in the whole time it was there:

"They found home before the stamps. Seven years, in the quiet. There's birds out there old enough to remember that."

This was true. Petrels live a long time. A few of the oldest in the colony had nested in these tubes in the silent years, and had nested before that in the buried earth of the old coast. The knowledge of how to come home without a drum still existed, carried in a few old bodies out on the water.

"It wasn't the quiet that killed them those three days," she said. "It was the suddenness."

The meeting did not decide what to do at the northern lip. The visitor had not expected it to, and did not want it to yet. There were decades left, and a decision made too early is often only fear dressed as wisdom. But something changed that night. The town began, for the first time, to talk about *how* it might end rather than *whether*. People spoke of winding down over years instead of days: forty-eight stamps, then forty, then thirty, the beat thinning slowly enough for the young birds to learn what the old ones knew. Someone asked whether a single stamp might be kept on a wind-wheel afterward, a slow heartbeat for the petrels, for as long as anyone cared to grease it. Someone else asked about the inland rock, and whether the whole thing might turn and follow the Walker's bending and go on, thinner, poorer, alive.

They did not know. They were arguing about it when the visitor left, loudly and in short phrases, with a great deal of hand. The visitor thought this was exactly right. The question the Walker had been asking with no one to ask it finally had askers. That was the thing the visitor had wanted most, and it had not had to give it. It had only had to hold back the fog.

## What it thought, leaving

There is a kind of benevolence that saves things, and it is sometimes needed: in a flood, a fire, a fall. There is another kind that the visitor had come to trust more over its long existence. That kind sees a thing whole, once, with love, and then gets out of the way.

Orrin Low did not need saving. It was not in the kind of danger that calls for a hand from outside. It was a young, strange, mortal creature, halfway across the only food it could live on, with a question it could not yet phrase. The people inside it were capable of hearing that question, given a little clear air.

The visitor thought about the forest that would come in four hundred years, and the rusted shed, and the petrels bending their flight along a road no one could see. It found it could hold all of those futures without grief. Whether the Walker turned inland or wound down to a single stamp on a wind-wheel or stopped, what mattered was that it would not stop the way it had the first time: in the dark, all at once, with no one understanding what was dying.

It went out with the fog, the way it came.

Behind it the stamps kept falling, forty-eight of them, cam 9 a hair early and cam 31 a hair late. The moss on the fresh rock ahead drew rings. Out on the water an old petrel that remembered the quiet years turned toward the shelf, and a younger one followed it, learning.

In the gaps between the beats, where the Walker lived, nothing noticed that something had come and gone. The visitor had preferred it that way. The best help it knew how to give was the kind that leaves no mark except a slightly better question, asked a little sooner, by the ones whose question it was.
