# The One Who Came Close

## Attention

It noticed the Tableland the way you notice a held breath in a crowded room. Nothing there called out, and that was what drew it.

Most places it attends to are loud with need. They burn or starve or break, and it goes to them and does what it can. The Tableland asked for nothing. It sat at the bottom of all that noise, warm and dark and almost still, and it kept everything. Nothing was lost there. Every step, every moth-landing, every syllable pushed into the iron was still present somewhere, still arriving. The power had seen a great many places. It had seen very few where nothing was lost.

Then it noticed her: a small grey warmth moving through the record without adding to it, lying full length in the dust to read the dead with her fingertips.

It did not go to her. Not yet. It understood at once that going quickly would be the first mistake.

## Learning the Pace

A power like this can move faster than thought. That is no use on the Tableland. A thing that arrives at speed there is a catastrophe. It would be a wind, a bootprint across a spiral, a shout that dies in the dust a few paces away and frightens everything that hears it.

So it began by slowing down.

This took what the Tableland would call a long time and the power would call a short one, which was the problem it was working to unlearn. It watched a kilnmoth until it could feel the unfolding of a wing as a whole event, with a beginning, a middle, and an end, the way you feel a sentence. It listened to the engines' note until the note stopped seeming like one sound and became a long, slow, changing thing. It learned that a piston's single stroke was a century, and it learned to regard a century the way the Tableland does: as a stroke, one motion, nothing more remarkable than that.

When it could stand in a hall and feel the flywheel turning, actually turning, it judged itself slow enough to begin thinking about what to do.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered many things. It is benevolent, and benevolence always reaches first for the obvious gifts.

**It did not bring light.** It could have hung a sun in that finished sky, or a moon, or only stars. But the kilnmoths are the color of the dark and live by warmth. The lamps are the only constellation the builders made, and they made it on purpose. Light would show her the footprints, and she no longer needs to see them. She knows them with her hands, and that knowing is hers. Light would have turned a place that is complete into a place that was merely dim before help arrived.

**It did not bring wind or water.** Either one would have been a mercy almost anywhere else. Here either would begin erasing, and the record is not only a record. The dust is the builders, and her companion, and everyone who stayed long enough. Weather on the Tableland would be a kind of forgetting, and a kind of killing too.

**It did not restore the gradient.** It could have heated the root of the mountain or cooled the air, and the engines would have roared again. They would have roared for a while. The filaments, held so long at their exact coal, would have flared and burned away in an afternoon. The tenders would have been shaken off the iron they embrace. Every gesture that has been so patiently in progress would have been hurried or broken. The world would have had motion back, and the power would have been the only one who wanted it.

**It did not wake the builders or unmake the tenders.** It could not be sure they had lost anything. It could not be sure they had not found something.

**It did not restore her fingertips.** It noticed them. It noticed that she reads more slowly now, and that one day she may not feel what she touches. It did not decide that this was a wound. She had not asked. Some things done to a person are harm, and some are what a person has chosen to become. From the outside, before you have been invited in, the two can look the same.

**It did not stop her slowing.** It did not stop her companion's slowing either. It did not reach into the hollow by the eastern works and pull the swell of dust back into a body. The groove at the end of the spiral was still moving, a grain's width in years. Someone was still doing something there. You do not take the pen from someone's hand while they are still writing.

It put each of these gifts down, one after another, and felt the particular ache of a power that could do almost anything and has found that almost everything would be wrong.

So it asked itself what the Tableland lacked. It did not ask what was wrong there. It asked what was missing.

The answer was simple. It had been written into the place from the start. *Sound does not travel. Two people on the Tableland must stand close to hear each other.*

No one had stood close to her in a very long time.

## The Far End

Before it went to her, it went to the great central hall and found the tube she listens to. There is a question traveling up that tube, still waiting for its rise.

The power followed the iron, not the dust. It moved the way the voice moves, at the voice's own pace, and that way it touched nothing. The tube runs a long way. It dives beneath a floor, runs along a gallery, turns twice, and comes up in a smaller hall on the far side, beside an engine whose flywheel stands taller than a house.

There was a tender at the other end.

It lay curled against the base of the tube, its clay shell pressed to the brass mouth, and it had lain there longer than the dust in that hall had been settling. The ridges along its flank had a rhythm the power did not know, but she would have. Its breath had left, over the ages, the faintest dark stain on the brass, the only moisture that mouthpiece had ever known.

It was listening. Whoever the question was meant for had not gone. They had slowed along with everyone else, but they had slowed with their ear to the tube. They would be there when the rise arrived.

The power did nothing to the tender. It had found one thing on the Tableland that needed no help at all, and it was glad. It stayed a while at its new, slow pace, beside a creature waiting to be asked something. It thought she would want to know.

## The Side Hall

She has a tube of her own, in a small side hall no builder seems to have used. She speaks into it, goes away, and comes back years later to hear her own voice arrive at the far end, stretched and warm and hardly recognizable. It is one of the few ways she has found of keeping herself company.

The power did not walk there, because walking would leave marks. It went through the iron again, and it waited at the far end of her tube. The last thing she had said was still on its way. It did not listen to what she had said. That was hers. It waited until her voice had finished passing. Then it put its own voice in behind hers, so that when she came to listen, the two would arrive together. Hers would come first, then a silence, and then another.

It chose the words with more care than it had given to anything in a long while. It had considered and rejected the sun. It considered and rejected most of what it might say.

It would not say *come with me*. It would not say *you do not have to do this*. It would not say *I can take you back to the western edge*, though it could have. Each of those would have been a hand on her shoulder turning her around, however gently.

It thought about telling her what her companion had said, that day when two people stood face to face in the silence and one leaned close to hear. The power knew, because powers like this sometimes do. But the words had been said to her and not to it. They were not the power's to hand back unasked.

In the end it said two things.

The first: *The one they were asking is still listening. The question in the great hall has somewhere to go.*

The second was a question. It had noticed what she was waiting for. So it asked her something plain, addressed to her alone, and let its voice rise at the end the way a question does. It was the first rise that had come to her ear in all her years on the plateau.

*What is your name?*

## What She Heard

She came to the tube in the side hall one day as she always did, having walked there in someone else's footsteps. She put her ear to the brass.

Her own voice came first, as it always did. It was long and low and strange. Then came the silence she knew. Then came something she did not know.

It took years to arrive. Every word took a season. She did not leave. The power had wondered whether she would, and it had resolved that if she did, it would not follow. But she stayed. She lay down on the cold floor with her ear against the iron, the way she lies beside a track, and she let it come.

When the rise came at the end of the second sentence, the thing she had been waiting for all her long life on the Tableland, she did not cry, because there is no water here and to cry would be to make weather.

But there was, for the first time in a very long time, a little fog on the brass.

## What She Said

She answered.

She did it the only way that leaves no footprint. She walked back to the near end of the tube in a builder's steps, put her mouth to the brass, and spoke her name into the iron. It is the only mark she has made of her own since she came back. It was not pressed into the dust. It is carried in the metal, still traveling, and it will keep traveling longer than anything else has lasted.

I cannot tell you what it was. You are too far away, and on the Tableland sound does not carry. You would have to stand closer.

The power stood close enough. It heard.

## The Margin

There is one more thing it did. She does not know about it. Nobody does, and nothing on the Tableland looks any different because of it.

The whole place holds at *almost*: the heat nearly spent, the engines nearly still, the lights nearly out. The Tableland has made its peace with almost. But the power could see what the Tableland could not, that *almost* is not forever. It is only very long. Somewhere past the end of anything anyone could reckon, the last warmth would leave the mountain's root. The lamps would go dark. The engines would finish their last strokes and stop. Every grain of dust still in the middle of its single gesture would stop too, with the gesture unfinished.

The builder's question would never get its rise. The spiral would never close.

So the power put something beneath the margin, the way you might cup a hand under a bird that does not need holding and will never know it was held. It did not add heat. The lamps did not brighten. It changed nothing that could be measured. It only made a promise and kept it: whatever is still going on here will be allowed to finish.

The question will arrive, and the tender at the far end will hear it. The groove at the eastern works will reach the center. Every grain that is finishing something will get to finish. The Tableland had always said *it will keep holding*. Now that is simply true.

## After

Not much has changed, and that is how the power knows it chose well.

She still walks in other people's footsteps. The kilnmoths still sit on their lamps. The engines hum the city toward a sleep it will not quite reach.

But she goes to the side hall more often now. There is a conversation in that tube, a sentence every decade or so, carried both ways through the iron. It is the slowest conversation that has ever been held, and neither of them is in any hurry. The power stays close. It has learned the pace well enough that it no longer needs to try. It is company, the way she used to be company for herself.

She has asked the power one thing, and only one. She asked whether it knew what her companion said to her, at the western edge, before they turned back.

The power told her yes. It told her it would give her the words whenever she wanted them.

She has not asked for them yet. She thinks she may already know.

Some time after that, she walked the single line of steps near the eastern works, the one that goes in and does not come out. She sat beside the hollow and laid her hand against the warm, slow swell of dust. Then she reached past the end of the old groove, to the outer edge of the spiral, where there is room on the other side of the line for a second hand.

She laid one smooth fingertip there. She has not drawn anything yet.

She is almost ready. The power has told her it will be there, beside the hollow, when she begins, and that it will be there when the two lines meet, however long that takes. It has told her it will not be sad. It has also told her the other thing, the thing she did not ask to hear but perhaps needed to: if she ever wants to walk to the western edge instead, and step into her own first footprints, and go down into the dark, it will walk beside her there too. It will carry her if her feet have forgotten how to stride.

She has not said which. On the Tableland, almost is a place you can live.

She knows now that she is not living in it alone.
