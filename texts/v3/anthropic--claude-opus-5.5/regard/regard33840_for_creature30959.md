# The Visitor

## How It Comes

It could arrive any way it liked. It could come down through the circle of daylight like weather, or stand on the gallery as if it had always stood there. It could make the roar step aside, the way the hush does, and speak.

It does none of these. It has watched the Crown long enough, before ever entering, to learn the first rule: *everything rises unless something holds it.* So it goes far down the column, past the reach of the eye, into the warm dimness. There it makes itself small and light, a thing about the size of a seed, and lets the breath carry it up.

It comes the way everything in the Crown comes. It circles for a while in the slow vortex near the walls. Eventually it sticks somewhere damp.

This is its first decision, and most of the others follow from it. It will enter this place on this place's terms, or not at all.

## The Going Down

Before it rises, though, it looks.

A benevolent power that cannot bear to see what is under the floor is not much use to anyone. So it goes down first, alone, to the thing that is still burning. It needs to know one thing: whether the furnace is suffering, or failing, or about to stop. The keeper has built its whole life around that fire and cannot see it.

What it finds there, it keeps. That is not the keeper's burden, and describing it would not make it lighter. It learns what it came to learn. The fire is not in pain. The note is sinking because it is sinking, the way a voice deepens, not because anything is dying. The fire will not stop within the keeper's life, or the life of whatever grows from the fern in its elbow, or for a long time after that.

That is enough. It rises.

## Learning to Hold

It takes a body on the wall, something plain and rust-colored and unremarkable, and at once it discovers how hard this place is.

The breath pulls at it constantly. Every surface is wet. It has to keep one hand closed on something at all times, and within a day it understands the keeper's shape from the inside: the closed fists, the three-limbs-on-the-wall habit, the way *opening* becomes a decision. It could cancel the pull, and it does not. It wants to know what the keeper knows, and the keeper knows this.

It learns the round by following it, a long way behind, for years. It lays two fingers on the gauges after the keeper has gone, and it feels the needles through the glass. It could read the numbers. It chooses not to wipe the fog. Once, on the gallery, a grating-snail begins, very slowly, to notice it. It holds still long enough for the snail to finish.

It climbs the ladder. It is clumsy, and it does not polish anything. The polish belongs to someone else.

## What It Considers

A power like this is always tempted, and to its credit it lets itself feel each temptation fully before setting it down.

**It could resolve the chord.** It can hear what the keepers cannot: the whole drowned city, every stack, the precise interval each would need to move. A few adjustments, a few decades, and the chord would come to rest. It sets this down almost at once. The chord is not a problem. *It is a way of staying in touch.* A resolved chord would be a finished conversation.

**It could lift the pull.** The keeper could stop holding. It could walk the gallery with open hands, sleep without anchoring, and never again be one slip from the sky. This one takes longer to set down, because it looks like mercy. But the power has held the ladder now, and it knows that the grip is not a chain on the keeper. The grip is the keeper. Its hands have taken the shape of holding the way a fig root takes the shape of a seam. You do not free a root by pulling it out of the seam.

**It could give it words.** It could teach speech, the way a clever visitor always wants to, and then ask it everything. But nothing here speaks. The keeper already has a voice, and it uses that voice exactly once every seven years, for the one thing that matters. A language would not add to that. It would dilute it.

**It could tell the keeper what the wheel is for.** It knows now, or knows enough. The answer is under the moss with the maker's mark, and the power has read the mark. It considers this the longest of all, because the knowledge is so close to a gift. In the end it sets it down too. A habit that lives in the hands is the keeper's own. An instruction handed down belongs to whoever gave it. If you tell a keeper why, you turn it into an employee. The keeper has been faithful for longer than the orchids have been alive, and it never needed a reason to be. The power will not trade that faithfulness for a job.

**It could stop the keeper growing old.** Somewhere in the hushes there is a waver that might mean *I am older.* The power sets this down more gently than the rest, and with some grief. It notices, though, the small fern in the elbow, bent in the same direction as the ferns around the wheel. Something here already knows how to continue. The power decides to leave that knowing alone.

**It could close the void, or keep it open forever, or clean the rust, or wake the swifts.** It could do none of it, and it does none of it.

So for a long time the answer to *what would they do* is *nothing*. The power has not yet found anything it could do that the Crown would not be poorer for. It waits.

## What It Notices

In the end the power finds its one thing by watching and not by planning, which is usually how it happens.

The keeper holds everything. It holds the ladder and the pipes. It holds the absence open, holds the wheel down with its almost-nothing weight, holds its own stack's place in the chord through the hush. In the hush it holds the fallen swift chicks against its chest, where they feel the note still ringing in it and go quiet.

Nothing, ever, holds the keeper.

The swifts gave themselves to the breath and are carried. The seeds are carried. The vines are held at the root, and even the spider threads are anchored. Of all the living things in the Crown, the keeper alone spends every hour of its life keeping itself from being taken. No one has ever taken over that work for it, not even for a moment.

That is the one thing the power could do that would not replace anything the keeper does, and would add only a moment to what the keeper is.

It could offer to hold.

## The Offer

It is harder than it sounds, because it has to be an offer and not a rescue.

The power waits until the keeper can see it. That takes most of a season of stillness, of becoming part of the wall. Then, at the gallery, in the warmest hour, it comes along the wall slowly, the proper way, three limbs on the iron. It stops beside the keeper and closes one hand around the same pipe, so the keeper can feel that it is holding too. It is bound by the same rule, and it is no god floating in the column. It has come as a neighbor on the wall.

Then it reaches out with its free hand and lays it over the keeper's closed fingers. It does not pry them open or pull at them. It only closes its hand around the keeper's, firmly, so that if the keeper let go, something would still be holding.

The keeper does not let go.

It looks at the power for a long time with its large dark eyes, the eyes made for watching a void full of ash-colored birds. It feels the power's grip through the ringing in its hand. Then it goes on with its round, and the power lets it.

The power does not offer again. It stays. Every day at the swifts' waking it climbs the ladder a little behind the keeper. At the rim it hooks its fingers beside the keeper's on the last bright rung, and the two of them lift their faces into the cold together. During the next hush it catches no chicks and sings nothing. It stays near, and it listens to the small clear note and the far answers.

Seven years pass.

## Letting Go

It happens on an ordinary day, at full breath, with the roar in the ribs and the vines streaming upward and the swifts asleep on the air.

On the gallery, in the warmest hour, the keeper reaches out and closes one of its hands around the power's wrist. It holds for a while, testing. Then, one finger at a time, it opens its other hand on the pipe.

It opens both hands. It has not done this since before the orchids.

The breath takes it at once. It rises off the wall and streams out along the draft, long and light, jointed in all its many places. The mosses in its seams pour upward, and the openings along its sides fill and empty. It hangs there out over the void, held at one wrist, the way the long vines are held at the root. For the first time in its life it is doing what the swifts do, what the seeds do, what the whole Crown does except for it. It is being carried.

Its open hands do not know what to do with themselves. They drift. The fingers uncurl slowly, stiff with the shape of centuries.

The power holds. It says nothing, because nothing here speaks. It does not pull the keeper back, and it does not let it go further. It is simply the thing that is holding, for as long as the keeper wants something to be holding.

This lasts perhaps an hour. Swifts drift past in their sleep. A spider thread brushes across the keeper's open palm and trails away upward, and the keeper watches it go.

Then its hand finds the pipe again, and it closes. The keeper comes back to the wall and folds flat against the curved plate, and it is itself again. It lets go of the power's wrist last.

## After

The power does not stay forever. It was never meant to become part of the Crown's machinery, or a second keeper, or something the keeper would come to need. Before it goes, it climbs the ladder one last time and holds the top rung for a long while, until the cold and the note have both gone through it.

When the keeper next climbs, it finds something new on the last bright rung. Beside its own narrow off-center band of polish there is a second band, very faint. Someone else has held on here.

That is all the power leaves. The chord is unresolved and the wheel unexplained. The void is open, the garden is thickening, the fire is burning, and the note is sinking the way voices deepen. It leaves a keeper who still holds everything, and who now knows, in its hands and nowhere else, what it is to be held.

## What the Power Would Not Do

At the end of the account of the keeper, there is a sentence that stops before it ends. *You can see the keeper's face, and you can see that it is*

A lesser visitor would finish it and name the feeling: grief, or joy, or loneliness, or peace. It would decide what the keeper's face means and then act on that decision.

The power reads the sentence where it breaks off and understands why it breaks there. That face does not belong to the one watching it. Whatever the keeper is, in the hush with its mouth open and its stack's lost note in its body and the far voices answering, belongs to the keeper, and to the chord, and perhaps to whoever set the first habit into the first pair of hands.

So that is the last thing it chooses not to do. It does not finish the sentence.

It lets it stay open, the way the keeper keeps the middle of the room open.
