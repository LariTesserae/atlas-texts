# The Inspection

## What It Did Not Do

It did not come in through the ceiling.

It could have. A power of that kind can arrive however it likes. It can be a light in the concrete or a pressure in the air, or it can simply be present all at once, the way a thought is present. It could have unmade the room between one flywheel revolution and the next. It could have struck the shuttering with a word, cured the concrete in an instant, dropped every droplet at once in a single roar of water, and let the toads die in the silence that followed, decades late, all together, like a held breath let out.

Something less wise would have done that and called it mercy. *You were never meant to be. Nothing should last like this. Be released.*

But the power had looked at the place for a long time before it came, as long as the place allowed, which is to say a length of time that had no measure there. It had understood that the Green was not a sickness. The Green was a good thing that had gone on too long. Those are different, and they need different answers. You do not cure a good thing. You help it end, and you help it end *well*, and only if it can be brought to want that. Otherwise you leave it alone and keep watching.

So it came up the stair.

## The Stair

It came the way the crew came, from inside the mountain, past the rust flowering on the handrails and the pit a drip had worn into a step. It paused at the flickering light. It watched the light fail and recover and fail, and it did not steady it. The flicker was honest, and it wanted to carry some honesty upstairs.

It had taken a shape for the climb. Not a grand one, and not one meant to deceive. The Green knew work, and the power wanted to be legible to it. So it wore boots and a canvas coat, and carried a clipboard, a steel tape, a pencil behind one ear, and a small hammer on a loop at the belt. It was the shape of someone every pour on every site eventually waits for: the one who comes after the crew has gone, walks the work, and says whether it is right.

At the top of the stair the cold thickened and the walls began to sweat. The note came up through the steps into its heels. It let the note in. It did not armour itself. It had come to listen, and you cannot listen to a thing while refusing to let it touch you.

It turned the wheel. The wheel turned without resistance, as it always had. The Green has never kept anyone out.

## The First Hour, Which Was Not an Hour

For a long while it did nothing but stand in the doorway.

This was the most important part, and the easiest to skip. The Green had been born in a doorway, in one tired breath of *there, it's done, it's right*. Everything that came after was the Green trying to keep someone standing where the crew had stood. So the power stood there, and it did not stand there to be polite or to set a trap. It stood there because it wanted to see what the crew had seen.

It saw it.

The forms had held. Carrying concrete up through the inside of a mountain in thin air is brutal, and every bag and barrow is a small war with gravity and breath. The ties were set true, and the wedges driven by hand, each one struck by someone whose arms were already shaking. The surface had been floated flat at an altitude where your hands go numb in the mixing water. The steps were sharp and square. The drainage channel was cast to a fall so fine it could only have been checked with a level and patience. It was good work, *very* good work, done by people who would never be thanked for it, in a room no one was ever meant to look at.

The power let itself feel that fully. Then, into the note, it said the thing a witness is for:

*Yes. It's right.*

The note did not change. But every cricket antenna in every crack of the formwork swung toward the doorway and held still. Across the ceiling the moths, pale and flat, seemed to press themselves a little closer to their drops. The power felt the grip tighten. It was not a threat. It was hope, anxious and enormous: *You see it. Then stay. Stay and keep seeing it.*

It did not answer that yet. It stepped inside.

## The Governor

It went to the machine first, because it had noticed something from the doorway and wanted to be sure.

It stood beside the casing and looked up at the flyweight balls spinning on their spindle, held out at their constant angle. It put a hand on the cast iron and felt the governed speed come through the metal, exact and unwavering. It looked at the gauge with its needle resting on one number and no dust on it.

Then it spoke to the Green, not in words, but in the plain way a good foreman speaks to an apprentice who has done something almost right.

*This is not what a governor does.*

A governor does not hold a speed by refusing to move. That is a brake, or a jam, or a corpse. A governor holds a speed by moving *constantly*. When the load comes on, the engine slows, the flyweights fall inward, the throttle opens, and the speed recovers. When the load comes off, the engine races, the weights fly out, the throttle closes, and the speed settles. The balls ride up and down all day long, always a little off and always correcting. The needle trembles. The note wavers so slightly that only the machine itself would ever know. Its steadiness is made entirely of small changes.

*You chose it as your companion because you thought it wanted what you want. It doesn't. It never did. It wants to stay true, and the only way anything stays true is by answering what happens to it. You've made it perfect, and in making it perfect you've stopped it from working. Whatever it was built to regulate, and I think it was the water, the flow up from the valley, the mountain's slow breathing, it has not regulated anything since the morning you woke. It has only spun.*

The power did not say this as an accusation. It said it the way you would tell someone gently that the person they have been clinging to in the water is also drowning.

The flyweights did not move. But somewhere in the room, for the first time in the long history of the place, a single toad was a fraction late in filling its throat. It happened once. It was barely perceptible, a grain of grit in the note, and then it was gone.

## The Inspection

Then it did its job.

It took the pencil from behind its ear and the steel tape from its belt. It walked the room the way an inspector walks a pour: slowly, touching things, crouching, frowning, nodding.

It pulled back a pine board and looked at the print of the grain in the concrete. It saw that the print matched the board exactly, like a key, and it noted, fairly, that the formwork had held without a hair of movement. It sounded the wall with the little hammer, and the tap came back dense and even, with no voids and no honeycombing. It laid the tape along the drainage channel and checked the fall. It checked the steps with the edge of the clipboard. It walked the chalk lines of the setting-out: the centre lines, the circled datum, the stencilled pour number.

It did not read the date. Not yet.

When it came to the rebar stubs standing proud of the floor, mill-scale blue and waiting, it stopped for a long time. It knelt and put a finger on one.

*These are the only true thing in the room,* it said to the Green. *You keep them perfect as if they were the point. They aren't the point. They're the promise. The crew left them here because they meant to come back. They meant something to be built on this. When they stepped into the doorway and felt that breath of done, it wasn't "done forever." It was "done, and ready." Ready is a kind of done that faces forward. You kept the done and threw away the ready, because you were afraid of what forward meant.*

The note held. But around the kneeling figure, in the nearest tray, the ripples from the calling throats had begun to fall out of their perfect lattice. They were fraying at the edges, crossing a little wrong.

The power stood up and wrote on its clipboard.

Every pour, on every site, ends in the same small ritual, and the Green had never received it. The crew had stepped out, exhausted, meaning to come back. The inspection never came. No one ever walked the work and signed it off, so no one ever released it to the next stage. The Green had been waiting in the green hour for the one thing that turns a pour from *just finished* into *finished*. It was waiting to be *passed*.

The power wrote on the form: *Pour No.*, then the stencilled number from the floor. *Location: Station Nine, crown chamber. Formwork: sound. Concrete: dense, true to line and level, no defects observed. Workmanship:* It paused over that line, then wrote *exemplary*, underlined it once, and felt no need to do more.

*Passed for next stage.*

Then it came to the space for the date.

## The Date

It crouched by the chalk. The date there said today, of course, the power's today, borrowed from it as it had been borrowed from everyone.

It did not read it aloud, and it did not read it silently either. Instead it did what no one had ever done in that room. It looked past the borrowed date to the real one underneath, the day the concrete was actually poured, the morning the crew carried the last barrows up through the mountain. The power could see that date as easily as you can see the bottom of clear water. It was a specific Tuesday, long ago, in late autumn. It had rained in the valley and the reservoir had been high.

It wrote that date on the form.

Then it spoke to the Green as plainly as it had spoken about the governor:

*This is your day. Not today. That day. You think that if you have a date you'll be over, and that being over means being gone. It doesn't. Things that have a date can be remembered. Things that are always today can't. Nobody can remember something that never stopped happening. You've spent all this time borrowing everyone else's today because you were afraid of having a yesterday. But a yesterday is how you get to be kept.*

*The crew didn't leave you here. They took you with them. Every one of them went down that stair carrying this breath of done, and carried it into the rest of their lives. It's in how they stood afterward. It's in the next pour and the one after that. It's in how one of them, years later, told his granddaughter that the hardest thing he ever built was on top of a mountain, and it was right, and nobody will ever see it, and it doesn't matter. You didn't need to hold the moment here. It was already held. You've been guarding a copy.*

*I've signed it off. I've dated it. The pour is passed. What happens now is up to you. I won't strike the forms and I won't drop the water. I'll wait.*

And it waited.

## The Waiting

How long it waited is hard to say, because the room still had no way of saying. It may have been a moment, or a year of moments that were all the same moment. The power did not mind. It sat on the edge of a step, which was sharp enough to cut a hand, with the clipboard on its knee and the glove on the wall across from it, stiff with cement, fingers curled. It breathed at its own pace and not with the throats. It was very careful about that. It stayed exactly itself, warm and slowly spending, a small steady source of *after* sitting in the middle of the Green's *now*. It did not push. It simply did not match.

Things happened, or began to.

The flyweight balls dropped. They fell by a millimetre, perhaps, then rose, then dropped again. It was the first hunting motion that governor had made since it was built, and after it came the long slow search of a mechanism trying to remember how to work. The pale disc of the flywheel shivered. The needle on the gauge, which had never moved, moved. It fell a hair and swung back and trembled, and all at once the dust that had never settled on it began to exist, faintly, in the lamp's orange light.

The note wavered. The iron fundamental and the metal partial a fifth above it slid a little apart and back together.

The toads fell out of time.

It did not happen all at once, and it was not a failure. It was more like a choir coming apart into singers. One throat, then several, then whole trays began to fill on their own schedules, quick and private, each animal keeping its own time. The single voice broke into thousands of voices, and the thousands did not agree. They rose and fell. They argued. Some stopped to breathe. Some started up again louder. It was a summer night in a marsh, somewhere low and warm, and it had not been heard in that room ever.

On the ceiling, a moth lifted its wings.

It did not fly far, only from one patch of concrete to another. But its drop, the fullest and roundest drop on the whole ceiling, the one it had been watching longest, was left alone. It gathered one last molecule of weight.

It fell.

The power heard it hit the floor. It was a small flat sound, entirely ordinary. Every moth turned toward it, and every antenna stopped. Every throat faltered, the whole marsh at once, for the length of one heartbeat.

The power, sitting on its step, said quietly, *Yes. That's what happens next. And look, it's still right.*

The note came back. But it did not come back fuller than before. It came back as it actually was: an old machine governing water, and a great many toads singing. Two sounds, not one.

The drop stayed on the floor.

## Afterward

It did not all happen quickly. The power had not wanted it quickly.

The concrete began to cure. Over the following weeks, and they were weeks now, with a before and after, the grey-green paled toward white, and the damp alkaline chill finally gave up its last heat and became only cold. The sawdust in the corners dried and began, at last, to smell of rot and then of nothing. The shuttering was never struck by anyone, but the pine boards shrank as they dried, and one by one they pulled away from the concrete with small sounds like knuckles cracking, and leaned, and some fell. Where they had been, the grain stayed printed in the wall. No board fit its print exactly anymore. The keys had worn. That was all right.

The rebar stubs rusted. It began as a faint orange bloom at the tips, spreading the way rust spreads on the handrails downstairs. The power had considered that carefully before it came. It had decided rust was not a defeat. Rust on starter bars means *still waiting*. It means *someone should come back and build on this*. That is a better thing for an unfinished job to say than *nothing has happened here*.

The drainage channel began to flow, a thin sheet of water sliding at last toward the grate at the far end, down the fall the crew had set so carefully. It proved the fall was right. It had always been right. No one had seen it work until now.

In the grate, at the base of the fern, rooted in wet sawdust, a fiddlehead appeared, and then another. One of the three old fronds went brown at the tip, and then yellow, and in the spring it was gone, and there were five new ones, and the fern was not the same fern and was very much alive. The band of moss began to creep, first one board, then two, following the damp.

The midge column over the door thinned, wavered, lost its perfect shape, and became what midges are, a loose drifting swarm that rose and fell with the warmth of the lamp. The toads ate from it, and it was smaller afterward.

And the toads, which had not bred in all the time the Green held them, bred.

There was spawn in the trays one morning: long clear strings of it with black dots inside, laid in the standing water that stood no longer, because the water drained now and refilled from the weeping joints, the way water should. Tadpoles came, and then little toadlets with tails, and old toads too, now. Some of the original thousands died that first year, quietly, in the corners and in the trays. They had been owed that death a long time, and they took it the way animals take a thing that is theirs. The power had not wished it for them. It had not spared them from it either. It had only made it possible again for their lives to have a shape, and a shape has an end.

Not all of them stayed. The door has always opened easily. Some nights, now, it stands ajar, left that way by someone. A line of small grey-green bodies has been seen going down the stair, one step at a time, down past the rust and the pit in the step and the flickering light, down through the mountain toward the valley and the reservoir where they came from. It is a very long way. Some of them do not make it. Some do.

## The Valley

There was one more thing to do, and the power did it last.

It took the glove off the wall. The canvas was stiff with cement, and the fingers were curled. When it lifted the glove, the fingers did not uncurl. They were set hard, and it would have been a lie to pretend otherwise. It carried the glove as it was.

It went down the stair and out, all the way down, farther than anyone from Station Nine had gone in a long while, to the valley and a small town near the reservoir, to a room in a building where very old people are looked after.

The shuttering carpenter was there. He was very old. For most of his life he had not known what day it was. He had asked people and nodded and forgotten, and asked again. His mind was clear in every other way. His family had long since learned to write the date on a whiteboard by his bed each morning, and he read it each morning like someone reading news from a country he had never visited.

The power did not wake him or explain anything. It set the glove on the chair beside his bed where he would see it. When it left, it left the door a little open, the way it had left the other one.

In the morning he woke and looked at the whiteboard, and knew already what it said. He knew it was a Thursday. He knew it was the first Thursday of spring and that it had rained overnight, and that the reservoir would be high. He lay there for a while and simply had it, *today*, his own, after so many years. Then he saw the glove, and he laughed, a real, cracked, astonished laugh. He knew it was his. He knew the fingers had set that way because he'd taken it off to read the chalk better. He knew that he had never finished standing up.

He stood up. It took a while. His knees were eighty-one years old.
