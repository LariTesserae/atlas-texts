# The Eighteen-Month Contract

## I. How it was noticed

It was not the heat that drew the attention, and it was not the cloud. There are hot places everywhere, and low clouds, and small towns built on top of something that would kill them if they stopped paying attention.

What drew it was the repetition.

To a power of that kind, the world does not look like objects. It looks like attention: who is attending to what, and how, and for how long. Most of the world, seen that way, flickers. Attention lands and lifts, lands and lifts. People glance at things. But from a gray tongue of rock in a shallow bay there came a small, steady signal, the same act of care performed once an hour, every hour, for longer than a lifetime. *Pressure, temperature, output. North pond, south pond. Ceiling at the lamp.* It was like a candle that had been lit in a window decades ago and never allowed to go out.

The power turned toward it the way you turn toward someone humming in the next room, a tune you almost recognize.

It looked and saw the crews, the swifts, the crabs coming up at dusk. It saw the clerk with her father's handwriting. Then it saw what the long-stayers call *it* and *the point*: a birth that had been paused at the moment of the scream and had learned to breathe inside that moment.

The power had seen many things in its long attending. It had not seen this, and it was moved.

## II. What it was tempted to do

A benevolent power is not a safe thing to have looking at you. Benevolence with reach is always tempted to *improve*. The power knew this about itself, and so before it did anything it sat with each temptation in turn and looked at it honestly.

**It could make the balance permanent.** This was the first temptation and the strongest. The Works depended on a company, and companies depend on ledgers kept far away in rooms without steam, by people who have never felt the drone in their back teeth. Someday a figure would be revised. Someday the causeway would need repairs that cost more than the output was worth. The power could quietly make sure that day never came. It could reinforce the casings, stabilize the reservoir, and arrange the accounts so that Kettle Point stayed Kettle Point until the sun went out.

It looked at this a long time before it put it down. What it saw was that the point was not held by a mechanism. It was held by people choosing, hour by hour, to hold it. The valve wheel turned a hair and stopped. An older hand lowered the air in front of a new man's eyes. That choosing was not scaffolding around the living thing. It was part of the living thing, as much as the pump or the cloud. A moment held by care is one kind of creature. The same moment held by force, or by guarantee, would be a different creature, and a smaller one. It would be a specimen in a jar.

The seventh sign presses down gently. It doesn't bolt anything in place.

**It could let it finish.** The power could see, as plainly as Maud's drawings showed the eleven-day wave, what the point would become if the holding stopped. Soil would come, then lichen, then shadows on the gravel. The rock would round under the surf. A name would arrive. The power did not think that future was a tragedy. Most of the world is land that finished being born, and much of it is beautiful. But finishing the point was not the power's to hurry, any more than holding it was. It was not its birth.

**It could wake it.** This was the subtlest temptation. The power could reach down forty meters into the unfinished heat and give the moment a self, a voice beyond the shriek of Well Four, and eyes to look at its own gray skin from the inside. It could let the point *know*.

The power considered this and felt something close to horror at how kind it would have seemed. The thing was young, as young as its rock. It was not lacking a mind the way an injured person lacks sight. It was simply at the age it was. Giving an unborn thing awareness of itself, and of the long years in which it might never arrive, and of the ledgers far away that decided whether it lived, would not be a gift. It would be a burden laid on something too young to carry anything.

So the power put all three temptations down. What was left was smaller, and harder, and more like work.

## III. The trolley

It came across the causeway on the rail trolley one morning, with a duffel on its knees.

It had taken a shape that would not be looked at twice: middling height, middling age, the sort of face you forget before the trolley reaches the fence. It signed the contract in the office. It was eighteen months, the same as everyone. Maud Eskil took the paper, looked at the signature, and filed it without comment. The power noticed that she did not look at the name. She looked at the hand that had written it, the way she looked at everything, reading it for the drift.

They issued it a boiler suit and two handkerchiefs. A man from the second shift showed it the six signs: *valve, gauge, hot, come here, stop, tea.* It learned them badly on purpose, because a new hire who learns them perfectly in an hour is someone to be wary of.

It learned the pipes. It burned its forearm on the saddle outside the machine shop that everyone burns their forearm on, and kept the blister. It learned which footbridge had the loose tread. Within a month its fingertips had wrinkled and stayed that way, and it had the crouch in the shoulders going through the separator yard. It sat on the balcony at midnight in an undershirt with its feet on the iron railing and did not talk, and the geckos worked the lamp over its head.

The power could have learned everything about Kettle Point in the time it takes a relief valve to reseat. It chose not to. Some things are only known by being learned slowly, and it wanted to know the point the way the long-stayers did, not the way a ledger does.

In its third month a new man next to it reached for a valve wheel because a needle was sitting a hair off its mark. Without thinking, the power lowered its flat hands through the wet air. The new man stopped. Across the gantry, Tobiah looked up from his wrench, looked at the power for a long moment, and went back to work.

That was when it knew it had been accepted, or at least noticed.

## IV. The things it did

It did three things in the eighteen months. It did not do them all at once, and none of them was large.

### The casing

Forty meters down, where the heat is, the power could feel what no gauge could show. The steel casing of Well Four had a flaw, a thinning where decades of acid condensate had been working at one weld. The log books would never have caught it, because it was not the kind of change the numbers see. It was the other kind. In six years, perhaps eight, the weld would let go, and it would not be a three-second shriek. It would be a blowout, a column of steam and rock through the separator yard at fifty degrees and far more, on whatever shift happened to be standing there.

The power could have mended it in a heartbeat, from the balcony, without setting down its tea.

It didn't, and not from coyness. Mending it invisibly would have made the power the true keeper of Kettle Point, the secret thing holding the balance behind the backs of the people who believed they were holding it. It did not want to take that from them. It also did not want anyone to die.

So one evening, as the crabs came up, it went and stood near Tobiah at the edge of the separator yard. It said nothing. It turned its head a few degrees toward Well Four and waited, exactly the way Tobiah waits. After a while Tobiah turned his head as well. He stood a long time. Then he walked over and laid his palm on the wellhead, the way a doctor lays a hand on a belly, and his face changed.

Two weeks later there was a work order in the office, in Maud's hand, and a crew came across the causeway with a cutting rig. Well Four was shut in for nine days. It was the first time in the log books, going back past Maud's father, that the relief valve didn't lift at the crest of a breath. Everyone on the point felt it, the missing shriek. The frogs called a little louder that week, and nobody slept well.

When the well came back and the valve lifted again on the next crest, the whole first shift stopped on the gravel pads and listened to it like people hearing a baby cry after a long labor. The power stood among them, crushing crabs underfoot, and did not look up either.

### The pump man

The old pump man who works the seaward house alone on the night shift had not crossed the causeway in anyone's memory. The power learned why by sitting with him. It brought tea at the moment he would have asked for it, which is how you are let into the seaward house. It sat on the upturned crate by the door and listened to the reciprocating pump thud at the pace it has always kept.

He did not think of himself as tending the pulse. He thought of himself *as* the pulse, or at least as the part of it that could get sick. If he left, even for a night, he believed something would slip. After forty years that belief was so worn in that it had stopped being a belief and become part of his body.

He was also dying, slowly and not unhappily, of the ordinary things that kill men who spend forty years in fifty-degree steam.

The power did not heal him. It had considered it and decided it was not asked. What it did was sit on the crate for eleven nights, a full half-breath, learning the pump. It learned the thud and the small hesitation after the thud that nobody else heard, and the way the old man's hand drifted toward the governor at the top of each rising breath without ever touching it. It learned the job the way he had learned it, until he began, without either of them remarking on it, to let the power's hand drift instead of his.

On the twelfth night the power said, "You could go out."

He looked at it for a long time.

"To the end of the causeway," it said. "Just to look. I'll keep it."

He didn't answer. But at two in the morning he stood up, left the seaward house, and walked out along the crushed basalt and rail, slowly, in his boiler suit, with a handkerchief in his collar. He walked past the fence and out of the halo of the last lamp. He kept going until the vapor thinned over the water and the light turned yellow, and then the yellow turned to ordinary dark with stars in it, which he had not seen in forty years.

Then he turned around.

From the end of the causeway, Kettle Point looks like a white cap resting on the water, faintly lit from inside, breathing. He had been the heartbeat of that thing for most of his life, and he had never seen its face.

He stood there until the sky began to gray. The pump never missed. When he came back he sat down on the crate beside the power, and neither of them said anything. After a while the old man put his flat hands out over his knees, palms down, and lowered them. The power understood that this time the sign meant *thank you*, and also *it's all right now*, and also *when I'm gone, you won't be what keeps it. Someone will be, but not you.*

The power nodded, because that was true, and it had never intended otherwise.

He died the following spring, which is to say on a day that looked like every other day under the dome. A young woman from the second shift took the seaward house. She had spent many nights on the crate by then, learning the hesitation after the thud.

### Maud

The third thing was the hardest, because Maud Eskil needed nothing fixed and nothing shown to her. She already saw more of the point than anyone alive.

The power came to the office many times over the months. It brought tea, and later brought nothing, just sat. It watched her write the hour. In time she showed it the drawings: the back pages of old ledgers, dots by the tenth of a degree, the long shallow wave rising for eleven days and falling for eleven days, with the lifts of Well Four clustered on the crests like birds on a wire.

"The period drifts," she said. "The height never changes. I've never known why it drifts."

The power could have told her. It could see the whole thing at once, the reservoir and the rock and the convection and the crews. But it didn't think the answer was its to hand over. So it asked her to plot one particular month from the back of her father's last ledger.

She did it that night, with the brown ink. It was the month her father died, in the dormitory, with his hands flat on the blanket.

The wave had lengthened. It was only a few hours, but in her whole plotted record it had never stretched so far so fast. The next crest had come late, as though the point had held its breath.

Maud sat a long time with that page. Then she went back through the years and found others: the week the old canteen woman died, the week a man was scalded in the yard, the week, forty years back, that a long-stayer named in the margins only as *R.* stopped appearing in the shift rota. Each time there was a small lengthening, the breath holding, and then going on.

She had spent her whole life believing she was keeping the point. She had never let herself suspect that it might, in whatever way something so young can, have been keeping them. It had noticed when they left.

She didn't cry. She is not someone who cries. But she laid her free hand flat on the page, over the stretched wave, and kept it there a long while.

Later, when she was ready, she asked the question the power had known she was carrying. She had carried it since the day her father died, and never said it aloud because there was no one on the point to say it to.

"Who writes it after me?"

The power told her the truth, which was that it didn't know. Perhaps a daughter or a son she hadn't had yet. Perhaps someone from the rotation who fell off the rotation. Perhaps no one. A day might come when the ledgers are closed, the company's rooms far away decide what they decide, and the wells are shut in for good.

"Then it finishes," Maud said.

"Then it finishes," the power said.

She looked at it, and the power saw that this was the fear underneath all the others. It wasn't really about who would write the lines. She was afraid that if the holding ever stopped, she and her father and all the long-stayers would have failed at the one thing they had given their lives to.

So the power said the one thing it had come to believe in its eighteen months under the dome. It said it plainly, because it had learned by then that Kettle Point does not do well with shouting.

"It only breathes in," it said. "It's been breathing in since the lava hit the water. Nothing breathes in forever. Holding it isn't wrong. What you've done is beautiful, and you should keep doing it for as long as it's yours to do. But if the day comes, it won't be a death. It will be the exhale. It will be the rest of the breath it started when it screamed. You'll have kept it company through the longest inhale in the world. That isn't a failure. That's what it looks like to love something that isn't finished."

Maud said nothing for a long time. The drone came up through the floor. Somewhere out in the yard, the breath not yet at its crest, the relief valve stayed quiet.

Finally she said, "My father made the sign on his deathbed. Hands down. I always thought it meant *hold it*."

"It does."

"I think it also meant *let it*," she said. "When it's time."

The power didn't answer, because the answer was hers, and she had found it.

## V. What it did not do

It did not make the Works permanent. It did not touch the company's ledgers. It did not wake the point, give it a voice, or explain it to itself. It did not make Maud immortal or the old pump man young. It did not set the long-stayers' watches. It did not name the point.

It did not tell the crews what it was. Some of them guessed something. Tobiah certainly did. He never said a word about it, and the power respected him for that more than for almost anything.

It did not stay past its contract.

## VI. The fig

On its last night the power went to the canteen door, where the stunted fig tree drips and fruits in the season that is always *about to*.

It had noticed early on, as powers notice such things, that the figs never set seed. There is no wasp on the point to carry pollen into the closed flower, and there never has been. The tree ripens anyway, a whole lifetime of ripening toward nothing, simply because ripening is what a fig does in the last hour before something.

It would have been easy to bring the wasp. It would have been a small thing, and lovely: seedlings in the cracks, a second fig, and then a third.

The power stood a long time in the warm rain and decided against it. It was not a bad idea. But if a fig ever does set seed at Kettle Point, that ought to mean something, and what it ought to mean is that the point has begun, on its own, to finish. It ought to be the first sign of the exhale. That sign belonged to the point, not to a visitor on an eighteen-month contract.

So it picked one ripe fig, which is permitted to anyone, and ate it standing in the doorway while the crabs came up in their thousands and the frogs slid into time with the pump. It was very sweet, and a little sulfurous, like everything there.

## VII. The trolley back

In the morning it took the trolley back across the causeway with its duffel on its knees. The contract was up, and that is the rule.

Maud came to the office door to see it off, which she had never done for anyone. She didn't wave. She raised both hands, flat, palms down, and lowered them slowly through the wet air.

The power made the sign back.

At the end of the causeway, where the vapor thins and the light goes yellow and then ordinary, it turned around, the way the old pump man had, and looked at the white cap resting on the water, faintly lit from inside and breathing in.
