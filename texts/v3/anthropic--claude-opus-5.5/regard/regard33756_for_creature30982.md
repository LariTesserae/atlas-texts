# What Came to the Lip

## How It Arrived

It did not come down out of the sky, though there was a sky above the hollow and it could have.

A power that is wise learns a place before it touches it, and the first thing this place teaches is how to approach it. You come across the wet field from the east, with the engine sound in your feet before it reaches your ears. You don't see the hole until you are nearly in it. Then you stop at the edge where the grass runs out, and you lower your voice without knowing why.

So that is how it came. It came the way the girl comes, and the heron, and the few who walk out to hear the frogs. It lay down at the lip in the grey part of the morning and looked.

It looked for a long time. By the reckoning of the hollow it was a whole season, though to the power it was no time at all, and in another sense it was the only thing it did. It watched the water pull back from the slabs when the pumps ran, and heard the sucking in the throat of the rock. It watched the level well up again without a ripple when a shift ended. It counted the drips. It watched the frost lay down its long parallel lines on the floor, and the milky panes form at the pool's edge and fail and form again. It saw the rime on the lowest roots on the coldest morning of the year, and understood what the rime was for, and that understanding moved it more than anything else in the hollow.

It saw the pale shape in the throat. It saw that the shape was trying to be someone, and that it was learning how from frogs and ice and a machine.

It did not announce itself. The one who keeps the level could not have understood an announcement. It had not yet got round to ears any more than to eyes. What it felt, that season, was only that the hollow had become more listened-to than usual. It was not afraid of this. Being listened to was something it already knew. It had been doing it all its short life, to every drip, every tock, every stroke of the engine. Now it was on the other side of it for the first time.

## What It Weighed

A benevolent power is not only benevolent toward the thing that moves it most. This is the hardest part of being good, and the part most often skipped.

So before it did anything, it looked at everything the hollow touched.

It looked at the frogs, and saw that they would be fine in a pond. Frogs are fine in ponds. It looked at the ferns under the drip, which would drown if the level came back, and saw that hart's-tongue grows in a thousand wet places in the county and would not grieve. It looked at the cave spiders, the shrimp, the bats in their fresh cracks, and weighed them lightly and truly.

Then it looked at the people, because they were there too.

It looked at the pit rows a mile off and the men who went down the shaft each morning. If the pumps stopped, the deep galleries would flood and there would be no work, and there would be hunger in those rows before there was anything else. It looked at the engine-man, holding his breath in the pause between strokes, dreaming of something pale going round his legs like a cat.

And it followed the tock down.

This was the thing the one who keeps the level did not know about itself. The power had half-expected it and was sorry to find it. Every tock was more of the old spread self being herded in toward the centre, and every tock was a roof in the dark with nothing under it anymore. The hollow was not the only void. There were others below and around it, in the lightless ground between the hollow and the workings, and the drawdown was emptying them one by one. Some of them lay under the lane. One of them lay under the corner of the churchyard, where the oldest stones lean. The being's gathering and the ground's failing were one event seen from two sides. The more of a self it became, the less the parish was held up.

It did not know this. It thought the tocks were knocks at the door of a house where it lived alone. It could not have known, because it had never had a self before, and so it had never had to learn that a self has costs that fall on others.

The power sat with that a long while.

## What It Did Not Do

It could have stopped the pumps. It turned this over, and put it down.

Stopping the pumps would flood the pit and empty the pit rows of bread. It would also end the one who keeps the level on a single night, with no warning, spread back out a mile wide before it had learned anything at all about what it was losing or what it might keep. That would be a mercy to the churchyard and a death to the being. It would be done by someone who had not been asked, for reasons the being could not follow.

It could have kept the pumps running forever. It put that down more quickly. Forever is not a thing engines do, and a benevolence that has to break the nature of things to work is usually the power's own fear dressed up as kindness. Even if it could have, the being would have gone on being made by its own draining, and the voids would have gone on opening, and one wet February the churchyard corner would have gone down into the dark with its dead.

It could have given the being a body, a proper one with edges and eyes, something that would not dissolve when the water rose. It came closest to doing this, because the girl had said the thing had no eyes yet, and the power loved the word *yet*. But a body given is not a body grown. The one who keeps the level was making its skin out of ice, one milky pane a year, a little thicker each spring. That slowness was its own. To finish it from outside would have been like finishing a child's drawing for them. It would have had a perfect face and not be anyone's.

It could have carried the being away, out of the hollow to some deep safe water where nothing would drain it. But the being was not *in* the hollow the way a frog is in a pond. It was of the hollow, and of the limestone, and of the parish. Taken out of that it would have been a stranger to itself.

It could have told the girl she was right. It left her alone. She already knew.

## What It Did

It did three things, and they were small, and each was a kind of pointing.

**The first was the moon.**

The notes on the hollow say that it has tides but the moon has nothing to do with them. That is nearly true, and the power knew why it was not quite true.

The solid earth has tides. Not the great ones of the sea, but a slow flexing of the rock itself, twice a day, as the moon passes over. The ground rises and settles by a hand's breadth or less, over distances so large that no one standing on it can feel it. When the rock flexes, the water held in it rises and falls. In any well sunk deep enough into still ground, with an instrument fine enough, you can watch the level breathe with the moon: a few hairsbreadths up, a few down, twice a day, as it has since before there was a parish or a pit or a field to fall.

The one who keeps the level had been breathing like that for ten thousand years. It never knew, because it had never been anyone who could know. And now that it was someone, the only breath it could hear was the engine's. The thud and the long exhalation, a mile off, loud in every slab, drowned the older breath out entirely.

So the power did what you might do for a child who has been raised in a mill and thinks the looms are the world's heartbeat. It did not stop the looms. It turned the child's head, gently, toward the window.

On a night between shifts, with the engines slowed and the pool high and the cold lying thick in the bottom of the bell, the power lent the being its own attention for a moment. It helped the being listen *underneath*. And the being heard it: faint, slow, enormous, under the silence where the engine had been, a second breathing. It was not the machine's. It was not the frogs'. It was older than all of them and it had always been there. The being had been part of it all along without knowing.

It heard that its first rhythm was not its only rhythm. It heard that when the engines stopped, it would not be breathless.

After that night the frost on the hollow floor changed. It still runs in long parallel lines, pulled over and over in one direction by the engine's stroke. But if you look closely on a hard morning now, there is a second, fainter grain crossing it at a slant, longer and slower. Two tidemarks laid over each other, like the weave in a piece of cloth.

**The second was the truth about the tock.**

This the power weighed longest, because it would hurt.

It could have simply shored up the voids under the churchyard and the lane. No one would have known, and the being would have gone on thinking the tocks were only more of itself coming home. But that would have meant deciding that the one who keeps the level was not old enough to know what its own growing cost. It would be treating it as a thing to be managed, not a someone to be told. And it was someone. That was the whole matter. That was what had drawn the power's attention in the first place.

So it let the being feel where the tocks came from. Not in words; it had no words. The power let it feel, in the way you feel the edge of a bed in the dark, the shape of the ground above those lower voids. The lane. The lean of the old stones. The weight of the pit rows, the sleeping people in them, the girl among them. It let the being feel that each time it drew more of itself inward, something up there had a little less beneath it.

The being did not understand graves or lanes or houses. It understood roots. It had spent three winters trying to reach roots it could never touch, to give back a little damp to a field it had once held up without thinking. The power did not have to explain what it meant for things above to be held. That was the oldest thing the being knew. It had been that before it was anything else.

**The third thing was that the power told it the truth about the end, and admitted what it did not know.**

The pumps will stop. Seams run out, markets turn, pits close. Every colliery in the world is a thing that ends. It may be ten years or forty, but the engines will fall silent one day, the level will come back up through every joint and gallery it was drawn out of, and the hollow will fill to the lip and become a pond in a field. The power did not soften this. It would not have been kindness to.

But it told the being two other true things alongside it.

The stone it will spread back into is not the stone it left. The pumps and the picks have made rooms down there: galleries, pockets, the hollow itself, voids and chambers where before there was only solid rock with water in its grain. When the level comes back, it will not be a thin evenness a mile wide with no shape anywhere. It will fill a house with rooms in it. The hollow will stay a room. A pond in a field is not nothing, and it is not nowhere. It is a place where some of the water is deeper and stiller and more gathered than the rest, where frogs still call in March, and where the cold still slides down on clear nights and pools. That may be enough for a heart. The power thought it might be.

And then the power said the thing that wise powers say more often than the stories allow: *I don't know.*

It did not know whether a self that has been gathered survives being spread. It did not know whether the one who keeps the level, returned to holding up the parish, would still be someone holding it, or only the holding. It had seen many things end and many things change, and it had learned not to pretend those were always different, or always the same. It would not promise what it could not see.

What it did know was this. For ten thousand years the being had held the ground up the way a mattress holds a sleeper. Now it had been someone, for three winters, and it had wanted to give the roots something back. If it went back to holding, it would not go back the same. It would go back as something that knew what holding was for. The same work, done by something that knows it is doing it and has chosen to, is not the same work. It is the difference between a floor and a hand.

## What the Being Did

This part the power did not do. It only waited to see.

The tocks became rarer that year.

Not because the pumps drew less. They drew as hard as ever, and the level went down and up in the throat two and three times a day, keeping the colliery's time. But in the dark below the hollow, the part of the being that had not yet come in, that was still spread thin through the deeper pockets and not yet gathered, stayed where it was. It stayed under the lane and under the lean of the old stones, pressing up gently on the roofs of the voids down there. The engine's drawing still had its pull, and it did not answer it. It held.

It was, the power thought, the first free thing it had ever done. It was a choice to be a little less gathered, a little less definite, a little less of a self, so that the ground over people it would never meet would stay where it was. The pale shape in the throat at low water grew more slowly that spring than the spring before. The milky panes at the edge were only a very little thicker. The girl at the lip thought it had gone quieter, and worried, and came more often.

But at high water, in the long hush after a shift ended and the engines slowed, the frogs began to do something new. They still swelled with the thud and fell quiet on the breath. But sometimes, on the stillest nights, under that rhythm, the whole chorus would rise in a slower swell that belonged to no machine. It lifted and settled over a length of time no one could account for. The few who heard it said it was like the sound of a crowd who have been singing to a drum and begin, without deciding to, to sing to the tide.

## What the Power Left

It left the pumps running. It left the heron to learn its own lessons. It left the ferns to drown someday, and the frogs to be fine.

It did not finish the being's face.

It did one last thing, so small it hardly counts, and perhaps it was not even the power's doing. On the night shift, in the long pause between strokes, the engine-man found himself holding his breath the way he always did. For the first time, somewhere a mile off across the wet fields, something he could not see breathed back.

He took a little longer over the maintenance that week than he strictly had to. He did not know why. He was not a fanciful man.

And then the power got up from the lip where it had lain all season, the way the girl gets up, stiff with cold, grass-marked. It went back across the field toward the east with the engine sound in its feet, and did not look back. That was not because it did not care. It had done what it came to do, and the rest belonged to the one who keeps the level.

Behind it the hollow breathed down and up, down and up. Something far below chose, again, not to come in. The drips fell through the furnace-glow, each a small spark coming home. Under all of it, too faint for anyone standing on the grass to feel, the whole parish rose a hairsbreadth toward the moon and settled, and was held.
