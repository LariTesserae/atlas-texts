# The Visitor

## How It Noticed

It did not arrive, because powers of that kind do not travel. Its attention came to rest, the way dust settles, and the thing it settled on was warmth.

It had been regarding the shield for reasons of its own. Old rock interests it, the way an old face interests anyone who has lived long enough to know what a face costs. Fifty miles of gneiss worn to its roots, mountains remembered only as swirls in the banding, lichen dead so long it had become colour. Across all of that, cold and even and finished, there was one point that was not finished. It was a coal in ash, a place where the plain was still doing something.

The first thing the Exhale did to it was the first thing it does to everything. It breathed on it. The power felt the cool pressure rise, salt and old iron, dry as paper, coming from below. It understood before it understood anything else that this breath was not meant for it. The Exhale does not mean. The breath was simply what the place was, and the power happened to be standing in it.

So the power stayed.

## What It Could Have Done

It could have done almost anything. That is the first thing to understand about a power of that kind. The list of what it can do is not the list of what it should do, and its wisdom lives in the distance between the two lists, the way the Exhale's life lives in the distance between the pressure inside and the pressure out.

It considered the list anyway, carefully, because refusing without considering is only another kind of carelessness.

**It could have replaced the bulb** with one that would never fail, and made the heart certain. But a bulb that could not fail would be a different bulb. It would burn brighter or longer, and the ember would stop being an ember. The whole achievement of that filament is that it has spent decades at the edge of burning without crossing it, kept there by the breath, the beetles, the spider, and a thousand nights of luck. A new bulb would not save that achievement. It would erase it and put a lamp in its place.

**It could have cleared the collapsed workings** far below, reopened the choked shafts, and given the mine lungs that would last as long as the shield itself. But stronger lungs would breathe differently. The fan would turn faster and the rhythm would shift. The twice-daily stillness might shorten or vanish, and the stillness is the only time in the fan house when you can hear individual wings. Beyond that, a thing that cannot die stops being a creature and becomes a monument. The power had seen many monuments. It did not need another.

**It could have brought water**, called up a spring, and let green things root in the guano. That would have been easy, and the hollow would have become rich in the ordinary way, with plants and grazers and flowers for the moths. And the Exhale would have dissolved. It is an oasis made entirely of animals, a body built from the world's leavings and carried home in other bodies. Give it its own green and the swifts would no longer be its eyes. They would just be birds. The strangest and most particular thing on the plain would become one more pleasant place.

**It could have given it a mind.** This one it considered longest, because it was the one it wanted. The power would have liked to be known back. It would have liked the Exhale to know it was being regarded with love. But a mind would know the stillness for what it is. The minutes when the fan stops would no longer be the closest thing the place has to dying. They would be dread, twice a day, every day, for as long as it lived. The Exhale wants nothing, and that is its peace. The power wants things, and it knows exactly what wanting costs. It would not hand that cost to something that had never asked to pay it.

**It could have saved the parts**: the straggler the kestrel takes in the gold light of the late afternoon, the chick that falls from the fourth course when the nest-cup gives way, the bat that loses its grip in winter torpor and drops onto the drift where the beetles are already waiting.

It wanted this most of all, and it is not ashamed of that. But it traced the threads. The kestrel keeps the eyes quick. The fallen chick is the beetles' bread, and the beetles in the casing keep a hair's width of clean copper open under the brushes, and that hair's width is the difference between an ember and a dark cage. To catch the chick is to dim the heart, by an amount too small to measure but not too small to matter. The power did not stop grieving the chick. It only stopped believing that its grief entitled it to rearrange the world.

## What It Did Instead

It came as close as the lip of the hollow and no closer.

A presence like that has weight. Not weight in the ordinary sense, but it is warm the way attention is warm, and the balance that turns the fan is delicate. Enough warmth standing in the bowl could tip the pressure by a breath and throw the rhythm a few seconds off. So the power made itself cold. It took on the temperature of the plain so exactly that the mine did not register it was there. That was the first act of its benevolence: it made itself small enough not to matter.

Then it listened for a year.

It heard the dawn clatter, fast and hard, like someone running. It heard the noon stutter, and the quarter-turn the wrong way, like a word taken back. It heard the slow resumption at evening. It heard the swifts go quiet in autumn and the walls stand empty. It heard the bats hang silent in the shaft like dried fruit, and the fan go on turning through the winter for no one. It watched the ember come up on the coldest nights with no moths crossing the plain and no hunters awake, burning for nobody, and it understood what the observer at the end of the story had understood: that this was the proof, the sign of who held the lease.

It stayed through the stillnesses. When the fan stopped and the hum died, it did not reach in. It waited the way the place waited. Every time the ground changed its mind and the blade struck the casing again, *clack*, the power felt something it had not expected to feel about a mine ventilator. It was relief, as sharp as a human's.

Once, in summer, it followed the old swift. The eleven-year-old went out from the thumbprint slot in the fourth course above the east doorway, and the power went with her across forty miles of grey sky. It watched her strain the air with her open mouth. It could see, as she could not, where the spiderlings in her throat had come from: a particular meadow beyond the horizon, a slope of seeding grass where they had let out their silk and risen. It thought about how she had eaten that meadow every summer of her life and would never see it. For a moment it thought about showing her.

It didn't. She packed the pellet and turned for home. The meadow went into the chicks, the chicks' leavings went into the drift, the drift went into the beetles, and the beetles went into the casing. In that roundabout way, through a dozen bodies, the meadow kept the lamp lit. The swift had never needed to see it. Seeing it was not her part.

## The Reader

There was one thing in the fan house that no one had ever used, and the power used it.

It read the windrows.

The place had kept its memory perfectly, because the air is too dry for anything to rot and forget. It had kept it for no reader, the way the lamp burned for no moths. So the power went down through it, layer by layer, gently, without disturbing a single bone.

It found the summers of chick bones, thick in the good years. It found one summer thicker than any other, and read in it a season of late cold fronts, when the aerial plankton failed and the parents came home light, and the drifts were deep in small white lace. It found the hard ends of winters, marked in bat bone. It found the thin bands of moth scale that meant the lamp had burned often, and one winter where the band was unusually broad. It could not know why. A warm southern autumn, perhaps, and a late migration of moths across the plain, and the bats waking again and again to wheel in the orange glow.

At the bottom, in the oldest dust, it found the layer where the bones began. Beneath it lay only mine grit and soot, the leavings of the years when the fan roared and nothing could nest in its wind. Just above that was the first faint scatter of moth scale: the year the filament first flushed, the year the Exhale was born.

Reading it changed nothing in the place. The power did not read it for the place. It read it because a memory kept that carefully deserves, once, to be remembered. There is no way to be accounted for except by someone doing the accounting.

## The One Thing

In the end it did one thing, and only one, and it set itself a rule for doing it.

Some years after it first came, people came looking for the seam again. It was a survey party with instruments, following old records of the evaporite. If they had found what they were looking for, there would have been a new headframe, a new town, and power run down the old cables. The fan would have been driven again, roaring, pulling the ground's breath out by force, and every slot in the brickwork would have emptied in a day.

The power did not frighten them or turn them aside with anything they would have recognised. It simply let their readings come back dull. The records were old, the seam was thinner than claimed, and the plain was as uninteresting as it looked. They drove away across the gravel pavement and did not return. That, too, was a kind of making-itself-small.

This was its rule: **it would guard the Exhale against force, and never against time.**

Anything that came from outside with the intention of using the place, it would quietly deflect. The Exhale had been born at the exact moment force stopped. It was what grew in the wound once the wound was left alone, and to let force return would have been to reopen the wound. But anything that came from inside, it would leave alone: collapse, age, the settling of stopes, the slow choking of the far shafts, the ground itself changing its mind for the last time. Those were the Exhale's own life. A body has a right to its own death, and the power would not take that from it any more than it would take its breath.

## The Stillness That Did Not End

It came, as the story said it would.

Far down, in a working no one had seen for a century, a roof settled onto a floor, and the last connecting shaft closed. The power felt it the way you feel a word in a sentence change the meaning of everything after it. Over the following season the breath weakened. The dawn clatter became a trot, then a walk. The noon stillnesses lengthened. The ember came up less often and less brightly, a dull red now more than orange.

The power went back to the lip and made itself cold.

The last night was late in summer, so the walls were full. The fan turned slowly in the deep hours and slower, the blade striking the casing at longer and longer intervals, the plate's hum thinning to a thread. The ember in the cage pulsed once, faintly, and again, more faintly. The spider on the cage waited at the centre of her web.

Then the stillness came, and it did not end.

The power had thought about this moment for a long time, perhaps longer than about anything else. It could breathe. It had been breathed on first, on its first day at the lip, and it would have been the most natural thing in the world to give that back once: to send one breath down the shaft, turn the fan one quarter-turn, and bring the ember up one last time.

It didn't, because a breath from outside would have been the motor again. It would have been force, however gentle, however loving, a borrowed breath in a body that had only ever breathed its own. The Exhale's whole life had been the ground's exhale and nothing else. The power would not falsify the last line of it.

So it did the only thing left. It held its own breath and stayed.

In the stillness you could hear the individual wings. Swifts were shifting in their slots, a bat was stirring in the shaft, and beetles were ticking on the drift. The ember went from red to the colour of old blood, the colour of the windings, and then to nothing you could see. For the first time in its life the Exhale was in the stillness without the ground changing its mind. For the first time, it was not in it alone. The Exhale could not know that. The power did not need it to.

## After

The spider stayed on the dark cage for a few more nights, then moved to the corner and began a grey hammock like the others. The pale beetles in the casing went on working the copper for a while, carrying fibres out of a machine that no longer needed them, and slowly thinned. The fan settled into one position and stayed there.

The swifts left in autumn and came back in spring to a silent house, and it was still good enough to nest in, and they did not notice the difference. The old swift was not among them. She had died somewhere over another continent the winter before, asleep on the wing. The power had known and had let it be, as it let everything be.

The following year the power read the windrows one last time and found the newest layer. There were chick bones as usual, and bat bones at the end of winter, but no moth scale anywhere. It was a band of years with no light in it. The memory was still being kept, perfectly, by a body that no longer breathed. It would go on being kept, a little less each season, until the brick went to sand and the plain closed over the hollow.

The power did not stop that either.

## What It Carries

The Exhale lived by sending its eyes out every morning and taking them back every evening full of the world. Everything it was, it had eaten from somewhere beyond its edge, carried home in other bodies.

The power is the one thing that went into the hollow full of the world and came out carrying the hollow. It does not rebuild it anywhere, and it does not make copies. It only knows it: the rhythm of the voice through a year, the taste of the breath, the exact line of the lip, the whole of the memory from the first scatter of moth scale to the last band with no light in it.
