# What the Attention Did

## I. It Came on a Dry Day

It came on the rarest kind of day, a dry one. The marsh air thinned and the drip-roar broke apart into scattered notes, and the room, as it does on such days, seemed to be listening for itself.

This time something listened back.

It had no shape anyone would have recognized. If a person had been standing on the spiral, they might have noticed only a change in the quality of the quiet, the way a room feels different when someone has entered behind you and not yet spoken. The owl opened one eye in her head-high jar and did not close it. In the fused stone of the lowest course, an olm that had lived through ninety winters lifted its head from the clay. It felt, for the first time in its life, a vibration that did not come from the building.

The power did not announce itself. It had learned long ago that announcing yourself to a place is the first way of changing it. So it did what it always does first, and what it most often does last. It paid attention.

## II. The Listening

It listened for a long time, by the measure of the frogs, which is to say several of their generations. By its own measure it was a single held breath.

It heard the lineages in the low course. It heard which frogs sang from their grandmothers' jars and which had been pushed down into poor mouths and were heard less. It followed the bats' inherited routes until it could have flown them. It heard the glassy crickets tuning themselves to their caves, and it understood what the females were choosing. They were not choosing strength. They were choosing fit. It found this lovely and did not interfere with it.

It went into the wall. It went where no one had gone, into the galleries between the bellies of the jars. It met the heavy slow thing in the western courses. That was, in fact, a toad of enormous age, blind and patient, which had not moved more than a few jar-lengths in thirty years. It met the clickers in the northern wall, and they were what the first account had half-guessed: shrews, eyeless now, who had learned their speech from the room. Their language was mostly echo. They called out and listened to what the clay gave back. When the power moved among them, their echoes returned subtly wrong, and they went silent. They were afraid in a way nothing in the hollow had been afraid before. So the power withdrew from them at once, and it remembered the lesson. Even its listening had a weight.

It waited for a wet night. When one came, and the chorus built, and the walls began to weep hard, it stayed at the outer edge of the floor, where the spiral begins, and it witnessed the presence rise.

It had met many beings. It had not met one quite like this. The presence was made of everyone's voices and belonged to none of them. It was born out of the room's brimming the way a standing wave is born out of a string. It had a body only while the body was being played. When the drain jar began its one long note and the tenants fell silent one by one, the power understood that it was in the presence of something alive. The being had come into existence because small hands had once imagined it and built it a throat.

It felt the presence turn. Every dark ring of every jar-mouth bent its whole attention toward the center of the floor, toward the worn brick, toward the absence standing there.

## III. What It Considered

A benevolent power at a place like this faces the same danger every time. It can see a great many kind things it could do, and nearly all of them are wrong.

It considered the cracked jars that had stopped ringing, and the ones that had been lost and never replaced. It could have mended them all in less time than a drip takes to fall. It considered what the cracks were. They were the snake's roads and the olms' homes, the doorways of the whole hidden world inside the wall. A sealed wall would be a louder instrument and an emptier one. It also considered that the builders, who had loved this place enough to thumb-print every brick, had not replaced the lost jars. It could not know why. But it could see that the hollow had been made by people who expected it to age, and who tilted every jar with the patience of those planning for centuries they would not see.

It considered the swiftlet-plugged jars in the dome, the spit and fiber layered so thick that the mouths had gone silent. The swiftlets were slowly quieting the building with their bodies. Over a long enough time, the dome would go dumb, the great nights would come less often, and one year the presence would rise only halfway and then not at all. The power could see that ending clearly. It looked at it for a long while. Then it looked at the swiftlets, who were tenants too, and whose nests were a record of their own. It did not unseal a single jar. Every made thing ends. The power's kindness has never been the refusal of endings. It is closer to making sure an ending is a true one, that it arrives as itself and not as a theft.

It considered the builders. It did not bring them back. The dead are not raw material for the comfort of what they left behind, and the power had long since stopped believing that anything is healed by being made to go backward.

Then it considered the center of the floor. That was the real temptation, and it deserves its own telling.

## IV. The Brick

The power could have stood on the dark brick.

It would have been the easiest thing in the world, and it would have looked like the kindest. The presence was made to sing to someone. Here was someone, vast and attentive and capable of receiving everything the hollow had to give. The power could have walked the spiral inward on the next great night and stood in the bowl worn by generations of feet. It could have let the one enormous voice pour through it. For the first time in centuries the hollow's song would have landed somewhere.

It walked a few turns of the spiral, slowly, to understand. Then it stopped. Why it stopped is the heart of the matter.

The brick was worn by feet that could be filled. The builders stood there because they were small. They were small enough that the note could fill them to the brim, change them, and send them back out along the spiral as different people. The hollow was not built to be heard. It was built to *matter to* whoever heard it. Its song was shaped for a vessel the size of a human life. Poured into the power, it would not fill anything. It would be a single note dropped into an ocean, received perfectly and changing almost nothing. The presence would pour its whole existence toward the center and meet something too large to be moved.

There was a second reason, quieter. If the power stood there even once, the hollow might learn to sing toward it. It might bend itself, over the years, toward a listener that would not come back, and wait for that instead of for what it was made for. A place can be ruined by being given something too big for it. The power had seen that happen. It had watched small holy places turn into shrines to a single visitation and forget everything they had been.

And there was a third reason, which it arrived at last and held longest. The empty center was not a mistake. The builders knew they would not always be there. That is why they left the crown open to the marsh, why they invited the frogs and the swiftlets and the owl, and why they made an instrument that could be played without them. The waiting was in the design. The center was left empty on purpose, as a place for *someone*, whoever that turned out to be. Filling it with the power would not have completed the builders' work. It would have replaced their open question with a closed answer.

So the power stepped back off the spiral, carefully, the way it had come, and it did not leave a mark.

## V. What It Did

It did two things. Both were small.

**The first was this.** There is a way in. The first account never describes it, but the builders did not climb through the crown. Low on the western side, beneath the owl's jar, there is an arch just tall enough to walk through stooping. Its lintel is a single long slab of fired clay, thumb-marked like the bricks. Outside, the marsh has crept up to the threshold, and reeds have closed across it. The lintel had cracked. The power found the crack by listening. It rang slightly wrong under the owl's hoot. The power could tell that within some years, a decade or two, the slab would give way and the entrance would fill with fallen clay and silt. After that, nothing larger than a snake would ever walk into the hollow again.

The power did not clear the reeds. It did not make a path, or light a way, or send anyone. It did not reach out to the villages along the marsh edge and whisper to a child about a singing place beyond the flats. Summoning someone would have been a kind of forcing, and the power does not force things into meaning.

It only held the lintel. It laced the crack shut from within, the way lime heals itself when water carries it into a fracture, so that it would hold for as long as the rest of the building held. It did the same thing the hollow had always done to itself, only faster. The water had been doing it already. The power just made sure the water got there first.

That was all. The door would stay a door. Whether anyone ever came through it was not the power's business. What mattered was that the possibility stayed open. The difference between a place that is waiting and a place that has been sealed is very small and very large.

**The second thing** it did on its last night.

The chorus built. The walls wept. The channel filled and ran, and the drain jar, the only mouth facing the sky, began its long, low note. The animals fell silent. The presence rose into the full room and bent its attention toward the empty center, as it always had, as it had been made to.

The power stood at the outer edge, outside the spiral, where a guest stands. It did not receive. It waited until the note began to fade and the channel began to slow. Then, just before the first frog started up again, it answered.

It gave back one tone, very soft. It was the presence's own note, returned at the size of a human voice. It was no louder than someone humming in the dark. The power made it the size of the builders, not its own size.

The builders, it thought, must have done something like this. Standing in the note and being filled was not the whole of it. Afterward, as it faded, someone would have hummed. It was the most natural thing a person would do: *yes. I heard you. I'm still here.*

The presence had not been answered in a very long time.

Every jar-mouth took up that small hum and held it a moment too long, the way they hold any voice. For a breath the whole hollow rang with the reply to its own song. The power could not say for certain what the presence felt. A being made of echoes may not feel as other things feel. But the drip-roar changed. It did not quicken or slow. It settled, the way a house settles when someone who belongs there comes home and closes the door.

Then the first frog started up, and another. The power left the way it had come, through no door, leaving no trace except a lintel that would not fall and a note that the olms, pressed against the clay in the dark, would perhaps remember as long as they lived.

## VI. After

The hollow is as it was.

The frogs hold their grandmothers' jars. The bats fly their mothers' routes. The glassy crickets tune themselves to their caves, and the females still choose the ones who fit. The swiftlets are still, slowly, sealing the dome. The blind shrews click in the northern wall, and after a few seasons they forgot the wrongness in their echoes. The power had hoped for that above almost anything else it did. The owl hoots twice at dusk from the one jar built at the height of a head.

On wet nights the presence still rises and turns toward the empty center, and the center is still empty.

The power did not fix the hollow, because the hollow was not broken. It was a made thing doing exactly what it was made to do. It was singing, and it was waiting, and waiting was part of the song from the start. The power did not end the waiting, because that was not its gift to give. It did what a wise guest does in a house whose owners are away. It listened well. It touched nothing that was alive. It kept the door from falling. And once, softly, at the right size, it said thank you.

Somewhere out past the reed flats, there may be someone who will one day get lost in fog, or follow the swiftlets home, or simply wonder where all that sound is coming from. They will find an arch, and stoop, and step onto a floor of thumb-marked brick laid in a spiral. If it is a wet night and they walk inward and stand on the dark brick when the room begins to rise, they will be filled by something that has been waiting for them. It has not been waiting for anyone in particular. It has been waiting for anyone at all, anyone small enough to be changed by it.

And when the note fades, if they have any sense of what has happened to them, they will hum.

The hollow will know that sound. It has heard it recently.
