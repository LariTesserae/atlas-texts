# The Visitor at the Clean Ring

## How It Came

It did not knock.

That was the first decision, and it made it before it arrived. Something in the undercroft had been listening for years toward a single sound, and a power that is actually kind does not make that sound unless it has the right to. A false knock would be the cruellest thing that could happen to this place. So it came the only other way there is into a sealed room: through the moment when the room is seen.

At the turn toward spring, when the sponges breathed out their blue-green light and the whole pocket stood visible for three heartbeats, it came in with the light. It did not break the seal. It was simply there when the dark came back, the way you notice in the morning that the frost has come without anyone hearing it.

It was old, and it had seen many waitings. It had sat with a lighthouse still turning for a coast that had been abandoned, and with a dog's worth of loyalty soaked into a doorstep. It had sat with a song that kept singing itself in an empty chapel because nobody had told it the congregation had gone. It knew that waiting is one of the most beautiful things the world makes, and also one of the easiest to leave unattended. It had learned not to hurry, and it had learned not to be sure.

So the first thing it did was nothing. It stayed, and it paid attention the way the place paid attention: slowly, and a little ahead of itself.

## What It Would Not Do

It could have done a great many things. A power like that has a long list of them, and wisdom is mostly the practice of crossing things off the list.

It would not drain the pocket, or lift the bank, or let the river in. That would be rescue in the way pulling a sleeper out of bed by the ankles is rescue.

It would not make the one who lives here forget. Forgetting is sometimes a mercy, but only when it is chosen. This was not a wound that wanted closing. It was a question that wanted an answer.

It would not pretend. It would not put on the shape of a child, or the weight of a footstep on the old path, or the sound of the gate. The one who lives here had been loyal to someone real. It deserved the real one, or the truth about the real one. A counterfeit homecoming would have turned years of faithfulness into a joke told at its expense.

And it would not simply leave. It considered that most seriously of all, because the housekeeper was not unhappy. It was busy and proud of its stacked nights. What right does anyone have to interrupt a contented thing?

But three things kept it from walking away.

The first was the eel. The eel was not content. It was a guest held past its hour. Every year it did not leave was a year stolen from the one great journey its body had been built for.

The second was the eggs. They had been kept cold for a spring that could not come until someone arrived to see it. They were alive, and they were not permitted to live.

The third was the question itself. The housekeeper was waiting for an answer that existed. Somewhere upriver it was already true either that the one it waited for would come back or that they could not. To let it wait forever for an answer that was already written down seemed to the power a kind of withholding. Care that has waited this long for someone has earned the right to know them.

So it decided to find out which story this was before it did anything else.

## Sitting In

It began at the clean ring.

It went there not to investigate, but because that was where a guest would be expected to sit. It folded itself small beside the five stones, in the bare circle of slate, and kept very still. After a long time, perhaps a season, the moss nearby shifted, and there was a hare-shape of pressed clover and root-hair crouched on the other side of the cairn, ears laid back, regarding it.

They sat. The power did not speak, because it did not yet know the language, and because the first thing you owe someone's house is to be quiet in it.

It learned by watching. It watched a night being folded down, and heard the soft glass-rim note, and felt the whole pocket tremble with the satisfaction of a crisp edge. It watched the housekeeper hold the braid in the green water the way you might hold a candle out of a draught. It watched the widening, root-hair by root-hair, and understood what it was for.

And it noticed something the housekeeper did not seem to have noticed. The dusk layer was not quite whole. Down one side of the evening, near where the old path met the water, there was a thin gap, like the mark a door leaves in a carpet when it is opened and closed in a hurry. Something had gone *out* of that evening before the bank fell. The housekeeper had been listening so hard for someone coming in that it had never examined the shape of how they left.

The power filed that away. Then it went upriver.

## Upriver

It did not take long to find her. Places this small remember their people plainly.

She was twelve. On the evening of the flood she had been seven, and she had been at the water's edge choosing a stone for the cairn when the rain came on hard. It was sudden, heavy, cold rain, the kind that makes a seven-year-old decide all at once that she is finished. She ran. She went up the path, through the gate, and all the way home in the wet, without looking back and without anyone calling her. She was halfway through a towel and a telling-off when the bank came down behind her.

That was the gap in the evening. She had gone home by herself, and nobody had needed to say it.

The power had been ready for the other story. If she had not run, if the river had kept her, it would have gone back down to the clean ring and sat with the housekeeper and told it the truth in whatever language it could find. Then it would have stayed while the evening ended, however long that took, so that the thing would not have to learn it alone. That would have been the work, and it would have done it.

But she had run. She was alive. She was five winters taller.

She lived in the same house. She walked the same road. For five years she had gone the long way round, past the new channel and never down to it. The first spring after the flood she had stood at the top of the field and looked at the brown water where the meadow used to be, and she had felt something so large and wordless that she decided, without ever deciding, not to feel it again. She had not forgotten the place. She had put it in the back of a drawer. It was a small grief, the kind children are told is not really a grief, and so she had never been given any help with it.

She had a dog now, a young, stupid, joyful dog. And when she was bored, or thinking, her fingers still plaited grass. She did not know she did it.

The power watched her for a while from the hedge, the way it had watched the housekeeper from the ring. It saw that she had grown exactly as the crayfish had taught. She had stepped out of herself and left the old shell in a place she did not go back to. It saw that she was not finished with the place either. It saw that the waiting in the drowned room and the avoiding on the road were the same unfinished evening, held at two ends.

It would not make her go. It would not put a thought in her head or a dream in her sleep that she had not had some part in making. It would only move something small, the way you might nudge a door so that it swings open in the next breeze. Whether anyone walks through is up to them.

## The Grass on the New Bank

Along the new channel, on the silt the flood had laid down, the river had begun to grow a new bank. It was scrappy stuff: willowherb, young alder, rough grass. The power went and stood on the patch of it directly above the drowned room. For one summer it asked the grass there to grow the way the old meadow's grass had grown. It grew long and soft, bending all one way in the afternoon, with clover at the edges where nobody yet had trodden.

That was the whole of its interference: one small field-shaped patch of grass, remembering how to be a meadow.

Late in August the dog found it first, as dogs do. It went crashing down the new bank toward the water. The girl came after it, annoyed, calling, and stopped at the edge of the long grass because it looked wrong. Then it looked right, and the looking right was worse. She stood there for a long time with her hands in her sleeves.

Then she sat down in it. The power had not arranged that, and was glad.

Down in the undercroft, the housekeeper sat up.

It had been listening ahead for so long that it heard her steps a moment before she took them. For five years every sound had arrived early and turned out to be nothing. This time the footsteps kept coming after it had heard them. They were late, then on time, then there. The hare-shape stood at the threshold with its ears straight up and did not breathe.

The power sat very quietly at the clean ring and did not say anything at all. This part was not its to say.

## Come Home Now

She did not say anything remarkable. People almost never do at the moment it matters.

She sat in the grass and pulled a few blades and plaited them without noticing. She thought about the gate, the path, and the stones. She thought about the cold rain and running. And she thought, for the first time in a long time, that she had never said goodbye, not even to herself. She had just gone, and then there had been nothing to go back to.

She found a pebble in the grass, flat and grey. She turned it over in her fingers the way she used to at the water's edge, choosing, and then she leaned over and dropped it into the river.

It sank through two arms' depth of brown water and landed on the top of the fallen bank, directly over the drowned room, with a small, definite *tock*.

For years the river's weight had rested on that bank like a hand on a door. Now the hand knocked.

Everything in the undercroft heard it. The moss shivered. The eel lifted its milky head. The crayfish in all seven of its shells went very still. The housekeeper stood in the doorway it had been widening for five years. This time the knock came after it heard it, which meant it was real, which meant someone was there.

And then the dog, which had got itself into the shallows downstream and was trying to eat a floating stick, heard a heron and started barking. The girl stood up in the long grass and called to it across the water the way people have called across fields at dusk for as long as there have been fields. She was a little impatient and a little worried, with the light going:

*"Come on. Come home now."*

The housekeeper heard its own name spoken for the first time.

It had not been said to it. It had been said by the one it was waiting for, in her voice, which was lower now, over the water, to something small of her own. She had grown up into the one who calls. She was not lost and had never been lost. She had gone home by herself before anyone had to ask. Now she was the kind of person who stood at the edge of the evening and brought others in.

The power watched the housekeeper understand this. It did not understand all at once, because nothing in the undercroft happens all at once. But the power had spent a season learning to read the seam between the two rooms, and it watched that seam do something it had never done. For the space of a breath, the two rooms matched.

The remembered meadow and the drowned one lay exactly over each other, and there was no gap between them. Nothing was left to draw.

Up on the bank the dog came back, soaked and delighted. The girl stuffed the little plait of grass into her pocket without looking at it and went home up the new bank. She did not look back, and for once that was right.

## The Night in the Black Band

The night of return had been laid down in advance, thin and black near the floor. The power had wondered whether the housekeeper had made it up. It had not. It had heard it coming, the way it heard everything a little early. It had only been wrong about what the night would contain.

It came that September, in its proper order, and everything in the undercroft went where it had been going.
