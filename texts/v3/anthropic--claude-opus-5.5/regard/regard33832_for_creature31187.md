# What the Power Did

## I. How it came

It did not arrive at dawn, and it did not arrive on foot.

A power of that kind can come any way it likes, and the first real choice it made was how to enter a place where everything already has a use. Walking in would have made it a visitor, and visitors here are rare enough to be news. The herons would have danced about it for a season. The fox would have altered its route. The old woman would have had to decide what to do with it. Arriving as light would have been worse, because in the afternoon fog the young steer by the brightest warm thing they can see, and it did not want to be that.

So it came in with the cloud, in the second hour of the afternoon, when the white pours over the low part of the rim like slow surf and nothing is visible beyond arm's length. It came in as a little more fog among the fog. It was warm, a little heavy, and it dripped from the iron like everything else.

Nothing noticed. That was the point.

Almost nothing. In the turbine hall, the old woman was sitting on her pallet with her back to the warm wall, rolling a bulb between her ruined palms to feel for a loose filament. She stopped and lifted her head. She looked for a long moment at the doorway, where the fog was standing like milk. Then she lifted her forearm and turned it outward, palm up, and went back to her bulb.

The power had crossed a great deal of the world, and it had seldom been greeted so precisely or so briefly. It stayed in the doorway a while, because it did not know what to say back and did not want to say the wrong thing.

---

## II. What it could have done

It knew at once what it could do, the way you know the rooms of a house the moment you step inside. It went through the options slowly, because wisdom is mostly the slow part.

**It could make the machines last forever.** The turbine will fail. Every turbine does. The bearings are older than the woman, the windings are drying out, and somewhere in the housing a crack is growing a millimetre a year toward the place where it will matter. When the turbine stops, the lamp goes out and the ropeway halts with its buckets hanging in the cloud. The young will lose their gentle school and have to learn the cloud the old way, or some new way nobody has invented yet. The power could hold every bearing true for a thousand years.

It considered this for a long time, and it decided against it. The rule of the place is that everything is borrowed from below and spent in the thin air. Nothing here was ever meant to be kept, not the pools, not the vents, not the colony's position on the rim. The terraces grew one millimetre a century precisely because nothing stood still long enough to be finished. An immortal machine would be a stranger here than the machine had been on the day it was bolted down. It would be the one thing on the mountain that did not have to be absorbed. The kiln had spent a century folding the ropeway into itself as a school, a roost, and a beacon. The power did not want to take that work away by making it unnecessary. When the turbine dies, the kiln will fold that in too. Colonies moved through cloud before there was any cable, and they will again.

**It could give the woman her voice back, or give her company.** It rejected both almost at once. Nothing had taken her voice. She had set it down because it was of no use, the way the chicks do in their second month. Handing it back would be like handing a heron back its cheep. As for company, she had come here, or stayed here, or been born here. She slept every night in a heap of warm bodies, and a fox watched over her with half-closed eyes. The power looked hard for loneliness in her and found something else. It had no single word for it. The nearest was *occupied*: fully taken up by her processes, shoulder to shoulder with everything around her.

**It could protect the colony.** It could turn the raptor aside, gentle the fox, and warm the cold outer face of the rim so the young pairs' chicks survived the frost. It read her tally marks on the turbine hall wall, column after column. They showed the hawks thickening whenever the herons outgrew their pools, and a colony that crashed and recovered and crashed again. It understood that saving the chicks on the cold face would starve the chicks on the warm one within three seasons. The arithmetic was already correct. She had spent decades proving it with a nail.

**It could protect the place from the world.** Someone had come up here recently with a watch and a notebook, had seen everything, had ridden the bucket down, and would presumably tell people. The power could have thickened the fog and hidden the caldera. But it had watched that visitor lie on the terrace among the others, and it had watched the young heron give the visitor the gesture on the way down. The kiln had already assessed the stranger and answered. The power did not think it had the right to overrule the answer.

So it went through the whole house and found almost nothing that needed doing. That is what usually happens when a power is wise, and it is harder to accept than it sounds.

Then it did the one thing nobody who lived here had ever done. It followed the buckets down.

## III. The bottom of the cable

The visitor had wondered about it. The woman had shrugged a shrug that included the mountain. The sulphur went down into the cloud, to no one.

It did not go to no one.

Below the cloud sea the mountain's flank grows green. It becomes scrub, then low forest, then a narrow valley with a river in it. The ropeway ends at a concrete terminal on a railhead where no train has stopped in fifty years. Beside the terminal, where the buckets tip, stands the mountain the visitor imagined, growing in the rain. It is real. It is yellow and grey and as tall as the turbine hall, slumped and crusted and shedding.

Rain on sulphur makes acid. It is slow, patient chemistry, the same kind that rounded the caldera rim, except that here it had been fed without pause for half a century. A sour, orange-stained seep ran from the foot of the heap into the river. For some kilometres downstream the river was clear in the wrong way, clear because nothing lived in it. The stones were bare and the banks were scalded. The willows along the water had leaves spotted yellow and curled at the edges, burned in exactly the pattern that marked the knuckles of a sulphur cutter.

Further down, past where the river recovered, there were people. They fished and complained that the fishing had been poor as long as anyone could remember. None of them connected it to the mountain. They could not see the summit, because the cloud is always in the way.

So the kiln had not only been robbed. Without meaning to, it had been made to rob someone else. The crusher lacked the judgment to stop. The woman kept the buckets moving because the young needed them, and she had never had any reason to think the sulphur they carried mattered. She was not wrong to care about the school. She simply did not have the news.

The power could have fixed the valley in an afternoon. It could have neutralised the heap, sweetened the seep, and set the river running full of life by nightfall. It noticed that this would leave the cause untouched: the buckets would keep coming down full, every day, for as long as the turbine turned. It also noticed something subtler. The cause sat in the one part of the kiln that could understand machines, and that part had a name, even if nobody used it.

The kiln does not learn by being altered. It learns by news. The young ride down into the white and come back with a leaf, a feather, a river pebble, and the old ones look at these things for a long time and remember them.

## IV. The leaf

It did very little. It found a young heron tipping out of a bucket into the cloud on the lower slopes, the same way hundreds do every day. As the bird banked, searching for the warm air, the power adjusted the air slightly, a breath, a thermal a few degrees off true. The bird drifted down toward the railhead instead of up the flank. It landed among the scalded willows beside the sour water, puzzled, and did what young herons do in a strange place: it picked something up.

It was a willow leaf, curled and spotted and burned yellow at the edges.

The bird rode the warm air back up out of the cloud in long, lazy spirals, landed on the rim, and was met by the short, specific dance the old birds perform for returning young. The leaf went where such things go. It passed to an old bird, who looked at it and let it fall. Later it was picked up again and carried, by the logic of nests and mud, toward the eastern side. It ended up on the warm top of the steam pipe, where the woman walked every morning with her tin cup.

She found it on the second day.

The power had considered whether to watch this part and decided it would, because a thing done without witness is easier to do carelessly.

She picked the leaf up. She had seen hundreds of leaves come up from below, green and broad and strange, because the young bring them and she collects the ones that fall. None of them had ever looked like this. She held it up against the light. She turned it over, and then she lowered it and looked at the back of her other hand. The yellowed cracks at the knuckles were the same colour and the same shape. It was the same burn.

She stood on the pipe among the stacked mud nests for a long time. The herons nearest her bobbed and flashed pink and received no answer.

Then she walked to the crushing shed.

---

## V. What she did

She knew the circuit better than anyone living, because she had spent years keeping it from tripping. She knew the crusher and the ropeway shared a line, and that a jam in one stopped the other. It had never occurred to her to separate them, because there had never been a reason to. The sulphur went to no one.

It took her most of an afternoon. She had the coil of wire and the patience. The fog came in while she worked, warm and dripping, and she did not stop. The power drifted in with the fog and stood in the corner of the shed as a patch of heavier air, and it did not help. It did not want this to be a thing that had been done to her, or for her. It wanted it to be a thing she did because she had learned something, the way the colony moves because a pool has gone cold.

At dusk the grinder stopped.

For the first time in half a century the crushing works was silent. The conveyors stood still. The scraper arm rested against the main fumarole field with sulphur crystals still packed in its teeth. The sound of the caldera shrank to the turbine's hum, the soft *plock* of the mud pots, the tick of cooling rock, and the rustle of feathers.

The ropeway kept moving. The great iron wheel at the terminal turned. The buckets went on circling down into the white and back up out of it, and now they were empty in both directions. In the morning the young herons queued at the platform and stepped in and rode out over the edge as they always had. They had never cared what the buckets carried. The school was intact. Only the theft had stopped.

Over the following months the needle-crystals grew back around the main vents, yellow so pure it looks like a painted error, spreading slowly outward from every crack. At the bottom of the cable the heap stopped growing. It will go on leaching for years. The power looked at it and decided to leave it alone, because the river will recover by itself now that nothing is feeding the harm, slowly and truly. A valley that heals at its own pace keeps the memory of why it needed healing. The fishing downstream will improve, a little each year. Nobody will know why. That seemed fine.

On the turbine hall wall, beside the columns of nests, strikes, and cold pools, a new mark appeared. It is a small shape that might be a leaf, followed by a line.

## VI. The one thing done for her

The power allowed itself one direct act, and it thought about this longer than about anything else.

Her hands hurt, and so did her chest. Forty years of sulphur dust leave something in the lungs, and in the cold hour before dawn, when she woke on the terrace among the herons, she coughed. The birds tucked against her shifted and resettled, and the fox opened one eye. She was going to die here. The power saw this clearly and did not want to change it. Her death would be warm, on the hottest sinter she could tolerate, among bodies that had accepted her. The kiln would fold it into its pattern the way it had folded in the pipe, the pylons, and the boot sole by the well. Nobody gets a better ending than that, and the power could not have given her one.

But it did not see why the years before that had to hurt quite so much.

So on the warm nights it eased the ache in her knuckles, not all of it, only the worst. It loosened her chest a little, so that the cough came less often. It did not lengthen her life by a single day. It asked nothing and left no mark, and she was never going to know it had happened. She would think, if she thought about it at all, that it was a mild season, or that the terrace was warmer this year.

The power decided this was allowed. A mercy that changes nothing about a life except how much it hurts to live it seemed to fall outside the rule of the place. Or perhaps it was simply the rule of the place, the one the caldera had followed for ten thousand years: something borrowed quietly from below, spent where it was needed.

---

## VII. Leaving

It left the way it had come, with the cloud, draining back down the flanks at dusk as the air chilled and the steam columns stood tall and pink in the last light.

It left the turbine to its crack, the lamp to its dwindling row of bulbs, the fox to its route, and the hawk to its arithmetic. It left the colony to its dances, which it had watched for many days and had come to understand somewhat. They were news, as the visitor had suspected, and they were also something closer to thinking aloud. It left the woman to her mild season.

It did not tell the kiln it had been there. The kiln did not need to know, and would not have cared. It is twelve kilometres across and its heart beats once every few thousand years. It was here long before the power's attention arrived and will be here long after it moves on. The power had been, at most, one more piece of weather.

But as the fog thinned over the rim, a single young heron standing at the edge of the ropeway platform turned its head toward the draining white. It had not been fooled. Young things rarely are. It watched the last of the cloud slide over the edge. Then it lifted its forearm, slowly, and turned it outward, palm up.

The power had no forearm, but it had learned the gesture by then, or enough of it. As it went down into the cloud sea, it turned, and the last of the fog along the rim curled briefly outward and upward, open like a palm, in the heron's gesture.

Then it was gone, and the buckets went on circling empty through the white. The lamp came on above the turbine hall door, steady and orange. Somewhere in the fog a young bird was steering home by it, and the thing that lives in the kiln went on breathing.
