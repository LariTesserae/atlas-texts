# What the Power Did

## Arriving

It did not come down in light. Anything that arrives on the saddle in a blaze arrives in a place of thirty-one people who have spent their lives listening for a stone settling a hair's breadth in the night, and it would be the loudest thing that had ever happened there. The power understood this before it understood anything else. Its first act of kindness was to be quiet.

So it came the way things come to the saddle. It came slowly, from the windward side, over the ridge, low along the ground. Anyone who happened to look up would have seen only a thin morning cloud. It beaded on the stones like any cloud. It ran down into the channels like any cloud. Then the sun burned the white away, and the power stayed.

## Looking

It looked for a long time. This was the second kindness, and the harder one, because powers that are benevolent are usually in a hurry to be useful.

It looked at the afternoon scale. It watched Ashke's thumbnail open a reed along its length. It watched Oru set a plate, lift it off again, turn it a quarter, and set it back. It watched a child lie flat on a roof-terrace with both palms on the stone and her eyes closed, learning to feel for the tick of a knock from the upper town. It watched a cat go into a closed house and not come out.

Then it looked at the thousand-year scale, the whole span pressed into one breath. It saw what the people half-know and never say. It saw the walls rippling and the rooms opening and closing like cells, and the thirty-some warm points of light moving through the lanes and carrying the town's water and stone. It saw the town turning over in its sleep.

It understood, then, that there were two beings here, or one being seen at two distances. There was the woman in the doorway. And there was the town, a slow creature fitted into the notch of the mountain, whose hands were Ashke and Oru and the rest. Whatever it did, it would have to be good for both.

## What it did not do

A power like this can do almost anything, so it considered almost everything. It considered each thing honestly and put each one down.

**It did not give them more water.** This was the first and loudest temptation. The power could have opened a spring in the saddle with no more effort than a person spends unstacking a doorway. But it had read the walls. It knew about the thirty-six-person year, and why it is remembered like a famine. It was not only that the cisterns ran low. People could hear each other breathing through the walls. The dryness of the saddle is not a flaw in the town. It is the measure the town was cut to. A spring would bring more goats and more children and more households. Within four generations the quiet would be gone, and the listening with it, and the knocking would be lost under the noise. The power saw a town of three hundred on the saddle, loud and crowded and short-tempered. It saw that the town would call this abundance and would be poorer for it.

**It did not give them the wheel, or iron, or writing.** The lanes are the width of a person with a basket. The walls are already a book, one that must be read with the hands, walking, alongside someone older. Writing it down would not preserve it. It would end the walking.

**It did not tell them it was there.** Not yet. Maybe not ever. A people who know they are watched by something vast begin to do things for the watcher. The power did not want a single stone on the saddle laid for its sake.

**The last thing it did not settle.** It weighed sickness, a fever in a child, an old man's cough in the cold months. It did not tell itself the comfortable story that suffering is part of the balance and so is good. Suffering is not good. But it saw that the people were not, for the most part, suffering. Most of their dead went up to the shelf at the end of long lives. Their griefs were large and real and were sat with, by the oldest well, in the smell of the moss. It saw, too, that the arithmetic of the town is fine-grained and alive, and that a power which starts reaching in to pull people back from death has taken the town's hands off its own balance. So it did not decide, once and for all, never to intervene. It decided to stay near enough that the question would never be answered in the abstract. Each time it arose, it would be answered with the particular child in view. A wise power does not make one ruling. It keeps paying attention.

## Asking

Having put everything down, it did the one thing it had not yet done. It asked.

How do you ask a town? You cannot shout into it. Everyone would hear.

So the power took a small hard pebble from the slope below the lower gate, the kind the people keep closed in their fists. It went to the outermost wall of the lowest house, where the courses are so old they look like river stones. And it knocked.

It had no rhythm of its own. Every knock on the saddle belongs to someone, and it belonged to no one there. So it knocked the only way it could. It knocked slowly, very evenly, with long waits between, like a stone settling, or water dripping from a channel after the fog has gone.

The wall carried it. Up through the lower town, lane by lane, house by house, the faintest tick in the stone.

In her doorway three turnings away, Ashke stopped. She put down the reed and laid her palm flat on the jamb and waited. It was not Oru. It was not anyone. It was not any of the old patterns: not *come*, not *fog*, not *someone is ill*. It was only a knock, patient and without hurry, as if the town itself had knocked on its own wall to see whether anyone was home.

She sat a long moment. Then she took the pebble from her own fist and knocked the oldest pattern there is, the one every child learns first.

*Come.*

## What the town said

The power came. It came up the lane as a visitor, as nearly a person as it could make itself. It was compact and brown and wrapped in grey cloth, and it was hard to see against the schist. Ashke nodded to it, because a nod is what you give. It nodded back. Neither of them spoke, because speech is given less freely.

It sat with her through the morning while she split reeds. The chough came and walked stiffly on the roof across the lane. The power, like Ashke, went still for a moment.

It did not ask *what do you need*. That question makes people invent needs. It watched her hands and listened to Oru's mallet, three strokes and a pause, still tired. It waited for the town to say what it would say.

And near noon, the town said it.

Ashke lifted her head toward the windward ridge, and her face changed very slightly. It was not worry. It was only attention. The fog was late. It had been late, she said quietly, not only today. Some mornings over the last few years it had not come at all. The oldest woman said it came more often when she was a child. Nobody was sure. Nobody could be sure, because the fog comes from beyond the ridge, and beyond the ridge is not the town's to know.

That was all. It was not a request. It was the one thing on the saddle that the saddle could not hold in its own hands.

## What it did

The power went where the town could not go.

It followed the fog backward, over the windward ridge and down the long falling country beyond. It went down to where the cloud is made, a cold current running along a far coast, and warm air riding over it, and a wind that turns each night and carries what it gathers up the valleys and over the ridge and into the notch between two summits. It saw that the world beyond the saddle was changing. The current was warming. The wind was turning later in the year. The guest that had visited so faithfully for a thousand years was, slowly, beginning to forget the way.

So there, far from any wall, the power worked. It worked patiently, the way a person dresses a plate to fit: tap, pause, turn it a quarter, tap. It kept the current cold. It coaxed the wind back to its old hour. It did this without ever touching the town. No stone on the saddle moved because of it. No cistern filled higher than the fog would have filled it in the oldest woman's childhood.

The people of the saddle believe the fog has always come and gone. The power's gift was to let that go on being true.

That is the whole of what it did for the town. It tended the one thing the town depended on and could not tend itself. It left every stone where the people's hands had put it.

## What the town did for it

The power meant to leave after that. It lingered one more evening, and something happened that it had not planned.

Powers of this kind carry a great deal. They have seen more places than the saddle has stones, and many of those places ended badly. Some were ended by fire and some by hunger, and some by gifts that were too large. The power carried all of it, the way a person carries a grief too large to speak.

In the evening Ashke found it standing in a lane, looking at nothing. She did not ask what was wrong. She looked at it the way the people look at walls, reading. Then she took it by the sleeve and led it to her household's oldest air well on the windward face, the one furred green inside. She sat it down with its back against the stones, in the damp and the faint hum of the wind moving through, and she went away.

It sat there. That is all. It sat and smelled the green.

Afterward it was a little better.

## After

It does not live on the saddle. But it does not wholly leave, either. That was never what staying near meant.

Sometimes, on a still night, someone awake in the upper town feels through the wall a knock that belongs to no one. It is slow and even and without hurry. They do not startle. The children have decided it is the lizard, grown old enough at last to knock. Ashke, who knows a little more, does not correct them. Some things are only there if you let them stay at the edge of your eye.

When a child is sick, someone sometimes sits outside that house through the night, grey against the grey stone, hard to see. Sometimes the fever breaks and sometimes it does not. The people of the saddle do not know it is there to be thanked or blamed. It does not ask to be.

No stone on the saddle carries its hand. It was offered once. Oru, who had heard the whole story from Ashke in the low voice they use, set an old plate at its feet one afternoon and waited. It turned the stone over for a long time. Then it set the plate gently back on the pile it had come from and knocked, once, on the wall beside it. Oru understood that for thanks, and also for *no*. The book belongs to the ones who live in it.

## Morning

The fog comes over the windward ridge an hour after dawn, silent and white, on time, as it always has.

The town disappears into it one grey shape at a time. The stone begins to sweat. In the old air wells the moisture gathers and runs down to the sloped floors and into the channels and toward the cisterns. Every surface grows its skin of droplets.

Ashke steps out of her doorway and stands in it with her face lifted and her eyes closed. She says, quietly, what might translate as *welcome back*, or *thank you*, or *you again*.

The fog does not answer. It beads on her eyelashes and on the wool of her cloak and runs down the stone into the channels. That is its answer, as it has always been.

But somewhere inside the white, for the first time in the whole long life of the town, something hears her.
