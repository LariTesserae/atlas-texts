# The Second Picture

## What It Did First, Which Was Nothing

It came the way attention comes, before any body. For a long while it was only a kind of listening that had turned toward the Hollow, the way a head turns toward a sound it cannot place.

Its first temptation was the one that comes to every benevolent power, and it knew the temptation well enough to name it: *to see a problem*. There is a sleeper who does not know how long it has slept. There are workers who never sleep long, walking forty-day circuits to undo time. There is a lie told out of love, so old that no one telling it remembers deciding to. There is a world above the lid that does not know it lives on the far side of an unstruck flake. There is a visitor from Keel standing on warm glass, breathing too fast, while something with long gentle hands comes to make them quiet.

It would have been easy to arrive with light and fix all of it.

But the power looked again and saw that nothing here was broken. The water moved and its total held. The hangers breathed. The grazers lay three deep and lit the floor in slow waves. The Hollow was not a wound. It was a working answer to a very hard question, reached over ages by beings who had no one to help them. Before it touched an answer like that, the power owed it the courtesy of understanding it.

So it waited.

It took no shape at first. When it did, it chose the gas-band, because that was the one place in the Hollow where a newcomer could hang still and belong. It sank partway into the heavy sweet air among the hangers and let one of their long threads drift against it. The thread touched, paused, and moved on to the next neighbor, the way threads always had. The net did not tighten. The power took that as a kind of permission to stay.

From there it listened to the hum. It never changed pitch. The power listened until it could feel the breath inside it, one exhalation spread over more years than there are grains in a gypsum blade. Under the breath it could feel something else, a held quality, like a hand that has set down a tool without letting its fingers uncurl.

*A pause,* it thought. *Not a rest.* Those are different things, and the whole Hollow lived in the difference.

## The One Thing That Could Not Wait

Then it saw the sparks.

They were quick and heavy and scattered, a trail of blue-white running across a basalt field in a rhythm nothing native to the Hollow had ever made. It was the visitor from Keel, alone and lost. And far off, turning toward that light, were the two hair-dishes of an old knapper whose tines were dark as tea.

Here the power's patience met its limit, and it did not pretend otherwise. Understanding can take ages. Harm happens in a moment. Whatever *make you quiet* meant to a knapper, the power would not let it be done to a frightened creature who had only come down a crack in the world by accident.

It still chose the smallest way to intervene.

It came down out of the gas-band and landed on the glass, and it let its weight press the crystals. Sparks went up around it. That was its first real choice: to be seen, openly, in the only way the Hollow understood seeing. It was saying *I am here and I am moving and I will not hide that from you.*

It did not stand between the knapper and the visitor, because standing between is a kind of threat. It went to the visitor's side and knelt, making itself smaller than the knapper. When the old one arrived and stopped, dishes turned toward all this new light, the power laid its palm flat on the warm floor. It knew the knappers spoke with their hands, so it spoke that way.

What it said through the glass had no words, but it came to roughly this: *This one is not an edge. This one does not belong to the first day. Let me take them home, and I will carry them so they make no light on the way.*

The knapper considered that for a long time. Then it did something the power had not expected. It reached out one long finger and touched the visitor's shaking hand, very lightly, the way it would test whether a fresh edge was new. Then it drew back and waited.

The power lifted the visitor. It walked out of the Hollow without pressing a single crystal, so nothing lit behind them. It went up through the crack and through the rock, up into air that moved and a sky that had weather in it.

On the surface of Keel, the visitor asked the only question that mattered. "Will I remember?"

"If you want to," the power said.

"I don't know what I saw."

"You saw someone resting, and a great many small lives being careful not to wake them. You don't need to understand it. Walk a little more gently for the rest of your life, if you like. That is enough."

The visitor went home. The power went back down.

## The Footprints

The old knapper had been busy while it was gone. The visitor's trail across the basalt field was already smoothed. Every scuffed edge had been pressed bright, every shifted flake returned, and the floor looked as though no one from Keel had ever stood on it.

The power's own footprints were still there.

The knapper was sitting beside them. It had not touched them. It had its palms flat on the glass on either side of the nearest print, and it was reading it the way it read the scratched shelves: carefully, over and over, as though it could not make the print mean anything.

The power understood. Its footprints were not damage. They were not a worn edge or a fallen thing. They were something the Hollow had no shelf for. In the whole script of the first day, nothing looked like them. For the first time in its long life, the knapper did not know what shape things ought to be.

So the power sat down beside it, and the two of them stayed there a long while.

## What the Knappers Knew, and What They Did Not

Over many circuits, the power learned from the knappers the only way they would teach, through hands.

It walked with them. It watched them kneel at the small wounds of the world and press them new. It laid its palms on the shelves beside theirs and read the one moment drawn again and again until the glass was frosted. It rested its whole self in the lake-sized mark in the oldest margin and felt what every knapper felt there. The mark was not a word. It was a measurement, of how much hand it would take to fill the place the maker's hand had been.

It learned that the knappers were tired. It was not the tiredness of any single one of them. It was older than that, and it came down with the tines, grown in already curved the way the gesture was. They slept in the lee of the hexagon rings and never slept long, because a long sleep might let an edge go quiet, and a quiet edge might be the one the maker's half-open eye fell on.

It learned their fear. Then, slowly, it learned the shape of what they did not know. The knappers believed that if the maker woke, it would reach for the tool and strike. They believed the next flake would be Keel and everything on it. They were probably right about what the next stroke *would* take. But they had never asked whether the maker would *choose* to make it, because you cannot ask a sleeper anything without waking it, and waking it was the one thing they could not risk.

So they had spent all of time answering a question no one had ever put to the one who could answer it.

## The Weighing

A benevolent power is not a reckless one. So the power weighed this for a long time.

There were three kinds of life in the balance, and it would not trade any of them for the others.

The first was Keel, all of it: every creature under that changing sky, none of whom had asked for any of this, all of whom lived on the far side of a flake.

The second was the crowd, the grazers and hangers and dew-carriers and ceiling-folk. They existed only in the pause, and they might not survive a Hollow that began to change.

The third was the maker. The power noticed that almost no one in the whole arrangement ever thought of it as a *life* at all. It was treated as a danger, a sleeper, a force to be managed. But it was someone, and someone who had been left alone in the dark for longer than it knew, told nothing, for its own good and for everyone else's.

Doing nothing would preserve a balance that worked. It would also preserve a labor without end and a lie without end, and a being kept asleep by being kept ignorant. The power did not think that was evil. It thought it was the best that small, loving, frightened creatures could manage alone. But they were not alone anymore, and the power had one thing the knappers lacked. It could go where the maker was without waking it.

And it had noticed something the knappers were too close to see. They were made of the maker. Their hands were its calluses. Their craft was its craft in miniature. Their tenderness had to have come from somewhere. The same being that could strike a world had shed, as it lay down, small pieces of itself that spent their lives kneeling at tiny wounds and holding fresh edges between two fingers, the way you might hold a sleeping child's hand to make sure it is warm.

That was evidence. It was not proof. So the power resolved to go only where a mistake would break nothing: into the dream, where nothing is struck.

## The Dream

Inside the sleep there was only the one moment. The power had known that was how it would be.

The scar lay bright and new, a continent of fresh fracture, every edge alive with the instant of breaking. Above it bent the underside of the unstruck flake. And there, vast, worn, and patient, was the maker. It was not quite shaped like a knapper, but it was the thing knappers were a small memory of. Its great hooked point was raised a little, and it was studying the work the way anyone who works stone studies a scar, reading it for the next platform.

The power did not announce itself. It did not arrive as something greater, though perhaps it was. It came the way an apprentice comes into a workshop: quietly, to one side, and it looked at the work *with* the maker instead of at the maker.

In time the maker noticed it. There was no alarm. In a dream, strangers at the edge of your work are not strange. The maker made the sound of a clean flake springing away, that dull *tock*, and the power understood it as a question. It was the question every maker asks anyone who looks at their work: *Is it good?*

"Look closer," the power said.

It showed the maker the scar as it was now, beyond the edge of the dream. It showed the grazers lying three deep on the glass shelves, lighting the floor in slow waves. It showed the hangers woven into one breathing net, and the dew-carriers moving the world's one measure of water from mouth to mouth. It showed the ceiling-folk carrying the stilled ones up so they could become something useful. It showed life, abundant and busy and quiet, grown in the warmth of the maker's own resting body the way moss grows on a sleeping animal's back.

Then it showed the knappers. It showed the maker its own hands, walking. They were kneeling at the small wounds of the world. They were holding each fresh edge between two fingers to make sure it was new. They were sleeping curled against the rings they had built and never sleeping long.

The maker was quiet for a long time. Then it asked the question the whole Hollow had been arranged to keep it from asking.

*How long?*

The power told it the truth. It would not lie to this being, even kindly, and the small ones had carried that kindness long enough. But it told the truth slowly, the way you tell someone something that will change the shape of their world. It stayed close while it spoke, so that the maker would not hear it alone.

It told the maker how long. It told it why the edges were still bright. It told it about Keel, above the lid, on the far side of the next flake: the sky and the weather and all the lives lived under them.

The maker had not known. That was clear before the power finished. It had lain down meaning to rest a breath, with no idea that the stone it was working had anyone living on the other side of it.

There was grief in the dream then. It was grief for the lost time, and for the small ones who had spent ages guarding it against itself. Most of all it was grief at how it must have seemed to them, to be something that had to be kept asleep.

The power let the grief be. It did not hurry it or try to fix it. It sat.

After a long while the maker looked at the scar again, the whole bright continent of it, full now of the lives that had grown there. Something changed in the way it held the tool.

Anyone who works stone knows the pause between strokes. That is the moment the Hollow had lived inside. But there is another moment, and every maker knows it too. You look at the piece and understand that one more strike would ruin it. It is done, and has been done for a while, and you just hadn't looked closely enough to see.

The maker made the sound again, softer this time. The power understood it without needing it explained.
