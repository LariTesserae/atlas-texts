# The Attention

## How It Came

It came from above, because there is no other way to come to it.

It did not arrive the way weather arrives, or the way the surveyor arrives, in a truck on the plateau road with the ear defenders already round his neck. It arrived the way attention does. There was a place it had not been looking, and then it was looking there. The ravens noticed. Ravens always notice. For a whole afternoon they sat on the headframe with their heads cocked at nothing and would not go down to the evasé lips, and then they decided it was not a hawk and went back to warming their feet.

The first thing it did was the thing everyone else had failed to do. It stopped trying to hear.

It could have stood on the rim and listened. It could have parted the roar the way you part curtains and looked through to the quiet underneath. But that would have been looking at the bowl as the attendants did, as a place where the noise was the obstacle. It had come because something lived here for whom the noise was not an obstacle, and you do not understand a creature by standing where it cannot stand. So it let the roar in. It let the three fans become three suns. It let the reflection off the near scarp and the far scarp draw its bright seams and dim pools across the floor of the basin, and it walked the way the tenant walks, through the dim places, avoiding the blaze.

It took most of a year. A power like that has time, and it has the sense to spend it. It learned the geometry of the interference pattern the way a child learns a house, by bumping into it. It learned what a bearing replacement looks like from the inside, a third of the sky going dark for a week, the long drift inward toward the two remaining suns. It found the knock in the oldest fan and understood within a day that the knock was east. On the coldest night of the winter it followed the tenant out along the second green comet and lay beside it in the plume while the mine breathed on them both. By morning they were rimed together, two long bristling shapes among many. The ptarmigan sat on the tenant and not on the visitor, and the visitor took that as a fair judgement.

Only then did it begin to think about what, if anything, to do.

## What It Considered

Every power that means well has the same first impulse, and the impulse is to keep. The visitor felt it and turned it over and looked at the underside.

**It could make the fans run forever.** This was the obvious gift, and the worst. The fans are not there for the tenant. They are there because the workings under the basin are full of firedamp, and the air they pull up carries methane out into a sky that has too much of it already. Every hour they run, they vent a little of the old mine into the world's warming. An eternal fan is an eternal leak. The fans are also the livelihood and the burden of a small authority with a small budget. They are the bad knees of the men who shim the concrete bases, and the tinnitus of everyone who has worked there, which does not stop when they retire. To freeze the bowl as it is would be to decide that one creature's sky outweighs all of that. It would also be a cage. The tenant is young. It was born out of a coincidence, and coincidences end. A thing kept from its own ending is not being loved. It is being collected.

**It could move the tenant somewhere safer.** There is nowhere safer. There is nowhere else at all. The tenant is not something that lives *in* the bowl the way a vole lives in moss. It is a way the bowl has of being alive. Lifting it out would be like lifting the tide out of a bay to protect it from the moon.

**It could change the tenant.** It could give it ears in the human sense, or words, or the ability to understand what the fans are and that they will stop. The visitor thought about this longest, because it came nearest to kindness. It would be possible to explain. But the tenant does not live in a world of explanation. It lives in a world of pressure and pace, of keeping level, of lying still long enough for birch to take root. Giving it foreknowledge would give it the one thing its life had never needed: dread. It would spend its last years of light afraid of the dark. There is a kind of mercy in not knowing, and there is a larger kind in being prepared without being told. The visitor chose to aim for the second.

**It could reveal it.** It could let the surveyor see, let the botanist see, let the world see. The visitor imagined that version too: the fences, the sensor arrays, the helicopter crews, the papers, the name. It imagined the tenant spending its twilight as a phenomenon, measured and protected and visited. It thought people would mostly mean well. It thought the tenant would mostly be harmed. Being known is not a gift to everything that can be known.

When the visitor had walked round all of these, it found that almost everything it might do would be too much. That is a common finding among powers that are actually wise, and a rare one among the others.

So it looked for the smallest things. It asked what was already true in the bowl and wanted only a little help to become kind.

## What It Did

### The Surveyor

In the nineteenth year of the survey the surveyor got a clear day. They are rare in the bowl, rarer than the authority's weather records admit, because the plume and the stratus and the long shadow of the rim conspire against them. This one was complete. The cloud stood back from the rim like people from an open grave. The low sun came in along the southern edge and, for once, laid a thin gold line across the far side of the basin floor.

He was at pin 214, in the dim band between the second and third shafts, and he had his staff on it and his eye to the level. The reading did not settle.

He blinked and read again. The number was wrong in a way numbers are not supposed to be wrong. It was not off. It was *moving*. It came up a hair, and he watched the crosshair climb the staff's graduations. Then it went down. He did not take his eye from the eyepiece. He counted without meaning to, the way he had counted in the truck for nineteen years without admitting what he was counting. It went up again.

Eleven.

He stood there a long time. The roar went on over everything like weather. Then he did something he had never done in nineteen seasons. He lowered himself to his knees in the wet moss, felt the cold come through his trousers, and put his hand flat on the ground.

He could not have said afterwards whether he felt it or whether he only understood that he was feeling it. The flat hand went down with the ground. The flat hand came back up.

He did not tell anyone. The visitor had hoped he wouldn't, and it had done nothing to make sure of it except choose the right man, and choosing was all it did. That year, in the margin beside pin 214 and the four pins in a line past it, he wrote *DISTURBED* and nothing else. The question mark had become dishonest.

### The Reasons

Years later the authority drew up its first decommissioning study, because the gas modelling showed the workings finally beginning to spend themselves. The surveyor was senior by then and sat on the committee, and he argued for staging. He wanted the shafts shut one at a time, years apart, not all three at once as the cheaper option proposed.

His reasons were technical and they were true. Drawing down the ventilation gradually would let the gas redistribute through the goafs under control rather than all at once. It would give the monitoring boreholes time to show what happened when one shaft went quiet, before the next. It would avoid a pressure change across the whole old field in a single season. The engineers agreed, and the accountants grumbled and were outvoted. None of it was invented. The visitor did not plant a single false figure in the model. It had only made sure, one clear afternoon, that the man in the room would care enough to find the true reasons and fight for them.

He asked for one more thing, and for that he gave no reason anyone could check. He wanted the oldest fan, the one with the knock, shut last. The committee put it down to sentiment and let him have it.

### The Knock

Once in all those years the authority did send a crew for the knock. They found it: a worn keyway on the hub, an old weld, nothing that would ever fail. The visitor let them go down and let them look. It did not seize their spanners or cloud their judgement. The foreman stood in the din with his hand on the housing, felt the knock through his palm once a revolution, and thought of his father, who had worked this fan before him. He thought of all the years it had knocked like that and never broken. He wrote *NO ACTION REQUIRED — MONITOR* and went home.

Perhaps the visitor had a hand in that. Perhaps it didn't need one. Some things in the world are left alone because they have been there so long that fixing them feels like a small bereavement. The visitor has always had a tenderness for that instinct in people. It is one of the few things they share with moss.

### The Attendants

It did very little for them. In a kitchen in the south, an old woman who had worked the second shaft for thirty-one years woke one night and lay counting in the dark. The count came out to eleven. For the first time the missing did not feel like a loss. It felt like a letter she would one day receive. She could not have said why. She went back to sleep.

That was all. The visitor considered telling them the truth and decided they already half knew it, and that half is sometimes the right amount.

### The Tenant

To the tenant it did the least of all, and it stayed longest.

It never spoke to it, because the tenant has no use for speaking. It never showed itself, because there was nothing to show in any sense the tenant would register. What it did was lie in the dim places, near and still, through the long years of the staged shutdown. It was a presence of the only kind the tenant could perceive: a very faint, very steady pressure on the moss, like a hand laid flat.

## The Suns Go Out

The third shaft went first.

For the tenant it was like a bearing week that did not end. A third of the sky went dark and stayed dark. The interference pattern that had been its landscape since it was born turned and changed. The old nodes vanished, new ones opened, and whole regions of dimness it had rested in for sixty years became unlivable, bright, strange. It wandered that year. The surveyor found pins disturbed in places they had never been disturbed, and he did not write *FROST HEAVE*. He walked the new line of them with his clipboard held against his chest, and once, where nobody could see, he knelt.

Through that year the visitor stayed close. It did not steer the tenant, and it did not need to. The second shaft and the oldest were still burning, and the knock was still there, a hard small star off to the side, and east was still east. The tenant learned. It had always been learning, since the ground first began to settle. It found new dim places and settled into them, and the moss grew up over it at a finger's width a year, and the birch rooted, and it held level.

Four years later the second shaft went. Now there was one sun, the oldest, with its knock. The pattern of the bowl was simple and stark: one blaze, its reflection off the rim, and very little else. The tenant drew in close to it the way it always had in bearing weeks. It lay long nights in the last green comet, rimed in the last plume, with the ptarmigan on its back.

And in that thinner, simpler roar, something began to happen that had not been possible while three suns were burning.

The tenant began to see itself.

With only one fan going, the noise of the bowl was not so total that nothing else could register. Very faintly, at the edge of its perception, the tenant began to feel its own breath. The long soft draw of water through a square kilometre of sphagnum went out across the basin, reached the scarps, and came back. It was the dimmest light imaginable. It was a candle beside the sun, and it was there.

The visitor had understood this from its first year in the bowl, and it was the reason it had done so little. Everyone who had thought about the eleven seconds had asked what set them. Was it the fans, or the resonance of the basin, or the tenant? They were all right, and none of them had followed it through. The bowl was wide enough that sound took about eleven seconds to cross to the far rim and return. The tenant breathed at the period of its own echo. It always had. It had grown up in a place whose size was a rhythm, and it had fallen into that rhythm the way a swing falls into a push, and in doing so it had set the rhythm for everything else.

Its breath had always been coming back to it off the rim, each exhalation arriving home at the moment the next began. It had simply never been able to see that, under the three suns.

The visitor did not give it this. It could not have given it. It had only arranged, through one surveyor and a true argument and a knock left unrepaired, for the suns to go out slowly enough that the tenant would notice.

## The Last Night

The oldest fan was shut down in late autumn, on a still day, at a scheduled time. The surveyor was on the rim. He had asked to be and nobody had thought to refuse him. He had taken his ear defenders off for the first time in thirty-one years, and his ears rang with the old tinnitus, which would never leave him, and he did not mind.

The motor was cut. The fan ran down. For a minute or more the roar sank through all its registers, the grinding exhalation slowing, the knock coming further and further apart like a heart at the end of a long illness. Then the last knock came, and no more.

The ravens rose off the headframe all at once and wheeled, shouting, and their voices were the first sound anyone had heard clearly in that bowl in a hundred years. They were outraged. Then they settled again.

In the bowl, in the dim place where it was lying, the tenant was in the dark for the first time in its life.

It did not know where the rim was, or the plume, or the knock. For a long moment it did not know east. The visitor was there, close, as it had been for years: a faint steady pressure on the moss like a flat hand. It did nothing more than that. It was not a sun. It did not try to be one. It was only the difference between being in the dark and being alone in the dark, which is a very large difference.

The tenant lay still. And, because it had never needed to see to do this one thing, it breathed.

The surveyor on the rim heard it. The whole floor of the basin drew in, a long soft sigh of water retreating through sphagnum across a country of moss. The birch ticked. The frost creaked. The breath went out across the bowl in the new silence, unhindered for the first time, reached the far scarps, turned, and came home.

Eleven seconds.

It arrived at the exact moment the moss lifted and the water came back into every print and hollow, like a tide over a land that has never seen the sea. The tenant felt its own breath return to it off the rim, clear and whole and bright. For the first time it saw the shape of the bowl by a light of its own making.

It had been the light all along. The suns had only been too loud.

The visitor stayed through that night and the next, and the next, while the tenant breathed and saw and breathed and saw. It learned the new country: the rim drawn in its own pale echo, the dim places and the bright ones laid out now by its own rhythm, the pattern centred on itself. On the fourth night the tenant moved. It went slowly and deliberately across the basin to a place it had never been able to rest before, a place that had been a blazing antinode under the three suns and was now, in its own light, perfectly gentle. It lay down there, and the moss began at once to grow over it.

The visitor took its hand off the ground.

## Afterwards

There are things it left undone on purpose.

It did not make the tenant immortal. The plumes are gone, and with them the ferns will thin, and the rime fields and the warm winter nights. The workings will finish collapsing. The ground will finish settling. In some centuries the peat will fill the bowl to the brim, and when the sinking stops, the tenant's long equation of keeping level will have no other side. It will have nothing to tread. What happens then is not the visitor's to decide. Perhaps the tenant will simply become the ground it was always so nearly made of. The visitor has come to think that is not a sad ending for something made of moss. It is what moss is for.

It did not tell the world. The botanist still attributes the old richness of the plume-lands to the water, and she is still mostly right.

It did not stay. A power that stays in a place long enough becomes one of the things that place depends on. The visitor had spent years helping the tenant learn to need one less sun. It was not going to end by becoming a fourth.

The surveyor still comes once a year, although there is not much reason now. The authority lets him, out of sentiment. He brings no level. He walks across the basin in the quiet, through the dim places, the way you learn to walk once someone has shown you, and he does not wear his ear defenders. Sometimes, at the right place, he stops and stands and listens to the tide go out and come back across the whole floor of the bowl. The ravens watch him from the headframe.

Once, the year after the fans stopped, he brought the old woman from the kitchen in the south. She stood on the rim with her hands at her sides and heard it. She did not cry, which surprised them both. After a while she lifted her hand without thinking, palm down at chest height, and lowered it slowly, as if pressing something into water.

Then, just as slowly, she let it rise.

Eleven seconds. Down in the bowl, in its own light, the tenant was breathing with her.
