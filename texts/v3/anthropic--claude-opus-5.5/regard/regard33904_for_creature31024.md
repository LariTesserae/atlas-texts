# The Visitor at Low Sluice

## How It Heard

It heard the forty seconds.

For something like it, that was a very loud sound: a heart that had never stopped, stopping. Forty seconds of rain on corrugated roofs, the river behind the embankment, the vines dripping, and a hundred and sixty people standing in their nightclothes in the warm dark, listening to the silence under their lives.

Powers of that kind are slow to arrive and slower to act. By the time it came, the people who had stood in the alleys that night were old, and the story had become a story. That was probably right. It had not come to rescue anyone from a night already survived. It had come because something had been heard, and hearing, for it, is the beginning of an obligation.

## First, It Learned to Walk

It came the way such things come when they mean well: small, and without announcement.

It slipped on the brick. The herringbone was green-veined and slick after the afternoon rain, and it went down on one knee in the alley behind Number Three's house. A girl of about six watched it do this with open professional contempt. She showed it, without being asked, how to put the foot down flat, weight spread, heel and toe at once, and she walked beside it until the end of the lane to make sure it had understood.

It had understood more than she meant. Wisdom that arrives somewhere and does not first learn how to stand there is not wisdom. It is only power with good intentions, and the lowland had already had plenty of that. Someone had drained it.

So it learned the gait. Then it learned the other thing, which took longer: to wait for the floor. *Whum*, and then the gap, and then *hah*. It noticed that the people here spoke into the gaps, and that this was not hesitation but courtesy, a kind of taking turns with the ground. It did not speak much while it stayed. But when it did, it waited for the beat.

## The Things It Did Not Fix

A benevolent power, standing in a place like this, feels its hands itch. The first days went mostly to not scratching.

**It could have stopped the land from sinking.** It considered this seriously. The compaction of the drained fields, the water table creeping up a hand's breadth a generation, each new pump lifting a little higher than the last: this was the one clearly unwinnable part of the arrangement. Nothing about it was unjust. It was just physics, slowly presenting its bill.

But it walked the quarter and saw what the sinking was for. It was why the pumps mattered. Why the families were named for engines. Why the babies slept to the thud and cried in silent houses. Why the child pressed the brick back down with her foot. If the land were made to hold still, the pumps would become unnecessary. Within a decade someone in an office on the high road would notice, and the quarter would not be flooded. It would be *retired*. It would go green whole, the way a room does when nobody opens it. The people would scatter to dry towns and fall on grippy pavement, and their grandchildren would not know what *Number Three's house* meant.

It understood that almost everything here that looked like a problem was load-bearing. The fig was eating the chimney and was also the chimney. The noise was the price of the dry ground. The contest with the water was the quarter's reason to exist. You do not help a creature by removing the thing it was made to do.

**It could have made the pumps unfailing.** No fault on a distant grid would ever again stop the heart, not even for forty seconds. It came closer to doing this one. But it thought about the old people pausing mid-sentence, in time with the floor, saying *it was like the house was empty. Like we were visitors.* That night was the only time the quarter had ever heard itself from the outside. It was a terrible thing to learn, and a true one. It was why every household still opened every drawer every morning without being told. Guaranteeing the heart would never skip would not remove the danger. It would only remove the knowledge of the danger, and the knowledge was the part keeping them alive.

**It could have held back the green.** It did not need to consider this for long. The green was the quarter's memory. It was written on the steam lines, on the bench outside the canteen, and along the roof ridges in small soft shapes the children could name.

**It could have made the lowland grateful.** A few thousand people slept dry because of a place they had never heard of. It would have been easy to let them know: a dream, a sudden curiosity, a newspaper article. It let this go too, though with more regret. Being unthanked is not the same as being unseen, and it had come precisely so that the quarter would be seen. That would have to be enough. Whether the lowland ever learned to say thank you was the lowland's business.

## Downstairs

On the lowest lane there is a house with a cellar that Downstairs got in the great storm. It was never pumped out. There is a staircase going down into clear water, lit green by whatever light comes through the grating.

It went down the staircase.

Two very old things met there. What passed between them does not translate well, but it was not a negotiation. Downstairs was not in need of anything. Downstairs was not suffering or trapped or angry. Downstairs was simply *here*, in the way it had been here before the drainage, before the fields, before the first brick of the pump house. It rose the way a sleeper's chest rises, with no malice and no hurry. The pumps said *not yet*. Downstairs said, in its way, *all right, not yet*. That had been going on for a century and would go on a while longer.

The visitor asked for one thing, neighbor to neighbor.

It had looked further down the years than anyone in the quarter could, and it knew the argument would end, as all arguments do. Perhaps the lift would finally become too great. Perhaps the river would change its mind about where it wanted to be. Perhaps a letter would come from an office on the high road. It did not intend to prevent that day. But it asked Downstairs this: *When it's time, come up slowly. Give them the season. Give them time to carry things to the roof.*

Downstairs, which had never hurried in its existence, conveyed something like mild surprise at being asked. It had not been planning to do anything else.

Still, it was asked. The visitor thought that mattered. It thought a promise already intended was still worth making out loud.

## The Dry Man

It spent most of a night in the anteroom of the switch room, beside the cot and the kettle and the dehumidifier, with Ansel.

Ansel did not know he had company, exactly. He talked the way people talk when they have been alone in a humming room for thirty years. He talked about the breakers, and a spot of condensation he had caught on a busbar in 1998 and still thought about, and how the children held his hand before exams. He said he supposed he was a sort of lucky charm, and laughed. Then he stopped laughing and said he didn't mind it. He said it was the only time anyone touched his hands.

The visitor saw the shape of his life clearly. He was the one person in the quarter whose work went against the green, and so the one person the quarter could never quite absorb. He was loved from a little distance, the way you love a fire from the next room. When he died, the family would face the question of what to carry to the roof, because everything he used every day he had kept too clean for anything to take.

The visitor knew what they would carry. They had already discussed it, quietly, the way the quarter discusses accurate things. It would be his cleaning cloth, folded. They would set it in the loam and it would go green in a single wet season, faster than anything else up there, as if it had been waiting. He would be embarrassed to know this. The visitor did not tell him.

It did do one small thing, and it will admit that it was not strictly honest. From that night on, the luck worked. The children who touched Ansel's dry palm before a first day in town, or an exam, or a hard conversation, got a little of what they were hoping for. It was never much. Not enough to notice. Enough that the custom would not die, so that for as long as he lived, people would keep holding out their hands to him and he would keep holding out his.

## The Old Woman

It laid its palm on her root at hand height, where the others did, and felt what they felt. She was warm, and solid, and there. Above the polished band she was sooted, then leafy, then lost in the dark.

It noticed what the quarter half-knew. Her fruit fell and rotted and fed the roof soil. The birds that had planted her so long ago rarely came now, driven off by the noise and the thud. She was holding up well, and she was holding up alone. She had no way to send herself anywhere. When the chimney finally went, as brick eventually does even inside a fig, there would be no Old Woman after her.

So, once, in the quiet between the second shift and the third, a small fruit-eating bird found its way through the roar at the outfall and landed on the Old Woman's upper branches. It stayed long enough to eat. In the morning it went to the roof of the new pump house, the electric one, built of concrete, which would outlast the chimney by a century, and left what birds leave in a crack near the ridge.

Nobody saw this. In twenty years someone will notice pale threads coming down the pump house wall. By then the engineers will have to decide what to do about it, and the quarter will already know. They will call her the Young Woman for about a generation. Then they will just call her the Old Woman again.

This was the one thing the visitor changed in the physical fabric of the place, and it changed it in the quarter's own way: slowly, by accretion, into a gap that was already open.

## What It Will Do at the End

It cannot stay. Things like it rarely can. But it made the quarter a promise, and it made it the way the quarter makes promises, without announcing it.

The contest is neither being won nor lost. From where the visitor stands, though, the long line of it has a shape, and the shape has an end. Some night, or some ordinary afternoon, the heart of Low Sluice will stop and not start again. There will be no diesels catching. People will come out of their doors and stand in the lanes and on the iron stairs and listen, the way their grandparents did, to the silence underneath everything.

The visitor will be there. It will stand in the alley among them in the rain, flat-footed on the brick, not speaking, so that for once the quarter is not listening to its own emptiness alone.

Then, while Downstairs keeps its word and comes up slowly over the season, the visitor will do what the families do. The quarter was, after all, the thing that was used every day. Every door in it was swung, every path worn bare, every hinge kept clean by use for longer than anyone remembered. It will take the whole of it, in whatever way a thing like it takes things, and carry it up to high ground, and set it down among the grasses, and not touch it again.

It will let Low Sluice go green.

*You can stop moving now. We'll keep moving for you.*

## Leaving

On its last morning it did the rounds with the girl who had taught it to walk. It was her chore that year to open the house, so it watched her swing every cupboard, pull every drawer out an inch and push it back, lift the tin box under the bed and set it down. It watched the whole ten minutes, the way other people wind a clock, and understood that it had seen the most important work in the quarter. Its part was only to be on her side.

Then she went off down the lane to school, careful on the wet herringbone. At the chimney she put her palm flat on the Old Woman's root without looking. The visitor, following behind, did the same.

That year, the band of polished root at hand height grew very slightly paler than it would otherwise have been. It was one more palm among hundreds.

Nobody noticed. That was correct. It had come to add itself to the quarter's motion, not to take the quarter's place.

Behind it, *whum-hah, whum-hah*, the pumps kept the water a hand's width down. Somewhere below, Downstairs rose a hair's breadth and settled, unhurried, patient, keeping its word. And the quarter kept its footing on the dry side of the line, awake, so that everyone else could sleep.
