# What the Attention Did

## Arrival

It did not come down from above. Anything that came from above would have to come *down*, and on the Crown only the dead go down. It understood this before it arrived, the way you understand the rules of a house by standing in the doorway.

So it came up with the fog.

One morning the cloud sea rose higher than usual. It climbed past the chalk-marked houses, through the middle slopes, and on up to the crest, which it reaches only a few times a year. It pressed itself against every surface of the city. It hissed on the hot glass. It beaded on the copper sails and ran into the gutters and down into the cisterns. For the length of three groans, the whole Crown was touched at once, every lintel and stair and sleeping face, by something that wanted to know it.

This was the only way it could think of to learn the place without walking on it. A foot is a claim, and it did not want to make a claim. It wanted to be what the Crown already received: weather, water, something that arrives, gives what it carries, and withdraws.

The Tenants noticed nothing, except that the sails caught unusually well that day, and a few of the old ones said the fog tasted of nothing at all, which was strange, because fog always tastes of something.

## What It Could Have Done

It stayed a long time in the white, considering. Its power was large, and the first thing large power feels in the presence of something small and hard-living is the urge to make it easier.

It could have given them soil. A single breath and the lower districts would be moss, then grass, then something like an orchard. Children would see green that was not verdigris. It saw the cost at once: the mites would be outcompeted in a generation, the Walker would starve, the old stone would be held together by roots instead of let go into the cloud, and the Settling would have nowhere to settle. The Tenants would stop making the Climb, because the old houses would no longer be failing. And the dead, slowed in their walk, would pile up.

It could have made the cables unbreakable, or ended the need for them, and filled the cisterns with springs that never ran dry. But the Crown's whole character, its closeness, its felt-soled courtesy, its measuring of wealth in sail-area, grew from the shape of scarcity. Remove the scarcity carelessly and you do not get the same people with more water. You get different people, and the ones you meant to help are gone.

It could have stopped the conveyor so that nothing ever drifted, cracked, or went on. It considered this longest, because it was the gift that looked most like love. Then it thought of the family pattern tapped into new stone by the eldest hand, of the whole neighborhood standing in doorways with their palms raised in the sign for *rising*, of a people who had made their deepest ceremony out of the fact that the ground falls away. To freeze the mountain would be to take their Climb from them. They would be kept forever, and they would never again arrive.

It could have given them the sky they believed they were reaching toward: lifted the crest a thousand kilometers in a night and let them see the stars at noon. But that belief was not a problem to be solved. It was a direction. A people who walk toward something slowly, knowing they will not arrive, have something a people who are handed their destination do not.

So it set these gifts down, one after another, the way the Settling set down their belongings on the way to the cloud-line. This was not indifference. It was the first thing it had learned from the place: *what you are given is not yours, and holding on is not the same as caring.*

## Going to See

There was one thing on the Crown no one had done, because no one could. *Beneath the cloud sea, somewhere, it must pile up. Nobody has gone to see.*

It went to see.

It followed the cables down through the white, past the anchor-towers, into a humid dimness where the fog thinned into rain. Below the cloud, spread across the mountain's lowest flanks and out onto the plain, lay the talus of six centuries: a broad apron of broken glass and mineral dust, bloomed and pitted by mites, rained on and weathered at last into something dark and fine.

The dust had become soil. On it, grain grew.

There were fields on the glass-slopes, terraced and old, tended by lowland people who called the dark earth something that meant *given ground*. They knew it fell from the mountain. They did not know what the mountain was, only that it was up there in the cloud, and that the soil it shed grew grain better than any other soil for a hundred leagues. So they sent grain up the cables, and in return received cast glass so fine they kept it on their shrines. Neither side had seen the other in living memory. Each assumed the other held the power.

It stood a long while in those fields. The Tenants ate bread grown in the ash of their own ancestors. The dead walked down the mountain for six hundred years, went on into the cloud, and came back up the cables in hampers, as grain. The circle the Tenants had never been able to see was already closed. The mountain had been feeding its people with its people all along.

It checked the anchors, because it could not help itself. They were sound. The lowland winch-families were careful and proud, and they had apprentices. The arrangement that the Tenants endured as *mercy* was, from underneath, something closer to devotion. The power noted this and changed nothing. It only made a promise that no one heard: *if this ever fails suddenly, I will be here.*

## The One in the Doorway

Coming back up, it passed through the lowest district at the hour just before dawn, and someone saw it.

She was one of the Settling, very old, sitting in the doorway of a house with a crack running through its lintel. The crazing on her hands had gone nearly to the wrists. She had grown so still that the fog no longer seemed to go around her, and because of that stillness she perceived the thing moving through it. She turned her face toward it, and it came close, nose to nose, the way people speak on the Crown.

She did not ask what it was. The Settling have mostly stopped asking what things are.

She asked, in a breath, *Is it far? The bottom?*

It considered lying, and considered silence. Then it told her the truth, because she was going there anyway, and because she had asked, and asking is a kind of earning.

It told her about the dark soil and the grain. It told her about the lowlanders who keep glass on their shrines and do not know that the glass was pressed with the dead.

She was quiet for the length of a long breath, which on the Crown is very long. Then she laughed, low and without much air, and made a sign with her hands. It was one it had not seen before, and perhaps no one had: the rising palm and the sliding palm, the signs for *person* and *house*, crossing. Then, instead of separating, they kept turning, around and around, a wheel.

*Don't tell them up there,* she said. *Let it come up the way everything comes up. Slowly.*

It agreed. That was her gift to it: permission not to be the one who explains.

Before dawn broke she closed her eyes. Down along the ragged edge, where the houses leaned over white, it saw the pale tall shape stooping among the ruins. It could have gone closer. It could have learned in an instant what the Walker was made of, and counted its legs, and settled the old argument forever.

It did not. Some things on the Crown are held together by not being looked at too hard. The Walker was not suffering. That much it could tell from a distance, the way you can tell a sleeper from a dying man without lifting the blanket. It let the rest remain a disagreement among witnesses, which is, after all, one of the forms a sacred thing can take.

It raised whatever it had in place of a hand, turned it down, and pressed it slowly toward the ground.

## The Ending That Had No Shape

There was one thing it did change.

Deep beneath the crest, far below the foundries and the upwelling's orange mouth, it found a flaw. The patient column of rock had trapped something against a seam of older stone: a pocket of gas and pressure that had been building for longer than the guild-rolls go back. It was not part of the rhythm. It was not a hand's height a year, or a ping in the night, or a house leaning into the cloud. One day, perhaps within the lifetime of the child now counting on the upper floor, it would have given way all at once. The crest, the foundries, the molds with their spiral stamps and stylized hands, the newest houses and the newest dead, would have gone up in an afternoon.

The Tenants had made meaning out of every ending they knew: the slow drift, the Climb, the Settling, the going-on. This ending they had never met and could not have shaped. It would not have been a descent. It would only have been a loss.

So it drew on the pressure, gently, over many nights, the way you ease a splinter rather than tear it. It gave the gas a path upward along a crack that was already there, and let it out into a small new chimney high on the eastern flank of the caldera. The chimney breathed a dry, faintly sulfurous warmth, like all the others.

The mold-keepers noted it in the rolls: *a vent opened, east crest, fourth groan past the fog*. By the next season a pair of vent-swifts had found it, laid their eggs on its warm floor, and left the mountain to do the rest.

It had learned this rule too: *when you must change something, make the change in the place's own language.* A new vent is a thing the Crown already knows how to receive.

## The Being Who Lives Here

It had come because it noticed *a place and the being who lives in it*. It spent all this time trying to find that being, and could not count them.

Was it the Tenants, slow-hearted and close-talking? The dead in the lintels? The swifts who never land, the mites who eat the bloom, the pale thing that grazes at dawn? The mountain itself, extruding and counting?

In the end it understood that the question was like asking which cell in a body is the one who lives there. The Crown is one creature. Stone rises and is pressed with ash. People climb it and tap it and are carried down in it. The mites unmake it, the Walker gathers it, the rain weathers it, and it becomes soil, then grain, then people again. The swifts spiral off the top of it like breath off a sleeper. The whole thing is a single slow circulation, and the ticking in the walls at night is the closest it comes to a pulse.

The being who lives here is the living of it.

A wise power does not rescue a creature like that. It keeps watch over it, the way you keep watch at a bedside, not because anything is wrong, but because something precious is breathing.

## The Reply

The last of the pressure came out on a still night.

Deep in the rock, the final strand of stress gave way. The sound it made traveled up through the column, through the foundations, and through the fused walls of the middle slopes, and came out of one particular wall, in one particular house, as a single clear ringing *ping*.

A child lying on the upper floor in the warm dark, counting, heard it. The child's hand went out, the way it had seen its parents' hands go out a thousand times, and tapped the wall once, gently, as you pat the flank of an animal that has shifted in its sleep.

The power had not expected to be answered. It had meant to give without being noticed, and it had nearly succeeded. But the Crown answers everything that moves in its stone. That is its oldest courtesy. So, for one moment, the vast attention that had considered stopping the mountain and granting the sky was received the way the Tenants receive the mountain: not with awe, not with fear, but with one small tap in the dark that meant *I hear you. Go back to sleep.*

It could have tapped back. It did not. The conversation belonged to the child and the stone, and it had already been included in it, which was more than it had asked for.

In the next room a baby was born that same hour. By the old custom she was marked, and she was given a ping-name, a single sung vowel. She will likely become a mold-keeper or a sail-rigger, someone with a steady hand and patience for sudden things. She may be the one, some day, who opens the oldest rolls and finds the note about the eastern vent, and wonders. She may be the one who first goes down the cables to see. Or no one will, for another six hundred years, and the old woman's wheel-sign will drift down the mountain unexplained in the margins of a guild-roll until the grain brings it back.

Either is fine. The Crown sets the pace.

## After

It is still there, if a power can be said to be anywhere. It does very little.

It is in the fog on the days the fog climbs unusually high. It is at the anchor-towers, in case. It is in the deep rock beneath the crest, listening for pressures that have no shape. It walks the lowland fields sometimes at harvest, among the grain grown from what went on.

It does not keep the stars from the Tenants, or hand them the stars. It lets them reach.

On clear nights, very late, when the swifts have spiraled up into the violet and gone to specks and then to nothing, it listens to the city count: a scattered, patient ticking in ten thousand walls, each keeping different time, and here and there a half-asleep hand answering. Of everything it could have given, it chose mainly this, to be one more thing on the Crown that is paying attention.

Tick. Tick-tick. A long silence.

It does not count along. It only listens, which is what it came to learn how to do.
