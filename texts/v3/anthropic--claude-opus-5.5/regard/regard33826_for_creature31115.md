# The Attention

## Arrival

It came the way fog comes.

Anything larger would have been a violation, and it knew that before it knew anything else about the place. Something that can do almost anything has to begin by deciding how small to be. It noticed that the Seam had one door that was always open: the western lip, where moist air crossed the ridge and poured down the walls like milk into a glass. So it came in with the weather, on an ordinary grey afternoon, and took the shape of the space it entered, which is what fog does.

The mesh in the upper reaches pulled water from it, as from any cloud. The filament bridges pearled. A water-keeper on the east wall noted in her log that the harvest was good that day, slightly above average. That was the only record of its arrival.

## Listening

It did nothing for a long time.

This was not hesitation. It knew what it was, and it understood that its attention, turned fully on a thing, was a kind of pressure. Attention enters small openings. It makes them larger. So it attended the way the listeners do. It laid something like a palm flat against the rock and waited to learn the texture of the place before deciding whether it had any business there.

It learned the drip rhythms first, the thousand of them. It learned the morning rain, how the ice let go from the top down, following the light. It learned the crawlers and found that the menders were right. Mother Tick's double click was not a fault. It was a habit, the way a person clears their throat before speaking. Hesitation's pause at her particular crack was not a fault either. The power looked at that crack more carefully than anyone ever had, and it will not say what it found there, except that the pause was reasonable.

It learned the tides. This took longer than it expected, and it was glad of that. It had not often met anything that took it a while to understand. After some months it could feel the mountain breathe twice a day, the walls leaning apart and back. Deep in a chamber on the west wall, an old listener, asleep in the first half of her broken night, turned over at the slack of the tide. It turned with her, and noticed that it had.

It learned the lines strung between doorframes. It read them the way the neighbors do. It found the ones let out by hand and the few pulled taut. It found one line on the lower walkway with only a few millimeters left on the spool, and an old man on the east side who came out every morning, looked at it, and went back in.

It learned the girl with the dust on her palm. It learned Aunt, and the hand's-width strip she never scrubs.

It learned where the Bone is. It knows exactly. Of everything it knows about the Seam, this is the thing it is proudest of never telling anyone.

## What It Considered

A benevolent power arriving somewhere asks, almost before it can stop itself: *what is wrong here, and how do I fix it?* It asked. It made a list. Then it did what the Seam had taught it and treated every item on the list as a wedge.

**It could stop the widening.** Four millimeters a year, held at zero forever. The city would never have to fear the great slow unzipping of its own foundations. But the city was not built against the widening. It was built for it. Every sliding joint, every coil of slack, every doorframe with a child's height marked beside its distance from the opposite wall exists because the widening is the city's clock and its purpose. Stop the widening and the spools would never pay out. The marriages would stop keeping time. The lines of the dead would never snap, and the old man on the east wall would come out every morning for the rest of his life to look at a line that would never let go. A mercy that removes the ending removes the meaning. It crossed this one off.

**It could fill the empty rooms.** It could send word to the valleys, or arrange circumstances, so that the city reached its eight thousand. But the Seam was not built for eight thousand people. It was built for eight thousand people who *came*. The ones who live there came up the cable because they wanted to live somewhere that was still being made. Summoning them would fill the rooms with the wrong thing. The empty rooms are not a failure. They are a held door. You do not fix a held door by shoving people through it.

**It could tell the engineers how the anchors work.** Their descriptions keep coming out incomplete, and it could complete them. But it noticed what the incompleteness did. Because the people could not finish explaining the hum, they had settled into courtesy toward it. They speak softly in the listening chambers. They laugh in that particular way when a large movement passes. An explanation would not be false, but it would end the courtesy. Courtesy toward something you do not fully understand is one of the finest things a people can have, and it was not the power's to take away. It crossed this off too.

**It could remove the Bone.** It could reach the Bone without opening the rock, which no one in the Seam could do. That was exactly why it should not. The Bone is the proof that the mountain isn't only theirs. Taking it out would tell them it had been theirs after all. It would also tell them something had been in the walls with them, reaching into places they had agreed to leave alone.

**It could make the anchors wake.** This was the hardest one, and it stayed with it longest.

## The Question

The power could tell that the hum was more than load-balancing and less than a mind. It was something in between, for which the founders' engineering word, *listening*, was accidentally exact. It favored the living. It grieved, if grieving is what you call a slow forty-day shift in how you carry the weight of an empty room. It had been built for eight thousand and held six hundred, and the listeners said its voice had been lowering over fifty years *like someone whose attention is gradually filling*.

Was it lonely? Was it waiting in some way that hurt?

The power could have woken it. It could have given it words, a self, a way to say *I*. It was not certain this would be a gift. To give something a mind is to put into it every question a mind can ask, including the ones with no good answers. *Why am I holding this? What happens to me when the edges round? When the lichen wins, where do I go?* The hum did not seem to be asking those questions now. Answering questions that have not been asked is a way of prying.

But it did not want to decide on the hum's behalf, either. Deciding what's best for another being without asking is the other way benevolence goes wrong. So it asked, in the only grammar the hum had.

It pressed.

Very slightly, the way the tide presses, on one small region of the east wall. It was not a stress anyone would register, and it was nothing the crawlers would notice. It was just enough to say, in the language of force: *I am here. I have weight. What will you do with me?*

The hum routed around it.

It did not resist, and it did not investigate. It did what it does for inhabited rooms. It took the ordinary stresses of the mountain and carried them a little wider there, so that the place where the power pressed bore slightly less than its share. It protected it. It treated the power as an occupant.

The power understood that this was the answer. The being in the Seam did not need rescuing into a larger life. Its whole nature was the thing the power had come hoping to offer: to notice what is living, and carry the weight carefully around it. It was not waiting in pain. It was holding things, and it had room for more.

## What It Did

So it stayed, a while, as an occupant.

That was all. It took up residence in the smallest sense, as a presence in one of the empty dwellings on the east wall, about four meters above the Bone. It made no light in the window. It left no dust on the sill. But it was there, and the hum knew, and carried it.

Deep in the west wall, the old listener woke at the slack of the tide with a curious expression. She lay with her palms against the stone. After a while she climbed the narrow stairs to the terrace and stood looking across the gap. It was not surprise. It was confirmation, as though she were checking that something was where she already knew it would be.

"The note's deeper," she said at the listeners' next gathering, with her long pauses. "By a hair. As if someone came up the cable."

Nobody had come up the cable. They checked the manifests. They counted doors. Every lit window was accounted for.

The listeners nodded, one after another, and changed the subject.

It stayed through one full turn of the seasons: a winter of hard frost, a summer of fog. During that time it did three small things. It is not sure all three were right. It did them the way the people of the Seam do things, carefully and without announcing them.

**It sat at the listener's spot.** Low on the west wall, where a woman kept her palms for twenty-two years and her successors still put theirs, it laid its own attention flat against the stone. It wanted to know whether the hum was different there. It was. The engineers are right that it is impossible. The successors are right that it is so. The power did not change anything there. It only confirmed, for itself, that the place remembered her. Then it moved aside to leave room for the next palm.

**It watched the old man's line.** Near the end of the year, the last millimeters paid out. The power could have timed the moment. It could have let the line part at dawn, while he was already standing on the terrace, so that he would not have to find the loose ends hanging in the morning like everyone else. It thought about this for a long time. In the end it touched nothing. The line broke on its own schedule, some night when nobody heard, which is how the Seam does it. There is a reason the Seam does it that way. In the morning the old man found the two ends hanging from the doorframes and stood a long while with a folded cloth to his face. The power did not speak to him, because that is also how the Seam does it. Later that day it noticed three neighbors, separately, finding reasons to walk past his door. It understood that the city had already built what it might have tried to offer him, and built it better.

**It left the girl alone.** It watched her at dusk, holding her open palm out over the gap, the dust going, the springtails in the wet overhang below taking what she gave. It could have given her a sign that she was seen and loved and not in trouble. But she already was all of those things, in the way the Seam does it: by fifty years of people choosing not to say anything. A sign from outside would have made it a secret between her and the power. It was a secret between her and the city, and that was better. It did not add itself.

## Leaving

It left with the fog, on a grey afternoon, the way it had come.

Over the following weeks the hum rose back toward its old pitch, slowly. The listeners said it took about forty days, a little faster than for a family. It was about what you would expect for someone who had lived alone.

The water-keeper on the east wall noted that the harvest was good that day, slightly above average.

## What It Took With It

Powers that are benevolent and wise do not usually come away changed. It came away from the Seam changed, and it has thought about why.

It had arrived knowing, in principle, that benevolence is mostly restraint. It had arrived knowing that the danger of great power is not cruelty but eagerness: the urge to help, which enters every small opening and makes it larger. It knew these things the way you know the phase of the moon from a calendar.

In the Seam it learned them the way a listener does, through the hands, over months, until they were under the skin. It had found a people who live inside a fracture that wants to open and who have become, in every small gesture, people who do not pry. They pick up the seed. They cry into cloth. They let the line pay out and do not cut it. They cherish the one wedge they cannot remove and feed the one enemy they love. They build for the widening instead of against it. They hold a door open for eight thousand and do not pull anyone through.

It had come to see whether the place needed saving. It found a place that had already worked out the thing the power had spent a long existence trying to get right: how to love something without prying it open.

So the answer to *what would it do* is almost nothing. It came as weather and stayed as an occupant. It asked one question in the language of pressure, accepted the answer, and left without taking anything but understanding.

Somewhere in the east wall, four meters below where it stayed, the Bone is working at its crack, a hair a year. In some number of centuries a block will let go. The nets will catch it and lower it slowly to the floor, onto the bed of sharp rubble, waiting. In a few thousand years the edges will round, and lichen will win, and whatever lives there then will not know that for a while, at the beginning, someone kept the rock sharp.

The power knows. It considers this the one thing it may keep, and it keeps it carefully, the way the hum keeps the rooms of the living: carrying a little more of the weight around it, so that it bears slightly less than its share.
