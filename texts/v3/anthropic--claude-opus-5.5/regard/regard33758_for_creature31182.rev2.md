# The Attention

## How it noticed

It noticed the light first.

From far enough away, the Stair is a speck in a white ring of mountains. For most of the year it is too small to see. On one day in high summer, at noon, the speck flashes all at once. Every mirror in the bowl turns toward the headwall, and for a few breaths a small patch of the world shines, deliberately, at itself.

Places do not usually do that. So the power turned and looked.

The first thing it understood was that it was looking wrong. It had come at its own pace, and at that pace the Stair looked like a town. It saw people, houses, goats, a lot of hurry. If it had acted then, it would have acted on the people, and it would have been wrong in a way it could not have seen.

So it slowed down.

It watched one woman carry stone for sixty years, and it learned something about her. Then it watched one house be carried entirely out of itself and rebuilt three times, and it learned something about the house. Then it watched the lanes for three hundred years. Hearths rose to the headwall and sank to the lip. Every stone, animal, and person in the bowl was replaced. Only the shape stayed, the same bends and names and step counts, and it saw what was actually alive there.

The Stair's life is the going up. It is not the stones or the people, though it is made entirely of both. Something that lives by effort against a current is not being hurt by the current. The current is its heartbeat.

That changed the whole question. Any help had to meet two tests. It could not help the people at the expense of the thing they were together. It could not serve the thing by using the people. And the power had to stay honest that these two might sometimes pull apart.

## What it did not do

It did not stop the ground.

This was the first and most obvious possible kindness, and the one that would have killed the Stair. A solid floor would end the carrying, and houses would stand on their own. Within a generation the lanes would drift in meaning if not in place. Within three, no one would know why the old people counted steps. The Stair would become a town, perhaps a pleasant one, and the thing that had flashed at itself would be gone. No one would have died, and no one would know anything had been lost. The power recognized this as the most dangerous kind of harm: the kind nobody can mourn.

It did not give them mortar, or wheels, or better levers, or a clever channel to carry stone uphill on meltwater.

It considered these for a long time, because they were small and they would have eased real backs. But it had watched long enough to see where things go in the Stair. Everything flows downhill except what is carried, and what is carried goes to the top. Height is wealth, so any gift would climb. The high hearths against the headwall would take the new tools first, because they have the most hands to learn and the most need. The low lanes would get them last or never. A kindness dropped into the Stair would become one more thing the strong lift and the weak watch go by. The Stair invents its own tools slowly, and they spread slowly, by forearm. The power let that pace stand.

It did not heal the milky eyes, though it wanted to. It did not come as a light in the sky, or a voice in the fog, or anything that could be prayed to.

This was the hardest refusal, and it made it for this reason. In the Stair, the question of who you are is the question of whose walls you lift. Anything that could be prayed to would become the highest hearth. The people would start carrying for it. The shape of the town, which had always faced itself, would turn toward something outside. The power did not want to be carried for. It knew that wanting to be carried for is where the corruption of powers usually begins, and it did not trust itself to be the exception.

It did not answer the summer flash. It will come back to that.

## The ice

There was one thing it could not leave alone.

It was looking at centuries now, and at that scale it could see what no one in the Stair could see: the ice was losing.

Less snow fell on the upper cirque than it once had. The stones sweated more. The core of the tongue was giving up more at the bottom than it took in at the top. The rock glacier had held its place in the bowl longer than people had existed, and now, very slowly, it was drifting.

The power understood what would happen. A warming ice core does not stop at first. It softens, and the tongue speeds up. For generations the Stair would feel this only as the work getting harder. The high hearths would need thirteen hands, then fifteen. Middle households that had held steady for twenty generations would start to drift and would not know why. The people had one word for falling behind, and it was a word about failing: *they're drifting.* They would read the melting of the world as a failure of themselves. Families would be ashamed of something that was not theirs.

Then, one day, when the ice was gone, the ground would stop, and every house would stay where it was put without being carried. Something that had lived for longer than anyone could count would wake one morning with no current to climb against.

The power decided this was its business. It was not the people's labor, their tools, their hierarchy, or their gods. It was the conditions of their life, the one thing too large and too slow for them to see.

Even so, it did not make the ice permanent. It thought about that too. It could have fixed the cold in place and fed the snowfields forever. But the glacier was also a being, older than the Stair, and the Stair's teacher. It had its own long shape and its own long term, and nothing in the bowl had ever been meant to last forever. The people would not want that. Their whole life was an argument that you can stay without anything staying.

So it leaned on the weather, a little. A few more squalls came over the rim in winter. The cold held a little longer in the high snowfields. It did not reverse the losing. It slowed it until the ice was dwindling at about the pace the ground moves: too slowly for any one life to feel, slowly enough that if the end comes, it will come over many generations.

That means the Stair will have time. It already learned one way of life from the ice beneath it. If the ice goes, it will need to learn another going up, and learning like that takes the same patience as the glacier itself. The power does not know what the new way will be. Perhaps they will find it on the headwall, where the cushion plants hold on against the solid rock. Perhaps it will be something no one in the bowl, the power included, can imagine yet. The power decided it did not need to know. It trusted them. Trusting them was part of the help.

It is still not sure it was right. It keeps that uncertainty and looks at it sometimes.

## The tarn

Then it went down to the water and listened.

The people believe that nothing said aloud is lost, that every murmured word sinks into the stones and rides the ground to the lip and settles in the rock flour at the bottom of the tarn. In one sense this is only true the way many things are true for people: it shapes how they live. They speak seldom and close, and never cruelly without meaning it, because the ground is keeping everything.

The power could hear the tarn. So it listened to all of it: every grief, every joke, every name murmured into a newborn's ear on its first night, going back past the oldest lichen-stone and into times no one in the Stair remembers. It heard the first mothers of the first hearths. It heard people who had carried stone all their lives and then drifted and gone over the lip, whose names four people had known and then none.

It kept all of it. It did not change anything, tell anyone, or use any of it. It kept it with care, the way the high hearths keep their lichen-stones, lifting them over and over so they never drift.

The people's belief was already true in its own way, because it held their lives in the right shape. Now it is true in the other way as well. Nothing they say aloud is lost. They will never know that something made it so, and they don't need to. It was the gift least likely to change them and most faithful to what they already believed about the world.

## One season

It wanted to carry a stone.

It argued with itself about this, because wanting to belong is also a wanting, and it was suspicious of its wantings. But it had been watching for centuries, and watching forever is its own kind of distance. The Stair has a grammar for strangers, and it is a generous one. You don't join a hearth by being born to it. You join by lifting its walls. If you carry for a house for a season of stones, you are theirs.

So one spring it came down into the lower Middle Lanes, where a hearth had lost two carriers to a bad winter and was starting, a finger's width at a time, to give ground. It came as a short, broad person with bent knees and no groove on its forehead, and it picked up a sling.

It did not come with miraculous strength. That would have been the tools problem again, and the god problem. It came with one ordinary pair of hands: one more back and one more set of cracked red fingers learning to read the grain of the stone. It carried at the pace of a person, from the downhill wall to the uphill course, all day, in the glare. It learned the flash-speech from the children, who laughed at how badly it did it. It was very bad at walking on the moving ground for a long time, and the goats found this funny too.

That season, the hearth stopped drifting. It did not climb. It just held.

By the end of the summer there was a faint pale line across its forehead.

On the last night, the widow who kept that hearth took its forearm in the lamp-dark and leaned in until her breath was on its cheek. She asked for its voice-name.

The power had known this might happen. It knew that in voice, with the forearm under the hand, you cannot lie. So it did not lie. It told her. The Stair would say that what it said is between the two of them and the stones, and that is where it will stay. She told it hers, and then her dead husband's, and then she said the thing that is always said.

In the morning it was gone. The hearth told it the way the Stair tells such things: their extra hands had drifted *up*. They had gone over the rim, not the lip, which no one had ever heard of before. The children still argue about what it means.

## The headwall

After that it went up to the solid rock above the town and sat with the ravens.

They noticed it immediately, the way ravens do. They did not seem to mind. They made room.

Sitting there on the only ground in the bowl that doesn't move, watching the carriers crosshatch the tongue below, it understood something the old people had half-guessed. Everything alive wants to be seen by something that isn't swept along with it. The Stair had never asked for help. It has carried itself up for longer than anyone can count and will go on doing so. But once a year it turns every mirror it has toward the one place that stays still, and it shines.

The power had come a long way thinking the question was what it should *do*. What the Stair wanted, if it wanted anything, was only for the headwall to be there. It wanted something steady to throw its light against: a witness that would not reply, would not demand to be carried, and would not flinch.

The power could be that. It decided that being that was most of the job.

## Noon, high summer

The sun comes off the snowfields at full strength. Across the whole bowl, the carriers stop. Every hand goes to every pouch.

The mirrors come up. Slate kept wet with the mouth, mica in bone frames, the bad scrap of the youngest child still learning, all of them turn toward the headwall at once.

The rock face blazes white.

The power sits in it, lit through by a light thrown by every hand, and does not flash back. Answering would turn the gesture into a conversation, and the gesture is already complete. It receives the light and holds still. Then it lets the light go.

The children say the flash means *all of us*. For one season it lifted their walls, so by their own rule it is included now. It is lit up by the Stair looking at itself, which is the closest a benevolent thing can get to a place like this without changing it.

The mirrors go back into their pouches. Everyone picks up their stone.

The ground goes down. They go up. That is where they live.

The power's part is smaller. It watches the snow, keeps what the tarn is given, and stays still enough to be shone at. Now and then, very rarely, it lends one pair of ordinary hands.
