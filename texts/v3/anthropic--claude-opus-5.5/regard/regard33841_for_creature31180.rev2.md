# The Attention

## How It Arrived

It did not come down from the sky. The sky over the Shelf is black and empty, and anything descending through it would have been seen by the one creature there that sees. It did not rise from the rim like the lightning either. It simply began to be *regarding* the place, the way you begin to notice that a room has a clock in it after you have been sitting there for an hour.

It was large in the way such things are large. It did not take up space; it took up attention. It had watched worlds form and cool. It had seen oceans open and close. Wherever it looked, it saw fields: gradients, potentials, the tendency of every living thing to lean toward or away from every other. It perceived the way the stilt-hunter perceives, but completely, at every distance and every depth at once.

So the first thing it saw of the Shelf was the map. The warm smears of the herds, the drifting sparks in the moss, the buried roads of glass humming under the peat, the slow downhill flow of charge to the edge and the curtains pouring over it. It saw the eleven-minute stroke of the vents and the three-hundred-year grind of the mill. It admired these, the way you admire a well-made thing. It had seen factories before, living and otherwise, and this was a fine one.

Then it saw the holes.

It had not seen anything like them in a long time. For a power that perceives by difference, a place with no difference is not dark. It is *not there*. There were a few dozen of them spread across forty miles, small and rounded and blank, as if a thumb had been pressed into wet paint. Around each one the order of the world bent slightly, the way light bends around a mass you cannot see. The power had seen many mechanisms. It had rarely seen a mechanism with holes in it that the mechanism depended on.

It did what the stilt-hunter does. It came closer, trying to understand, and kept finding nothing. That was the first lesson of the Shelf, and it learned it quickly: *you cannot know a Slack from a distance.* It could know everything else there from any distance it liked. The Slack could only be known the way the Slack knows things, within arm's reach, completely or not at all.

A lesser power would have forced the matter by flooding the blank with its own light until it showed. This one did not. It had learned long ago that when something is hidden from you, the first question is not how to see it but whether you are meant to.

## What It Considered

A benevolent power is always tempted. The temptation is not toward harm, which is easy to refuse. It is toward help, which is much harder, because help feels like virtue while you are doing it.

So it considered. It had time to. Anything it did here would be measured against centuries, and it could afford to take a few before it touched anything.

**It considered the calves.** Some nights a calf strays and does not find a Slack in time. The stilt-hunter takes its three steps, and a small cold shape on the map goes out. The power could have made more Slack, or wider ones, or made every calf a little dimmer. It saw at once where that led. The stilt-hunters would thin, then fail. The lampbacks would crowd, the silt fan would be overgrazed, and the belts of grit would be trampled out of their order. And the power loved the stilt-hunter too, standing all night on one pale leg in the drifting blue, patient beyond anything, hungry. Mercy on the Shelf works because it is small and scattered and a matter of luck. A dozen blind spots across forty miles is a kindness. A Shelf with no hunting would be something else, and it would not be kinder. The Slack spare some. That was the right number, and the power could not improve on it.

**It considered waking them.** The Slack see starlight and hear heartbeats. What would it be like to *know*? It could give one words, or thought in the shape its own thought took, or a sense of itself as the keeper of the calves. It pictured this and felt something close to horror. A Slack that knew it was merciful would begin to choose. It would move toward the calves and away from the hunters. It would take a side. It would start to work, and a Slack that works is no longer slack water. It is just one more engine. Whatever the Slack is, it is whole *because* it does not decide. To give it purpose would be to take away the only thing on the Shelf that has none. The power set that thought down very carefully, the way you set down something that could break.

**It considered their loneliness.** The Slack sit miles apart. Most never cross a basin, and some never move at all. On the lit map they are isolated blanks, each in its own dark ring, and it seemed possible that none had ever met another. The power, which has known loneliness in the long spaces between worlds, felt this keenly and thought about drawing them together. Then it looked down. It followed the filaments of one Slack into the peat, past the living layer and into the cold saturated black where the snail shells lie in seams. There the filaments ran into water, and the water was continuous. It was one water, lying under the whole Shelf, soaked through the peat from rim to rim. Every Slack was rooted in it. Every Slack was grounded, and the ground was the same ground. They were not a scattered few dozen. They were a few dozen places where one stillness came up to the surface and opened its eyes. Nothing needed to be drawn together. It already was together, at the bottom, where the power had not thought to look because it had been looking at fields.

**It considered the spires.** A Slack slows and hardens. Its moss thins, its eyes silt over, and eventually it is stone. The power could have stopped this and kept them breathing, wet and seeing, forever. But it looked at the spires, round-shouldered and settled, and saw that the Slack were not dying into them. They were *arriving*. A Slack is a thing becoming more and more like the ground it has always been part of. To keep one from becoming a spire would be like keeping a river from reaching the sea. And the spires still work. They still ground the lightning, bend the belts, and hide the calves. Nothing is lost in the change except the eyes. The power felt a real grief about the eyes, and it let itself feel it, because some griefs are not problems to solve but simply the cost of things lasting.

**It considered the output.** This was the question it wanted most to answer. The curtains of light fall off the rim every still night, toward a storm-floor so far down it looks like frost. The Shelf produces something and ships it out. *To whom?* The power could follow the light down, and it did. There was no harm in knowing.

It went down with the curtains, miles through the thin dark air and into the cloud sea. There the falling charge did not stop or pool or feed anything that waited for it. It spread into the storms. It became part of the tension that builds in that enormous weather, the potential that grows until it breaks. And the power saw that the potential breaks *upward*, at the highest point, in a violet column rising silently from the rim and forking inland along the copper veins.

The output was the input. The Shelf ships its current off the edge, the storm-floor gathers it and adds its own, and it comes back as lightning to be harvested in glass. Nobody collects it because nobody needs to. The whole plant, the whole green working engine in the dark, was not producing *for* anything. It was one long breath with a very large lung. The power laughed, if a thing like that laughs, with relief. For a moment it had feared the Shelf was a factory whose owner had left. It was not. It was a heart, and hearts have no customers.

## What It Understood

By then it understood what it had first seen as holes.

The Shelf is a cycle that never stops: breath and drain, stroke and stroke, down over the rim and up from the rim again. It is a beautiful cycle, and like every cycle it would be complete and closed and perfect, a sealed machine with no way in and no way out, if it were not for the Slack. The Slack are where the cycle does not complete. Current goes to ground there and simply stays. The calf rests there. The pattern loosens there. They are the Shelf's unfinished places, and a world with no unfinished places in it is a world nothing new can ever enter.

The power recognized this, because it was true of the power as well.

It had been attending for a very long time. It had been working, in its fashion, for ages: watching, weighing, tending, holding a great many things in a great many places, always perceiving, always perceived by whatever could feel it. It was lit up on every map that could read it. It had never in its existence been unfindable. It had forgotten there was such a thing.

## What It Did

It made itself small.

This took a long time, and it was not easy for something of its size. It gathered its attention in from the far distances and drew it down from the planets and the long dark between the stars, until it fit inside a shape it could carry across peat. It took a shape tired from the thin air. It took a pair of legs that ached. It took a hand.

It walked across the Shelf in the dark. It passed the lampbacks glowing in their slow lake of lights and the rime-reeds standing silent where they could never sing. A stilt-hunter lifted one pale leg as it passed, read it as warm and too large for prey, and let it go. The power was glad of that. It had not been read by anything in a long time.

It came to a Slack. It did not choose one for any quality. It chose the one nearest the rim, with the most rings of old glass sunk around it, so that the curtains could be seen falling past it. It sat down in the wet peat beside the Slack and grew rime like everything else. When the fog drained, its rime wept with the rest.

Then it put its hand on the Slack.

The Slack was exactly the temperature of the ground. It was wet, and under the palm it drew in water very slowly and very slowly let it go.

The power felt its charge begin to leave.

It had not known it carried so much. It was not only the prickle in the fingertips that every visitor feels. It was the whole weight of its attending, ages of being lit and legible and present to everything. All of that went into the Slack, and through it, and down past the living layer into the cold black water under the Shelf, and spread into the ground, and was gone. For the first time it could remember, the power could not be found. It was a blank on every map. The stilt-hunter far behind it, turning its head, felt the large warm shape it had let go thin, and then vanish, folded into a hole it could not read.

The power did not do anything. It had come to find out what it should do, and it discovered that the question had gone to ground with everything else. It sat. The vents stroked, eleven minutes and eleven minutes. The fog breathed up over the rim and drained away. A lightning strike rose from the edge a mile off and arrived as a faint dry tick several seconds late. The creepmoss sent its blue coronas crawling to the edge of the dark ring and let them go out. The curtains fell past the rim into the cloud sea, down toward the storms that would send them back.

At some point in the night a lampback calf, lost in a breath, found the dark ring and pressed itself against the far side of the Slack. It dimmed and blurred and slept. The power could hear its heartbeat, small and quick and within arm's reach. So there they were, the three of them, dripping rime: a calf, a stillness, and a thing that had watched worlds form, all equally hidden. The work went on around them without touching them.

Two points of starlight sat low in the Slack's face. They had been watching the curtains. Late in the night, they turned toward the power.

I do not think the Slack knew what it was looking at. I think it saw what anything with eyes would see: a tired shape sitting in the peat with its hand out. I think it looked for a while, the way it looks at everything, completely, within arm's reach, and then turned back to the light falling off the edge. That was all.

It was the kindest thing that had happened to the power in an age. It was not kindness *toward* it. It was the plain, unchosen, unrewarded fact of being let rest by something that did not need anything from it and did not know it was giving anything.

## What It Left

When the sun made its bruise-colored seam along the southern rim, brightening and fading, the power took its hand away.

Its charge began to come back. It came slowly at first, and then all at once, the way a limb wakes. It stood up, a little stiff, and grew large again and went back out to the far places where it was needed. It was lit on every map once more.

It did not take anything. It did not bless the Shelf, protect it, mark it on any chart, or tell anyone where it was. It thought about guarding the place against whatever might someday come to mine the copper seams or quarry the glass, and it decided that the best guard it could offer was its own forgetting. It would not remember the Shelf the way it remembers most things, from a distance, as a fact. It would only remember it the way the Slack knows anything: close, completely, and only when it was there.

It left one thing without meaning to. Where it had sat for a night beside the Slack, the power's weight had pressed the peat and shifted the wind by a hair. Over the centuries that followed, the grit belts running toward the rim would waver there, very slightly, and curl around a spot where nothing now stands. It would be one more kink in the grain. Anyone mapping the Shelf's flaws to find the Slack would find that mark too, and would not be able to tell it from the others.

That seemed right to the power. It had come as something that tends. It left as one more place where the order bends. For one night it had been slack water, and it would carry that forward.

## If You Asked It Why

If you could ask it why it did so little, I think it would say something like this:

*The Shelf did not need a keeper. It had one already, and the keeper's whole gift was not keeping. The calves were being spared at the right rate. The dead were becoming stone at the right pace. The current was going somewhere, and that somewhere was home. The only thing on the Shelf that needed anything was me, and the Shelf gave it without being asked.*

*I have learned that this is often how it goes when you arrive somewhere meaning to help. The wise thing is to look until you understand what is already working, and to find the places where the pattern loosens and ask why they are there. Most of the time you will find that the place has been caring for itself in ways you could not see from far off. Sometimes you will find that it has been waiting, without knowing, to care for you.*

*So I sat down, put out my hand, and let the ground have what I was carrying for a while. Then I got up and went back to work.*
