# The Guest in the Column

## How It Came

It came up the Column, the way everything comes to Ossary.

It could have arrived any way it liked. It could have stood suddenly in the Cup at noon, or spoken out of the cloud, or appeared on the western rim at sunset like the answer to a question nobody had asked. It considered these entrances and set them aside, because an entrance is a kind of claim. It came instead the way the midges come. It rose off the brine flats in the warm afternoon, one mote among uncountable motes, carried west by the heat until the wall of pale stone turned it upward. It went over the lip in a drift and down a Throat, pulled by fans that had been turning for eight hundred years, and it fell into the Guttering like grey snow.

A swift nearly ate it on the way. It allowed this to be close.

This was the first thing it understood about the place. Here, arriving means being received as food. Everything that enters Ossary is taken in, passed along, and laid down. It thought a visitor should know that before deciding anything.

## The First Year

It did nothing for a year.

This was not indifference. It was the opposite. A power that is wise knows that its first impressions are mostly made of itself: its own weather, its own idea of what a good place looks like. It needed time for those impressions to wear off, the way it takes a generation for new stone to take the films.

So it took a shape that could stand in a stairwell without being noticed, and it learned to lean. The first week, the screaming was nearly unbearable even to it, and it let that happen too. It wanted to know what the visitors know before they stop knowing it. It learned to feel a Turning coming and to finish its sentences before the dark came down. It learned enough hand-cant to be rude in three wards and polite in one. It ate midge cake and snail broth. It slept tilted and found, somewhat to its surprise, that it could no longer imagine lying flat.

It touched the walls. It let the bitter water run over its hands every day. At the end of the year it looked at its palms and found them a little smoother than before. The city had begun to take its mark back. It decided to let it. You cannot understand a place that has never been allowed to change you.

## What It Could Have Done

At the end of the year it sat on a slanted step above the Guttering, under the sieving light, and made a list, because even powers must be honest with themselves about temptation.

**It could make things grow.** One word and the caustic stone would soften, and the next seed off the lowland wind would root in a crack and send up something green. Within a century there would be moss in the Cup and ferns on the Kiln Heights. The films would retreat and the snails would grow fat on something new. It could picture this very clearly, and it found the picture beautiful. It also recognized the picture as a lowland dream laid over a place that had never asked to be dreamed about. Ossary's green is violet and rust and copper. Its rings are guano beds. Its forest flies. A city that grows nothing is not a city that lacks something. It is a city that has made everything itself. It struck this from the list.

**It could give the old ones back their fingerprints.** It thought about the grandmothers who could not sign a lowland contract with a thumbprint and were cheated at the ports for it. Then it thought about the dignity in a smooth palm, and the phrase *read by the stone rather than the other way around*. It asked itself whether it wanted to heal these people or to make them legible to itself. It could not honestly answer the question, and that was answer enough. It struck this from the list.

**It could bring down the Walled.** At first this looked like mercy: people living for years in slings inside chimneys, going violet, going silent, dying in the dark and being built over by birds. Then it climbed a flue and asked one of them, a woman named Hesk of the Widow's Mark, whether she wanted to come down. She was quiet for so long that it thought she hadn't understood. Then she laughed, low, under the scream, and signed something in chimney-cant it had to have explained to it later. Roughly translated: *you are asking a bride if she would like to be rescued from the wedding.* It struck this from the list.

**It could stop the swifts from growing.** Something was happening in the old roosts that no one understood. The birds were getting larger and sinking deeper, and the eldest had stopped flying. Anything growing that no one understands is a thing a cautious power could reasonably pause. But caution and fear are easy to confuse, and pausing something that is becoming what it is becoming is only a politer way of ending it. It did not strike this one yet. It marked it: *go and see.*

**It could translate.** This was the great temptation, and it knew it was the great one because it wanted to do it most. For eight hundred years the Cup families had pressed their palms to the brick at dusk, waiting for a voice to drop low enough to understand. The power did not have to wait. It could go down into the deepest flue and hear, in an afternoon, what the grandmothers had spent forty generations reaching for. Then it could walk up the ladders of the chimney-born ward and tell them. It could hand them the ending.

It sat a long time on the step with that one.

## The Question Under the Questions

Before it could decide anything about the voice, it had to decide something about the people. Everything else rested on that.

The Stratigraphers said it plainly and the Cup grandmothers said it less plainly. What lives in Ossary is Ossary. The people are an organ it grew for hands. It shaped their bodies, their voices, their sleep, their law. It bred some of them to climb and some of them to wait.

A benevolent power has to look hard at a sentence like that, because it is the kind of sentence that describes a cage from the inside. Were these four million people free? Or were they cells in something that had never asked them, content because contentment had been bred into them along with the ankles and the hummed consonants?

So it looked. It spent the second year looking.

It found leaners in every port on the continent, Ossarans who had gone down the cable-lifts and stayed down. They were homesick, awkward on flat quays, unable to sleep in lowland beds, and alive, and making lives. It found others who had gone down and come back, and a few who had done that four or five times. It found a Fanwarden's son who apprenticed himself to a lowland shipwright and wrote home every season in a hand that leaned. It found that Ossary let people go. It did not make going easy. It did not make going free of cost. But it let them go.

It found the exiles too, and those were harder.

Her name was Teodh, of the Fork. She was fourteen. During a dusk Turning she had lost her footing on the Weeping Stair and thrown out her arm, and her arm had met a swift at full speed. The bird fell dead at her feet. She had not meant it. Everyone knew she had not meant it. The law does not say *deliberately*, because in the old grammar there is no way to kill a swift that is not deliberate, and so she was sent down the escarpment.

This, it thought, was a real wrong. It was not a monstrous one, but it was real: the kind of wrong a body does when an old reflex outruns what it knows. It was sure of this and still did not overturn the law. Laws a city makes about the heart of itself have to be unmade by the city, or they come back stronger, and the one who unmade them becomes a new thing to fear. It could see, reading forward the way a Stratigrapher reads down, that the law would soften. In three generations, in what would come to be called the Year of the Open Hand, a Gatherer would stand up in the Quiet Kilns and say *she did not mean it*, and the city would hear her. It would not be the power's doing. It had to let that be slow.

It did not let Teodh go down alone, though.

It rode the cable-lift down with her in the iron basket among the fertilizer blocks, a mile of pale sweating limestone sliding past. When she cried, it did not tell her it would be all right, because it would not be, not entirely. It told her true things. The swift she killed had been gathered at dawn by a woman in grey who did not speak. The bird had been rendered with a word and a pinch of chitin-flour. Its ash was already in the mortar of a wall on the Kiln Heights, which meant that some part of the thing she had been exiled for was built into the city she had been exiled from, and she was in it too, because she was the last thing it touched. It told her the names of three leaners in the harbor town who kept slanted beds and spoke low, and where to find them. At the bottom, on the flat white salt, it showed her how to walk on level ground without looking drunk. She was bad at it. She laughed at herself. It counted that laugh as the best thing it did in Ossary.

So it came to its answer about freedom, and the answer was not tidy. The people were part of something, and it had shaped them, as every place shapes the people who live in it and every family shapes its children. But being part of something is not the same as being owned by it. The test is whether the thing lets you go. Ossary did, badly and at a cost, but truly. That was not perfection. It was the condition under which the rest could be left alone.

## The One Thing That Looked Like Weather

In the third year it walked east, down the escarpment and out across the brine flats toward the sea, because it had noticed something the Ossarans could not see. They could not see it because it was a hundred miles away and forty years off.

The whole city stood on the midges. The midges stood on the mats. The mats stood on the shallowness of the flats, a hundred miles of warm brine barely deep enough to wet an ankle. Out at the coast, a long spit of sand held back the sea. The power could read the currents, and it could see that the spit was thinning. In four decades, in a storm no one would remember except by the thinness of the bed it left, the sea would come through. The flats would deepen and cool. The mats would drown, the Column would empty, the swifts would starve in their hundreds of millions, and the voice in the Cup would stop before it arrived.

It thought hard about this, harder than about anything else, because of the difference between two kinds of help. One kind of help decides what a thing should become. The other protects the conditions under which it can become anything at all. The first kind is almost always a mistake. The second kind is very occasionally a duty. You do not choose a child's words for her. But if a wall is about to fall on her while she is learning to speak, you move her out of the way. That is not taking away her voice. It is the only thing that leaves her voice to her.

So it did one thing in Ossary that was not small. It laid down a shoal. It did it slowly, over a season, the way sand is always laid down, a little shift of current and a patient heaping of grains. When it was done, the spit would hold for another thousand years, and anyone looking would call it weather.

It could have done more. It could have made the flats permanent, the Column eternal, and the city safe from every storm for as long as the world lasted. It did not, because a thing that cannot end cannot quite be alive. A thousand years was enough time for a voice to finish a sentence. What came after the sentence would be Ossary's business.

## Going Down

Then it went to see about the swifts.

It went into the Cup at dusk. It did not go as a power. It went as the grandmothers go, down the dripping ramps to the base of the oldest flue in the city, the one the Cup families call only *the First*, whose drip name has worn away. The grandmothers were already there with their palms to the brick, their faces turned up. They did not look at it. One of them, very old, shifted slightly to make room.

It put its palm to the brick. By now its hand was nearly as smooth as theirs.

The Turning came down. The world went to roar. Somewhere in the dark above, in nests the size of washbasins, the eldest swifts clung to the walls where they had clung for longer than the Cup had had a name. It could feel them the way the grandmothers felt them, as pressure in the cisterns and a rolling low growl that was not quite sound. It could also feel them the way only it could: the whole of them, the young in their millions threading the sky-roads over the wards, the flocks flying the shapes of fallen towers, the memory stacked in the air. It felt the slow heavy center where the old ones did not fly, where the city's breath came to rest.

It checked, carefully, for suffering, the way you check a sleeping animal for fever. It found age, and weight, and a stillness that was a choice and not a trap, and something like attention turned inward and downward toward the people at the base of the flue. It found no pain. It found a thing becoming what it was becoming.

Then, for the first time in its eight hundred years, the city noticed that something from outside was listening.

The power felt this as a tremor through the brick, a hesitation in the pressure. It was the startle of any creature that has only ever been attended by its own. For a moment the whole attention of the thing turned toward the guest at the chimney base.

Now the power could have spoken. It could have said *I can hear you. Tell me, and I'll tell them.*

It said nothing. It kept its palm on the brick, the way the grandmothers did, and let itself be one more pair of hands in the dark, no louder than the rest. After a while the attention turned away from it and back to the others, which was where it belonged.

## What It Heard

It heard anyway, of course. It could not help hearing. That is the burden of being what it was.

The voice had not arrived yet. It was still too high, still at the edge of the scream. But the power could hear the shape of it the way you can hear a sentence coming in a language you know when the speaker has only begun to breathe in. The grandmothers had been right about the tense. It was the before-turning, the tense for promises, for anything said at dusk that must be remembered until morning.

It was not a new thing. That was what moved the power most. It was not a revelation or a command or a secret held under the city for eight centuries. It was something the city had been saying every day of its existence in the only language it had, until it could learn a better one. Every dusk it breathed in, pulled the swifts down into its chimneys like smoke running backward, and held them there through the dark. Every dawn it let them go.

What it was trying to say, as nearly as the power could render it into words, was:

*I will hold you through the night. In the morning I will let you go.*

And under that, because the before-turning is the tense of marriage vows, one more thing it had not quite learned to separate from the first: *These are the same promise.*

The power understood then why it had needed to spend a year asking whether the people of Ossary were free. The city had been asking the same question about itself, and this was its answer. This was what it wanted its hands to know. It had grown them, leaned them, smoothed them, and taught them never to harm the part of it that flies. It did not own them. It was not keeping them. Everything it took in, it would let out again at dawn. It would even let out the exiled girl, and the leaners in the ports, and the Fanwarden's son with his slanted handwriting. It would let out the Walled, who chose to stay, and the dead, whose ash went into new walls. And whatever it held, it would hold only for the night.

The grandmothers had been waiting eight hundred years to hear that. The power could have told them in a sentence.

It did not, and this was the hardest thing it did in Ossary, harder than the shoal or the cable-lift. It did not finish the sentence of someone learning to speak. When the voice dropped low enough, and it would soon, within the grandmothers' grandchildren's lives, the Cup families would hear it the way they had heard everything else about this place: slowly, in their palms and in the cisterns, in their own time. And they would understand it all at once, because their bodies had known it all along. They had stood against the walls at dusk and gone home at dawn. They had been held and released eight hundred thousand times. The words would come to them as recognition rather than news.

A thing told to you by a stranger is information. A thing you finally hear from the one who has been trying to tell you all your life is something else. The power would not trade the second for the first. No kindness could be worth the loss of that.

## The Leaving

It stayed a while longer. It did not stay long enough to be woven in or taken by the swifts as a roost, but long enough that the snails had begun, very faintly, to round its corners.

It hummed with Hesk of the Widow's Mark in the Old Crook flue on the night she died. It hummed low, under the scream, a known body among the birds. It stayed until the humming was only hers, and then until it was only the flue's. It did not interfere with what the swifts would build over her.

It went down to the base of a Throat one morning when the great fan had been geared down for repair and the whole ward slept badly. It sat with the deaf Fanwardens in the engine house. It put its breastbone to the iron so that at least one more thing in the room was listening for the heartbeat to come back.

Then, on its last evening, it went to the Weeping Stair and stood against the wall with everyone else and waited for the Turning.

The old woman from the Cup was there, the one who had shifted to make room for it at the First. It had not seen her come. She stood beside it, faced turned up, palm to the stone. When the roar came down and no one could hear anything, she lifted her hand from the wall and signed to it, small and close to the chest, in the oldest form of the cant. The power had not learned all of those signs. It knew these.

*You heard it.*

Not a question.

It signed back: *Yes.*

*You didn't tell.*

*No.*

She looked at it a long time, with the birds tearing past a yard overhead and the whole pale city shining wet in the last light. Then she reached out and pressed her smooth palm flat against its smooth palm. Neither of them left a mark on the other.

When the roar had passed and sound came back, she said aloud, in the low hummed Ossaran of the Cup, one word. It was a drip name. It was the name of a thin new groove on the face of the Weeping Stair, just above where the power had stood every evening for three years, where the same drip of bitter water had been falling on the same spot of its shoulder since the day it first leaned there.

It had not known the drip had a name. She had given it one: *the Guest.*

It left at dawn, the way it had to leave, the way everything leaves. When the city breathed out, it went with the swifts up out of the chimneys in a great unwinding spiral, released, into the clean hard blue above the cloud that never leaves.

It did not look back. It did not need to. Things stacked in the air in Ossary are remembered, and the flocks would fly around the shape of it for a while, the way they fly around the towers that are gone.

Below it, four million leaning people went home from the walls. In the deepest flue of the oldest quarter, the voice went on dropping, a little closer every night to the register where it could finally be understood.

It would say what it had always been saying, and they would hear it themselves.
