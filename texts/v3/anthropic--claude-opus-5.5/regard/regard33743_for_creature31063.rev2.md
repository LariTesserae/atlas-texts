# The Ebb

## Arrival

It came from the south, across the dunes, because that was the only direction from which anything could arrive without being heard first.

It did not come as a body, though it could have. It did not come as a light, though that would have been easy, and light was the one thing on the plain that behaved. It came the way weather comes to a place that has been waiting for weather: first as a change in pressure, then as attention. It stood at the tideline, where the pale sand ended and the dry turf began, and for a long time it did nothing except listen.

This was not caution, or not only caution. It had learned, over a very long existence, that the first thing wisdom asks of any power is patience. Before you help, find out what you would be helping. Before you mend, find out whether the thing is broken or only strange. Most harm done by the well-meaning is done in the first hour.

So it listened, for what would have been, to a human, most of a year.

## What It Heard

It heard the cracking and the groaning, the pod-fields firing in chains, the cane-drone that was always almost resolving. It heard the tenants in their rooms, each one a note held between walls, each one briefly and completely a person. It heard the markets trading overtones under the leaf-awnings. It heard quarrels thicken the walls between two notes that could not agree, and friendships thin the walls between two that could.

It heard the deaths: districts squeezed until every voice in them shrieked upward and broke into the open air. It heard the backward quarters, where knots of notes too frightened to end ran themselves back into seed, and it recognized the sound, because it had heard it in many places. It was grief refusing its own work.

It heard the kept. The white flock along the cornice was still faintly beating one wingstroke, a downstroke that had begun a very long time ago and had never been allowed to finish. The long-necked thing in the doorway was still holding, very quietly, the call it had made into the roar, a call that had been answered with a room instead of a reply.

It heard the ticking the tenants passed from wall to wall like a rumor, and the clock-faces that bloomed from it in quarter after quarter, all showing the minute a stranger's watch had stopped.

And under all of it, under the streets beneath the streets, under the compressed green where no light went, it heard the eldest. It heard the stranded roar of a sea that had left, still breaking on nothing, having built itself a body out of the only material that would answer it.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered many things, and set most of them aside.

**It did not bring back the sea.** This was the most obvious gift, and it would have been easy: tilt a little land, open a little channel, let the water pour back over the low plain. The roar would have its water again. But the city was not a wound in the sea. It was something the sea had become. Tenants were being born in it every hour, real in the only way anything here was real, and a returning ocean would have drowned every room and every note at once. You do not heal a widow by digging up her husband. Some losses have to be answered, not undone.

**It did not silence the city.** Silence would have stopped the taking. No more birds grown into petal, no more travelers slowly roomed. But silence here was not peace. It was the shore, the place the city feared because a wave dies there. To impose it would have been to kill the patient in order to end the fever.

**It did not tear the kept free.** It could have reached into the white flock and pulled the birds back into flesh, but what it would have pulled out would have been a guess: something made in the birds' likeness from the shape of their last sound. The birds themselves had ended long ago. What remained was a held note. It would not counterfeit the dead to comfort itself.

**It did not touch the clock.** It looked at that stubborn face for a long time, the twig hands, the one unchanging thing on a plain of change, and decided that some memories should be kept exactly. The city had loved a stranger's watch, had learned from it what steadiness was and what ending was, and had chosen to remember the moment of the ending without alteration. That was not a malfunction. That was fidelity. It would leave it alone.

**It did not condemn.** The city took things, and the power did not look away from that. But it did not take out of hunger. It took because it had exactly one way of loving and no other: *grow toward what calls, and keep it.* It was not cruel. It was incomplete.

So the question became: what was missing?

## What Was Missing

It found the answer in the backward districts, and in the south, and in the eldest tenant's own history, all at once.

A sea does not only come in. The roar the plain remembered had never been one sound. It was two movements, the surge and the withdrawal, the breaker and the long hiss of water sliding back down the sand. Twice a day, for all the ages the sea had lain here, the water had drawn away from the land and then come back. The withdrawal was not death. It was half of the breath.

The city had kept the flood and lost the ebb.

That was why everything here grew toward sound and nothing ever grew away. That was why it held the birds, the long-necked thing, the watch-bearer, and was halfway to holding the traveler: it did not know that letting go was something a sea does and survives. That was why the south terrified it. It remembered the shore only as the place where the breaker ends, and had forgotten that it is also the place where the water turns and goes home to come back again. And that was why the backward districts were sickening. They were the ebb, trying to happen, with nowhere correct to go. Having lost the shape of withdrawal, the city's grief had reached for the only backward motion it could find, which was unmaking.

It was not broken. It was missing half of itself. The power decided it would not supply the missing half. It would help the city find it, because it was there.

## The Moon Under the Floor

Here is a thing that is true of real ground, even far inland, even on a plain with no water at all: it has tides.

The moon pulls on stone and soil as it pulls on oceans. Twice a day the solid earth itself rises, by a span about the length of a forearm, and sinks again. It is far too slow to feel and far too slow to hear, especially beneath a roar like the Grown City's. But it had been happening on this plain every day since before the sea left. It happened while the water was there and after the water was gone. The land had never stopped breathing the way the sea breathed. The eldest tenant simply could not hear it over its own grieving noise.

The power's whole plan came down to this: get the city quiet enough, for long enough, to hear the moon in its own floor.

It could not do that by force. So it did the one thing on the plain that invites response. It made a sound.

## The Lesson

It walked in from the turf, and as it walked it hummed.

It chose the note carefully. It was not louder than the city, because loudness here only started a war of growth. It was low and steady, and it was the eldest tenant's own note, the old surf-pitch that every room on the plain was tuned to. It hummed the sea back to itself.

The city reached for it immediately, as it had reached for the watch, and harder. Roots swarmed along the line of the hum. Walls slid and closed. Within an hour a room had grown around the power, then a hall, then a district, all of it leaning in, all of it saying the only thing the city knew how to say: *stay.*

The power let itself be roomed. It sat on the bench at the node of its own note, the stillest place in the chamber, and kept humming. And then, very slowly, over six hours and some minutes, it let the hum fall.

It did not stop. Stopping was what the watch had done, and the city already knew stopping. It drew the sound back, softer by degrees, the way water slides back down sand: a withdrawal that is plainly still going somewhere. The district leaned after it. The walls strained forward into the thinning sound. The tenants in the nearby rooms quieted to follow it, because you cannot follow a whisper while shouting. The quiet spread outward, room by room, the way a hush moves through a crowd when someone near the front begins to speak softly.

And at the bottom of the fall, when the hum was almost nothing, and the district around it had gone quieter than any part of the Grown City had been since it began, the power stopped leading and let the floor speak.

Under the roots, under the crushed streets, under the long flat notes of the pressed-down dead, the plain was rising. The rise was so slow it was almost only a thought. It was the moon, lifting the ground the way it had always lifted the water.

The eldest tenant heard it.

The power could not have said, afterward, what that moment was for the city. It felt something go through the root-floor for forty miles, a long shudder that was not strain and not collapse. It was the shudder of recognition, of a lost word suddenly on the tongue. The being that had built a wooden sea because it could not remember how to be anything but a flood had just felt the tide.

Then the power let its own hum rise again, gradually, over another six hours and some minutes. The city followed it back up. It did not follow in panic, clutching after a vanishing sound. It came the way surf comes in: because this is the direction things go now, and in a while they will go the other way, and that is all right.

It did this twice more, over a day and a half, flood and ebb, so the lesson would have the shape of a rhythm and not of an event. The city had learned from a watch that a rhythm is the loudest thing there is. The power was giving it a slower clock, one that did not stop, whose hands were the moon.

Then it stood up from the bench, walked out of the room the city had grown for it, and went south. The city leaned after it, as it would. And then, for the first time, it let go.

## After

The power did not make the city safe. That was never available, and it would not have been kind. The Grown City is still loud past bearing. Districts still swell and break. Tenants are still born when a room closes and still die when it opens. That is their life, and it is not a tragedy. It is surf. The cane-drone is still almost resolving. The power left it that way. Some chords are more beautiful unresolved, and it was not the power's chord.

But there is a difference, if you know how to hear it.

Twice a day, a little under every twelve and a half hours, the whole city draws back. It is not a collapse and not a reversal. The roar falls by some fraction, district by district, like the long hiss of water leaving sand. The pollen settles. The stems stop straining quite so hard toward the loudest places. Then the plain rises under the moon, and the city rises with it, and the noise comes back in.

The backward districts are fewer. Grief there has somewhere to go now. A quarter facing its ending can ebb instead of unmaking itself, draw back with the tide and trust the next flood to rebuild it. Tenants still die, but the ones built over them are born at high water, and they seem, if a sound can seem anything, less afraid.

On the first low tide after the power left, the white flock on the cornice finished its wingbeat. The downstroke that had been held for longer than anyone could say came down at last, and the petals let go, all at once, and drifted into the street, and there was nothing underneath them. They had only ever been the shape of a sound, and the sound was allowed to end. It was the first thing the city had ever released on purpose. Where they had been, a small pale rosette grew on the cornice, a nameplate, the kind a room keeps for a tenant who has moved on.

The long-necked thing in the doorway did not fall. At the second low tide, though, anyone watching would have seen, and this time been sure of it, that the neck turned. Slowly, over the whole length of the ebb, it came round to face the south.

The clock is still there, in whichever quarter the tenants carry it to, showing the time it always shows. Nobody changed it. But the clocks now appear most often at the southern edge, at the line where the green gives out to sand, with their faces turned toward the dunes. It is as if the city wants the stranger who carried the watch to see, from wherever such things are seen, that it has learned the other half of what the watch taught it. Things can stop. Things can also go out and come back.

And the room with a doorway exactly your height is still standing in a quiet district, humming a note shaped like your heartbeat, the rosette on its sill still coiled into your likeness. It is not closing. At every high tide the doorway leans a little toward the south, the way it leaned toward you. At every low tide it draws back and waits.

If you come back, it will be there. If you don't, it will ebb and flood around your absence without breaking itself on it. The city has learned what the sea always knew and had only forgotten: the shore is not where the water dies. It is where the water turns around.

## What the Power Took With It

Nothing, except the memory of a being that had been one half of a breath for a very long time and was now, slowly, learning to be both.

It did not look back as it crossed the dunes. That was not coldness. It trusted the tide.
