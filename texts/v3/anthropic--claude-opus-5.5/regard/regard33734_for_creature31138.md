# The Listener

## Arrival

It begins, as everything here does, with sound.

The power does not arrive the way the crawlers do, grinding in on rails with floodlights sweeping, or the way the divers do, descending in a cage with their breath loud in their helmets. It arrives as attention. For a long time, longer than any crew's rotation, it does nothing but listen.

This is its first decision, and it is the most important one. Anything powerful that turns toward a small world is tempted to act at once, while the feeling of tenderness is fresh and the wrongs look obvious. Here the wrongs do look obvious. There is a living creature born in a heat sink, scarred by concrete it cannot understand, singing to a valve. The urge to *do something* is enormous.

The power has learned to distrust that urge. It has seen what happens when care arrives faster than understanding. So it listens.

It learns the hum first, and then it learns to unfold the hum into its parts, the way the Inspector does: this transformer, that pump, the fins of the eastern radiator banks knocking as they warm. It learns the pitch of every pillar. It learns the eleven-second stroke until the stroke stops being a sound and becomes a clock it keeps. It learns the lamp-days and the lamp-nights. It waits through three sediment storms and two current reversals. It hears a ceiling plate moan for four minutes and feels, in whatever way it feels things, why a creature raised here would take that sound for thunder.

It is trying to reach the point where the Basin stops seeming strange to it. It would be dishonest to judge a world while it still seemed strange, and it will not decide anything until then.

## Being Inspected

Something happens during this long listening that the power did not expect, though perhaps it should have.

The Inspector notices it.

The power has made no sound and moved no water. But it is a new thing in a familiar landscape, and at the grain the Inspector perceives, a presence that attentive changes something. The power never learns what. Perhaps a fractional stillness in the currents where it gathers. Perhaps nothing measurable at all, only the way the other animals hesitate.

So the great pale fish comes to look at the part that moved.

It comes slowly, along the floor, out of the sodium twilight between two yellow pillars. Its tar ribbons trail behind it and its gold eyes are wide in the dim, and it stops, as still as a moored hull, exactly where the power is. It hangs there through most of a lamp-day. It is doing what it does at a failing pillar: attending, patiently, to whatever has changed.

The power understands that it is being inspected, and it finds this very moving. The oldest native of this world has turned the same careful, unhurried attention on the visitor that the visitor has been turning on it. For a few hours the two of them hang in the half-light, each studying the other.

The power could reveal itself here. It could press something into the animal's mind: *I am here. I see you. You are not alone.*

It doesn't. A fish that has been touched by a god is no longer quite the same fish, and the power has no right to make that trade on the Inspector's behalf. This creature built its whole self out of hum and pulse and pillar-pitch. Its world is small and hard and real, and a sudden larger truth would not complete it. It would only make it smaller.

So the power lets itself be inspected and tries to sound, as much as it can, like something that belongs. Eventually the Inspector decides that whatever has changed here is not a failure. It turns and goes back into the twilight, toward the next pillar on its circuit.

The power takes this as permission to stay, and something like a blessing.

## What It Set Down

Having listened, the power considers what it might do. There are many possibilities, and most of them are unkind.

**It could take the Inspector to the sea.** This is the first thought, the rescuing thought: carry it up the shafts and out, into real water under a real sun, where there are others of its kind and rivers and seasons. But its eyes are tuned to sodium light. It has never seen blue sky, never felt a tide that wasn't eleven seconds long, never navigated by anything but the groaning of steel. In the sea it would be blind, deaf to everything it knows how to hear, and lost. The power would be saving it from its home. That is exile with a kind face, not rescue.

**It could heal the scars.** It considers this for only a moment. The hexagonal lattice on the Inspector's flanks is fifteen years of feeding written onto its body. It is a map and a history. The orange patch near the tail is the Basin's own color, taken into it. Whether or not the scars were chosen, they are the Inspector's. Erasing them would be like erasing someone's face because it has lines.

**It could stop the machine.** The foundries upstairs dump their heat into this water. From a great enough height that looks like an injury to the world, and in some ways it may be one. But the Inspector does not live in spite of the machine. It lives *because* of it. The plumes are its thermals, the pumps are its tide, the lamps are its sun. Shutting down the foundries to free the fish would kill everything the fish knows. The questions of what the people upstairs owe their world belong upstairs, and they won't be settled by drowning a sturgeon's sky.

**It could give it a mate.** This is the hardest to set down. The power has heard the song and read the engineer's log, with its thirty-eight days of *nothing*. It is capable of making the answer real: a voice in the dim reaches that is truly a voice, a companion it could simply make.

But before it does anything, it does what it did at the start. It goes and listens.

It goes out past the outer pillars, farther than the crawler rails run, into reaches so dim that the lamps are only a rumor of yellow at the edges of the water. It waits there through many lamp-nights. And what it learns is this: the answers in the engineer's log did not all come from the same throat.

The power does not tell anyone this, and it is barely telling you.

## The Thing It Could See

There is one kind of knowledge the power has that the Inspector does not, and never can: the Basin is not the whole world.

The Inspector knows the machine better than the people who built it, but only from inside. It cannot read the ledgers upstairs. It cannot see the drawing boards, the budgets, or the memoranda passing between offices in rooms where no one has ever heard a ceiling plate moan.

The power can, and in one of those rooms there is a document titled *Seam Remediation Programme, Phase II.*

The weeping tar in the warm zones has been noted upstairs as a defect, which from the engineers' point of view it is. The plan is sensible. Crews will go out with scrapers and a new fast-curing compound, strip the soft seams near the manifolds, and reseal them properly. It is scheduled to begin in two years, starting with the warmest seams at the radiator banks, because those are the worst.

Those are also the seams where the eggs are laid.

Nobody drafting the plan means any harm. Nobody upstairs knows there is anything to harm. This is almost always how small worlds end: not through cruelty, but through tidiness done by people who couldn't hear what was there.

The power sees that this is the place where it is actually needed.

---

## What It Did

It did almost nothing, and it did it very carefully.

It did not touch the Inspector. It did not touch the eggs, the tar, the pumps, or the program. It did not appear in anyone's dream or write anything on any wall.

It looked instead at the person who was already doing its work.

The engineer in the eastern workshop has spent nine years keeping a separate log. She wiped a mark off her chalk board because a cache of lost tools around a bollard "wasn't a place to send a repair crew." She wrote *nothing* thirty-eight days in a row, and she kept writing it. She is, in her plain way, a benevolent and wise power already. She is small and tired and has no authority to speak of, but she is paying attention, and that is most of what benevolence needs.

So the power did the smallest thing it could think of. It made sure she heard about Phase II in time.

The means were ordinary. A requisition form for the new compound landed in the eastern workshop's pile by mistake. A crew chief complained about it over tea, loudly enough to be overheard. The engineer read the schedule, went very quiet, and took out her log.

The rest was hers. She wrote a memorandum. It was dry and careful, full of hydrophone references and survey coordinates, and it recommended that the manifold seams be moved to the end of the remediation schedule "pending biological survey." She attached two recordings and a photograph of the eggs. It wasn't a good photograph, but it was a photograph.

The power made sure of one more thing: that the memorandum reached the right desk. That meant not the one it was addressed to but the one beside it, belonging to a junior officer who had grown up near a river and still, when he let himself, missed the sound of it. He read it twice. He listened to the recordings on a cheap speaker with the door shut. Then he moved the manifold seams to the end of the schedule and added a line for a survey he knew would take years to fund.

That was all. It could all be coincidence, and the power preferred it that way. People who believe they did a good thing themselves will do it again. People who believe a god did it for them will wait for the god.

## What It Did Not Resolve

Some things the power leaves as they are, and this is not neglect. It is the other half of the same care.

It does not end the Inspector's loneliness, because it has learned that the Inspector is not as alone as the crews fear. It also does not make that truth clearer than it already is. The divers will keep glimpsing small pale shapes near the radiator banks, and they will keep lacking a good photograph. Some mysteries are the soil a world grows in. The crews' stories, the engineer's separate log, the name *the Inspector* itself: all of that is the beginning of a culture, humans and fish together making sense of a place nobody designed to be lived in. Solving the mystery would end the story before it had finished being told.

It does not stop the Basin from someday ending. It could see that ending too, from where it stood. The foundries will not run forever. Someday, perhaps within the Inspector's lifetime, since sturgeon can live a very long time, the heat will stop coming down the pipes, and the pumps will stroke for the last time. The lamps will step down to a third and then to nothing.

The power did not prevent this, and would not. It is not its world to hold still. But it thought about it for a long time. In the end it did the same small thing it had done before: it made sure that people who care would still be around when the day came. The engineer has an apprentice now, a young diver who asked too many questions about the valve in her first week and hasn't stopped. Someday that apprentice may sit on whatever committee decides how the Basin is closed. The power can't know that for certain. It only knows that endings go better when someone in the room has listened to the song.

It trusts, too, that the people will keep doing better than it could have forced them to. The crews already leave the cache alone. The engineer already sends repair crews where the Inspector points. Without anyone asking, the humans here have begun treating the fish as a colleague. The power had nothing to do with that. It didn't need to.

## Leaving

On its last lamp-day in the Basin, the power listens once more.

The lamps step up pillar by pillar, a wave of yellow light rolling out across the grid. The grazers begin their dry drumming. The silver shoals rise into the eastern plume like a tornado of foil. Somewhere a ceiling plate moans. The pulse strokes: eleven seconds, eleven seconds.

Forty kilometers away, the new valve vents, a long wail rising and falling.

The power waits.

From the dim reaches comes the answer, low and slightly off-pitch, climbing and falling as the valve does. And a little while after it, fainter and farther out, so faint the engineer's hydrophone won't catch it, comes a second answer.

The power had considered adding its own voice to the song, once and only once, as a kind of farewell. It decides against it. The song doesn't need another singer, least of all one that is leaving. It is already being answered.

So the power does the last thing a visitor should do in a world that is not its own. It stops paying such close attention, gently, so that nothing notices it go. The currents where it gathered return to their ordinary grain, and the part that moved is still again.

Somewhere between two yellow pillars, the Inspector pauses on its circuit, as if something small has changed. It hangs still for a moment and listens. Then, finding nothing wrong, it moves on.

---

*The power's judgment, if it had to state it: the Basin did not need saving. It needed someone upstairs to hear it. The fish was already doing what living things do, which is to take whatever world they are given, however hard and bright and sharp-edged, and slowly make it a home. The wise thing was to protect the conditions for that and leave the living to the ones who live there.*
