*...does not flinch.*

The masons say this with approval: the old stone is cured, settled, trustworthy. The levellers say it more carefully. They have knelt on the old ground of the western districts and pressed their palms flat and felt nothing come back. There was no swelling or settling, none of the held tension of the bad days. There was only cold, and a slow giving-way, like a hand going slack in yours.

It is here, at the seam between the stone that flinches and the stone that has stopped, that something else begins to pay attention.

---

## I. Coming Quietly

The first problem is that attention has weight.

A power of this kind knows its own size, the way anything wise does. When it turns toward a place, the place feels it: a pressure, a brightening, a sense of being looked at from every side at once. Most worlds can bear this. This one cannot. In a city where a dropped crate of bottles once calved four acres into the tidal flats, a great power arriving all at once would be the sea-born grandfather waking from his nightmare, shouting.

So the first thing it does is learn to walk.

It comes in with the fog, because things come in with the fog here and nobody remarks on it. It sets itself down heel and toe together, its weight spread across the whole sole of whatever it is using for feet. It spends what is, for it, a moment, and for the city most of a winter, learning the hush. It learns the pitch of the public whisper, and the flattened palm drawn sideways for *slow down*. It learns that the amber lamp is the hour and the slow red pulse is the tide. It learns the cupped hand tipped toward the ground, and it makes that sign to itself, often.

No one notices it. That is the point.

A few things do. A mole-warden in the Kettle district sees every molehill in a square stop rising at once, for about the length of a held breath, and then resume. A pale deer in a settling quarter lifts its head from a lichened wall and looks for a long time at a doorway where nothing is standing, then goes back to eating. On a chimney above the Hollis, a gull turns its head, considers the empty air beside it, and holds out one wing, flat and steady, not trembling.

The power, which has manners, holds something out in return.

## II. What It Sees

Then it looks, and it sees what the people have always almost known.

There is someone under.

It is not a god, or a monster, or the sea, though it came out of the sea. The power has met many kinds of being and has no ready name for this one, and it does not insist on finding one. The people call it Under, and that will do.

The first and largest fact about Under is that it is young. The city speaks of it as a light sleeper in the next room, which is close, but the power would put it differently: an infant. Its whole body is sixty years old, and at its eastern edge it is made new every day, so that at its growing margin it is always only a few days old. There it is soft, warm, and wet, and startled by sharp sounds the way a newborn's whole body jerks at a slammed door. Green stone does not shatter because it is weak. It shatters because it is frightened, and it has not yet learned that a noise is not a blow.

The power reads the first engineer's notebooks without opening the vault. They are not, as the Survey believes, mostly drawings of fog. They are drawings of someone in the fog: the same shape again and again, sketched by a man who had seen something drifting in the cold sea-air and wanted very badly to give it somewhere to rest. The Condensers are not really machines. They are cupped hands. The power understands this, and it understands too that it is not going to tell the Survey.
