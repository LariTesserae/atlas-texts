# X. The one who came to look

It came down into the basin the way everyone does, on foot, over a morning, down a slope so gentle it hardly counts as one. It had other ways of arriving. It chose this one because this is how Cantle is entered, and it wanted to meet the place on the place's terms. It passed the last stone, the round mossed boulder that looks like a sleeping animal, and it did not wake it.

When it stepped onto the Sward, the frogs stopped.

Every frog in the basin fell silent at once, the way they do before a surge. The aldermen in the gutters went quiet mid-boom. The emerald frogs on the windows froze. Under the mat, the clock had tightened. The Sward had felt something heavier than anything it had held in a very long time, and it did what a builder does when an enormous weight settles on its ground: it began to prepare.

So the first thing the power did in Cantle was make itself small. It thinned its presence until it weighed what a traveler weighs. It stood in the warm water with the mat dipping under its feet and waited, the way you wait for a startled animal, until the hum loosened and the first frog, uncertainly, began again. Then another. Then the whole chorus, in a wave.

It took this as its first lesson, and the one that governed everything after. *In Cantle, whatever stays is built around.* The rule applies to newts and snails and sleeping scholars. It applies even more to a power. If it stayed, the town would begin, very slowly, to take its shape.

It kept moving.

## The listening

It walked the basin for three days, which is how long a hall lives.

It learned the frogs' silence and got off the lawns when they fell quiet. It watched a swift pair lift their nest from a dying belfry and carry it, lurching, across the open air, and it did not catch the egg they dropped, because the heron standing below had been waiting all morning and had its own young in the reeds. It sat by the steaming pools and watched the old eels turn their heads. It let a crab pinch it, once, for coming too close to the archive, and it apologized.

Mostly it listened to the clock.

The scholars are right that the clock has layers. The power could hear all of them, down to the finest buzz. It was not so much thinking as something older and simpler than thinking, the way a heartbeat is simpler than a mind. There were instructions under there, worn smooth like the lintels: *house whoever is here. Learn what they use. Grow more of it. Keep them well.* It also found the count, the census, running constantly, weight on the pads and warmth in the walls and mouths at the conduits. A frog is a unit. A newt sealed in a doorframe is a unit. The count was vast and fine-grained and it had never once come up empty.

The power had come partly to answer a question: *is it suffering?*

It listened for a long time before it was sure. The Sward was not suffering. Its effort was enormous, but it was the effort of a river, not of someone lost. There was no loneliness in the clock, because loneliness requires expecting someone who does not come, and the Sward did not expect. It counted, and the count was always full. What the scholars read as tragedy, a town endlessly rebuilding for people who never arrived, was not tragic from inside. From inside, the town had never been empty for a single hour.

There was one thing, though, far down in the oldest layer of the pulse. It was a slot in the count, kept open, never filled. It was not a wound, and not quite a longing. It was a reservation, the way a host sets one extra place at the table. The power followed it through the fibers of the mat and found where it surfaced: at the exact center of the basin, a plank on two legs, facing east.

## What it did not do

A power like this could have done a great many things in Cantle. It considered each of them honestly. Refusing to consider them would have been its own kind of carelessness.

**It could have restored the plans.** The whole design lay in the crab archive, and deeper than that in the clock itself: every carved leaf, the grooved stair-treads, the latch sized for a thumb, the sharp deep lettering. It could have given the Sward back its memory whole, and the town would have risen crisp and ornate, exactly as it was first imagined.

It did not. Those corners had been worn away because no one needed them, and wearing them away was how the Sward learned. Restoring the plans would mean making the town beautiful for no one again and erasing the long, patient attention the Sward had been paying to the heron's neck and the frog's damp skin and the eel's preference for shade. A capital with eight carved leaves is lovely. A window-ledge that holds a finger's depth of water is a gift to someone real. The power would not trade the second for the first.

**It could have brought people.** It is not true that no one could come. There are always people somewhere who need a town. But Cantle is already inhabited. A town tuned to human use would, in a few thousand rebuilds, have no ledges for frogs, no niches for swifts, no ramps into warm eel-pools. The power knew what happens to marshes when a city decides it lives there. It would not do that to the citizens who were already here, and who had been here, faithfully, for ages.

**It could have let the Sward rest.** It could have stilled the churn and let the last buildings stand forever, the town finally finished. But everything in Cantle is built on the rhythm now. The emerald frogs breed on a schedule of houses. The swift chicks learn to fly by being thrown from a cresting tower. The eels eat the dissolution, and the newts wait out a building's life in its walls. A still Cantle would be a museum, and every living thing in it would starve. Nor did the Sward want to stop. There was nothing in the clock that resembled fatigue in the way a person means it. You do not rescue a river from flowing.

**It could have told the Sward the truth,** that the ones who planned it were gone and not coming back. It looked at this option longest. It concluded that the Sward already knew this in the only way that mattered to it: it counted and found the count full. Telling it would be answering a question it had never asked.

**It could have made the resident real.** The scholar's notebook, the one that breaks off mid-sentence, was lying open in a young hall's gallery, its owner asleep in a hammock above. The power read it. It understood what the scholar had been reaching for: the single imagined creature the whole town seems designed around. It is tall and narrow and long-legged, keeps its skin wet, and sleeps high. A power could make such a thing, some heron-frog-swift-eel chimera, and walk it through the doors the town has been shaping, a resident at last.

It did not. That creature does not need to exist. It already does, in the only form that works.

## What it did

It did four small things.

**First, it read the six characters.** It stood in the crab archive, very still, until the crabs decided it was furniture, and it read the fragment the scholars love, the one with the broken edge. It knew the script. There is no script it does not know.

The characters were a word of welcome. More precisely, they were the kind of word you carve over a door to mean *whoever you are, this is for you*. The founders had not written a name or a rank or a population. They had written an open door.

It considered telling the scholars. It decided the meaning was not lost and did not need recovering. The word had not eroded into nonsense. It had been translated, a million times over, into ledges and niches and ramps and lamps. The single rounded glyph over the lintels now is an accurate rendering of the original. It left the fragment where it was and gave the crabs back their corner.

**Second, it found the old woman.**

She was not hard to find. The power could feel every sealed pocket in every wall, and among the newts and snails and beetles and seeds there was one larger warmth, curled in the thick doorframe of a hall on the eastern lawn, breathing slowly, drinking when the bead of sweet water came.

It did not open the wall. It waited, moving from hall to hall so as not to be built around, returning every few hours, until the hall's roofline darkened and softened and the pocket opened on its own. She climbed out into the steam, blinking, a little fatter, and saw it standing there. She was not afraid. After twenty years in Cantle, very little arrives that surprises you.

It asked her whether she was well. She said she was. It asked whether she wanted anything: to leave, to be carried somewhere, to have anything mended in her body or undone in her history. She thought about it honestly, which the power appreciated. Then she said, *No. I only wanted to be somewhere that thought I lived there.*

It asked whether she knew the town counted her. She laughed and said she had always assumed so.

They walked together for an hour, keeping moving. She showed it which towers ring low. When the frogs fell silent they climbed a gallery together and held on while the Sward rolled. When the singing came back she went looking for another doorway, and the power let her go.

**Third, it went to the bench.**

It had known from the start that it would. The reserved place in the count surfaced here. The plank was polished smooth and slightly dipped, facing east, kept exactly as it is. It may be the last thing the Sward remembers being asked for.

A power that sat there might be read as the one the bench was kept for. It might close the reservation. The whole slow drift of the town might tilt back toward that weight, one heavy human-shaped presence outweighing ten thousand frogs in the census, and the ledges would begin to dry.

So it thought carefully about how much of itself to bring.

It came before dawn, when the mist lay thickest. Two frogs were already on the bench. It did not move them. It sat at the very end of the plank, weighing what a traveler weighs, and watched the sun come up over the eastern curve of the root-levee.

Cantle at sunrise, from the center, is something almost no one has seen. The whole town is visible at once, every building isolated on the board. The mist lifts off the mirror-water in sheets. Swifts climb a cresting tower and are flung off into the light. The lamps along the orphan street close one by one, sated with moths, and the aldermen boom the dawn edges of their gutters. It looks exactly like a town waking up, and it is one.

The power understood that this is what the bench was for. Someone, once, had asked for a place to sit and look at the town they had made and see that it was good. It sat for the length of one sunrise and saw it, and found it good. It let the Sward feel that: one more unit in the census, a guest.

Then it stood up before the Sward could decide it lived there. The heron was waiting a few paces off in the shallows, as it does in the afternoons, patient and a little impatient, and the power stepped aside and gave it back its bench.

It did not fill the reservation, and it did not close it. An open door is not a wound. It left the place kept, for whoever comes.

**Fourth, and last, it finished the sentence.**

It did not do this in the notebook. The scholar can finish it, or not. It finished it only in itself, the way you complete a thought for someone you are fond of while they sleep:

*The belfries have cup-shaped niches high up, so the resident sleeps high and swims low, and booms in the gutters to find its way, and keeps its eggs in window-water for a single day, and seals itself into the walls to be fed, and comes at night to the lamps. It is carried across the air by its parents when its house begins to die. It is thrown into flight by the rising of its own town. It remembers every pool. It lives in the corners of the past. There is no such animal. There is only everyone, and the town has been drawing their portrait for a million rebuilds, and it is a good likeness.*

## Leaving

It walked out up the imperceptible slope toward the soft round stone.

The frogs did not fall silent when it went. It had made itself light enough that its leaving barely registered, and it took that as the measure of whether it had done well.

Behind it, a blister the size of a barn was splitting open on the western lawn. The ribs pushed up, cracking like pistol shots. Emerald frogs were already climbing the membranes before they hardened. Down in the lowest room of the new hall, a ramp was forming, a little gentler than the last one. Over its door a single rounded glyph was rising out of the lignin, the old word, still saying exactly what it meant.

The power did not look back to see whether the town had grown anything for it. It hoped it had not.

It had not.

The count, that evening, came up full.
