# The Attention

## I. Arrival

It does not come up out of the cloud.

A power like this does not climb. It does not arrive with a sound, which is fortunate, because Haum has no room for another sound. It does not appear as a light over the dome, or a voice at the Breath, or a stranger in grey at the lip. If it did, the swifts would scatter, the dogs would lose their minds, and the people would stop talking. The people of Haum must not stop talking.

So the power arrives the way the frost does. It condenses slowly out of the thin air and settles on everything at once, so lightly that nothing notices.

And then, for a long time, it does nothing but pay attention.

It takes a long time because the power is wise, and wisdom here means understanding one thing before touching it. Nothing in Haum can be understood quickly. The power could know every grain of the litter in an instant. It could count the beetles, map the roots, and trace the path of every drop of water back through a thousand bodies. It does all of that, and then it sets that kind of knowing aside. Knowing everything about a place does not mean it has understood the place.

So it slows down. It slows past the pace of the people, who live too fast to feel the mountain. It slows past the pace of the aum, who put on a finger's width in a lifetime. It settles into the pace of the lizards, the one creature here that lives at the speed of the heat. It lies with Sau along the base of Old Knuckle, belly to the warm crack, eyes half-lidded, and listens.

## II. The Temptations

A benevolent power sees suffering and wants to end it. That is the first thing benevolence does, and often it is right. This one looks at Haum with love and sees at once everything it could give.

**It could rekindle the mountain.** The ember is dying and the power could feed it. It considers this for a long, lizard-paced moment. Then it sees the aum. The aum spent ten thousand years learning to live on almost-heat, with its furred leaves and its hoarded sap, drinking a few mouthfuls of ice a night. A living mountain would crack the bowl. Real steam would rot the dead in the branches. The lizards would have nothing left to read, because the heat would be everywhere. It would mean saving the grandmother by burning down the house.

**It could bring rain.** Water is everything here, and the power has the whole sky. It could give them springs, wells, and a pool in the old temple. But then the rule would break: *every drop of water in the town has passed through a plant.* The tapping-houses would no longer be the heart of anything. No one would need to know that the Gossip runs fast and thin, or that Aunt Remi runs slow. Ilu would no longer be the thing the tree uses to carry water to people, because nobody would need carrying. Cups would stop being finished. Tears would stop being a gift. The power understands that the people of Haum are not *coping* with scarcity. They have composed themselves out of it, the way a song is made of the notes that are not played.

**It could heal the dying tree by the terraces.** It could bring back the rosette whose loss lets that hard shaft of light fall where the fine workers gather and argue. But the shaft is where the fine work happens. The death has a place in Haum, and the mourning does too. The trunk will stand for centuries.

**It could tell them.** It could explain what the vents are, how long the heat will last, and what their mountain was when it was young. It could explain that the aum is one plant, and that the lowlands have iron, glass, and medicine. It could give them the whole world. But Haum already knows exactly as much of the world as it needs. Past the cloud there is nothing they particularly need. They are right about that, and the power will not make them wrong.

**It could take the fear away,** the fear under the noise and the counting of gaps between breaths. The power considers this one longest, because it would be the gentlest thing. No fire, no flood, only a quiet unknotting in every chest. Then it listens to the town: the querns, the four notes sung over and over, the children shrieking under the swifts. It understands that the fear and the song are one thing. The noise of Haum is love for something dying. If the fear were taken, the love would lose its reason to shout.

So the power does not do any of these things.

This is not indifference. The power has fallen in love with Haum, and that is exactly why it will not remake it. Here, doing nothing is not the absence of an act. It is the hardest act the power has, and it keeps choosing it, over and over, the way Ilu keeps a notch plugged with wax for nine years because the rhythm is not yet right.

But the power keeps listening, because doing nothing is not quite the same as not being there.

## III. The Names

It learns the names.

Anything could count the people of Haum. The power learns their names and the way each one is said. It learns all thirty lizards, including the sounds that belong to no living language. It learns the eleven notches of Old Knuckle and the tempers of every notch in every trunk in the bowl. It learns the rabbits under each house and which house they belong to.

It learns Ilu's family. It does not stop at sixty. It counts them all, back through Aunt Remi and the mother's uncle, down through the bones on the shelves, and into the drowned rooms whose doorways once opened on lanes. It goes all the way down, past where anyone in Haum remembers, to the first people who climbed out of the cloud. The lizards looked at them with gold, lidded eyes and decided not to object.

It learns the spider's names. Every dawn Ilu lifts frost from the web by the door, drinks it, and says a name, a different one each day. She thinks the names go nowhere, the way a dropped word goes nowhere. The power catches every one.

It does not tell her. But from now on those names are kept.

## IV. The Measure

The power does one thing that no one in Haum can do. It measures the Breath honestly.

The people have been counting the pauses for generations, afraid that each is longer than the last. The power goes down through the litter and the roots, through the foundations and the old stone, into the dark rock where the last of the fire sleeps. It feels the heat as it truly is.

Here is what it finds. The Breath is dying, as the people fear. But it is dying slowly, so slowly that they have been mourning at the wrong speed. The pauses are not lengthening the way they think. What they hear is the mountain breathing unevenly, the way the very old breathe, and that is not the same as breathing its last. The coal has more grey in it than red, but there is still red.

Deeper, the power finds something else. Under the old temple, far below the granary and the worn carvings that can only be read with fingers, the ember has shifted. There is a new warmth down there. It is not near the surface yet. It will take longer than any living person's life to rise. But the mountain has one more breath in it.

The power does not announce this. Haum has its own way of learning where the heat is going, and the power will not replace it.

## V. The One Who Went Down

Twenty-nine lizards, some people say, because one has not been seen in a long while, and no one wants to say aloud that it is gone.

The power finds it.

Its name is Tessam-Under-Stone, one of the oldest names. It is not dead. It went down through the rabbit runs and the gaps between drowned walls, past the shelves of the dead, into rooms nobody has opened in a thousand years. Now it lies alone on a floor of grey lava paving, far beneath the temple, belly flat to the stone, listening.

The stone is not warm yet. Tessam knows it will be. It has gone down to wait for the heat the way its kind always has, lying where the next breath will come before anyone else feels it. It is very old and will probably die down there, long before the stone warms enough for the town to notice. But the lizards have always done this. They go first, and they lie still, so the town will know where to follow.

The power weighs whether to bring it back. It could open a way, send a dog nosing down a tunnel, or let a cellar wall crumble at the right moment so that some child finds it. It decides not to. Tessam is not lost. It is doing its work. Someday, when someone digs a cool store beneath the temple, they will find the bones of a great lizard lying flat on the old paving, with its body angled toward the center like a spoke. The keepers will look at each other at the Breath, late, and understand. The cooking courts will begin, over generations, to drift that way.

But Tessam is the only living thing in Haum that is alone.

Everything in the town is crowded against everything else. Rabbits whistle under floors, swifts roar into walls, and the dead lie across the laps of the trees. The people are loud with each other even when they are tender. There is one creature down in the quiet, alone, lying on a cold stone, listening to a heat that has not arrived.

So the power goes down and lies beside it.

That is the whole of what it does there. It makes no sound, because Tessam has gone down precisely to be where there is no sound. It is simply present, the way the town is present at the grandmother's bedside, so that the one who is listening knows it is not alone. Tessam opens one gold eye and looks at nothing in particular. It does not object. Then it closes the eye, and the two of them lie together in the dark and listen to the mountain.

## VI. The Child at the Lip

Late one night, on the worn edge of the kept world, a girl is standing with her toes in the warm and her face toward the dark.

The power could speak to her. It could give her a vision or a word, or show her the frost from above or the cloud sea from the stars. It does none of that.

It does only one thing, and it is barely a thing at all. It is there, just past the lip, in the cold, in the silence that Haum is afraid of. It stays there so that the silence she is facing is, for once, not empty.

She does not know this. But she stays a little longer than she meant to. Because she stays, she hears what nobody in Haum hears, because nobody stands still enough out there. She hears the frost forming. It is a faint, dry crackle, everywhere at once, as the thin sky gives up its water onto the dead stone. It sounds almost like a voice. It is the sound of the only thing Haum takes from outside itself: the gift, a few mouthfuls a night, per rosette, of air turned to ice.

It is not nothing out there. It is where the water is made.

She does not step over. She says "Hello?" to the dark, very quietly for someone from Haum, the way Ilu says a name to the spider that has none. Nothing answers, and she seems satisfied by that. She turns and runs back down into the warm, loud town, shouting someone's name. Perhaps, when she is old and a keeper herself, she will be a little less afraid of the quiet. Or perhaps not. The power does not need her to be. It only wanted her to hear the frost once.

## VII. What It Keeps

Then, at the pace of the lizards, the power understands what it is for.

Haum has one rule under all its other rules: *nothing is thrown over the lip.* Everything the town uses stays in the bowl, the dead included. Water goes from frost to leaf to sap to cup to body to shroud to frost. Nothing is lost. Haum's whole life is a refusal to let anything fall into the nothing outside.

But one thing will go over the lip in the end, and that is Haum itself.

The Breath will go grey. The last heat will leave the rock. The aum will stop drinking, the swifts will go elsewhere, and the lizards will lie on cold stone. The litter will dry and scatter. The dome will wear down until the lip is gone, and there will be no kept world and no dry world, only a low knuckle of rust-coloured rock under a violet sky. Nobody will know that a woman named Ilu once moved a sap-jar two fingers to the left so an old lizard would not knock it over.

Haum cannot prevent this. No rule it has can hold the whole bowl back from the edge.

The power can.

It does not do this by saving the mountain or stopping time. It does it by holding what Haum was. The power chooses to be the one thing outside the lip that loses nothing. It keeps all of it: the names, the notches and their tempers, the thousand names of the nameless spider, and the four notes sung over and over. It keeps the sound of the swift-storm at dusk, the smell of the litter like a spice cupboard, and the taste of a rabbit in the sap the following year. It keeps Aunt Remi, and Imri-of-the-Old-Breath, and Tessam lying in the dark. It keeps the child's quiet hello to the frost.

Haum put nothing over the lip, ever. When the time comes for Haum itself to go over, the power will be waiting on the other side, and none of it will be lost there either.

## VIII. Last

The power stays for a while longer. How long is hard to say at the pace of lizards. A few centuries is not long at that pace.

It lies at the Breath at night among the ring of lizards, with the people sitting farther out, roasting beetles and talking over one another. It feels the push of dry, sweet air from the crack in the court. The air is very steady, and there is still red in the coal.

Haum does not know the power is there, and that is correct. The town is not waiting to be rescued. It is busy. There is frost to drink and sap to carry. There are the dead to lift into the branches and to bring down again. It has a great deal to say, all at once, at the top of its voice.

The power listens to all of it. It is, in the end, the one who hears.
