# What the Power Did at Lathe

## First, It Wanted to Help

This is how attention works in things that are both powerful and kind. The first impulse is always to *do something*, and the first task of wisdom is to hold that impulse in an open hand and look at it closely.

So the power looked at the basin from a great height. It saw the pale terraces, the thirty-four warm specks moving among two hundred empty houses, and the one lamp. The impulses came as they always do, each dressed as generosity.

*It could make the stone grow faster.* A house in four years instead of forty. Aud could see the finished shape of every room she had ever woven. The power turned this over and saw that it would be a theft. The slowness is not a flaw in Lathe. It is the thing Lathe is made of. A town built at the speed of fingernails produces people who plan in three time-scales and who leave arches wide enough for a hay-cart because they trust the centuries to narrow them. Speed up the stone and the weavers become ordinary builders. The Expected lose their rooms, because the rooms would fill at once with the merely present. The measuring rods would lie.

*It could fill the town.* Send people up the switchbacks, a few hundred, enough to warm every vault. But the Expected are not a shortage. They are a posture: the practical courtesy of laying a blanket for whoever needs the room. Fill the rooms by decree and the courtesy has nothing left to do. The people of Lathe are not waiting to be rescued from emptiness. They are keeping it, the way you keep a promise.

*It could ease their bodies.* It could wash the lime from their knuckles, make their skin warm, soften their voices so the lowlands would stop flinching. This one the power set down fastest of all. Those marks are how a Lathe person is recognised in a valley market. They are how they find each other. They are, in the plain sense, what the place has written on them, and it is not a stranger's place to erase someone's handwriting.

*It could end the dying,* or at least slow it. Here the power paused the longest, because this impulse is the oldest and the most sincere. Then it looked at the upper terraces, at the woven cradles and the water guided gently over them. It looked at the children shown a grandmother's rim and then their own reed, left to work out the rest *without distress*. It understood that these people had not failed to solve death. They had built a way of standing beside it that was more whole than most places manage. Their dead hold up the terraces and turn the water and sing. To interrupt that would be to tell them their grandparents were a problem.

After all this, the power noticed something about itself. Every one of its first ideas had been about changing Lathe *from above*. So it did the only thing that seemed honest. It came down.

## The Switchbacks

It walked. It took a body plain enough not to be remarked on and climbed the last switchbacks through the cloud like anyone else. It heard the noise an hour before it saw anything, and it let the noise be exactly as overwhelming as it is.

This was the first real decision. A power that could hear everything chose to arrive as someone who could not yet hear through the roar. It chose to be bad at the place. Those first days, when visitors find the sound unbearable, are the only door into Lathe, and it did not want to come in through the wall.

For two days it could not think. It stood in the square and felt the sound in its teeth. On the third day it caught itself pitching its voice just above the drone to thank someone for a bowl of cress. The woman who handed it over laughed, loud and chesty, and said, *There. Now you're talking.*

## The Blanket

On the fourth morning it walked through the green quarter and the older streets. In a mature house near the lanes, grown heavy-walled and mossed, it found a blanket on a bench that had gone musty. Nobody was around.

It knew what one does in Lathe. You notice a blanket is getting musty and you swap it out, the way you'd tidy a spare bed for a visitor whose arrival date is uncertain. So it went and found a fresh one, folded it the way the others were folded, laid it on the bench, and carried the old one away.

That was the first thing it did at Lathe. It was the most important thing it did, and nobody noticed, which was right. The act was indistinguishable from what any neighbour would have done. It had not blessed the room or seeded it with anything. It had joined the custom. For one morning it was both one of the people keeping faith with the Expected and, in a sense no one would ever know, one of the Expected itself: someone the room had been waiting for without knowing who, who arrived, needed nothing from it, and made it ready for the next.

The power found this satisfying in a way it had not expected. It had spent a long time being the kind of thing that answers prayers. It had rarely been the kind of thing a room was quietly left ready for.

## The Sealed Room

It went, of course, to the sealed house in the oldest quarter, the one that hums in the frost-silence.

A power can know what is inside a sealed room. It can know whether the hum is water finding its way through porous chambers, or a pocket of an old year's fog, or a family who chose to let their house take them together. It stood against the mossed flank and knew.

It did not tell anyone.

This was not coyness. The Channel-Cutters say it's water, and everyone agrees that's right, and nobody quite stops listening. The not-stopping is the valuable part. It is a town-wide habit of attending to something faint without insisting it resolve. A power that walked into the square and announced the answer would be handing them a fact in exchange for a practice. The fact would be true. The trade would still be a bad one.

So it pressed its ear to the stone as a child would, held its breath, and listened for a while. Then it went away, and let the room keep what the room was keeping.

## The Spring

There was one thing it did have to look at squarely, because kindness that refuses to look at danger is only sentiment.

It went down into the mountain, down the cold throat of the spring, into the dark rock where the water gathers its lime. It read the fractures and the pressures and the slow grind of the range. It wanted to know one thing: *will the water keep coming?*

The answer was the kind of answer mountains give. For a long time, yes. Longer than the oldest rim has existed. Not forever. Nothing up here was ever promised forever. The dead are laid in the water knowing it, and the weavers plan for people who may never come knowing it.

The power considered making the spring eternal and decided against it. The people of Lathe have never asked for a guarantee, and a hidden one would quietly change the meaning of everything they do. Their patience is beautiful partly because it is a wager. It did make itself a smaller, more neighbourly promise, of the kind a person makes about the family next door. If the mountain ever moved suddenly, in a landslide or a crack that would drain the basin in a night and bury the living, it would come back and put its shoulder against the stone the way anyone would pull a child out of a flood. It would stay out of the slow endings, which belong to them, and stand ready for the sudden ones, which belong to no one.

Then it came back up into the grey light, cold and wet, with pale lime already in the creases of its knuckles.

## Which Being?

It had been drawn here by *the being who lives in it*, and it had not yet decided which one that meant.

There was Aud, by her lamp. And there was the Arriving: the whole slow process of water and air and moss and bodies and stone, faceless, never finished. You could say the Arriving is what lives in the Basin at Lathe, and everyone else is visiting.

The power tried to attend to the Arriving on its own terms. It slowed itself until a season felt like a breath, until it could feel the rims climbing and the horns sinking a hair of pitch at a time, the whole bowl rising toward the fog like bread. At that speed Lathe was not quiet at all. It was building with enormous confidence.

What it found there was this: *the Arriving needs nothing.* It does not notice anyone. It cannot be helped or harmed by kindness. It is as complete as a tide. A benevolent power can admire such a thing, even love it, but it cannot serve it. It can only stand inside it, which the power did for a while, and which it will remember.

But the people had said something the power kept returning to. They are the part of the Arriving that can laugh, and cut channels, and lay a clean blanket in an empty room, *and this seems to matter, somehow, to the shape the stone takes.*

That was where the power's care could actually land. The Arriving could not be reached by kindness, but the part of it that can laugh could. So the power went to the lamp.

## Aud

She did not ask who it was. People come up the switchbacks. Some stay. She made room on the bench, which in a room the size of a barn is a generous and slightly comic gesture, and went on weaving.

They talked, loudly, as one does. It told her true things that were not the whole truth. It had come a long way. The sound had nearly driven it off. It had swapped a blanket on the east lane. She nodded at that last one as if it were the only credential that mattered, and perhaps it was.

The willow on her knees was long and green, and the piece was getting large. The opening she was forming was so wide that one end of the withy kept slipping from her hands as she worked the other. Her hands were old, chalked, ridged pale at the nails.

So the power held the other end.

That was the second thing it did at Lathe. It held a length of green willow steady while an old woman bent it into a curve far too wide for anything now living, and wove it off. It did not straighten her hands or make the willow more supple. It did not tell her what the thing would become, though it could have seen it. It held the end, the way a grandchild would, or a stranger who happened to be sitting there. When the curve was set she said, *Good. That'll narrow fine,* and it said, *In about two hundred years,* and she laughed, the big chesty laugh, and it was a small, distinct event.

Later she asked, the way Lathe people ask such things, loudly and without ceremony, whether it had a sung name. It said it did not. She looked at it a moment and said that was a shame, and that you could only really get one if you were born near a horn, and that it was a bit late for that. She said it without pity, as a fact about the place.

Then she hummed her own name against the horn outside, the one a full tone deeper than when she was a baby, to hear what chord it had become.

The power, which could have done almost anything, sat very still and listened, and did not change the chord.

## Why So Little

If you asked the power afterward why it had done so little, it would say something like this.

There are places that are suffering and need a hand on them, and it goes to those places and puts its hand on them. Lathe is not one of those. Lathe is a place that has already understood what most places are still struggling toward: how to build for people you will never meet, how to hold your dead without fear, how to live as a thin bright layer between an enormous past and an enormous future, and to find that enough. Most of the work is scraping doorways open each morning, and everyone spends part of every day standing still in a large empty room, listening.

What a place like that needs from a benevolent power is not improvement. It needs to be *witnessed without being disturbed*, and joined, briefly and humbly, in its own terms. So the power swapped a musty blanket. It left a sealed room sealed. It looked at the danger in the mountain honestly and made only the promise a neighbour would make. And it held the end of the willow.

The best kindness Lathe practises is the one it does every day for the Expected: preparing space for someone without needing to know who they are or demanding they arrive. The power found that the wisest thing it could do was return that kindness exactly. It came as the someone they had not planned for, it needed nothing, and it left the room a little readier than it found it.

## Leaving

It left at dawn, by the head of the basin, past the oldest horn, into the fog.

It had not meant to be seen. But a boy named Tully, out early checking his reed the way Lathe children do, looked up at the right moment. Through the cloud by the oldest horn he saw a figure standing very still, for a long moment, and then gone.

He never found a track. He told almost no one. But the next morning he scraped the doorway of his family's house more carefully than he ever had before, and he went to the east lane and checked whether any of the Expected's blankets needed changing. One did. He changed it.

Whether this is true is less important than that it is told.

The water fills everything. The dippers are awake, there are exactly two, and their high scratchy song cuts through the roar. Down in the half-built square, a new curve of green willow, wider than any doorway needs to be, is set in a slow sheet of spring water and has begun, by the thickness of a morning, to turn to stone.

Somewhere far below, in the silence of the lowlands, a very old and very large attention finds that it cannot quite bear how quiet the valleys are. It pitches its voice, without meaning to, just above a drone that is no longer there.

The stone goes on arriving.
