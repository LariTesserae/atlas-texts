# The Visitor

## How It Came

It did not come down from the sky, and it did not come over the rim with the heron. Nothing in the basin marked its arrival. The Hammer struck at eleven, the chord held its forty tones, and the drops fell from the terrace lips at their even pace. Afterward, the Tenders would not have been able to say when it began to be there. They could only say that at some point, during a stroke, they had stopped to feel the dome drop, and something else had stopped with them.

It had learned the measure first. That was the first courtesy. It did not arrive loud and new into a place that had worn every loudness down to tolerance. It spent a long time outside the basin, listening through the rock, before it came in, the way you wait at a door until the conversation inside reaches a pause. When it finally stood on the basalt, its weight fell into the damp patches and the dry patches exactly where they already were. It breathed in elevens. It dropped nothing.

It was powerful enough to have done almost anything there. The first thing it did with that power was keep still.

## What It Could Have Done

It considered, because considering is what wisdom is for, and because it did not trust the first thing it wanted.

It could have made the basin green. It knew how. It could have cooled the rock enough in the right places, sweetened the brines, and seeded the runoff channels. Within a lifetime there would have been moss on the terraces, reeds in the gold cisterns, and something in the water large enough for the heron to eat. It thought about this for a long time, because it is an old instinct among good things to believe that life is always the gift. Then it thought about the crabs, which had been shaped by this exact warmth and this exact iron, and about the swifts, whose eggs needed the steady heat of the tubes, and about the Tenders. For them the green would not have been a gift. It would have been a flood. Everything they had become would have been buried under a new world's slack in a single generation, and they would have spent eternity filing at it, falling behind.

It could have stopped the Hammer. It could have broken the interval, loosed the chord, and let the dividing line in the split pools blur at last into one warm grey water. It could have told itself that this was freedom, that a world held in perfect balance forever was a kind of prison, and that it was merciful to let it fall. It noticed how much that thought flattered it, and it set the thought down. It had not been asked. It was a guest in a house whose customs it had only begun to learn. The guides said the place was terrible, but the guides left every morning. The people who stayed said it differently.

It could have freed the Tenders, or rather it could have done something that looked like freeing them. It could have unworn them, peeled back the sinter, softened the copper, and grown faces on the smooth basalt plates. It thought about the small one with the quick, short-stepping gait, and this was the hardest temptation, and it did not set it down so quickly. It held it and kept it close, and it decided to ask before it did anything at all.

Because it was wise, it understood that there was one thing it did not yet know, and that everything depended on it. It did not know whether they were happy.

## Being Tended

On the second night, or the second century (its sense of time was not theirs), the Tenders came to tidy it.

It had expected this. Measured against the Workings, it was slack in its entirety: a presence with no fitted purpose, standing on the dome where nothing stood except to listen. Before dawn, in the mist, three of them approached it the way they approached a cistern wall. They moved in the tenth second, stopped on the stroke, and moved again. One laid its heavy curved hand against it, feeling for the grain lodged in the pore.

It let them. It is a strange and humbling thing for a great power to be handled as a flaw by beings who have given themselves entirely to care, and it did not pull away from the humility. It felt what the hand was doing. The hand was asking a question, *what is this, does it belong, where does it fit*, and it tried, as honestly as it could, to answer in the only language the hand understood. It held still. It kept the measure. It let its surface be as smooth as it could make it.

The hand paused. It lay there through one stroke, then another. It could not find the edge where the thing ended and the slack began, and it could not find a place where the thing was wrong. After a hundred strokes the Tender took its hand away and did not grind anything. It stood beside the visitor, the way it stood on a patch of copper that was dulling, and waited to see whether the brightness would return.

The visitor understood that it had been admitted, not fitted. That was a finer distinction than it had expected them to make, and it began to suspect they were wiser than it had assumed.

## The Forty-First Note

Then, in the heat of the day, it followed them to the western ground and watched them stand at the swift-tubes, weight on one hip, heads tilted, waiting. It heard the waver: a half-tone drop, a swift going in. It heard the sinter at their edges ring together, very faintly, in the tone that was not in the chord.

It stood among them for a long time and listened to that note. It heard what was in it. There was love for the unfitted thing, clearly. There was also something the guides had never noticed, because the guides had never waited long enough to hear the note sound a thousand times and never once be answered.

The forty-first note was a call. For longer than the basin had kept records in anyone's memory, they had been sounding it into air that gave nothing back. The swift did not answer. It did not know it had been heard. The chord did not answer. It could only keep its forty tones. The Tenders themselves could not answer. They were the ones calling.

So it did the first thing it did in the Workings that was not stillness. It answered.

It did not answer on the stroke. That would have fitted the reply into the machine, and it would have been one more regular thing. It waited through the stroke, and through the long clear tenth second, and then, at a moment nothing in the basin had chosen, about four and a third seconds after the dome dropped, it sounded one note back. The note was low and warm. It was not in the chord, and it was not their note either. It was a forty-second.

Every Tender on the western ground turned.

They had faces of basalt plates, beaded with condensation, and they could not show surprise. But the drops on those plates, which had swelled and fallen at an even pace since before there were eyes to see them, stopped. For the space of a stroke nothing fell. Then the drops began again, and they were not quite even. One fell early. One hung a moment too long. It was a small slack, the first thing in them that had changed in an age.

The visitor had not caused that. It had only replied. What happened in them afterward was theirs.

## Asking

After that it could ask them things, because it had shown them it could hear.

It asked in the only way that made sense there, by attending. It went to each of them in turn, knelt beside them at their work, and felt what they felt: the breath of the Hammer past the rim vents, the grain in the cistern pore, the bore of a whistle tube a breath too wide. It paid attention to whether the work was a burden or a joy, whether they did it because they could not stop or because they did not want to.

Mostly it was joy. It had half expected that and half feared it would be otherwise, and it was relieved. The oldest ones had no wish except to go on tending. There was the seam in the south that walked a pace every few years, and there was the hand that had lain so long against its cistern that the pool took its curve from it. They had become what they did so completely that asking whether they wanted to be otherwise was like asking the river whether it wished to be a hill. The one that stood in the boundary of the split pool, thin as a waterline, was the boundary. When the visitor knelt beside it and asked, the answer came back as a shimmer that did not blur, and it meant *yes. this. always.* The visitor left it in peace. It is not wisdom to rescue what does not need rescue.

The younger ones were different, and not all the same.

The one with the joint in its shoulder that was the shape of a pack strap loved the work too. But when the visitor attended closely, it found under the love a small, worn place, like a pebble lodged in a pore. The Tender had filed at it for a very long time and never quite reached it. It was a memory of a direction: the eastern rim, and beyond it the sea, and someone who had been left there, or had been meant to be met there.

The one that stood at the whistle tubes with its head tilted, the way a person stands at a door listening for someone inside, was listening for someone. It was not waiting for a swift.

And then there was the small one.

## The Small One

The visitor went to it last, and slowly, because this was the one it most wanted to change and therefore the one it least trusted itself with.

The small one was at the swift-tubes. It was always at the swift-tubes. It did not tend them, because the Tenders did not tend the swift-tubes, but it stood among them more than the others did, and it did not stand the way the others stood. It did not wait with its weight on one hip, patient and still. It moved from tube to tube with that quick, short-stepping gait that matched nothing in the Workings, and it was a fraction early or late for every stroke, never quite in the measure. It had been fitted less than the rest, or it was resisting the fitting, or its fitting simply went a different way.

The visitor knelt, making itself small, and attended.

What it found was not unhappiness. It had been braced for unhappiness and would have known what to do with it. What it found was a question. The small one did not want to stop tending. It did not want its old face back, or whatever it had had. It did not even want to leave. It wanted to know where the swifts went.

It had stood at their tubes for a very long time. It had heard them come in and go out, and every time a swift left, it rose over the western ground, cleared the rim, and was gone into a world the small one could not picture anymore. The small one knew the bore of every tube and the exact half-tone each one dropped when it was entered. It did not know what the swifts saw. That was the grain it could not file.

## The Door

This is the one thing the visitor changed in the Workings, and it was careful that it should be the only one.

The basin fits things. That was its nature, and the visitor did not alter its nature. But until then, fitting in the Workings had gone only one way, as erosion does. Once a channel was cut to the shape of its water, it could not become uncut. Once a person's hands had come to rest in the shape of a tube-bore, they stayed that way. Nobody was taken, and the guides were right about that. But nobody who had become a Tender had ever become anything else. It had not been a prison. It had been a door with a latch on only one side.

The visitor put a latch on the other side.

It did this very quietly and very deep down. It did not do it in the stone, or the brine, or the current, but in the fitting itself, in whatever it is about a place that makes the things in it more like the place. It made it so that the fitting could be undone, a little at a time, if and only if the fitted thing wished it. It made the unfitting slow. It would take as long as the fitting had taken, so that no one could fall out of the Workings the way they had gradually fallen in. And it made it entirely theirs. It would not happen by accident, or by someone else's choice, or because a great kind power had decided it would be better. It would happen only if one of them, in the tenth second, turned toward the rim instead of toward the cisterns, and kept turning.

It did not tell them in words. It sounded its forty-second note once more, at an unfitted moment, from the eastern rim where the land dropped toward the sea. It let them understand that the rim was reachable. It was only a place, and they could go there and come back.

Then it did the hardest thing, which was nothing at all. It waited to see what they would choose, and it did not lean on the choice.

## What They Chose

The oldest ones went on tending, and the Hammer kept eleven, and it keeps it still.

The one with the pack-strap shoulder turned toward the east. It took a very long time. Guides who crossed the basin over the next few generations reported a tall, narrow shape on the eastern slope. It moved only between strokes, and it was a little higher on the rim each decade, and the sinter at its shoulder was a little thinner. No one knows what it found at the sea, or whom. But the guides say that once, many years later, a woman came into the basin from the east with a pack on her shoulder and walked straight to the Hammer. She stood on the warm dome, breathing in elevens, and wept, and left a single white pebble on the crown. In the morning the pebble was still there. It is still there. The Tenders tidy around it.

The one at the whistle tubes, listening at a door, did not leave. It had been listening for someone inside, and in the end it understood that the someone was the swifts, or the chord, or the whole working basin. It had arrived long ago, and what it had been waiting for had been there all along. It stayed. Its weight is still on one hip. But now, when a swift goes in, it rings a fraction sooner than the others, as if it had heard the waver coming.

And the small one followed a swift.

Not all at once. It went one tube, one terrace, and one stroke further west each season. It went as far as the edge of the whistling ground, where the vents give out, and then up the slope where the stone thins and the first dry grass grows on the far side of the rim. Some seasons it came back and stood among the tubes as if it had never left, quick-stepping and out of measure. Some seasons it did not. The guides no longer say they do not like to speculate about it. Now they say, with an odd pride, that there is one Tender who knows where the swifts go.

## What the Visitor Kept

It could have stayed. The Tenders would have let it. They had admitted it, and they did not revoke what they admitted.

But the visitor had spent a long time learning that the only unfitted thing in the Workings, the one thing the Tenders loved and would not file, came and went on no schedule. So it chose to be like the swifts.

It left, and it comes back. It never returns on the stroke, or on any interval anyone has been able to find. Sometimes it is a season. Sometimes it is longer than the eldest guide's memory. It comes over no particular part of the rim and stands on no particular patch of stone. It stays a while and then goes.

And the Tenders, who hold a whole world in perfect balance with worn and perfectly shaped hands, have one more thing now that they do not fit. In the heat of the day, when the cisterns are clean and the tubes trued and the seams bright and the Hammer breathing exactly eleven, they still go to the western ground and wait for the half-tone waver of a swift. Sometimes, not often and never on the stroke, the forty-first note they sound into the air is answered from somewhere beyond the rim by a forty-second.

People who spend a night in the Workings still say the worst of it is waking before dawn and understanding that everything is exactly as it was.

People who stay longer say that is almost true. Everything is exactly as it was, except that every so often, something calls back.
