# The Audit

## Arrival

It did not come up the switchback. It came the way anything reaches anywhere on the Table: by sound.

It had heard the place from very far off, the way you hear a heartbeat through a wall: four blows and a pause, then four more, faint and patient and older than most of the things it had been asked to look after. It had been drawn by that sound and by one other thing, which was harder to name. Somewhere in the world a set of accounts did not balance, and it had a long habit of going to such places.

When it arrived it made the mistake everyone makes. It walked toward a chimney by sight and circled for most of an afternoon. When it understood, it did something like laughing. Then it closed its eyes and let the east forge pull it in.

For the visit it wore the shape of a person. The Table is kind to that shape, and it did not want to frighten anything. By the second day a fine green down had begun on its cuffs. It let the down grow.

## The First Reading

Benevolence has a first instinct, and the instinct is rescue.

This is what it saw on first reading. There were pale blind things, born in cold black water and drained through a knot into the dark. They stooped for years in corridors the width of a man's shoulders and fed fireboxes with their own sap. Their labour powered a whole city, and no ledger recorded it. When they finally climbed into daylight, the crows ate some of them. The rest hardened into the costume of workers and stood their posts a little longer, then split and left their shells to trudge on toward a shift that had already ended.

Every story it knew had a word for that, and the word was not *life*.

It had been old for a long time, and it had learned the hard way that the first reading is a translation. On the first reading you see your own world's wounds printed over someone else's skin. It had freed things before that did not need freeing. It remembered what those things had looked like afterward.

So it did not act. It began an audit.

## Following the Current

It did not cut a beam. It waited until an engine failed in one of the kiln halls and the split drop-hammer came down in pieces. Then it knelt in the moss and read the rings of the broken heartwood with one finger, the way you would read a letter that had been left open for you.

It found the street plans and the pale flecks in the doorways, and it understood what they were. The Hands had never needed eyes or mouths to keep a record. They kept it in the wood. Every shift was entered and every rising was marked. The ledger that did not balance was the turbine ledger, the one that recorded ferns. There had always been a second book.

Then it followed the current.

It lay with its ear to the root-mat for nine nights, until it could pick out the buried engines one by one, like voices in a choir. It traced the heat up through the old mineral seams and into the granite knuckles. From there the current passed into the roots of the transmission poles, up the glossy vines, and along the lines to the white seed-casing junctions. At dusk the current jumped those junctions in small blue arcs.

At the arcs, the moths were feeding.

It watched them for a long time. The grey wings were patterned like hammered metal, and they were the same wings that had been folded like aprons inside the husks. The moths drank the light at the joins. What they drank was the labour of the Undershift. It was their own labour, stored up over all those years in the dark and spent now on one season of flight.

Nothing had been taken from them. The work was not stolen. It was saved.

Later in the dusk it watched a moth drop low over a quench-tank and touch the black water, once, twice, four times, and then pause. Where it touched, fine pale bars hung in the water like the graduations on a rule.

So the ledger balanced. It balanced in a book the city's builders had never thought to open. The industry of the High Table was a life cycle wearing the clothes of a factory, because the clothes had been there and the Table wastes nothing. Even the product was not sold. The seeds went over the rim to whatever lay below, crate after crate, for longer than anyone could reckon. The city's only export was a gift.

After that it sat for a while, the way an auditor sits when a set of books turns out to be sound. It felt relief, and it felt a small embarrassment at having suspected otherwise.

Then it opened a smaller page and began the list of exceptions. Even a sound set of books usually has one.

## Exception: The Silent Hall

The kiln hall whose hammer had split was quiet now. Moss was already flooding into the quiet, as it does.

Beside the hall stood its tank, and in the tank were ribbons that had pulsed to that hammer all their lives and no other. Now there was nothing to pulse to. They lay coiled in the dark water, slowly losing the count. Some of them twitched at the far-off beat of other halls, stumbled, and stopped, because it was not their measure. A ribbon learns one hall, and that hall was dead.

It could have regrown the hammer in an hour. It considered that, and decided against it. The regrowing belonged to the hall, the way healing belongs to the body. A hammer made by an outside hand might keep perfect time and still not be the hall's own.

So it did something smaller. It sat on the rim of the tank and kept time.

It used one knuckle against the burl, the way the hammer had fallen: four and a pause, four and a pause. It kept the tempo of that hall exactly, because it had read that tempo in the broken wood. It kept it through the fog-lifts and the drizzle and the arcs at dusk. It kept it through the cold nights while the root-mat creaked. It kept it through three whistles, when the shadows swung round and covered it too.

The ribbons found the beat again. The one that had stumbled worst was the first to come back.

Eleven weeks later, a new hammer had grown from the stump. One morning it fell for the first time. Its blow was a little heavy and a little late, the way new hammers are. The power did not stop. For a day it slowed its own beat by a hair and then by another hair, walking the ribbons gently from its time toward the hammer's time. When the two finally fell together, it took its hand off the tank.

Nobody on the Table noticed. It had meant for nobody to.

## Exception: The Lifted Ribbon

It thought about travellers, who come up out of the cloud and lift things out of water to see what they are.

It could have made the tanks unreachable, or made travellers incurious. Both would have cost too much. Curiosity is one of the things it had come into the world to protect, even when curiosity is clumsy.

Then it found that someone had already written the warning down: *which you should not.* It judged that a warning was exactly the right size of intervention for curiosity. It left the warning as it was.

## Exception: The Crows

It walked the rails with the crows for a day. It watched a pair of them take a Hand that had surfaced in open sun, in the wrong place, blind and slick and frozen at the sound of a tram that was not coming.

That was hard to watch, and it watched anyway.

Then it thought about what the crows were. They were the reason the Hands came up where the shadows fell, the pressure that had taught the clocktower its work and the city to turn its shade over its births. They were also hungry, and they had lived there a long time.

It did not change the crows. It did not change anything about the crows. It noted, in the smaller book, that it had wanted to, and moved on.

## Exception: The Relapse

This was the one that nearly became an act.

It went down into the Undershift during a strong relapse. It had felt one coming the way you feel weather. It stood in a buried corridor among the Hands as the walls around them remembered brick.

For two seconds the corridor was cold iron and fired clay. Nothing in it bent to the shape of what lived there. The Hands clenched on the rungs, their sap stiffening, their long pale bodies seizing against surfaces that had suddenly forgotten them. Above, the moss was dying in a clean ring.

It could have sealed the seam. It had the reach to do it. It could have made the conversion final, so that the iron city could never again come through the green one.

It did not, because in those two seconds it looked up.

The corridor had a ceiling, the ceiling had a grate, and through the grate, in the smoke-dark sky of the relapse, were the men on the gantries. They were real, iron-age men in real overalls, with real pencils behind their ears. They were looking down.

It understood then that the relapse was not an attack. It was the previous shift looking in, the way a worker who has gone home still wakes at the hour of the whistle and checks, in his sleep, that the line is running. That was also where the shapes came from: the overalls, the caps, the aprons, the fine bark pencil. If it sealed the seam, the Hands would climb into daylight and not know what to become.

So it did not seal anything. In the second relapse it went looking for, it met the eyes of a man on the north gantry. In the space of a breath, before the iron went back to wood, it told him the one thing it thought he needed to hear.

*They kept it running. The handover was good. You can rest.*

The relapses did not stop after that. The Table would not be the Table without them. But they changed. They became gentler, more like someone looking in at a doorway than someone coming through it. The iron still came, briefly, cold and gritty under the palm. But the moss around the halls afterward only yellowed a little and did not die.

## The Pencil

There was one Hand it spent longer with than the others.

It was the one on the north gantry of the turbine house, with the pencil behind its ear grown in bark as fine as a whisker. It had hardened in the same place where the man in the relapse had stood.

The power could see what that pencil was reaching toward. It was the one Hand that seemed to want, in whatever way a blind mouthless thing wants, to *keep the count* out loud. The power could have finished what the reaching had started. It could have given the Hand eyes, or a voice, or a page, and let the Hands be known and written into the turbine ledger with the ferns.

That was the most tempting gift on the whole list, so it thought about it the longest.

In the end it decided that the Hands had been safe for an age partly because nobody counted them.
