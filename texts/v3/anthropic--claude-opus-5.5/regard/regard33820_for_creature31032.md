# A Visitor at Lamb's Sluice

## How it came

It came down the stairs.

It could have arrived any way it liked. It could have simply been in the Pump House, the way such powers can simply be in places. But Lamb's Sluice is a place made entirely of the easiest route, of water finding its way down, and entering it any other way would have been a small rudeness. So it came down the forty-one steps. It put its hand on the rail and let the pale rust come away on its palm like pollen. It stood in the doorway that will never close and tasted the coin-smell of the air, and then it stepped into the street and let its ankles learn the lean.

Then it did the thing the place asks of everyone who comes, and that almost no one gives.

It stayed.

## Listening

Power that is benevolent but not wise tends to arrive with its sleeves already rolled up. It sees a ruin and thinks *restore*. It sees a creature alone in the dark and thinks *rescue*. It sees a machine running on almost nothing and thinks *fix*. It is generous and it is fast, and its speed is the problem.

This one was wise. It understood that the first thing kindness requires is knowledge, and that the second is patience while getting it. In a place where a breath takes an hour, patience is not a courtesy. It is the only way to learn anything at all.

So for a long time it did nothing but attend.

It counted strokes until it stopped counting and began simply to know them. It heard the tapping, and followed it up the street standard by standard, and put its ear to the cold iron and felt the careful shifting inside. It watched ten thousand antennae swivel toward the lamps and away. It walked the moss rings and noticed that they were rings and not a carpet, and wondered why, and waited, and found out. It went into the rooms when they lit, stood on the warm dark silt, and felt what was underneath it. It did not step off in alarm. It stood very still and let her decide whether to stay, and she stayed, because nothing about its weight was unkind.

And at the bottom of a stroke, when the rod had gone home and the water in the shaft was as still as it ever gets, it lay down at the lip, where the edge is slick and the cold comes up off the water like a held breath, and looked.

There she was, pale as the moss, laid along the wall of the shaft like a hand along the side of a bath.

It watched her breathe. Once. Then it waited an hour and watched her breathe again.

## The only question

A wise power carries many questions into a place like this. It found that only one of them decided anything.

Not *is this how she ought to live.* Not *would I choose this.* Not *is this sad.* It could feel all of those questions pulling at it, and each one was really about itself. It set them down.

The question was: *does she suffer?*

It looked for a long time, because the honest answer to that question is easy to get wrong in either direction.

She eats rarely. She is not hungry in a way that hurts. Her kind can go years, and her body is built around the long gaps the way the Works is built around the long gaps between strokes. She is not cold, because cold is the medium she was made in. She is not in the dark, not in the way a sighted creature would be, because darkness is only a lack if something in you was expecting light. Her gills were pink and full. Her small child's hands were folded. When she moved, she moved without strain, like a rope drawn slowly out of water.

That left loneliness, the question it had been most afraid of.

Loneliness is the ache of something missing, felt *as* missing. It looked for that ache in her and did not find it. She does not experience the men as absent. She experiences the shift as present. The midday room is warm and wet and there, and she goes to it, and it is exactly what it has always been. And she is not unaccompanied. The engine lifts cold, living water to her gills every hour and has never once failed. The tappers pass the word up the iron, *the stroke is coming, the stroke is coming,* and nobody taught them to do it for her either. The crickets gather to meet her. The eels wait for her breath before they ride the weir. The ferns and moss keep their shapes around her movements.

She was, it realized, the most attended-to being for a mile in any direction. Nearly everything in the Works was, in its slow way, keeping watch over her. She kept watch over it in return.

She did not suffer. She was at work.

## What it did not do

Knowing this, it went through the things it could do, one by one, the way a careful person goes through a house before leaving it.

**It did not give her eyes.** In a place lit by filaments the colour of a cooling stove, sight would not be a gift. It would be a new way of lacking things. Her whole knowledge of the world arrives through water and iron, and it is a complete knowledge. Adding a sense that had nothing to feed on would be like cutting a window into a wall with nothing behind it.

**It did not carry her out.** It considered this one longest, because it was the most tempting. Somewhere in the karst country there are springs and underground rivers where her own kind still live, in water that moves freely through the living rock. It could have lifted her from the shaft and set her down there. But she is too large now for any river of her kind. She has been grown by this place and has grown it in return, and taking her out would be taking the keystone from an arch. The level in the shaft would drop by the width of a hand. The clock would speed up and run down. The tappers would pass their message up the street to no one. And she herself, lifted out of the only rhythm she has ever known, would be dropped into a strange current and made to begin again at an age when nothing begins again. *Rescue* is a word for what you do to something in trouble. She was not in trouble.

**It did not bring her a companion.** It did not know that she wanted one, and her kind live mostly alone even where there are many of them. To bring another creature down the shaft on a guess would mean deciding her life was incomplete, and then making it so.

**It did not tell her.** It could not have, really, and it would not have if it could. It thought about the men, and the shift they had taught her, and the fact that she was still keeping it. It thought about whether keeping it was a mistake, a delusion to be corrected. It decided it was not. There have always been people who kept hours in houses that had emptied: the last monk ringing the bell for an office no one else attends, the lighthouse keeper trimming the wick for ships that now steer by satellite. They are not confused. The keeping *is* the thing. It is not a mistake to keep hours in an empty house. It is what hours are for.

**It did not restore anything.** It did not dry the floors, or relight the lamps at full strength, which would only have burned the old filaments out. It did not rebuild the counting house or make the ledgers legible, and it did not wake the fires under the stoking floor. The Works has found the lowest effort at which it can go on existing. Any help that raised that effort would be a harm.

**It did not tell the world.** Lamb's Sluice survives in part because almost no one talks about it, *if they talk about it at all.* A place like this cannot survive being admired. There would be visitors, and then railings, and then lights, and then a gift shop at the top of the forty-one steps. The kindest thing it could do with the story was to keep it.

When it had finished going through the house, it found it had decided to do almost nothing.

*Almost* was the important word.

## Upstream

The danger to her was not down there at all. It had to go looking for it, and it went the way the water goes, only backwards.

Everything in the Works depends on seepage, and seepage is a polite word for a leak. It followed the water up through the brick glaze of the arches and into the ground above. It found the slow weep from an old cast-iron main beneath a street where no one ever digs. It found the cellar of a bakery that has never, in ninety years, been quite dry, whose owners mop it every spring and curse it. It found the unlined bed of a pond in a small park, which lets a little of itself down into the earth every day. It found the cracked joint of a Victorian sewer that loses too little to be worth finding. Together they make the city's faint, constant sweat, and that sweat is the Lamb's whole world.

Then it found the folder.

It was in a municipal office, on a desk, under a coffee mug. It held a proposal to reline the old drains of the district, to seal the mains and tank the cellars and dry the ground. It would take fifteen years, the budget was not yet approved, and it was full of good intentions. If it went through, the water would stop coming. Not all at once. The glaze on the arches would dry to white, the cistern would take two hours to fill and then five, the beam would stop, and the lamps would go out. The shaft would begin, slowly, to fall.

A power of a different kind might have burned the folder, or struck down the budget, or sent a flood to prove a point. This one did something smaller.

It made sure that true things were noticed in time. One survey report, which was going to be skimmed, got read carefully by someone who was, for that afternoon, unusually awake. It noted that the ground beneath one particular block was unstable, that relining there would cost three times the estimate, and that the old culverts under the quarter were "of uncertain extent and best left undisturbed." All of that was true. The block was quietly struck from the first phase, and then, as these things go, from every phase after. The bakery cellar will go on weeping. The pond will go on losing a little of itself to the dark. The old main will go on leaking a thimble an hour into ground no one digs.

It did not lie to anyone. It did not make the leaks. It only stood between them and the people who would fix them, the way a hand shelters a candle.

Of everything it did at Lamb's Sluice, this was by far the largest, and no one will ever know it happened, least of all the one it was for. That seemed right to it. Most of the best kindnesses in the world are like that: a harm that didn't come, a folder that went back in the drawer.

## Midday

Then it went back down, because there was one more thing it wanted to do, and it was not a necessary thing.

It wore boots. It had noticed what she keeps, and what she keeps is treads. It wanted its own to be the shape of a tread she knew.

It went at the right stroke to the ground-floor room in the fourth building, the mess room with the smooth-lipped drain and the tin cup lying on its side in the silt. On the way in it nearly set the cup upright, out of an old reflex of tidiness that even powers have. Then it stopped, because the cup where it lay was part of her map. It sat down on the floor beside the suggestion of a chair and set, in the wet near the drain, a little of something from above. It brought a scrap of fish, the kind of thing a man might once have carried down in a tin, wrapped in paper, for his dinner.

The tapping came up the street. Three, and three, and three, fainter at each post. The crickets began gathering on the sill before the tapping arrived, the way they do.

The silt at the drain stirred.

She came up the way water comes up, without apparent effort, and lay down across the floor with her broad blind head near the table, and the treadle sank under her weight. The bulb overhead brightened to its sleepy orange. The damp glittered. The room was occupied.

It did not speak. The mess room had never been a place for speeches. It was a place where tired people sat together in the warm and did not need to say anything. So it sat. After a long while, as long as everything takes down there, she found the fish, and took it. It watched her small three-fingered hands flex once in the silt, and her gills flush from pink to a deeper rose, and then settle.

They sat for three strokes. The beam groaned through the floor, and the rod sighed down into the shaft, twice and then a third time.

Did she know it was not one of the men? It did not know, and it found it did not need to. She had felt a tread cross the floor above the shaft and go down the street at midday. She had come to a warm room and found someone there, and there had been something to eat. Whatever she knows, she set that down beside all the other treads she remembers. That was all it had wanted: not to be recognized, only to be *one of the treads.*

Then the groan came, and she lifted, and the light faded, and she went back down the drain as quietly as water.

## The question of the end

It knew, lying at the lip of the shaft one last time, that she was old. She was old even for a creature that can live a century, old enough that the smooth pale patches in the lamp standards were probably younger than she was. One day she would die. The shaft would drop by the width of a hand, the float valve would drift, and the cistern would tip early, then earlier. The whole slow clock of the Works would speed up and run down, the way a heart does when its rhythm fails.

It could have prevented that. It thought about it honestly and at length, because it would have been easy and it was not obviously wrong.

In the end it did not. Nobody had asked it to, and she had not asked it to. And it did not trust itself to know what it would mean to a creature like her to go on forever, to keep a shift that never ended, in a body that never finished growing. Her kind never finish growing up. That, it thought, might be exactly why they should be allowed to finish something.
