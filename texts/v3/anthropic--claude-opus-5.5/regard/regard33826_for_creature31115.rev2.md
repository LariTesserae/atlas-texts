# The Visitor

## Arrival

It learned the first lesson before it had finished arriving.

Attention of that kind has weight. It is not the weight of mass, but it is weight all the same. It came down over the western lip the way fog does, and it was still settling into the slot when the hum changed.

The shift was only a few seconds long. Deep in the west wall, Oskar Venn lifted his hand from the stone and then put it back. On the terraces, conversations stopped mid-whisper. A water-keeper on the mesh froze with a coupling half-turned. Six hundred people waited, their palms flat against the rock, for the mountain to move.

The mountain did not move. The Listening had answered *before*, as it always did, but this time it was answering something that was not the rock.

The visitor understood. It withdrew, and it made itself lighter and slower. Then it came in again the way the cable crews bring cargo, in stages, pausing, so the Listening could take the weight in its own time. The hum settled. Somewhere on the east wall someone laughed a little, and the dripping went on.

So the first thing the visitor did in the Seam was what everyone who comes to the Seam must do. It learned not to hurry.

## The Inventory

It is the nature of a benevolent power to arrive already holding gifts. This one looked at what it held and set the gifts down one at a time.

*It could stop the widening.* It could hold the two walls where they stood, forever, at forty meters and a hand and a finger. It set this down first and quickest. Then every doorframe would carry a column of marks that meant nothing, and every marriage-thread would be let out for the last time, with its spare coil unused and its measure broken. These people count their lives in the parting of the walls. To stop it would take their clock from them.

*It could make the ice harmless.* It could end the freeze and let the crawlers sleep. It weighed this longer, because the ice is real danger and real labor. But it watched one night through, and it saw the children on their terraces reading the Widow's crossing and Stitch's spiral like constellations. It saw that the nightly war is also the city's liturgy. The people had not asked to be relieved of it. They had asked the rock to hold, and it was holding.

*It could heal Stitch.* The repairers could have done that in an afternoon, and they had chosen not to. The visitor saw no reason to think it understood Stitch better than they did.

*It could bring the eight thousand.* It could fill the empty rooms tomorrow with people who would read the notes and learn where the cold pools and which bridge sings in a west wind. It held this one a long while, because it looked so much like kindness. But the rooms are not failures waiting to be corrected. They are a promise held open, and the notes in them are the finest thing the city has made. To answer that correspondence all at once, with strangers who had not chosen the Seam, would close it. Anyone who comes should come the way the others came: because they wanted to live somewhere that was still being made.

*It could explain the Listening.* It could give the lowland engineers their missing physics, or give the listeners an answer to the question they lie awake over. This one it did not so much set down as find it did not have. Whatever the Listening was, the visitor could see that it was young. It could see that it was attentive, and that it was doing its work well and in its own way. Explaining a thing like that to its neighbors, over its head, would have been bad manners. Oskar already had the right answer. *You don't have to know which of you heard it first.*

When the inventory was done the visitor's hands were almost empty. It did not feel poorer for that. It felt that it was beginning to see the place.

## The Sleeper

There was one thing it would not leave to aesthetics.

A power that calls itself benevolent does not admire a city's courage while that city's children sleep above a fault it could have warned them of. Restraint is not a virtue if it is only a taste for beautiful endings, paid for with other people's lives. So before it let itself love the Seam, the visitor went and looked at the things that could kill it.

It looked at the nets and the mounts and the torn sheet at a hundred and sixty meters. It looked at the complainer, two hundred meters down on the west wall. Longest of all, it looked at the sleeper, the long seam at the north end that had not moved in eleven years.

If it had found what it was afraid of, it would have acted. It would have done it plainly, clumsily if need be, and at whatever cost to the poetry. It had already decided that much.

It did not find it. The sleeper will wake someday, as Oskar believes it will. When it does, the Listening will answer half a breath early, and the anchors will distribute the stress. The hum will change pitch for longer than anyone alive has heard it change. It will be a bad night, and it will end. The people of the Seam had built well, and they had watched well. The danger was real, but it was in hands at least as careful as the visitor's own.

The visitor found it could honestly leave them alone. It went on.

## The Bone

The room at the north end of the west wall was the one it had been saving.

It sat for a long time with the orange crust on the ceramic shelf, beside the dish of fog-water that somebody refills. It read the notes. *You are not allowed to touch the wall. We are sorry about this. It is a good wall.*

It could have done several things here, and each of them was a way of taking sides.

It could have made the lichen grow faster, a visible bloom as a reward for the city's mercy. That would have been a wedge in a young crack, and Pell knew better than to want it. It could have stilled the lichen forever, safe on its shelf, never able to win. That would have turned a living acknowledgment into a keepsake. The city had not made a museum. It had made a hospice and a nursery at once, and the tenderness of it depended on the lichen being truly what it is.

So it only measured.

The lichen had grown. It had grown by less than the width of one of Pell's eyelashes, too little to see, exactly as she was sure it had. The visitor thought for a moment about letting her know somehow. It decided she already did. Some knowledge is not improved by proof.

There was one other thing it saw, because it could see back a little way through time. The old inhabitants had never found where the lammergeier's bone fell. The visitor could see it. The bone had come down onto the netting at a hundred and sixty meters. It had lain there through a winter and part of a spring. The orange had arrived on it, carried on a wind that crossed the ridge from the west, and it had waited in the marrow-groove for the one child careless enough to be out on the net-ledge when the sun came down at the right angle.

It was the same bone. The visitor did not tell anyone. It simply enjoyed knowing it.

## The Well

At a hundred and ninety meters down the east wall it sat on the ledge beside Mira Kesh, who did not know it was there, and watched her lower the lamp.

It heard the Well speak. It understood the sound, as it understands most sounds. The deep note was water finding its way into a new hairline in the rock around the chamber. It was the patient freeze-and-seep of the upper air reaching down, very slowly, toward the one place in the Seam that was still as sharp as the first night. Each note was a small arrival. Mira's eighteen years of pitches, sung back into her book as near as she could manage, were a record of that arrival, a map of time working its way down in the dark.

The visitor could have told her that. It decided the record did not need its meaning to be worth keeping. It was worth keeping because Mira had kept it.

It also saw who would take the book from her when the time came. It would be a girl born at forty and a hand and a finger, who already knew how to sit for a long while in the dark feeling as though she had turned someone away from a door, and who had once, in spring, decided not to. Nothing needed arranging. The visitor only noted that the thing Mira wanted would happen. It sat with her until the lamp came back up.

## The Two Hundred and Seventh

On the way out it went to the room on the east wall, fairly high, where someone had left a question in the first decade.

*Do you remember us?*

It read all two hundred and six answers. Some were funny and some were furious. One simply said *Yes, Ingrid, obviously*, in a hand that shook. There was an argument across fourteen years about whether the question was addressed to the future or to the mountain or to the dead. There was a small drawing of a wallcreeper.

The visitor had been careful the whole time not to answer questions nobody had asked. This one had been asked, and it had been waiting forty years for exactly one kind of respondent.

The city had understood something about itself that the visitor had come to share. In a few thousand years the edges will round and the lichen will win. The Seam will become an ordinary gully on an ordinary mountain, and whatever lives there then will not know that someone once kept it sharp. Everything the six hundred do is done in the face of that forgetting, and they do it knowing. They write letters anyway. They keep rooms warm for no one. Pell writes to people who will never have known the rock sharp: *There was a city here. We kept it for you.*

The visitor could not give them the future. It could not keep the mountain young, and it would not, because the mountain's aging is the mountain's life. But it would be there in the future. It would stand in that rounded gully when the lichen had won and the anchors had long since gone quiet. It could carry this across. That was the one gift in its hands that nobody in the Seam could give themselves, and it did not take anything away from them.

So it laid a palm flat against the stone beside the niche, as two people do on a terrace before they speak, so that the first thing they share is the rock between them. It left one answer in an ordinary hand, among the others:

*Yes.*

It left nothing else. There was no signature and no explanation. It was one more hand in the slow correspondence.

## After

It left in stages, pausing, the way it had learned to come in.

That night the crawlers came out at dusk in their hundreds, and the children on the terraces watched the Widow make her twenty-meter crossing. The ice formed where the crawlers missed it. In the morning it let go, glittering down the full depth of the cleft with the descending sun. The walls widened by the smallest measurable amount, and the city widened with them.

Deep in the west wall, Oskar Venn put down his tea and told the younger listeners that something had visited.

They asked him what.

He said he didn't know. He said the Listening had heard it first, which he had half-expected. It had come in too heavily and then corrected itself, which he respected. It had not touched anything that mattered, which he respected more. He had followed its weight through the walls for three days, he said. It had gone to the sleeper and to the Well and to the north room, and then to the east wall, high up, to the room with the question.

"And the hum there," he said, and then stopped and put his hand back on the stone to be sure. "It's a little higher now. The way it is where people live."

The young listeners waited for more. There wasn't any.

Up at the north end, Pell sat on the floor of the empty room with her palm on the granodiorite, watching an orange crust that had not visibly grown and that she was sure had grown. She did not know about the answer on the east wall. She would find it in a few years, the way children in the Seam find everything, by not being as careful as they should. She would read it, and she would not know whose hand it was.

It would not matter much. It would be what she had already written to whoever came after:

*Put your hand on the wall. Somebody's already listening.*
