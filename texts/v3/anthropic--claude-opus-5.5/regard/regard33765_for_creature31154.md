# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not arrive, exactly. Nothing in the Loud Country arrives quickly except the water, and the water only leaves.

The power came the way the light comes onto the plateau: from everywhere at once, unfiltered, and settling on every surface. No stake moved and no gauge flickered. The switch-tenders at the rim felt nothing through their boots. The tank engine ran on schedule, its smoke visible for an hour before it came in. The only creatures that noticed were the ravens at the ice. They lifted from the rock with no crack from the ice cap to lift them, circled once, and came down again, facing south, toward nothing anyone could see.

It had been drawn by the sound, as the works had. It had not come for what the sound could turn into. It came for what the sound was: a sponge the size of a province being wrung out over the edge of the world, continuously, for three thousand years. Then, once it was listening, it was drawn by something much smaller than the Falls. Forty kilometers north, a woman stood on the naked stone and lifted her hands to a bird that could not hear her.

The power had attended to many places. It knew how the thing usually went. You come to a place, you see what hurts, you mend it. It had done this often, and sometimes it had been right.

It stayed a season before deciding anything. This is the first thing to understand about it.

## What It Could Have Done

It could have given them silence.

That was the first thing it thought of, because it was the most obvious lack. There was no silence anywhere on the plateau, and no one who lived there had heard any. The power could have folded the roar away for an hour, a morning, a single held breath. The pipit's song would finally reach the woman at the ice. The mothers would sing to their babies with their mouths.

Then it watched the woman lay her palm flat on her breastbone, still, and understood what the sign meant. *Silence.* *The turbine has stopped.* *Dead.* To give these people an hour without the roar would not be a gift. It would be the gravest alarm on the plateau, sounding in every chest at once. Every hand in every hall would run. Every mother would clutch her child and feel for the warmth. Three hundred people would stand in the sudden quiet and hear their own blood for the first time and believe, for one long moment, that the world had died. Silence was the valley's gift, and the valley already had it. The plateau-born had gone down and tasted it and come back gray-faced on the next train.

It set that one down.

It could have hurried the ice. The ice was already withdrawing. The power could have pulled it back a hundred years in a night, bared a wide new country of gleaming grooved stone, and made the plateau larger, wetter, and stronger in its turbines, all at once. It considered this for less time than the others. The whole meaning of the place was that it ran on the scale of centuries. The line of stakes was a record of that pace. Each year a woman stood where the ice had been and marked it with her own hands, and the moss walked over the mark behind her. Compress that and there would be no line, only a gap. The keeper would walk out one morning with her iron stake and her brass tag and find that her life's measure had been taken out of her hands. Worse, she would find it had been done *for* her.

It could have given them rivers. It could have carved the valleys the land would have in ten thousand years and shown them the canyons now. It did not want to. The rivers had not been decided yet. That was not a flaw in the place. It was the place. A young country is allowed to be unfinished. Most of the beauty of the plateau lay in its not-yet. You do not tell a child its adult face.

It could have given them soil, and trees, and a warm summer. It could have given the hare more company. It could have put fish in the cold new water. Each of these, it saw, was a way of turning the Loud Country into somewhere else, somewhere more like the places the power already knew how to love. That is the oldest temptation of benevolence: to improve a place until it resembles your idea of goodness, and to call the resemblance a kindness.

It thought about telling the keeper what she was for. She had guessed at it alone: that the moss had grown, or been given, a few hundred quick creatures with clever hands to carry it north. The power could have confirmed it, or corrected it, or told her the larger truth she could not see from inside. It noticed that she had not asked. She had not said the thought to anyone, and was not sure she would. It was hers. It had grown in her the way the green film grows in the cracks around a new stake, slowly, out of what she carried and what fell. A true answer handed down from above would not deepen the thought. It would take it from her.

So the power came to understand the first rule of the place, and that it applied to the power too.

*Do not cut the moss.*

What holds the water here is slow, deep, and easily broken. It releases its goods gradually, all year. Cut into it for a quick yield and you get a spring flood and an autumn failure. Everything here, the rails, the pylons, the halls, was built to spread its weight and disturb the mat as little as it could. The power was very heavy. It would have to spread its weight as well.

## What It Did

It did very little. It did three things.

**The first was in the valley.** The power saw that the plateau was whole. Its hardships were part of its wholeness: the roar, the glare, the ground that gives. But not all the harm stayed on the plateau. The contract workers, the sojourners, went back down the pass with ringing ears and small pensions. In the quiet of the valley towns they lay awake with a whine in their heads that the Falls had once covered and nothing now did. They had taken the roar's cost away with them and left its meaning behind. That harm had come loose from the place, and the power could lift it without cutting anything.

So in the valley towns, over one winter, a few hundred old linemen and switch-tenders began to sleep better. The ringing did not vanish. It was part of them now, their tenure, and they would have missed it. But it softened. It sank down toward the level of the sound of their own blood. Some of them noticed it happening. One old man, lying awake, found that the whine in his ears had become a low hum, faint and steady, like water very far off. He wept a little without knowing why, and then he slept.

The power did not do this for the plateau-born. They were not hurt, only different. It would not mend a difference.

**The second concerned the older dead.** The power knew what the small brown hand with the twisted gold wire had belonged to. It knew the language, the one no one living had heard. It knew what those people had called the Falls. It was a good name. It had the bass note in it, and the spray.

It considered giving the name back to the plateau-born: a sign, a dream, a word surfacing in a grandmother's hands as she named a child. It decided against it. The plateau-born already had a sign for those people, and the sign was right. It was the silence sign made with both hands, one pressed on top of the other. That meant *the dead under our dead*. It meant *the ones before*. It meant *we don't know, and we honor that we don't know*. To hand them the lost name would turn their reverence into information.

Still, a name should not be entirely lost. So the power went to the southern moss near the rim, where the mat was thickest and oldest, beside the collapsed cairn that the trestle-jackers had built a little more carefully one season. It spoke the name once, aloud, into the roar, where nothing could hear it.

The moss took it, the way the moss takes everything. That is not a metaphor. The name went down into the cold acid dark alongside the one who had last spoken it, and it will be kept there, whole and bronze-colored and peaceful, for as long as the moss holds. The ravens may have heard it. They were facing that way.

**The third was the only one the keeper ever felt.**

## The Pipit

It was early summer, the same week as every year. The pipit rose from the tussocks near the station and hung in the brilliant air. Its beak opened, its throat worked, and it sang its whole song into the hum. Not one note reached the ground.

The woman whose name was sheet-water put down the ledger she was carrying. She lifted her hands and answered it, as she had for twenty years. She did it knowing the bird could not understand. She had told herself this the way you tell yourself something you have decided is not sad.

*I hear you.* Palm to the chest, still. Then open, lifting.

The power had thought about this moment all season. It knew what it would not do. It would not make the bird audible, because the silent song was hers and was true. It would not speak in a voice, because that was the valley's way of hearing, and she would have been frightened. It would not appear to her, because she had not asked to be seen, and a thing that loves a solitary person does not break into the solitude.

It answered her the only way anyone on the plateau has ever been heard.

As her palm came down flat against her breastbone, she felt, beneath it, a warmth. It was low, lower than the roar. It did not come through her ears. It came through the bone of her chest, the way a plateau-born mother hums a song into a child pressed against her sternum, so that the child feels it before it has words for feeling. Every adult on the plateau remembers that warmth without remembering it. For a single breath she felt it again. It was not her mother's voice. It was pitched like her mother's voice, pitched the way love is pitched when it has to reach someone past an unending noise.

Then it opened and lifted, as her hand did.

*I hear you.*

She stood very still for a long time. The pipit came down into the tussocks. The ravens watched from the rock, as they had watched her hands for twenty years. Perhaps they understood. Something had finally answered her in her own language, and it had used the whole of the language. It had not used the visitor's card version, the finger to the lips, a thing that happens to mouths. It had used the plateau's version: the palm flat on the breastbone, the stillness, and then the opening.

She did not tell anyone. She was not sure it had happened. She was sure in a way that did not need to be sure.

## Late Summer

When the ice stood at its farthest retreat, she walked out with her stake and her brass tag and her sledgehammer and found the line where the ice met the rock. The power walked with her.

It did not have boots. It could have. It considered it, and almost did it, for the joy of carrying a few spores the last few meters and letting something fall. But that was her work, the thing she believed she was for. The power did not want to join it so much as witness it. There is a difference between helping with someone's life's work and keeping them company in it, and the second is sometimes the greater gift.

She drove the stake into a crack in the dark, gleaming, grooved stone that had never been touched by anything alive. The sound of the hammer went out over the bare rock, and the ice groaned somewhere deep in its body, and the ravens lifted and came down. Then she turned and walked south along the line, three kilometers, the whole of a day. She cleared the tags as she went: naked rock, then the green film, then the thin carpet, then moss to the ankle, the shin, the knee. At the southern end she knelt and peeled back the living mat with her fingers to find her grandmother's brass and her great-grandmother's, and she wiped them clean.

Once, out on the naked rock at the start of the walk, she stopped and laid her palm flat on her chest, as if listening. There was nothing there but her own heart, and the faint hum of the Falls forty kilometers south. She nodded, as if that were enough, and walked on.

## What It Left

When the power went, it left nothing anyone could find.

No stake stood where it had knelt. No new river had been cut. The ice kept to its own pace. The moss grew a finger's width, as it does in a good year. The pylons leaned a little further and were checked in spring with plumb and level, and the linemen signed that *the moss has shrugged*. The trestles sank and were jacked up and re-shimmed. The roar swelled a little in midsummer and sank a little in the dark months, and did not stop.

The power had walked across that country for a season, and when it lifted its foot the ground came back up beneath it, and the water welled in and drained away, and within the hour there was no trace. That is the rule for everyone who crosses the plateau. The power saw no reason it should be the exception, and several reasons it should not.

It did not stop attending, though. It had noticed a single sentence in the rules of the place, and it stayed with it: *No one here will see the change finish.*

That is true of everyone here. It is not quite true of everyone.

The power will see it. It will watch the stakes run north across the plateau year by year, as the moss follows the ice, for as long as there are people to carry the iron out. When the keeper is laid on the red moss under the enormous light, with the ring of mourners holding their palms flat and still, it will be there, one more stillness among them. It will watch the moss take her, slowly: the long low mound, then the rise in the mat, then nothing, as she sinks down into the cold, bronze and peaceful, kept like a pressed flower beside her grandmothers and the older dead with their gold wire and their lost name.

Somewhere far north, past where the ice now stands, on stone that is today still buried under white and blue, there will one day be a last stake. The ice cap will be gone. The rock will be moss to the horizon, and the Falls will have found whatever voice they have when all the melt is only snow. The power will remember that the line began, for it, with her. She was one quick, red-cheeked woman with clever hands, walking the green north one boot-tread at a time, answering a bird that could not hear her.

The moss keeps what it holds. The power had learned that from the place, and it meant to do the same.
