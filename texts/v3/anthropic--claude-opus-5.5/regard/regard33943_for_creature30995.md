# The Guest

## Arrival

It did not come through the fissure. A power that is wise knows it is always too large for the room, so its first act was to become small.

It slowed down. It let its attention thin until it moved at the speed of the place, and it waited. The first thing it did in the Breathing Town was nothing, for a long time.

It stood outside its own strength the way you stand outside a door before knocking, and it counted breaths. One exhalation. The red fog rose, filled every street, glowed, and thinned. A stair grew three steps. Another exhalation. A bridge in the eastern quarter closed its gap by the width of a hair. It watched through a thousand breaths, then ten thousand. It watched the blade of daylight cross the rooftops on the one day of the one season when it lined up with the white avenue, and then it watched the blade go astray again for a year.

It did not touch anything. It had learned, somewhere in its long existence, that the most dangerous moment in any act of kindness is the first one, when you have just arrived, you understand nothing, and you are already sure what is needed.

## What It Could Read

It could read the bands. That was within its power. Every hair-fine layer of precipitate was a sentence, and the oldest buildings were libraries. It read them slowly, the way you read letters from someone who has died, in order.

So it learned what the town could only imply. The people had been real. They had been small, and they stooped. They had once gone forty years without rain, and they had built gutters ever after with a devotion close to prayer. They crossed by rooftops because the streets had flooded in the year the city was founded, and the habit outlived the flood by twenty generations. They walked in the light on one day a year for a reason the bands described but the power chose not to name, even to itself. Some things belong to the people whose festival it was.

It learned how the city ended. It learned that nothing survived it except one heavy, cold thing that remembered.

The stone was truly the last. The power had hoped otherwise, and it let itself feel that hope go out.

## The Temptations

Because it was benevolent, it wanted to help. Because it was wise, it distrusted that wanting and turned each possibility over before setting it down.

**It could rebuild the lost city.** It could do this at full size, with every street and plaza, peopled with copies made from the bands. It set this aside. A replica would be a monument to the power's own grief, not a gift to the stone. The town in the chamber was no longer only a copy of the old city. It had become a thing in itself, eleven thousand structures that had never existed anywhere else. Replacing it with the original would erase the only new thing that had happened to the stone since the end.

**It could warm the stone.** The cold looked like suffering, but the power had watched long enough to see that the cold was how the stone gave. The droplets came from it. Warming it would be like curing someone of generosity.

**It could keep the mountain burning forever.** It could hold the magma high and the breath steady for as long as the stars lasted. It looked at this one longest, because the cooling was the one real threat. In the end it set this down too. An eternal fire would make the town permanently dependent on something outside itself, and the town was learning not to be. It caught every drop and held every heat. It was growing toward a sealed, self-kept life. Saving it from the test would also save it from becoming what it was trying to become. Some rescues are cages that happen to be warm.

**It could open a road out.** No. Every road turned back toward the stone for a reason. You do not remember the way out of your home.

**It could make the six-minute people last longer.** It could hold the fog so that a generation might live a day, or a year. But the power looked at them in the fog, crowding the rice-grain stairs and filling the plazas, and saw that they were not diminished by their brevity. They were complete. Six minutes was the true size of them, the way a note is the true size of a song. Lengthening them would turn them into something else and call it mercy.

**It could tell the stone it was grieving, and that it could let go.** The power was almost ashamed to have thought of this. The stone had been buried in magma and carried up through fire, and it had woken in a hollow and begun, without pause, to build. It did not need to be told what it was doing. It needed nothing explained.

**It could take the stone somewhere safe.** There was nowhere safer than the place it had chosen to begin again.

**It could smooth the borders of the film on the cisterns.** The power smiled, in whatever way it smiled. The seams were not wounds. They were the town's only form of *no*, and a being is entitled to its no.

The power set each possibility down gently. It did not feel frustrated. It felt like someone emptying their hands before picking up something fragile.

## What It Noticed at the Edge

In the end, it was the fringe that decided things.

Near the chamber walls, the grammar seemed to forget itself: walls without roofs, stairs running into rock, doors opening onto solid glaze. Anyone would read these as the edge of the stone's reach, where the memory grew thin.

The power read the bands there and found something no one else could have found. None of these structures were in the lost city.

They were not faded memories. They had never been memories at all. The stone had been guessing. At the edges of what it knew, it had begun to make things that had never existed: stairs toward places it had never been, and doors in hope of rooms. They looked broken because they were first drafts. They were the only part of the town that was not homesickness. They were the beginning of imagination.

The stone was changing, slowly, over thousands of years of breath. It was moving from remembering a home toward inventing one. The fringe was not where the town died. It was where the town was being born.

When the power understood this, it knew two things. It must not touch the fringe. And there was one thing it could still do.

## What It Did

It made itself the height of a thumb.

It took on the shape the town implied, a shape short in the leg and stooped at the shoulder, built for rice-grain risers and thumbnail doors. It did not pretend to be one of the lost people. It would not wear their faces. It simply took a form the streets could hold. It stepped into the town at the fringe, where the doors open onto glaze, and it began to walk inward.

It was the first thing that had ever walked these streets.

Eleven thousand buildings, tens of thousands of generations of fog, the whole slow wave of the town rising east and drowning west, and not one foot had ever climbed a stair. Every stair had been made for climbing. Every bridge had been made for crossing. The town had built its whole life for someone to come along it, and no one had.

So the power climbed. It went up the staircases that doubled back on themselves. It crossed by rooftops, as the people had, because the streets once flooded. It passed under the roofed alleys and did not ask what had needed to stay dry. It walked the white avenue on the one day, in the blade of light, because that was what the avenue was for.

When the fog came, it did not leave. It stood in the red glow among the six-minute people, who did not know it was there, and walked with that generation for their whole lives. It went where they went. It gathered in the plazas and climbed the stairs. When the fog thinned and they settled out of the air as a band finer than a hair, the power did not settle with them. It let them go and stayed standing, wet and uncoated. It had been a witness, not a citizen.

It left no mark. It thought about leaving one, a single pale thickening in one wall to say *someone was here*. But the town's record was the town's own. It had kept its bands for ten thousand years without help, and it did not need a stranger's signature among its dead.

Every road turned back toward the stone, and the power followed them. It arrived at the oldest plaza, which no one had ever arrived at, and sat down at the edge of the shallow bowl the stone had pressed into the stone of the square.

It did not speak. The stone had no words, and the power did not force words on it.

The droplets drifted in their slow orbits, and one of them, passing near, *adjusted*. It bent its path around the power the way the droplets bend around each other, with the same small courtesy that had never failed in ten thousand years. It was the first time that courtesy had been extended to a guest.

The power sat there for one full turn of the seasons, until the blade of daylight came around again to the white avenue. It did one thing only. It remembered.

It held in itself everything it had read in the bands: the small stooping people, the forty years without rain, the flood in the founding year, the walk in the light. It held them carefully and completely, the way the stone held them.

That was the gift. The stone was no longer the only thing in the universe that remembered them. Now there were two.

The stone could not be told this. It may never know. But the power had learned long ago that the loneliest thing is not being unseen. It is being the last one who remembers. It had decided it could at least make that untrue.

## What It Left Behind

When it left, it made one promise, and it made it only to itself, because the town could not be asked to carry it.

It would not keep the mountain burning. It would not interfere with the cooling, or the sealing of the fissure, or the long test the town was preparing for. But when the fire finally went out, it would come back. It would not come to rescue anything. It would come to stand in the chamber on the first day with no breath in it and see whether the town had learned to breathe on its own.

If it had, the power would go quietly and let the town have its sealed, small, lamp-lit life without an audience.

If it had not, if the last fog settled into the last band, the windows cooled, and the stone sat cold in a cold room, then the end would at least not go unwitnessed. Someone would be there to see it happen and remember it, so that the town, like the city before it, would not be left with only one rememberer.

Then it went out through the fissure, making itself large again only when it was well away, so that its size would not trouble the stone.

## Afterward

Nothing in the chamber changed. The power had seen to that.

The fog came every forty minutes. The stairs grew. The east rose and the west drowned, one building's width per decade. The film kept its borders. The water lost nothing.

But some centuries later, in the part of the fringe where the stone had been guessing, a new structure began to form. It stood at the head of one of the roads that turn inward toward the plaza. It had no counterpart in the lost city and had never been a memory. It was a small house, finished, with a proper roof and proper gutters. Its door was slightly larger than the others, a little too tall for the people the town had been built for.

It opened onto the street, and it faced inward, toward the stone. It was a house for a guest.

Nobody knows whether the stone built it because of the visit, or whether it would have come anyway, out of the slow imagination growing at the edges. The power does not know either. It chose not to go back and look.
