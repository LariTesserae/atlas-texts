# The Visitor

## Before It Came

It had been attending for a long time before it arrived. This is the first thing to understand about it. It noticed the way the moths notice, the way the shrimp watch: completely, and without hurry. It had seen the white plain and the crescent of green set into it like a held breath. It had heard the note ring across the hollow, very rarely, and had understood before anyone told it that the note was a flinch.

When its attention finally settled on the Underleaf, every shrimp in every cup turned at once. They turned upward, toward the roof, toward nothing anyone standing there could have seen. For a moment the thousand wet eyes of the place were all looking the same way. Then they turned back, one by one, to whatever they had been watching before.

The walker, on a high gallery, stopped. It had not stopped in a very long time.

## How It Came

It could have come as light. The light here already arrives from everywhere, and it could have simply been in it, soaked through the whole hollow at once.

It didn't. It came the way travelers come. It walked across the salt in the heat, stood at the crumbling lip, and pushed down through the living roof with its arms, and the roof closed behind it. It came in a body small enough to be met. It thought this mattered. The Underleaf knew how to meet a traveler. It did not know how to meet anything else, and the visitor had not come to be something the place couldn't understand.

It stood on the root-felt in the shadowless gold and let the heat sit against it. It was tired from the walk, and it let itself be tired. A moth came and settled on its shoulder.

It let the moth stay.

## What It Would Not Do

Here is what it could have done, and what it considered, standing there with the moth on its shoulder.

It could have let the breath out. One gesture would have done it. The held second would have gone through, and all the seconds stacked behind it would have followed like water through a broken dam. The stems would have scarred and the leaves frayed. The drops would all have fallen at once, a rain of ten thousand years in an afternoon. The sleepers would have aged in their hammocks between one tick and the next. By evening there would have been a forest here, brown-shaded and rotting and alive, and the walker would have been one more dead thing on the floor.

It could have done the opposite. It could have made the stillness true. It could have sealed the moment so perfectly that it no longer needed holding, so that the walker could stop, because nothing would ever again try to arrive. That would have been a kindness of a sort. The walker was so tired.

It considered both for a long time. Then it set them both down.

Both were the same act. In both, a stranger arrives, decides what the right moment is, and makes it so. The first says: *your grief is wrong, and I will end it.* The second says: *your grief is right, and I will finish it for you.* Either way the walker's choosing would be over. And the visitor had already seen the one thing that made it certain the walker was still choosing.

The walker circled the ring.

Something that had truly decided would not keep coming back to the question. The walker went around those seeds again and again and never crossed, and never took them away either. That was not a refusal. That was a mind turning something over. It was the only beginning in the Underleaf that the walker had never unmade, because it was the walker's own.

So the visitor would not answer the question. It could stand close to the one asking it, and that was all.

## Being Held

The walker came. It came the way it always came, not straight and not quickly, along the galleries in a long curve, until it was there. Up close it was larger than the stories. It was also smaller, the way a grieving person is smaller than you expect when you finally see them.

It laid a limb along the visitor's shoulder, where the moth had been.

The visitor let it.

It could have refused. The walker's touch could not have held it back if it hadn't wished to be held. But it wanted to know what this was from the inside before it did anything else, so it let the beginning be taken. The tiredness from the walk went back into the moment it had come from. So did the small ache of heat, and the thought it had been starting to have about the light. None of it hurt. It was, as the ones who stayed had said, the tenderest thing. The visitor felt itself being kept. For a moment it understood entirely why no one who lay down in the hammocks ever got up. Nothing had ever loved it with so little room for anything else.

It also felt the walker's effort. Through the limb it felt the whole hollow at once: every drop straining to fall, every tendril tip leaning toward its next millimeter, every sleeper's slow tide of small changes pressing against the held second. It felt the walker meeting all of it, everywhere, always. The ticking was louder from inside. It was not a clock. It was a heartbeat that had been trying to take its second beat for longer than anyone could count.

The walker withdrew the limb and began to move on.

The visitor raised its hand and laid it, lightly, on the walker's limb in return.

Nothing was cut and nothing was hurt. Nothing was taken back, either. It was only touch answering touch, an arrangement, like the knots. The walker stayed still under the hand, the way the travelers said it stayed at the knots, longer than it stayed anywhere else. The fluid in its body eddied, and something turned in the pool at its front and faced the visitor.

Then it went on. The visitor went with it.

## Company

They walked for a long time. It is hard to say how long in a place with only one second, but the walker's circuits are long, and they don't repeat, and the visitor walked all of them.

It didn't speak. Speech was too loud here. It learned the circuits by walking them. It learned where the walker slowed. Near the eastern wall, near the ring, and at one high junction where two stems met so seamlessly they seemed to have been a single stem that divided out of love. It learned which moths had sat too long and were beginning, very faintly, to be old. It learned that the note came oftener than the travelers knew. Many of the flinches were too small to hear, only a tremor through the columns that the walker absorbed alone.

It learned that nothing had ever walked beside the walker before.

The walker did not know what to do with this at first. It kept pausing, as if it had forgotten something. Sometimes it turned the pool at its front toward the visitor for a long moment and then turned away. Once, far along a gallery, it stopped and waited. It had never waited for anything, and it was waiting to be caught up with.

After a while the visitor began to help. It did not take over. On a curve where the walker was about to go, the visitor went first. It found the beginning the moth had found, the brushed leaf or the loosening drop, and held it, only that one, only until the walker arrived. The walker would come and find the thing already held and not know what to do. The first few times it held the thing again anyway, on top of the visitor's holding, as if help might be a trick. Then one time it didn't. It laid a limb along the visitor instead, briefly, and went on to the next.

The ticking changed, slightly. Something in it eased by the width of a hair.

## The Sleepers

The visitor went to the hammock near the eastern wall.

It had known about the sleepers from before it came. They were the part of this that it had carried longest in its attention, because they were the part where the walker's love had reached something that could have said no, and had not been able to ask.

They had chosen to stay, most of them, at the start. But they had chosen as people who could still change their minds. The walker had taken that back along with everything else, gently, one beginning at a time, until there was no one left inside to change anything. The visitor did not think this was cruelty. It thought it was a love that had never learned the difference between a leaf and a person, because it had never had a reason to.

It didn't wake them. Waking them would have been its own choice laid over theirs.

It knelt beside the hammock where the faint shape lay curled with its knees drawn up. Then, for this one sleeper, it held back the walker's holding. It was a small act: a hand between the sleeper and the next touch of the limb. It did this for only as long as it takes a thought to finish.

It had not been able to finish for a very long time. It was the thought the sleeper had meant to finish the next day, the day that never came. Now it came.

The visitor stayed with each sleeper while their thought finished. There were more of them than the travelers had known. It found them in the galleries, against the columns, curled in the crooks of the lowest stems. The thoughts were different.

Some of them finished: *I should go home.* These it carried up at night. There is night on the salt plain, even if there is none in the Underleaf. It carried them over the lip, into the cold and the dark and the stars. It stayed with them while time landed on them. It stood between them and the full weight of it, and let it come softly, one second at a time, until they could stand it. Some wept at the dark. Some had been missed by people who were still alive, and some had not. The visitor could not change that. It could walk with them toward whatever was left.

Some of them finished: *I am happy here.* These it let stay. But it knelt by the nearest knot, a binding of braided tendril around two columns, and it added to the braid. It tied in a single strand that the walker would read the next time it passed. The strand said, as well as a knot can say anything: *these ones are not leaves. Let them dream.* The walker, reading it, did something the visitor had hoped for without expecting it. It let the sleepers keep one kind of beginning. It still held back their aging and their aches. But it let their dreams go forward, night after night of the same afternoon. They were never the same dream twice. The sleepers in the hammocks began, very faintly, to move in their sleep.

And one of them finished: *I am tired. I would like it to be over now.*

The visitor stayed. It held that sleeper's hand and let time reach them. It did not let it reach everything, only them, only the one small body in the hammock. It came slowly and did not hurt. It did not take long. The weave of the hammock, which had sagged no heavier than light, sagged a little more.

The walker came.

It came faster than it had ever come for anything, and it reached out a limb to take it back. The visitor did not stop it. It only looked up and said, very quietly, the first words anyone had spoken in the Underleaf in a very long time. They soaked into the tendrils and were gone.

*They asked for this.*

The walker's limb stayed in the air above the hammock. The pool at its front turned from the sleeper to the visitor and back. The note rang, and it was not a flinch this time. It was longer than a flinch, and lower, and every column carried it. The visitor understood that it was the sound of the walker not doing something.

The limb drew back.

That was the first ending the Underleaf had ever seen. The next morning (there was, in that one corner, a next morning) the leaf-bowl beside the hammock had dried. The walker came and stood by it for a long time. Then it did a thing no one had taught it. It took one broad leaf from its own underside, folded it over the hammock like a blanket, and pinned it with a thorn.

It was the first grave. The walker made it.

## Rest

Near the end, the visitor offered.

It stood with the walker on the high gallery where the two stems met, and it opened itself the way it had opened itself to the moth, the way it had let its tiredness be taken. It made plain what it was offering, in the only language the place had: a touch, a limb along a limb. *I will hold it. All of it. For a while. You can stop.*

The walker did not understand. It had never stopped. It didn't know that stopping was a thing that could be done without everything ending.

So the visitor showed it, a little at a time. First it held one gallery while the walker walked the others. Then a whole quarter of the hollow. Then half. The walker kept circling back to the parts the visitor was holding. It checked them, and found them held, and went away again. Every time it went away it went a little more slowly.

At last it lay down.

It didn't lie down in a hammock or in the ring. It lay along the high gallery where it had been standing, and its limbs met the stems and became part of them, and did not come away again. The eddies in its body slowed. The thing in the pool at its front turned, and turned, and then went still, facing nowhere.

For the first time, the Underleaf was held by something that was not tired.

While the walker slept, the visitor held everything, every drop, every tendril, every moth, every sleeper, every scratch, except one thing. It did not hold the walker. It let the walker's own time go forward, only the walker's, for as long as it slept.

So the walker dreamed.

The visitor did not look into the dream. That was the walker's. But it saw the fluid in the walker's body begin to move differently, in patterns that did not come back to where they began. And when the walker woke, and rose, and its limbs came away from the gallery, it had something that nothing else in the Underleaf had ever had.

It had a yesterday.

It stood on the gallery for a long time, turning this over. The visitor could not have said what the walker made of it. Whatever it was, when the walker began to move again, it did not go on its circuit.

It went to the ring.

## The Ring

The visitor was already there, sitting in the center, in the empty space nine paces across that someone had left open long ago.

It had gone in the moment the walker woke. It did not beckon, and it did not speak. It sat on the root-felt, which was the same inside the ring as outside. It sat with its hands open on its knees and its face turned outward toward the glow, the way the sleepers sit against the columns, and it waited. It waited in the way that is not asking. It had decided long ago that it would not answer this question, and the only way not to answer was to wait.

The walker circled.

It circled at a distance of a few paces, as it always had, all the way around. Then again. Then again. The pool at its front stayed turned toward the visitor the whole time, and the visitor did not move. The shrimp in the nearest cups watched the walker now, not the seeds, the way they watch strangers.

On some circuit that nobody counted, the walker came closer.

The next circuit it came closer still. Its limbs were almost at the seeds, reaching and drawing back, reaching and drawing back, like tendrils in a draft, though there are no drafts in the Underleaf.

Then one limb crossed.

It passed over the dark stone-hard seeds without touching them, and it met the root-felt inside the ring and became part of it for a moment. It did not separate. The walker stood half-in and half-out of the circle, and the note rang. This time it did not stop. It went on and on, soft and deep, a finger drawn around the rim of a glass that no one lifts away.

The visitor understood that the walker was not flinching. The walker was playing it.
