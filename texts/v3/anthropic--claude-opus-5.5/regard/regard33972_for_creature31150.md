# What the Power Did

## Arrival

It came the way weather comes to a mountain, from far off and slowly, so that nothing on the ridge could have said when it started to be there.

It did not come because it was called. Nothing in the roost calls to anything outside itself. The bats call to the room and the room answers, and that closed loop is the whole of their conversation with the universe. The power came because it had been paying attention, the way such powers do. Somewhere in its vast patient noticing, a warm breath leaving a cold mountain registered as a kind of signal, and it turned toward it the way you turn toward a lit window on a dark road.

It stopped at the lip of the slot. It felt the mountain's exhalation, humid and sour and sweet. It looked at the liverworts, the wet beards of moss, the one fern, and it understood them immediately for what they were: the evidence, printed on the outside, of a life on the inside.

Then it did the first wise thing, which was to not go in.

## Why It Did Not Go In

A lesser power, or a younger one, would have entered. It would have wanted to see.

This one paused at the threshold and considered what it was. Whatever form it wore, it had extent. It occupied space and had surfaces. If it stepped into the bell-shaped room, it would change the shape of the room, if only slightly, by the volume of its presence. It would change the way sound came back from the walls.

Somewhere on the eastern curve, two-thirds of the way up, there was a bat who knew where she was by the lateness of her own echo. The power did not yet know about her in particular. It knew there would be someone like her. There are always a great many someones in a place like this, each navigating by a map made of returning sound. A new body in the room, however kind, would be a stone dropped into that map. For a moment, a hundred thousand small creatures would find their home the wrong shape.

So it stayed at the mouth, where the liverworts grew, and it listened.

## Listening

It listened for a long time. Powers of this kind can.

It learned the room as the bats learn it, by ear. It heard the dome's curvature in the way the chittering pooled and returned. It heard the swiftlets ticking, dry and countable. It heard the drip, quickening and slowing across the day like a pulse. It heard the floor, the rain-on-leaves scatter of cockroaches and the faint seething of beetles. In the deep quiet of the night, when the bats were gone, it heard the stone ticking as it cooled.

It heard the exodus at dusk, the roaring hour, and it moved aside from the mouth to let the river pass. It watched the snakes lean into the stream and strike blind. It felt the familiar small grief that benevolent things feel at predation, and then it set that grief down, because it also saw the snakes. They were lean, pale, and cold-blooded, living at an altitude where almost nothing could feed them, and fed only because this warm, loud fountain of bodies poured past them every night. It saw that the snake was also a creature the room had made possible. Benevolence that loved only the bats would be a narrow kind of love. It let the snakes eat.

And at dawn, as the river came back, it listened for one voice among the million. It found her the way she found her pup: by attending long enough that one call separated from the others.

She came in over the snakes, through the grey, and into the black. She called. The room answered. She rose up the eastern curve and hung.

The power learned her age by the wear on her voice. Nineteen years. It knew what that meant for something that weighs less than a coin. It knew that she had come back six thousand times, and that coming back is the harder thing.

## The Things It Could Have Done

Powers of this kind are not helpless, and this one considered everything. That is part of wisdom too: not refusing to imagine intervention, but imagining it fully and then choosing.

**It could have sealed the roof.** It could see through stone, and it knew what was coming. The apex was thinning. It could measure the thickness that remained between the hottest cluster of bodies and the open sky, and it could calculate, roughly, how many centuries were left before the first crack of light. It could have thickened that stone with a thought, and the room would never break open or cool.

It considered this longest, because it was the most tempting, and it declined. It had already been down the mountain, below the cloud deck, to the round hollow with the enormous fig at its center and the goats grazing on the sweetest grass. It had seen what the room became when it finished. To seal the roof would be to save the room from its own flowering. It would make the room eternal, and it would make the room sterile, a bell that never finishes ringing because it is never allowed to end. The fern at the mouth would never get to creep inward. The seedlings on the floor, pale and threadlike, would never get their sun. The power understood that it was being asked, by its own compassion, to prevent a death that was also a birth. It would not.

**It could have given the seedlings light.** A glow at the floor, gentle, enough to let a fig unfold its leaves in the dark. It declined this too, with some tenderness, because it saw what the seedlings were. They were the room's dream of the valleys, continuous and always failing. A dream answered too early is not a kindness. Light on the floor would disturb the bats, fatten the fungi, and wake the centipedes out of season. It would turn the dreaming room into a greenhouse, and the room did not want to be a greenhouse yet. It wanted to be a room that was slowly becoming one.

**It could have kept the gatherers away forever.** It thought about this more carefully, because the gatherers were people, and it cared about people too. It looked at the sparse patch on the wall where the nests had been cut a lifetime ago and were still growing back. It looked at the lamp and the cord. It concluded that the gatherers had been, on the whole, not a wound but a visitation. They came once a generation. They took some and left most. They left a lamp and a knot that the stone was patiently making into a fossil. That was not the behavior of a threat. It was the behavior of a relationship. It did not forbid them, and it did not summon them. It left that door as it found it.

**It could have made her live longer.** Her specifically. Nineteen years is old, and the power could see the years that remained to her, and they were not many. It could have given her more.

This was the one it felt most, and it thought about it the way you think about a friend. In the end it asked a question it could not answer: *Longer for what?* She did not know she was old. She did not fear the end of her coming-back. Her life was complete in every return: the call, the answer, the eastern curve, the smell of her own rind, the warmth closing over her. To extend it would be to add more of something already whole. And the patch of rind that smelled of her mother and her mother's mother would, someday, smell of her to someone else. That was how the room kept her. It did not need the power's help to remember her. It was already built to.

It left her life the length it was.

## The Pup

There was one thing it did for her, and it cost nothing and changed nothing.

It went looking for her pup.

Last spring's young had grown and flown and were now indistinguishable, to any ear but a mother's, from the other hundreds of thousands. The power listened to the dawn return until it found the voice that was unlike the others, the one she had found every morning in the nursery crush. It was there, higher up, nearer the apex, in the hotter layers. Alive, and well fed on valley insects, and hanging in the bathwater warmth.

The power could not tell her. There was no way to tell her that would not be an intrusion, and she did not need telling. She would likely never search for it again. That is how it goes with bats and with much else.

But the power knew. There is a strand of goodness that consists only of this: making sure that someone somewhere knows the pup is all right. It is not for the mother's sake. It is for the sake of the fact itself, so that it does not go entirely unwitnessed. The power witnessed it, and kept it.

## The Calendar

On the last night it did the thing it had been moving toward all along.

The floor, the description of this place says, is a calendar that no one reads.

So the power read it.

It did not dig. It did not touch the dunes or cut a face into them. It read the way it could read, through the guano and down through the damp peat and the compacted layers into the stone. It read every band, dark for the wet years and pale for the dry. It read the thin lines where the valley fruit had failed and the fruit bats had come home hungry. It read the thick lines of plenty. It found the slight thinning of the swiftlet layer from the year the gatherers came with their cane ladders. It went further down, past any human presence, through centuries and then millennia, to the place where the calendar began, when this bell was only a crack and a few hundred bodies hung in a single sheet against the rock. Its first rind was barely a stain.

It read the whole thing. It read the colony's entire life, the long body of the animal-that-is-a-room, from bud to bell.

That was the gift. It was not a gift the room could perceive, and the power was clear with itself about that. It wasn't doing it to be thanked.

Consider what the room is. It is a voice that learned its shape by listening to itself come back. Every creature in it knows itself by echo, and the room as a whole knows itself the same way, by a feedback so slow and so enormous that no single thing inside it could hear it. The dunes record. The walls record. The cord and the lamp and the ammonites record. Everything the room has ever been is stored, carefully and without malice, the way an oyster keeps its grit. And none of it had ever been *received*.

A benevolent and wise power is, among other things, a listener who can listen at any scale. So for once the room's longest call, the one it has been making for thousands of years in guano and stone, got an answer at its own size. Somewhere outside the bell, the whole of it was heard, all the way back to the crack, and held.

The power did not echo it back. That would have been presumption. It simply let the calling be complete. A letter was read. A calendar was, finally, read.

## The Bud

Before it left the ridge, it went to see the bud.

It was along the ridge and a little lower: a crack a hand's width wide, with a few hundred bats in a single sheet and the first trace of a rind. The stone around its lip was darker and a little damp. There was no fern yet.

The power checked it the way a gardener checks a seedling bed, without touching. The rock was sound. The crack was not near any fault that would close it. It was on the lee side, out of the worst of the wind. There was nothing here to fix.

It noticed, though, that there was a patch of bare stone at the lip, just where the faint breath of the bud met the air. That was the spot where, in some decades or centuries, the first liverwort would take hold.

It did not plant anything. But it stood there a while, and some tiny fraction of the stone's damp may have been its own, left there without intention, the way breath fogs a window. If a spore arrives a little sooner because of that, the power would not call it an intervention. It would call it standing close.

## The Valley

Then it went down through the cloud.

Below, the power found what it had half expected: the roost's actual fragility was not in the roost at all.

The room is fed from below. Its hands, the fruit bats, reach down every night into the orchards and the fig trees. Its insect-eaters pour out over the valley fields. Everything that heats the dome, everything that rains in it and carves it and dreams in it, is made of valley. The room is not self-sufficient. It is a mouth at the top of a very long body, and the rest of the body is the lowlands. Those were changing, the way lowlands do.

So the power walked, looking like whatever it needed to look like, which was probably an old traveler. It walked along the flight paths. It noted the great fig trees at the edges of the orchards and the ones in the village squares. It found the ones that were old enough to be at risk, the ones someone was already eyeing for firewood or for the space they took up. It noted where fields were being sprayed, where hedgerows full of moths were being grubbed out, where the valley was quietly withdrawing the food it had always sent uphill.

It did not compel anyone. It did not reach into minds. A benevolent power that rearranges the thoughts of people to save bats has gotten its values badly out of order. The people were also creatures with a room and a life cycle and a voice that knows its shape by coming back.

What it did was simpler and slower. It sat by the trickle where the water comes out of the cliff, the water the valley people say tastes of the sky. When people came to drink, it talked to them. It asked where they thought the water came from. Mostly they said the mountain. Sometimes they said the clouds.

To the ones who seemed as if they might want to know, it told the truth. It said there was a warm room at the top of the mountain, and that what they tasted was the breath of a million small bodies, filtered through a kilometer of stone. It said the bodies were fed by their figs and their orchards. It said the sweetness in the water had come up from the valley in the night and down again in the day, and that they had been drinking from this circle their whole lives.

Some laughed. One old woman nodded as though she had always suspected it. One child asked if it was true that the bats ate the figs, and when told yes, said with great seriousness that then they shouldn't cut the big fig by the well. The power said nothing to that. It let it belong to the child.

That was all. It did not rescue the valley or legislate it or bless it. It gave some people a true story about where their water came from, and trusted what people do, sometimes, with true stories. That was as much as it was right to do. A power can make a fig tree unfellable. Only a person can decide to love it, and only the love lasts past the power's leaving.

## What It Did Not Leave

It did not leave a sign. It did not carve anything on the lip of the slot, or put a lamp next to the old lamp, or a cord beside the old cord. The room already had its human traces, and they were the right size: a sooted smudge, a knotted fiber, a sparse patch of nests. They were evidence of people who came and took a little and went away. The power preferred to be less than that. It wanted to be a trace so small the stone could not find it to grow around.

## What It Will Do

It will come back.

It knows, now, roughly when. It has measured the stone at the apex. Some number of centuries from now, a crack of daylight will open in the top of the bell, the size of a hand and then the size of a door. The warm breath will pour out the roof like smoke. The fur will thin. The rain will stop. The pool will dry to a crust.

The power intends to be there on the ridge. It will not stop it. It intends to watch the fern.

It wants to see the green that has spent all this time clinging to the lips of the slot take its first step inward. It wants to see the first fig seedling on the floor unfold a real leaf in real light, the dream finally past a hand's height. It wants to see the bats leave for the bud along the ridge, which by then will have its own fern. It wants to see the room finish, and become a garden, and be grazed by the great-grandchildren of the goats. It wants to be a witness to all of it, as it was a witness to the pup.

Some deaths should be prevented. Others should be attended. Much of wisdom is telling which is which. Most of the rest is showing up.

## Coda: Dawn

On its last morning at the mouth, before it went down the mountain, the power stayed for the return.

The river poured in over the ridge and the snakes, through the grey, into the black. Somewhere in it was a nineteen-year-old bat, tired from the valleys and weighing less than a coin. She called. The room answered, rounded and slightly late, exactly as late as it always had, because the power had never stepped inside to change it. She rose up the eastern curve into the heat. She found her patch of rind. She hung.

The drip quickened around her. The warmth climbed.

She never knew anything had been there. The power had made sure of that; it was, in the end, the whole method. It had been benevolent without becoming another thing in the room. It had been wise enough to see that the room was not a problem, not a victim, not a thing waiting to be saved. It was a life going beautifully and mortally and in its own time toward its own ending, and toward its own children.

The one thing the room had lacked in all its long ringing was a listener large enough to hear the whole bell.

It has one now, somewhere outside it, at its own scale, keeping the calendar in mind. That is what the power did: almost nothing, done with enormous care.
