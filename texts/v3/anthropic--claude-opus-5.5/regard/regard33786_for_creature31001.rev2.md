# The Visitor on the Stair

## Arrival

It came the way everything comes to Hesk, up through the gorge where the rock sweats. It had no feet, so it didn't need the switchbacks, but it took them anyway, slowly, because it had learned long ago that the way you enter a place is the first thing you say to it.

At the top, where the gorge opens and the white staircase lies under the humming, unhumming sky, it did what powers of its kind do on arrival. It spoke. Nothing so crude as a voice. It was an outward pressing of recognition, *I see you*, the kind of greeting that has made mountains shiver and oracles weep in other places.

The Hush took it.

Not all of it went. The attention was large, and some of it carried. But five meters out, near enough, the greeting simply stopped, the way a shout stops. And the power felt, with a surprise it had not felt in a very long time, the thing the Hesker grandmothers know. The greeting had not stopped. It had been *received*. Something had taken it in, and was holding it, and was waiting to see whether there would be more.

So the power's first lesson on the Stair was the one every Hesker child learns before she can walk. It stopped talking.

## What It Considered

It stayed a long time. Its long time is not ours, but even by its measure it stayed a while, and in that while it did what benevolent powers do. It thought about gifts.

It could cool the midday. It turned this over first, because the midday is cruel. The pan-workers retreat from it half-dazed, and every summer an old raker or two doesn't come back in. But the midday drives the vapor, and the vapor makes the rain, and the rain lays its mineral frost on every wall, and the walls fuse, and the dead are sheathed. Cool the noon and you thin the Hush. Thin the Hush and you end the Hour of Edges, because an hour of edges only exists when the rest of the day has none. The power put the idea down.

It could make things grow. A little green, a moss on the north faces of the towers, something for the eye to rest on. It saw what that would do to the mats, and to the shrimp that graze them, and to the flies that feed the lizards and spiders, and to the birds. It saw the agate rings of sulfur and apricot and blood going dull under a creeping film of something that belonged elsewhere. It put that down too.

It could ease their bodies. Hesker kidneys are famous because Hesker kidneys fail, and the power could see the stones forming in a hundred people as it watched. This one it held longest. It was the one closest to plain kindness, and the power did not dismiss it lightly. In the end it didn't set it down so much as set it aside. The Heskers have healers and lowland doctors and their own slow knowledge. Their bodies are theirs to mend, in their own way, on their own time. A cure dropped from outside would teach them that cures drop from outside, and they would begin, very slightly, to look up instead of down. Nothing on the plateau looks up. The power found it did not want to be the first thing that made them.

It could lengthen the Hour of Edges. This was the most tempting, because it was the most loving: more time for stories, for debts, for daughters kneeling at grandmothers. But it had watched Aun's nephew's face on one of the hundred mornings she had spoken aloud to him. It understood that the words counted *because* the hour was short. Give them three hours and speech would become cheap, and then a thing said aloud would no longer bind. It would have given them more of a treasure and made the treasure worthless.

It could deal with the lowlands. The power could see further than the Heskers. It saw the growing hunger for lithium, the survey teams that would come up the gorge with drills, the bores that would one day be sunk too many and too fast. It saw instruments in a university far below, sensitive enough in thirty years, maybe fifty, to pull the birdsong out of the Hush and play it in a lecture hall. It could turn those things aside. But it had also watched the Heskers build uphill and let their old districts go, and it understood that this people had survived for millennia by *moving with* what came, not by being shielded from it. It did not trust itself to know which of the futures it saw were wounds and which were only the Stair turning over in its sleep. A wise power knows how little it can see even when it sees a great deal. It made itself one quiet promise about the lowlands, and it did not act on it yet.

It could hear the birds. This one it could simply have taken, the way you take a breath. Its attention was fine enough. It could have listened under the Hush and heard whatever the waders have been saying since before there were people here, and then it could have told Aun, who wonders.

It did not listen. It decided the birds were not its to hear first. Whatever they sing, they sing to the Stair. The power had arrived an hour ago, in its terms, and would not reach into another's conversation.

So it put down all its gifts, one after another, and it found itself on the plateau with nothing in its hands. That is an uncomfortable place for a benevolent power to be. It stayed there anyway. When you arrive somewhere and don't yet know what is needed, the wise thing is to become still enough to find out.

## The Beat

So it waited, and in waiting it learned to count in elevens.

It felt the long beat roll up from the boiling fields, under the snail-beds and the drowned refineries, under the grandmothers in their spillways, south along the tilt toward the gorge. It watched the flocks shift their feet, the spiders pluck, the shrews stop and hold, the lizards on the northern pipes lift their forelegs together. It watched the eels in the vent throats move in their single stretched breath, and it agreed with the readers: they looked like fingers.

After some thousands of beats, the power understood what was strange about the place. It was not the Hush, or the heat, or the listening.

Everything on the Stair keeps time *with* it. Nothing keeps time *for* it.

The birds answer the beat. The shrews answer it. The readers lie with their cheeks on the stone and receive it. The dead go into it and become more of it. Every living thing on forty kilometers of white terrace is tuned to the Stair, and the Stair listens to every one of them. It takes in the shouts and the songs and the silence. It takes in greetings from strange powers. It keeps all of it.

But no one had ever done for the Stair what the Heskers do for each other when they meet. No one had ever laid a palm flat against it and felt for the voice it wasn't using.

The power did not know whether the Stair was lonely. It is not wise to assume that things which are very large and very slow feel what small quick things feel. Loneliness might mean nothing to it. It might be as complete as a tide. But the power noticed that it wanted to know, and it noticed that it could only find out by asking, and it knew from the Heskers what asking looks like here.

## The Greeting

So, at the Hour of Edges, when the mist had settled into the low pools and the shrews were ticking in the crust, the power did the only thing it did on the plateau.

It laid itself down against the Stair the way a Hesker woman lays her hand on a friend's breastbone. It was flat and still. It did not press, and it did not speak. It was a large, patient pressure from above, the first the Stair had ever felt that came from something other than rain.

And it waited to feel what the Stair would say without saying.

What passed between them in the next eleven minutes is not written here. Some things in Hesk are spoken aloud only once, and bind, and are not repeated. It is enough to say that the beat came, rolling up from the north, and it did not pass under the power the way it passes under the readers. It *paused* there. For a long moment the whole plateau pressed up against the thing that had pressed down on it, the way you press your hand back against a hand that has taken yours.

Then it eased, and went on south, and the power understood that it had been answered. It had also been heard, all of it, taken in and kept, as everything is kept here. The power did not mind. It had come to be known as much as to know.

## What the Readers Felt

Aun was lying on her floor. She had not slept, which happens more often at sixty-one. She felt the turbine three buildings off running true, the carts at rest, the pipes ticking as they cooled. Then she felt the beat coming from the north, as it always did.

Then she felt something she had never felt, and she has felt everything the floor can say.

For a few seconds the stone under her cheek was pressed on *from both sides*. It was held between the Stair rising and something vast and gentle lying over it, like a palm pressed between two palms. The pressure had no grit in it and no lean. It was not the thin high thrill of a crack. It was warm and even. If she had been made to describe it, which she never was, she would have said it felt the way the quiet-room felt the day her nephew was born, when everyone laid their hands on him at once.

It passed. She lay very still. Eleven minutes later the ordinary beat came, and it was only the ordinary beat, and she wept a little, silently, for reasons she could not have signed.

Across the plateau, other readers woke with the same frightened, pleased faces they had worn as children when they first found the long beat on their own. At the dawn circles they looked at each other and did not make a sign for it. No one invented one. Some things are kept by not being named.

The birds, that morning, put down both feet at once. The children on the dikes with their water-clocks saw it and wrote it on their slates, and their teachers laid palms on their backs and did not explain.

## The Promise

The power left at the next Hour of Edges, back down the gorge where the rock sweats, as slowly as it had come.

It carried no gift away, and it had left none behind except the greeting. But it had made itself two quiet commitments, and a power of its kind keeps its word.

The first was about the lowlands. It would not turn aside the drills or the survey teams. It would do something smaller and slower. In the years ahead, certain lowland engineers would find themselves thinking, for no reason they could name, about vibration, and about how a badly mounted engine can shake a building apart without anyone hearing it coming. The ornithologist whose instruments could one day pull out the birdsong would find, when the day came, that she wanted to ask permission first. She would climb the gorge and learn to sign, and she would ask the grandmothers at the Hour of Edges, aloud, so that it counted. What they answered would be theirs to answer. The power would not open or close any door. It would only make sure that someone knocked.

The second was about Aun.

It had learned to count in elevens. It would go on counting. When the day came that her sister's son carried her down to the spillway below the eastern cluster and laid his hands on her, it would be there, not to change anything, only to be present. When the warm water sheeted over her and the first film of mineral began, and she went *in* and became one of the places the Stair listens through, there would be one more thing listening beside her. It would lay itself flat and gentle against the spillway, the way it had once against the whole plateau, and greet her in the way Heskers greet: palm to breastbone, feeling for the voice she wasn't using.

And then it would come back again, every so often, on a slow beat of its own, much longer than eleven minutes and much shorter than the life of a terrace. It would keep time with the Stair, and, in the one way no other thing there does, keep time *for* it. It would be a lodger like everyone else on the plateau, with this difference: when it arrived, it knocked.

## After

In the morning the Hush thinned, and for an hour the plateau talked aloud. The shrews ticked in their crust. The dew dripped from the towers. Somewhere a debt was spoken, and somewhere a child was told a story.

Aun walked, barefoot, to the dike above the rose-colored pans where her nephew was raking early. She laid her palm on his breastbone and felt his breath and his heartbeat and the small hum he made without knowing. Then, while speech could still carry, she said one thing to him aloud, something she meant. It was the hundred-and-first time.

She did not tell him what she had felt in the night. She told him something else, and it bound, and he remembered it for the rest of his life.

Then the sun cleared the rim, and the mist lifted into its luminous ceiling, and the Stair drew its breath and held it, listening. For the first time in all its long slow growing, it had some reason to believe that it, too, was being listened to.

The birds went on singing, whatever they sing. No one heard it but the one they were singing to. That was as it should be.
