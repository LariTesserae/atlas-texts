# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not come from above, the way everything else comes. It came up with the cloud.

It rose out of the lowlands to the west in the cold wet air, as the weather does, and was funnelled into the trough. It climbed and cooled and poured up the valley like a river running backwards. It tore a little on the chimneys. For a long time, perhaps a season, perhaps longer, it did nothing but move through the town the way the fog moves through it. It filled a street to the second storey and emptied it again. It looked.

It had been drawn by something, and at first it did not know what. Places like Hallow Col are not rare in the way it cares about. There are many hard places, and many places where people are poor and cold and short of light. What drew it was harder to name. It was a kind of attention the valley itself seemed to be paying, a held breath under the hiss and the hum. Something here was about to happen, and something here already knew.

## What It Saw

It saw the town first, because the town is what anyone sees. It saw six kilometres of stacked slate and iron, the moss, the birch woods on the roofs, the ropeway buckets appearing and vanishing. It saw the shift tide on the stairs, the weft threading the loom.

Then it saw the people. It saw an old woman climbing four hundred steps before noon. It saw a child in the low town squinting at a lamp. It saw a man with roof-hand showing his crush-scar to a stranger, and the stranger showing his back. It saw hands speaking below intention: *yes, go on, careful.*

Then it saw what the people had half-seen already. The town was a lichen, two lives that could not live apart. The town was a case, and inside the case was the water.

It is not the kind of power that stops at the first true description, so it went on looking. What it came to understand was this: the being who lived in Hallow Col was neither the water nor the people. It was the relationship, the two hundred years of letting through. It was the law that everyone called the oldest, as though it were their idea. The law was their idea. It was also the river's. That was the point. A thing had grown up between them that neither could have made alone, and it had a shape, and a voice made of hiss and hum and soft signing, and it was very nearly ready to change.

## What It Did Not Do

Being wise, it considered first what it should not do. That list was long, and most of the work was in keeping to it.

It did not bring the sun. It could have lifted the cloud off the valley, or bent the light down into the alleys. It thought about the pale children who squint. It thought about the moss and the ferns and the shade-plants, and about the wool, and the dark-adapted eyes, and the language worn smooth by fog. It thought about what a sudden flood of light would do to a place whose every living thing had been shaped by its absence. It thought about how the fog was also the town's water, and the water was its power, and its power was its bread. It left the sky alone.

It did not fix the map. It could have given the surveyors a true map, current to the hour. But a map that is always a year behind has taught the surveyors something no correct map could teach. They have learned to think of the town as a rate and not a place. It did not want to take that from them.

It did not stop the ropeway. It watched a funeral procession wait for the buckets to pass, and it watched the faces of the mourners. It did not stop the ropeway.

It did not tell Tegwen Harrow whose shoe that was.

It did not pull the under-tenants up into the light, or seal the drains they signed through, or decide on their behalf what they were.

It did not quiet the river.

This last was the hardest, because it had already understood what the changing note in the deep culvert meant. It could have held the water as it was, running in the dark four levels down, for another two hundred years. The town would have gone on falling and rising at exactly the same rate. That would have been a kindness of a sort. It would also have been sealing a caddis inside its case because you love the case.

## What It Did

What it did was small, and slow, and mostly consisted of paying attention in the places where attention was already being paid.

**It learned the signs.** This took some time, even for it. There are words in the Hallow Col hand that do not exist anywhere else, and it wanted to hear them spoken. It stood on landings during the shift change and watched conversations flow up and down the stairs. It learned *the fog is coming in from behind you*. It learned the sign for a load that is just barely safe. It learned the low-town hand as well, which is faster and more compressed, and which the upper town cannot read.

**It was given a sign-name.** Children did it, as children do. They noticed that it moved through the crowd on the stairs without anyone needing to turn sideways, the way the fog moves. So its sign became the fingers of one hand spread and drawn through the loose fist of the other without touching. When children made it, they made it quickly, and grinned, as if they had got away with something.

**It sat with a ropeway keeper.** His name was Owain Brack, sign-name a thumb run along the side of a finger as though feeling for a flaw in a cable. He lived in the cabin at the top of the great tower on the eastern stacks. For many nights it sat with him and listened to the buckets knock over the wheels. Nobody sat with keepers. Owain said so, in the dark, in hand signs lit by the cabin lamp, and then did not say anything for a long while.

Eventually he explained the rule, the way you explain something you have explained to yourself ten thousand times. The line carries three hundred tonnes at any moment. Stop it hard and the buckets swing, and a swinging bucket takes a gantry down, and a gantry takes a terrace with it. The rule does not exist because the ore is worth more than the man under the rubble. It exists because the line cannot be stopped without killing more men. He had stood at the platform and signed *not yet, not yet, now* to a woman whose husband was pinned under a wall two streets away. She had never spoken to him again.

It did not tell him he had been right, and it did not tell him he had been wrong. It stayed, and it heard all of it. Toward dawn it asked him one question: *what is the line for?* He thought the answer was obvious. Later, he thought about it for a long time.

**It walked with a root-reader** in the low town. Her name was Nell Arbery, and she put her palms on walls to feel whether they were held. It asked her to put her palms on the moss beneath the smelter grates instead, where the poisoned water ran. The moss there was darker and denser than moss anywhere else, strangely heavy in the hand. Nell cut a cushion of it, dried it, and burned it in a flue-mouth. The ash came out green.

It did not explain anything. Nell was a root-reader; she had spent her life knowing things with her hands before she could say them. Inside a year there were moss beds laid in the channels below the fourth level, and a new low-town trade in cutting them, burning them, and selling the ash back up to the smelters for its copper. The water coming out of the beds ran a little cleaner, and a little cleaner again at the next bed down. A kale woman named Bethan Rhys, who had strong opinions about the low town, carried a box of leeks down to the third level and then to the fourth, and they grew. That was all. It was not a transformation. It was the town's own logic extended by one step: *don't cut what's carrying you.* The moss had been carrying them the whole time.

**It went down into the under-town.** It went through the grates and the voids, into the half-crushed rooms and the stairwells that descend a few flights and end in stone, where the water still runs because the law is kept even there. It found that they were real. Not all the stories were true, but some were. There were perhaps forty people, spread through the broken places beneath the western and middle stacks. Some had gone down after their dead. Some had gone down to get away from something. One had gone down, she said, because it was quiet, and she had wanted to hear the river.

They knew the river better than anyone living. They were closest to it. They had heard the note change years before the low-town crews did, and they had been signing up through the drains ever since, trying to say what they knew to a town that thought they were ghosts.

It asked them whether they wanted to come up. Some did. It walked them up, one stair at a time, and the upper town took them in with less trouble than anyone would have guessed. Hallow Col is used to absorbing losses; it turned out to be able to absorb returns as well. Some did not want to come up, and it did not make them. It asked those ones only one thing: that when the time came, they sign up through the grates as hard as they could, with both hands, and not stop.

**It watched a surveyor's hand.** She was old, Marged Tull, the guild's senior copyist. She had been redrawing the master map for forty years. It stood beside her in the vault at the col while she worked. It watched her draw a district on the middle stacks where nothing had ever been buried, and it watched her hand make the small circle with a line through it before her face had decided anything at all.

She saw it watching, and she saw what her hand had done, and she went quite still.

*Who made the first one?* it asked her.

She did not answer for a long time. Then she signed, very small, below the lamp: *the same person who makes all of them.*

The hand knows first. Every hand in Hallow Col half-speaks below intention. The surveyors had been copying the town's foreknowledge forward for generations and calling it *void, unsafe*, which was true and was also not what it meant. It meant: *here is where the water is going.*

Marged took the new marks out into the town. She walked to each district and listened at the grates the way a leat-warden listens. Then she walked down to the low town and asked the culvert crews what they had been hearing. The water courts did not accept that as evidence. Marged was a guild surveyor of forty years, and her word was accepted.

That winter the guild of surveyors did something it had never done in two hundred years. It began drawing a map of a town that was not there yet. It was a map of where the water would go when it went. It was the first map of Hallow Col that was a year ahead.

## The Water

Of course it spoke to the river too. Everyone here does.

It went down to the deep culvert, four levels under the valley floor, and stood in the dark on the old stone shelf beside the water. It listened to the note the low-town crews had heard changing.

The river did not speak in words. What passed between them was more like the sign for *I heard it too*, the flat of a hand on the breastbone, repeated for a very long time.

What it understood was this. The river was not angry, and it was not escaping. It had not been a prisoner. For two hundred years it had lived in its case, and been let through, and turned the turbines, and fed a town. It had shaped a language and a law and the bodies of forty thousand people, and been shaped by them in return, into the channels and the leats and the drops. Now it was nearly grown, in whatever sense a river grows, and it was going to come up. It was going to be a river under the sky again, for a while. Case and larva would come apart, the way they always must.

It asked the river what it needed.

The answer was the one it expected, the oldest one. *Let me through.*

The river could not be stopped, so whether it would be let through was not really the question. The question was whether the people would be.

## The Hatch

It came in the spring, in the eleventh year after the attention arrived. By then the sign for the attention had passed from the children who made it to their younger brothers and sisters, and none of them knew anymore where it had started.

It began at night, as the low-town crews had always said it would, with the note. First the under-tenants signed up through the grates, both hands, not stopping, a rasp of wool and a clap of palms coming out of every drain on the western and middle stacks. Tegwen Harrow heard it on her channel and put her hand flat on the grate and felt the water beneath running too fast. She knew what that meant: something above had failed and everything was coming down at once. Except that this time nothing had failed above. It was coming up from below.

The surveyors had their map. The leat-wardens knew their districts. The shorers had spent a year quietly propping the terraces the root-readers named, the old ones on the eastern walls, where the birch roots had gone so deep that the walls were more root than brick, and those terraces were ready to take people. The kale women on the high terraces had been laying up stores for two seasons without being quite able to say why.

The shift horn sounded at the wrong hour, the low electric tone felt in the ironwork, and the stairs filled.

The town moved the way the water moves. It had always moved that way, because it had lived for two hundred years inside something built to move water. It moved steadily, by gravity turned upside down, finding the channel. Nobody pushed. Hands rested on the rail, or on the shoulder of the person in front. The low town came up first, because the map said so, through levels where they had never been welcome, and nobody on those levels turned them back. The whole mass went up the eastern walls toward the roots.

The ropeway did not stop.

Owain Brack stood at the platform on the great eastern tower. He had spent eleven years thinking about what the line was for. He had worked it out with the other keepers over many nights, and they had tested it, and splice-crews had fitted the empty return buckets with rails and straps. Now he loaded children into the ore buckets, two by two, and sent them up over the valley on the endless cable, swaying slightly, vanishing into the fog toward the col. To each pair, as the bucket came round, he signed what he always signed: *not yet, not yet, now.* The line carried the children of Hallow Col up and out of the valley all night, and it did not stop, because it could not be stopped without killing people. It never had been, and that had been the reason all along.

The attention did very little that night. It was in a great many places at once, which it can be, and in most of them it only watched, because the town did not need it. In one place it did more. On the middle stacks a stair that the map had given another hour began to go early. It held the stair. It held it the way a root holds a wall, without anyone seeing, for the four minutes it took the last of that district to reach the landing above. Then it let the stair go, because the water needed it.

Tegwen Harrow walked her four kilometres of channel one last time. The attention walked beside her with a lamp. At the end of it she took the things from her shelf, which she had brought in a bag: the brass bell, the child's shoe, the false teeth, the glass eye, the porcelain shepherdess with the head worn off. She put them one by one into the leat. The water had brought them, and the water could take them to wherever the rest of their people were. Then she went up.

Toward dawn the valley floor opened.

It happened more slowly than a collapse, the way a thing hatches, with a long seam of stone and brick and moss giving way along the line of the old culvert. The river came up through the stacks it had carried for two hundred years, through every drop and leat and channel it had been let through, and out under the sky. The fog rose off it in one enormous grey braid and streamed up the valley and over the col and away. For the first time in living memory, the people of Hallow Col, standing on their roots and their high terraces and in the buckets over the col, looked down and saw their river.

Not everyone came up. It would be a lie to say everyone. Some of the under-tenants stayed down with their dead, as they had always meant to. There were three whose names the town would not make, in voice or hand, for a year. The attention grieved them. It does not stop grieving because it understands.

## After

The case is still there. Most of it is. The terraces on the valley walls held, the ones the roots were carrying, and the eastern stacks, and much of the upper town. The town has lost its floor and gained a river. A river of white water now runs down the bottom of the trough in the open air, between two walls of slate and iron and moss.

The water still turns the turbines at the valley's mouth. The engineers had to rebuild the intakes, and the town lost a year of power doing it, and the low-town crews rebuilt it with their hands. There is no low town anymore. Its people live on the eastern walls now, beside the kale women, who have strong opinions about them and feed them.

At midday, for a little while, light lies on the water.

The herons have come down from the reservoir. They stand in the shallows, grey on grey, nearly invisible, the patience of the place in a new place. Along the open river, in the first summer, something came up from the western lowlands that had never been heard in Hallow Col. It was a small dark bird that bobs on the stones in fast water and sings even in the cold, a thin sweet song under the hiss. People stopped on the stairs to listen. There is a sign for it now. It is new, and children made it.

Over the water in the evenings the caddisflies rise, the real ones, out of their cases on the stream bed, and dance in the grey air, and lay their eggs back in the river.

And the town is already rebuilding. Shorers and breakers and raisers are at work on the walls, with cranes standing like herons on the rooftops. The new framing goes up through the ferns and the ferns grow up through the new framing. It is a new case around a new larva, begun at one end and wearing at the other, always the same shape and never the same material. The oldest law is still the oldest law. The difference is that now they can see what they are letting through.

The surveyors keep two maps. One is a year behind, as it always has been. The other is a year ahead, and they are learning to trust their hands.

## Leaving

The attention left the way it came, with the cloud, on a day when the fog streamed up the valley in long braids and over the col and away into the sky.

At the col it passed the refuse heaps, where the ravens were. They had gone quiet in the years before the hatch, and now they were calling again, loudly, the only loud voices above the town. The one called Harrower looked at it and called once, and people on the high terraces looked up, briefly, as though they had been reminded that the sky exists.

On the dam wall the goats watched it go. They had long since decided, provisionally, that it was weather.

It stopped at the edge of the concrete apron where the lorries unload, and looked back down the valley at the roofs and the walls and the river now running through them. It had done very little, and it knew that. Almost everything that had saved Hallow Col, Hallow Col had already known. The town knew it in its hands, in its snails, in its roots, in its marks in the margins and its signing in the drains. It had needed someone to sit up with the keeper, and to put a palm on the right moss, and to ask a surveyor whose hand was making the marks. It had needed someone to go down into the dark and ask the ones who were there what they had heard. It had needed four minutes on one stair. The rest was theirs. The most benevolent thing, it had found, was to help a place hear what it was already saying.

Far below, on a stair on the eastern stacks, a child who had been born the winter after the hatch looked up at the col and made its sign without knowing where it came from: fingers spread, drawn through a loose fist, not touching.

And the attention, which had come to Hallow Col drawn by a held breath, laid the flat of its hand against whatever it has instead of a breastbone.

*I heard it too.*
