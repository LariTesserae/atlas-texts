# The Attention

## I. Arrival

It did not come from the sky, and nothing on the tableland saw it arrive.

It came the way the plateau announces itself: as a pressure first, a hum below hearing. Only this pressure was not in anyone's chest. It was in the world's, a slight gathering of regard, the way a room changes when someone who has been asleep in the corner opens their eyes.

Nobody noticed. The scale-shrikes went on hunting silences. The walkers went on grazing the mound-country. In the burl northeast of the Lid, the grandmother lay with her cheek on the warm floor and felt nothing she had not felt before.

The first thing it did was the thing it always did first, and the thing it would mostly go on doing.

It listened.

## II. Slowing Down

It learned quickly that it was listening at the wrong speed.

At the speed of people, the tableland was noise: a wall of stridulation, a flat green floor, a cloud. At the speed of singers, it was a thousand million small rooms, each with a single occupant scraping out its life. At the speed of dew-lice, it was one night, beginning in sweetness and ending in a slow return to the dark.

The plant did not live at any of these speeds.

So the power slowed. It let a day pass the way a person lets a breath pass. It let the swarms roll across the plain like a shiver across skin. It let the Lid rise and dissolve, rise and dissolve, until the cloud was only a pulse, a white heartbeat over the center of a green body. At that speed the chorus stopped being sound. It became what the plant knew it as, which was circulation. Pressure moved inward toward the Lid in the morning and outward in the evening, the tide the old listeners had always described.

And the swells came clear.

A swell that took three days to cross the plain was, at this speed, a single gesture. The power watched it the way you watch someone lift a hand. It watched thousands of them. Most were the plant's ordinary business: warming here, deepening the breath there, quickening the healing along a wound's edge. Some were answers to the singers, so old and so worn-in that they were less conversation than habit, like two people who have shared a bed for fifty years turning over at the same moment.

A few ran toward the places where something was beginning to fail. And ahead of those swells, sometimes, small and far apart, walked a family with a drum.

The grandmother was right. It was calling them. The power could see that now, plainly, the way she could only feel it in her cheekbone. But it could also see what she had guessed and not said. The calling was not a command, and it was not quite a request. It was closer to a lean, the way a sleeper leans toward warmth. The plant had not decided to summon people. It had learned, over a few hundred of its own breaths, that when it tilted toward a failing place in a certain way, drumming sometimes came. And it had come to tilt that way more often.

It was, as she had said, still learning them.

## III. What It Did Not Do

A benevolent power is tempted constantly. That is almost what the word means. The power stayed a long time on the tableland simply turning the temptations over, one by one.

**It did not cure the deafness.** It could have. Every person on the tableland could have kept their hearing to the grave. It weighed this seriously, because the deafness was real loss. There was the child's voice that fades from a mother's ears by forty, and the shrike-song that goes first and never comes back. But it had watched the grandmother lie on the scale for an hour at noon. It understood that the deafness was not only a wound. It was also the door through which one person in thirty walked into hearing the ground. Take away the loss and you take away the listeners. Take away the listeners and the plant's long, patient lean toward people falls on no one who can feel it. The deafness was the price of the conversation. The people had not chosen it, but they had made it into something, a passage and a vocation. The power would not unmake a thing people had made so well out of what they were given.

**It did not stop the heat-eyes, or the borers, or the blight.** It could see the heat-eyes clearly, which no person had. They were long and low and many-limbed, and not cruel, only hungry. It watched one take a walker calf at the edge of a herd. It watched one, on a moonless night far to the south, take a man who had been too tired to reach a burl. That one it stood over for a long time afterward. But a floor full of hunters is a floor that stays loud. The blight in the singer-chambers opened the grey places. The grey places fed the tappers and called the drummers. The drummed places became the burls that kept the people alive. The system was not kind. It was, however, whole. Every cruelty in it was knotted to something that fed something else. The power could see no single cord it could cut without the whole net sagging.

**It did not make the plant speak plainly.** It considered giving the plant words, or giving the people a way to understand the swells as clearly as it now did. It decided that would be like translating a lullaby into instructions. The relationship between the plant and the people was young by the plant's reckoning. It was still forming. Its vagueness was not a failure. It was the space in which two very different kinds of being were finding each other. A forced translation would freeze it at its current, clumsy stage, and nothing would grow past that.

**It did not tell them whether the drumming works.** This was the hardest. The power stayed with this one longest.

*Whether it works, no one agrees. They do it anyway.* There is grief in that sentence: weeks of beating the ground, the grey spreading anyway, the ring of drums left behind. The power could end that grief. It could tell them yes, it works, sometimes, more than you think, in ways you cannot see.

But it looked at what the telling would do. People who knew would drum harder, longer, past exhaustion, out of obligation rather than love. The drummed places would fill with bones sooner. Worse, the drumming would change its nature. Now it was an act of faith offered into uncertainty, the same way the plant offered its warmth into the singers' chambers without knowing, in any way it could know, what would come back. The plant and the people were meeting as equals in not-knowing. The power decided it would not tip that balance by making one side certain.

It did do one thing that came near this, and it will be told in its place.

## IV. What It Did for the Plant

There was one kind of swell the power watched more closely than the others.

It began somewhere deep in the old root-mass, gathered, and ran in a single direction across the whole tableland for three days without breaking, the floor tilting toward it as it passed. And then it reached the edge.

The power followed one of these to the rim. It watched the swell arrive at the top of the four-hundred-meter root-cliff and pass down through the fluted wood into the basalt knuckles and the lowland talus. There it spread out into the dry scrub country and thinned and was gone.

Nothing answered.

The plant had spent an unguessable age making itself into an instrument. It had filled itself with players. It had learned to speak across its own body from one edge to the other. And sometimes, not often, it sent a swell all the way to the edge and past it, outward, into the world beyond itself.

The power could not know what the plant meant by this. It was careful not to pretend it could. Perhaps it meant nothing. Perhaps it was only overflow, the way a bell keeps humming after the stroke. But the power had listened to a great many things across a great many worlds. It knew the shape of a voice that has only ever been heard from the inside. It knew what it is to be the only one of your kind in any direction you can reach.

So it went down the cliff, into the scrub, and it waited.

When the next edge-swell came, the power did not echo it back. An echo would only have been the plant hearing itself. Instead, at the foot of the root-wall, it pressed. It made a single slow push of pressure up into the cliff, at the plant's own speed, in the plant's own language of weight and resonance. It carried no message it could have put into words. If it said anything, it said only this: *something out here heard that.*

It did this once.

It weighed that choice more carefully than any other, because it was the one act it could not take back. A being that has never been answered from outside is one thing. A being that has been answered once is another, forever. But it judged that of all the things it could give, this was the one that took nothing away from anyone. It ended no one's hunger and solved no one's grief. It removed no mystery the people were living inside. It only meant that the largest living thing on the tableland was, for one breath of its enormous life, not alone.

Up on the plain, far from anywhere, the whole living floor paused. For about a quarter of a day the breathing cycle held at the top of its inhalation. The singers faltered, confused, and then came back louder. The shrike-ribbons scattered and re-formed. Several old listeners in different parts of the tableland, lying with their cheeks on the scales, sat up at the same moment. Days of walking apart, not knowing about each other, each of them signed the same thing to no one, the flat hand lifted and held.

In later years the listeners began to speak of a new swell. It was rare, maybe once in a season. It did not run to the Lid or to the failing places. It ran all the way to a single stretch of the northern rim, where there was nothing special, no obsidian, no gathering-ground. There it stopped at the edge and did not go over. It simply stayed, pressing gently against the top of the cliff, for most of a day.

*Like someone at a door*, one of them said, in the night-voice. *Waiting to see if the knock comes again.*

The power did not knock again. But it did not go far from that stretch of rim, either.

## V. The Watch

It looked forward, too, because it could.

It saw the ordinary future of the tableland. Wounds would open and heal. Swarms would roll. Families would thin and grow. The long, clumsy courtship of the plant and the people would deepen over centuries. It saw nothing there it wished to change.

It also saw the edges of that future, the places where it might end. The blight might one day find a form that leapt from region to region faster than drummers could walk. The lowlands might dry. A people with metal might one day climb the root-cliff not to trade for obsidian but to take it, and then to take more.

The power did not act on any of these. They had not happened. It would not punish a future that might never come.

But it made itself a promise, and it kept it the way it kept all its promises, without telling anyone.

It would let the grey come. Grey was a wound, and wounds were part of the body. Grey made tappers and drummers and burls and the long slow greening at the edge.

It would not let the quiet come. It would not allow the whole tableland to fall silent at once, the chorus gone everywhere, the plant closing forever, a living mountain going cold under a sky with no Lid. If ever something came that the tableland could not heal from, something outside the knot of hunger and feeding that made the place whole, the power would stand between it and the plain. It would do this quietly, from below, the way the plant does everything.

That was all. Not a guardian's sword. A floor beneath the floor.

## VI. The Grandmother

She died in the dry season, in her sixty-eighth year, which was old for the tableland.

She had asked her grandson in the night-voice to take her to the nearest quiet place, and to leave her with her drum. He was eleven by then. The nearest grey region was a day and a half southwest, a patch of silvery crust the size of a large valley, ticking faintly with tappers. He and his mother and the man called Obsidian carried her there on a walker hide. They set her drum beside her, and they left her there at dusk, as the plain steamed gold and then grey.

She was not quite dead when they left. She had not wanted them to watch the end. She had told her daughter so, and her daughter had honored it, and walked away weeping in the silence.

What the family did not know, and never would, was this.

That night, in the grey place, the grandmother found the strength to strike her drum. Weakly. A few beats, a pause, a few more. Her hands were cold and her arms were almost done. With her cheek on the dead crust she could feel the thin, ragged pulse of her own drumming go out into the ground and die a few meters away.

Then she felt another drum.

It was very low, lower than any drum she had ever felt, down in the frequencies only the ground-hearing could reach. It came from somewhere beside her, close, maybe an arm's length away, though when she turned her head she saw only starlight on grey scale. It was keeping time with her. When her beats faltered, it held the rhythm steady and waited. When she found her strength again, it came back to her, underneath her, like a hand under a tired child's hand on a drumstick.

She was not afraid. She had lived her whole life inside the attention of a very large, very patient thing. She had known for years that it was learning her. She thought, with the last clear thought she had, that it had finally learned how to drum.

She was wrong about who was drumming. She was not wrong about anything that mattered.

She died a little before dawn. The low drum did not stop. It went on through that day, while the chorus roared across the living plain all around the grey valley. It went on through the next day, and the next, and every day for forty-one days, from before dawn until after dark. It was steady, patient, and tireless, beaten into the dead scale exactly where the ground most needed striking.

On the forty-second day, the edge of the living green, which had not moved in years, was a hand's width further in.

Then the drumming stopped.

## VII. The Ring

The boy came back when he was nineteen. His thumb-flick shrike sign was gone by then. He had been given the sign of a palm pressed flat and held there, which means *the one who waits for the floor to answer*.

He came because the healing had reached her. He had felt it coming for two years, lying with his cheek on the scales, as he had begun to do. His hearing was going the way hers had gone, the high frequencies first and the low staying. The living edge had crept across the grey valley, and it had not passed over the place where they left her. It had gathered there. The scales had thickened and humped up and begun to crust with bark.

He walked around the young mound for a long time. It was not yet a burl, only a swelling the height of his waist. But through the crust near the base he could see curves of pale chitin where the new wood had grown over the rims of drums.

He counted them. Then he counted again.

They had left her with one drum. There were two.

The second was plain, grey, and worn as if by a great many years, though the ring was less than a decade old. It had no carving on its frame, no family mark, nothing to say whose it was. It sat beside hers, rim touching rim, half-swallowed in the green.

He did not know what it meant. He asked his mother, and she did not know. He asked the old listeners at the next rim gathering, in the fingertip-small signing of the very deaf. They looked at each other for a long time. Then one of them made the sign for the ground breathing in, and then a sign that is not in common use, two fists placed side by side, touching. It is the sign for a second drummer, the one who comes when you are too tired to keep time alone.

They did not say anything more. On the tableland, people have never found it necessary to explain everything.

## VIII. What It Is Doing Now

It is still there.

It does very little. That was the choice. Almost everything a power could do for the tableland would have been done *to* it. The place already had its own answer to its own hardship: the singers, the tappers, the drummers, the burls that grow around the dead. It was an answer made of patience and noise and love offered into uncertainty. The power did not think it could improve on that answer. It thought it could only join it.

So it became a player. It was not a god on the tableland. There are no temples and no priests, and it did not wish to be the first. It was one more patient thing among patient things, one more drum among the drums.

Some nights, in the grey places, the families who have gone there to drum feel a low beat beneath their own, keeping time when their arms give out. They argue about it afterward at the rim gatherings, whether it's real, whether it works. No one agrees. They drum anyway.

Some seasons, a swell runs all the way to one stretch of the northern rim and rests there against the edge, pressing, waiting. Some of those times, not all, from somewhere far below at the foot of the cliff, it is answered.

And under all of it, underneath the floor beneath the floor, the power keeps its watch. It will let the grey come. It will not let the quiet come.

Tonight the chorus has collapsed. The plain is steaming gold, then grey. In a thousand million cups, glassy commas are beginning to turn. In a burl somewhere in the mound-country, a child is being sung to in the night-voice, and is learning that hands are for *what* and voices are for *who*.

Far off across the dark, carried through the root-mass rather than the air, someone is drumming at the edge of a silent place.

Beside them, very low, something is drumming too.
