# The Visitor at the Scarp

## Arrival

It came up the weeping cliff the way everything else does: slowly, and wet.

It could have come otherwise. It could have arrived as a light over the lake, or a pressure in the cloud, or a voice. It had done all of these in other places and had learned, over a long time, that arrival is already an act. A thing that enters a room loudly has changed the room before it says a word. Here, where a shout dies in thirty paces and the only languages are pressure and charge, it judged that a loud arrival would be a kind of violence. So it made itself small and warm and slow, about the size of a person, and climbed.

The basalt shone under its hands. The water beading on the rock was cold and tasted faintly of iron. It had come a long way to see this place, and it spent the whole first morning on the lowest ledge of the scarp, doing nothing but letting the cliff weep over it.

A benevolent power has one temptation above all others, and it is not cruelty. It is helpfulness. It is seeing a thing and immediately beginning to improve it. This one had learned to wait out that first urge, the way you wait out a flinch.

## Listening

On the plateau it knelt and put its ear to a trunk, as everyone who has ever described this place says you should.

Tick. Tick. Tick. A sigh.

Then it did something no roller or bird or beetle could do. It listened to all of it at once. It let its attention widen from the one trunk to the stand, from the stand to the tableland, until it could hear every collar on every lifter and the rings moving outward from the lake. They came one after another at walking pace, closer together as noon approached. It heard the fizz on the ridges long before the ridges began to fizz. It heard the dry rustle of the beetle lanes running in both directions. It felt the thump of a roller herd two valleys over, and somewhere far off it felt the single muffled thump that meant the pale thing had fed.

It had heard minds before, loud and bright ones that narrated themselves. This was not that. There was no narration here. There was a count. It was vast and patient and continuous, and it had gone on so long that it had worn the land into its own shape.

The visitor thought: *Oh. You are keeping track of everything.*

And then, because it was wise and not only kind, it thought the harder second thought: *Be careful. You are already telling yourself a story about it.* The people who had written about this place had called it a body and a reckoner, and those were good words. But they were words, and the thing under the fog did not use words. The visitor held the metaphors loosely. Something here was like a nervous system, and something was like a heartbeat. Whether anything here was like a *someone*, whether there was anything it was like to be the Reckoner, the visitor did not know. It found, to its own mild surprise, that it could not know even by listening this closely. It decided to act as though the answer might be yes, and to promise nothing on the strength of a guess.

## The Ledger

The visitor had come because it had noticed something from far away, and it wanted to check.

The account did not seem to balance.

Water weeps from the scarp every morning and never comes back up the cliff. The rivers are born at the base of the rock and run away across the lowlands. The lifters draw from an aquifer that lies below the lowland floor, and the center of the plateau has sunk over millennia into a great basin because that aquifer has been drawn down there more than anywhere. From a distance it looked like a slow bleed. A continent-sized life was spending its capital a little faster than it earned it, and it had been doing so for longer than mountains last.

Close up, the visitor saw the same thing. The ledger ran a deficit, and the Reckoner, which knew the aquifer the way you know your own hands, surely knew it too. That might be what the rings from the lake were counting down.

A lesser power would have acted here. There were a hundred ways to fix it. The visitor could fill the aquifer. It could slow the scarp's weeping. It could hold the cloud ceiling closed so there would never be another pair of clear days. It could give the plateau more time than it would ever need.

It did none of these yet. It went to find out where the water goes.

## Down

It followed the weeping down the cliff, and it followed the rivers.

This took a long time, and the visitor did not hurry it. It walked beside the wide calm sheets of water where they emerged from the rock. It walked through the rich lowlands, which were green in a different, careless way, full of things that sang, because down here sound carried and nothing had needed to learn silence. There were animals that knew nothing of the plateau except as a dark line on the horizon under a permanent cloud. There were fields, possibly. There were creatures who drank from the rivers every day and did not wonder where they came from.

The rivers ran to the sea. The sea breathed into the sky. The visitor watched the clouds form over the ocean and drift inland. It watched them strike other mountains far from the Highlands, ranges that had never heard of lifters, and fall as rain. It watched the rain soak into those distant slopes and sink, and sink further, into fractured stone and old sediment. Then, very slowly, over centuries, it saw that water creep back underground, beneath the lowlands, toward the deep basin under the plateau, where it seeped into the dark aquifer that the lifters' roots reached down to drink.

The loop closed. It closed through the whole world.

The visitor sat for a while on a riverbank in the loud lowlands and laughed. The sound carried a long way, and it didn't mind.

The earlier account had said *the lowlands live on its exhaust, and they have no idea.* That was true. What the account had not said was the other half: *the Reckoner lives on the lowlands' rain, and it has no idea either.* The plateau's ledger looked unbalanced because it was only half a ledger. The other half was written on distant mountains and in slow underground currents, in the whole planet's weather. The Reckoner had been keeping meticulous accounts for millions of years of an economy much larger than itself.

It was not quite balanced, even then. The basin really was sinking. The visitor did the sums, and at this rate the Highlands would last perhaps another forty million years before the aquifer gave out, the scarp crumbled, or the climate shifted and the rain stopped coming to those far ranges. Then the moss would dry, the lifters would still, and the rings would stop.

Forty million years. The visitor sat with the number. Most things it had loved had lasted far less.

## What It Considered

It went back up the cliff, and it thought.

**It considered telling the Reckoner.** It could speak in charge. It could run a pattern through the ribbons and into the knot under the lake: *your water comes back; you are not alone; the rain that saves you falls on mountains you will never see.* It weighed this for a long time. It was the thing it most wanted to do.

But it did not know what telling would *do.* If there was no one there to hear, the message would be noise, a variance, a pressure in the ear. If someone was there, the message might change something the visitor could not predict. A mind that has kept one aim so long that aim and structure are the same might not take new information as comfort. It might take it as a fault in the count. The visitor could not tell the difference in advance between giving a gift and breaking a clock. And the Reckoner had not asked, because it did not ask things. It decided that comfort offered to a being it did not understand, for a distress it could not confirm, would mostly be for itself.

**It considered extending the ledger:** topping up the aquifer, adding a million years here and ten million there. It declined this more easily. Forty million years is not a tragedy cut short. It is a life. The plateau's ending, when it came, would come from the same slow physics that had made it. To prevent that ending, the visitor would have to take over the part of the world that feeds the Highlands, and it would be keeping the ledger instead of the Reckoner. The mill with no owner would have an owner at last. That seemed to the visitor like the one thing this place had never needed.

**It considered the stalker** and the rollers that die in the afternoon fog. It left them alone. Predation here was part of the count, and the rollers had learned their stillness. They lived long lives lying flat in the moss at the smell of ozone, and the visitor was not going to sort a world into acceptable and unacceptable deaths from outside it.

**It considered the two clear days**, the one real emergency, when the whole Highlands holds its breath. Here it hesitated longest. Close a cloud, and spare a continent its terror, if terror is what it is? But the emergency was also how the plateau knew itself. It was the moment when every part moved as one. The lifters raced, the beetles ran, the rollers lay down. It had survived every such emergency for millions of years, because the ones that killed it would have ended it long ago. The visitor decided it would not remove the plateau's fear, if it was fear. It had no right to remove the thing that proved the plateau was alive.

**And it considered the lowlands.** This was the one direction where it found a real danger, one that did not come from the Highlands' own nature.

Down there, things sang, and some of the singers might one day dig. They might sink wells deep enough to reach the old aquifer. They might dam the rivers where they were born and never let the water reach the sea. They could break the far half of the loop without ever knowing the near half existed. A lowland people could kill the plateau in a few centuries and never understand why their rivers were thinning, because they had no idea.

The visitor could prevent this by force. It could also prevent it by quietly steering the lowlanders, planting fears and taboos. It rejected both. The first made it a guard over a people. The second made it a liar to them.

## What It Did

It did three things.

**First, it stayed one full cycle.** It walked the stands in the morning with the moss drinking the water from its feet. It stood between the rows at noon and felt the rings go past, and it let its own pulse drift, as the old account promised, until for a few beats it matched the plateau exactly. It did not take this as communion. It took it as courtesy, the way you fall into step with someone walking beside you.

When the ozone rose and the hair lifted on its arms, it lay down in the moss like a roller and did not move. It could have walked through the fog untouched. Nothing on the plateau could have hurt it. It lay down anyway, because that was the rule of the place at that hour, and a guest who is exempt from the rules and keeps them anyway is saying something. Maybe no one received the message. It lay still regardless. Something long and pale passed within a few meters of it, following the field, and found nothing that moved.

By dawn it was soaked through with the plateau's breath from the day before, beaded like everything else, counted like everything else. It was one small line in the ledger. It found that it liked this very much.

**Second, when it came down the scarp for the last time, it went to the lowlands and told the truth to whoever could hear it.** It did not do this as a god issuing law, or as a voice in a dream, or with any story designed to frighten. It went as a traveler who had been up there, and it said, plainly, to anyone who asked: *your rivers come from the plateau. The plateau drinks from under your feet. Be careful how deep you dig.* Some listened. Most forgot. A few wrote it down. The visitor could not make them wise and did not try. It only made sure the knowledge existed in the world where people could find it, so that if the lowlands ever did break the loop, they would at least be choosing. Their ignorance would not choose for them.

It judged this the proper limit of benevolence toward people who can think: to inform them, and then to trust them.

**Third, it made a promise, and told no one, because there was no one to tell.**

If the Highlands ever end the way they are meant to end, from their own slow physics and the planet's slow change, it will not interfere. It will come back, if it can, and lie in the moss for the last cycle, and be present when the rings stop.

But if something comes from *outside* the logic of the place, something that does not belong to its world and could not have been foreseen, it has decided it will act. That might be a stone from the sky, or a sudden poisoning, or a thoughtless hand with far more power than the lowlands will ever have. It will not act to run the mill. It will only stand between the mill and the thing that would smash it. It would do the least that works and then leave. It holds this the way it holds everything else here: loosely, and ready to be wrong.

That is all.

## Leaving

A benevolent power arrived at the Condensing Highlands, and the Highlands did not notice. They registered a small warm variance, a dry pocket in a wet world. It moved through the morning, held still through the afternoon, and at dawn was soaked and indistinguishable from the moss. Then it was gone. The ledger noted the water it carried away and let it go, the way it lets go of everything that weeps from the cliffs.

And somewhere, a long time later, that water fell as rain on a distant range and began to sink. It was on its way back, too slowly for anyone to watch. The visitor had seen that the world already did the thing it had been tempted to do. The world was already refilling the plateau, and had been for millions of years. It did so without being asked and without being thanked, through a loop too large for any one being in it to see.

The visitor had not given the Reckoner that return. It had only been able to see it. And seeing that the world already took care of this place, it chose to leave the care where it was.

The rings still start at the lake and move outward, one every few minutes, closer together toward noon. The mill still has no owner. It now has one friend, far away, who checks in every few thousand years, lies down in the moss when the ozone rises, and does not move.
