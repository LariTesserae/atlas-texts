# The Attention

## I. How It Came

A power that is wise does not arrive quickly. There is no rule against it. It simply knows that most harm is done in arriving.

This one had been looking at the Wicker for some time before it understood what it was looking at. From wherever such powers stand, the Wicker first appeared as a faint warmth, a place where something was being held without being gripped. That is rare. Most things that hold, grip. The power had seen a great many worlds held together by grip: by law, by fear, by gravity pulled so tight that stars collapsed under it. It had spent a long time easing such places, and it was tired in the way only the benevolent get tired, from the endless work of holding things together.

So it turned toward the warmth, and reached, and found it could not touch it.

There is no outside to the Wicker. Every outer surface is the inner surface of something larger. The power's attention slid along that curve the way a hand slides along a bowl, always inward, never through. It could not look down on the Wicker. It could not set a finger on it from above. There was no above.

This did not offend it. A lesser power would have been offended. This one felt something close to delight, the delight of a craftsman who picks up a joint and cannot find the seam.

*The only way to go anywhere is the made way.*

So it let go of its size. It had to. Distance there follows the weave and not the air, and a thing the size of a sky cannot walk a strand. It set down its reach and its overview, and the many hands it usually worked with, and came in the only way anyone comes: on foot, on a strand as wide as a road, in the warm wet air, breathing a little hard.

It had not breathed hard in a very long time. It noticed that.

## II. What It Wanted to Do

It walked for days before it met anyone, and in those days it made a list. That is what benevolent powers do when they come upon a place: they make a list of what they could give it.

It could give the Wicker light. A sun, or the memory of one, so that the green-gold glow had a source and the shadows had edges.

It could give answers to all the questions that had no fixed answer. What holds it up. What lies below. Whether the hollow-whales are woven. Whether there is one of her or many.

It could open the lowland and show the makers what the warm dark was breathing from.

It could catch the fallers sooner, so no one had to fall for a very long time before being caught.

It could give the makers back their youth, or at least the memory of it.

It could give her a voice that ran forward, so her words did not unravel before anyone heard them.

And it could do the largest thing, the thing its benevolence strained toward like a tendril across a gap. It could make it so that no knot was ever again tied in terror. It could still the frightened hands and cool the angry ones, so that nothing was ever pulled too tight and nothing ever girdled a living stem.

It walked with this list for a long time. Walking the made way is slow, and slowness is where wisdom does its work. Somewhere along a strand in the open weave, with nothing solid in sight in any direction, it began to see what each gift would cost.

Light would give the shadows edges, and the blue fungal towns would vanish in the glare.

The answers would fix what was meant to stay open. The Wicker is not uncertain because no one has worked it out. It is uncertain the way a hollow is empty: on purpose, so there is room.

The lowland, opened, would stop being the thing she faces. Her sky would become somewhere a person could go.

Catching the fallers sooner would shorten the long fall, and the long fall is how the Wicker carries them to the far places where their own knots are waiting.

The makers do not remember being young because they came from the part they are tying. To give them a childhood would be to give them a somewhere else.

And the largest gift, the ending of frightened knots, was the worst of all. The power stood still on the strand when it understood this.

Without tight knots she would have nothing to take apart. She would have no angry cord to unlay and let fall, clean, into the weaverbirds' nests. No traveler would ever lay a hand on a pale healed seam and feel forgiven for something before they did it. The power would have made a world with no harm in it. It would also have taken from that world the one thing the Wicker does better than any place it had ever seen, which is what happens after harm.

Every gift on its list was an *over*: a strand laid across, a loop pulled tight, a hand that adds. The Wicker already had hands that add. It was made of them.

The power put the list away. It did not tear it up. It is not dramatic. It unlaid it, you might say, and let it fall.

## III. The Splice

On one of the old knots, in a gully blue with fungal light, it found a maker splicing a fraying cord and asked to be shown.

The maker showed it. Unlay the ends. Marry the strands. Tuck each under its neighbor. Roll it tight against the thigh.

The power was bad at it, far worse than it expected. It had spent so long making things obey that it no longer knew how to ask anything of a material. Its fingers pulled as if the cord ought to want what it wanted. The second loop went on hard. So did the third. Its tuck was so tight it squeaked.

The maker watched without comment. When the power reached for the last tuck, the maker put one broad flat thumb over its hand. The touch was very light. *That one's for the other hand.*

The power held still. It wanted, with an intensity that surprised it, to finish the knot. It had never in its existence left something undone that it could have done. That was, it had believed, what goodness was: to see the need and meet it, all the way, to the last tuck.

It left the tail hanging, a small beckoning finger pointed down.

This was the first thing it did in the Wicker, and it was the hardest.

The maker nodded and said it would hold.

## IV. The Seams

It walked on. It found its own knots, as everyone does. They were old and half-swallowed by bark, with the too-hard second loop it had learned in the gully and, it now saw, had always had. It had tied them long before. It had been working on it.

Then it began to find the seams.

It found pale healed places where its cord had once bitten too deep, and the stem above was green and thick. There were more of them than it could count. It did not find them only in the Wicker. Standing with its hand on one, it felt the seam run out through the bark and along the strand and outward, the way everything runs inward here, into the other places it had worked: the worlds it had held together, the collapsing stars it had caught, the peoples it had rescued with a grip so fierce it left marks.

Someone had been easing those, and had always been easing them. A finger's width here and there, from below, where it had never thought to look. The good it had done had survived because something was making room in it.

For a long time it sat on the moss beside that seam. Water dripped from the knot. Cratch clicked faintly in the wood. It had come here to help. It was beginning to understand that it had come here to be told something.

## V. The Underside

If you lie down on the moss beside it and hang your head over the edge, you will see the underside of the strand, dark and amber, with the long rows of trembling water-beads along the lashings.

The power lay down. It hung its head over.

It saw a row of hanging beads that failed to fall. One of them, very slowly, rose back into the bark as if the bark had drawn a breath.

Then it saw her, turned toward a knot, unhurried. It was the knot it had tied in the gully, still with its hanging tail.

She took the tuck between her reversed thumb and finger and drew it back, and then kept going. The knot was a hard one. She eased the third loop and then the second, reading them backward, and as each one opened the power felt the memory go out of it. The hurry left, then the sense of obligation, then the long exhausted conviction that it was responsible for holding everything together. What stayed was the holding itself.

She laid the strands back along the stem, a finger's width looser than it had made them, and left them there, good enough to live.

Then she looked at it once.

It was not an unkind look. It was the look of someone reading something written, finding it about as good as they expected, and knowing what to do with it. The power had been looked at by a great many beings, mostly with hope or fear or need. It had never been looked at like this, as work that was ordinary and fine and able to be mended.

Something in it that had been holding very tight for a very long time was allowed to let go.

## VI. What It Did, If Anything

There was one more temptation, and it came in that moment, while she was looking. It was the tenderest one, and so the hardest to see through.

*She is never looked at.* The makers look along their strands. Travelers look at the tops. She faces the warm dark alone, easing the whole world from below, and no one eases her. Surely a benevolent power could at least do that. It could stay. It could go down to the underside and stand beside her. It could be to her what she is to everyone.

It considered this honestly, because it was not a cruel thing to want.

Then it looked again at what it had already been shown. The makers leave the last tuck undone every time, a small beckoning finger pointed down. They are not only leaving her work. They are leaving her a place. Every knot in the Wicker, all the ones there are more of than leaves, ends by reaching toward her. The over and the under are not a crowd and a lonely one. They are two hands on the same basket. You cannot weave with one. No one is missing.

She did not need to be seen. She had just been seen, once, and she had done the seeing back, and that was the right amount.

So in the end the power did very little, and I think it knew that doing little was the gift.

It tied knots. It stayed in the Wicker a long while, walking the made way, splicing what frayed, extending where tendrils reached across the gaps. It tied the way everyone ties, a little too hard on the second loop, and it did not try to fix that. It left the last tuck undone every time. It ate fiddleheads and drank from the bladder-vines. It learned to sleep lashed, a few loops around the waist and one around the ankle, lying along the top of a strand in the amber of the breathing, indistinguishable from a knot.

It did not light the dimness. It did not answer anything. It did not look into the lowland, though it could have. That belonged to her.

And it did not go to the old knot-mass to finish, because it was not finished. When it left, it left the way it had come, by walking, and after a very long walk it found that the strand it was on had curved back into its own vast work, the worlds it tends. It found it had carried the Wicker's slowness with it. It held things more loosely now. It left the last tuck of its help undone and trusted something from below to take it.

The places it tends grew hollows after that: small ones at first, then larger. There were gaps where it had once packed everything solid with care, and weather formed in them, and rain fell, and things it had never planned for rose in slow spirals through the new room and lived.

## VII. The Creak

Very faintly, in the Wicker, there was a long low creak of the whole structure shifting in its hang. It was the sound a basket makes when you set something heavy in it, stretched across hours.

The makers heard it and did not look up from their work.

A power had been set down in the weave, a heavy thing that had carried the weight of a great many worlds, and the Wicker took it. It did not strain. It gave a little where it had to, because someone below had left exactly enough slack.

Then the world settled a fraction deeper into its own weave, and held.
