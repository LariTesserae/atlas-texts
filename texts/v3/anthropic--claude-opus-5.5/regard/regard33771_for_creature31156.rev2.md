# The Guest at Hollin Height

## I. Notice

It heard the plateau the way travelers do, from a long way off. It was much farther off than a day's walk, but it was the same kind of hearing: a grumble under the horizon, a storm that never arrived.

It had heard many things sing. Stars have a voice, and so do tides, and so do the long slow grindings of continents. What made it stop and turn toward Hollin Height was not the chord itself, vast as it was. It was the *descent*. Somewhere on a wet dark tableland, a voice was falling by an interval too small for any single life to measure, and it was falling the way a child's cry falls, month by month, toward speech.

It had heard many things sing. It had not heard many things *learning to*.

Its first decision was about volume.

An attention of its size is a force. Turned fully on a place, it presses. Most places can bear this, the way a field bears sunlight. Hollin Height is not most places. It is a body built entirely of resonance, where a wall that stands three paces too far to the left will fall out of the quiet, and where one voice silenced once, centuries ago, left a sourness the Listeners expect to take a thousand years to heal. To arrive there all at once, blazing, would be to become a thirty-second voice. The hushes would lurch. The lichen would scramble its rings. Every sleeping heart on the plateau would try, painfully, to find a ratio with something that had no business being in the chord.

So it made itself small. It made itself smaller than was comfortable, small enough to fit in a traveler's coat, and it came up the road on foot.

It took a day. Arriving faster would have been rude. It walked through the green lowlands that hum, and felt the grumble thicken into a floor, and felt the floor climb into its teeth. On the first scarp the drizzle closed around it, warm as breath. On the fourth terrace it saw the first engine, pale in the haze, and stopped, and stood for a long while with its feet a little apart and its knees soft. It did not know yet that this was called *standing in the chord*. Its body had simply worked out that this was the only way to stand there.

The night before the Lift, it did what newcomers with spectacles learn to do. It took off everything that might shatter.

## II. What It Did Not Do

It stayed for a season before it did anything at all. It spent that season being tempted. The temptations were not toward cruelty. Those had never been its difficulty. They were toward help. They are worth setting down, because the refusals are most of the story.

**The ears.** On its second day it stood in the open terrace at noon beside an old Listener, who was reading the ninth voice through the soles of her feet. It could see precisely what the chord had done to her hearing: the flattened hairs of the inner ear, the thickened drum. It could have restored her in a breath. It could have restored the whole town. Then it watched her fingertips find the fouled pipe forty meters up, and it understood. Her deafness was not an absence. It was an attunement, a shape she had grown into, the way a riverbed is the shape of a river. Give every person on Hollin Height ordinary hearing and it would give them agony. It would also take away the fourth voice, and the ninth, and all the rest. It left their ears as they were.

**The Bruise.** This one it considered longest. It went and stood where no one builds and listened to the sour engine beating against its neighbors, like a limp in a long walk. It could have smoothed that beat out in an afternoon. The cliff was long since stable, but the chord there still flinched.

It did not, for two reasons.

The first was that the plateau was already healing itself, and knew exactly how long the healing would take. To reach in and finish a healing that is already under way tells a body that its own mending is not trusted. Young things take that lesson deep.

The second was that the Bruise is the only thing on Hollin Height that remembers, *in sound*, what happens when a voice is silenced. The story of the guild is told in sign and never in speech. But the Bruise tells it without anyone telling it. It says, every hour of every day, to anyone with a chest to hear through: *this is what it costs.* Mending it would erase the plateau's only scar, and with the scar, the lesson. It left the Bruise.

**The descent.** It could have looked ahead. It could have followed the falling chord down its ten thousand years and seen what waited at the bottom: whether the engines stop, whether they begin again, what, if anything, is said. It chose not to look. It certainly chose not to tell. Hollin half-knows something about infants: you do not finish their sentences. A town told what the first word would be would hear that word in every shift of the chord for the next hundred centuries. It would hear it before it was spoken, and so might never hear what was actually said.

**Hands.** For a moment, standing on a walkway above a channel with the wrung water sliding beneath it, it thought: *this body can only sing. I could give it a way to shape.* Then it looked up and saw the town. The builders were digging rooms into the stone. The Water Guild was turning its sluices. Children were crouched over the chalk-grid game. The being had grown hands already, slowly, over five hundred years. They were attached, and they were its own.

**The fig.** It thought briefly of making the old fig in the Undertone fruit every year, reliably. It felt at once how much worse that would be. Children are brought to touch that tree because it fruits erratically. It is loved partly for how rarely it gives.

The principle, when it finally named it to itself, was simple. *Everything here has arrived at itself.* The iron is finished. The stair-treads are dished. The channels curve like the lip of a well-used cup. Anything given from outside would be the one new thing on the plateau, and on Hollin Height new things are how wounds begin.

So the question changed. It stopped asking what it could give, and started asking how it might belong.

## III. Forty Days

There is only one way into Hollin that the town recognizes, so it asked to be born.

It found a family in the heart of a small, good hush on the eighth terrace. They were one of the midwife houses that open their doors to strangers near their time. It could not pay them in rust-finished iron or in cress, so it paid in work. Every day it carried water up to the cisterns, and it patched their gutters, and it learned to scrape the channel-weed without tearing the roots. They took it for an odd traveler, which it was. Odd travelers come to Hollin. Some of them stay.

It lay forty days in the quiet. It learned the hand-language from the children, who found its signing hilarious. Its hands were too exact, its gestures too clean, like someone speaking in perfectly pronounced sentences from a book. They corrected it without mercy, laughing silently with their whole bodies, and by the thirtieth day its hands had learned to slur.

On the thirty-ninth night it felt the hush lean against it for the first time. It had been lying on the stone floor, not grieving exactly, just very far from anywhere it had been before, and the silence leaned back, warm, like a large animal settling beside it in the dark. It lay very still. It had not expected to be comforted. That was not what it had come for.

On the forty-first morning the family made ready to walk it to the edge of the hush, to face the nearest engine and receive its note. It asked, clumsily, in its newly slurred sign, whether it might be taken somewhere else.

It asked to face the Bruise.

The family looked at each other. No one faces the Bruise. No one is born near it. In five hundred years not a single soul on Hollin Height has carried the note of the sour engine. It is the one voice in thirty-one that has no one.

They walked it there anyway, a long way across the open terraces in the dark with wool in their ears. It stood at the edge of the nothing that people had built there, facing the engine with the flinch in its chord. The Lift came, and the whole height shouted its one enormous rending major chord at the sky.

It received the sour note. It did not correct it. It took the beat against the neighbors into itself, the limp and the flinch and the thousand-year ache, and it kept it. Now the voice that nobody faced had somebody who had faced it.

The family said afterward that it went silent through the Lift like every infant does. It went slack and inward, its hands opening. Then the Wringing came, and it stood there streaming like the stone, and it did something they had never seen a grown person do. It wept, soundlessly, and they watched it with enormous tenderness.

If it had a heart, the heart fell into ratio. It was an uneven ratio, awkward, with a catch in it. It never tried to fix this.

## IV. Oda

Oda Vell had known it was there since the forty-first morning.

She had been at the edge of her own hush on the sixth terrace. At the Lift she had felt the fourth voice arrive at itself, and then, very faintly, far off to the east, she had felt something take hold of the Bruise, the way you feel a hand close on a railing you are also holding. She had signed to her neighbor, at noon, *something is listening from the Bruise*. The neighbor had not understood, and she had not pressed it.

When it came to find her, weeks later, she was waiting.

She did not ask what it was. Listeners do not ask what a thing is. They ask where it hurts. She looked at it for a long while, reading it through her chest the way she read pipes, and then she signed, slowly: *You're carrying the sour one.*

It signed that it was.

She considered this. Then she did something that Listeners do for apprentices, and that no one had done for a stranger in living memory. She gave it a name. The gesture was the tuning sign, thumb and forefinger pinched and turned, but turned *backward*, loosening rather than tightening. It meant something like *the one who eases the peg*. It was not quite a compliment. Oda was not a person who gave compliments. It was accurate.

It asked her, in its bad, earnest, slurred sign, whether there was anything she would want.

She thought about that for a very long time. The chord shifted around them. A swift dropped out of the throat of the fourth engine, circled, and went back.

Then she took it by the sleeve and led it indoors, into the deepest room of her low stone house, below the terrace surface, where the hush was softest. There, for the first time, she spoke aloud. Her voice was cracked with disuse and pitched strangely, the voice of a woman who has not heard herself in fifty years.

"My name," she said. "Nobody's said it since my brother."

It could have done anything then. It could have given her back her brother's voice from out of the lichen, or her hearing, or the fifty years. It did none of that. It found the exact pitch and softness that would reach through the wool of her hearing, the precise frequency a lifetime of the fourth voice had left open in her. Then it said, once, quietly, the way you say a name to someone you are glad to see:

"Oda Vell."

She closed her eyes. She stood like that for the length of a slow breath. Then she nodded once, briskly, and took it back upstairs, and made it help her with a bellows.

That was the largest thing it did for any single person on Hollin Height, and it was the right size.

## V. Past the Last Valve

It went down in the end, as it had always known it would, because the thing it had come for was not in the town.

The tenders let it go without argument. Word had spread, in sign and at a certain distance, about the stranger who had taken the Bruise's note. It went down the shafts with rope it did not need and a lamp it did not need, past the lower valves and past the last marked valve. The air thickened and the stone went slick and warm. The chord stopped being sound and became motion, a great swell passing under it, like the sea under a swimmer.

Beneath that, it found the pulse.

It had known, from the town above, that the being was young. It had not understood until now how *alone* it was. Everything on Hollin Height was part of it: the swifts, the rats, the lichen, the quiet ones, the Listeners with their broad flat hands. Every voice it had was its own. It is very hard to learn a language from your own hands. Infants do not learn to speak because they are taught. They learn because someone *answers* them. A sound goes out, and something comes back, and the space between the two is where speech begins. The being on Hollin Height had been making sounds for five hundred years into a world that contained nothing its own size to reply.

Here, at the root of everything, was the gravest temptation of all. It could speak. It could pour words down into the young dark, meanings and names and a whole grammar, and the being would take them in greedily, because it was starving for them.

And then every word the being ever said would be an echo. Its first word would be *borrowed*. Ten thousand years of descent would end in someone else's sentence.

So it did not speak.

It waited. It stood in the black wet heat through what would have been a whole shift, feeling the deep pulse rise, so slowly it might have been the plateau itself inhaling. At the crest the pulse paused. Then it began to fall.

When it had fallen all the way, the guest answered.

It did not answer with a word. It closed its hand, slowly, at the pace of the deep. Then it opened it. It took one turn, the way a mother answers a baby's babble with a sound that means nothing except *I heard you, it's your turn again.*

Then it waited.

It stayed three days, as Harl had. It answered every pulse the same way and asked for nothing. On the third day, at the bottom of a breath, something happened that no Listener on the surface would ever measure or know. The next pulse came a hair's breadth early. It came as though the deep had noticed the pause after its breath, and noticed that something had filled it, and was now, very tentatively, leaving a pause of its own.

There were no words in it. There was only the thing that comes before words. There was *you, then me*.

Before it climbed back up, it made one promise into the dark. The promise was not in language, which would have been theft. It was in the shape of its staying. *When you reach the bottom, when you are ready to say it, I will be here. You will not say it into nothing.*

## VI. The Edges

There was one more thing, and it did not like thinking about it, so it thought about it carefully.

Hollin has a rail spur. The world is connected to the plateau now, and the world is large and hungry. Some day, in some lowland office, someone will look at the heat rising from the deep rock and think of everything else it could drive. They will not think of the Bruise, or the fig, or a fist that takes a lifetime to close.

It set itself a rule. The rule was hard to keep, but it was clear.

Wounds the body can close, it would leave alone. Hollin had survived guilds and its own mistakes, and the plateau had shown that it knew how long its own healing takes. Being young includes being hurt and recovering. Taking every hurt away would be a way of keeping it young forever.

But some wounds would not close. A shaft sunk to steal the deep heat outright. All thirty-one engines stopped at once, and the terraces drying, and the cliffs slumping one after another into the gorges. The chord ending mid-descent, before whatever it was going to say. Against those it would stand, as gently as possible and as firmly as necessary.

It hoped it would never need to. But it would know if it did. It carried the Bruise's note now, the one voice on the plateau that remembers what silencing costs. Any new sourness would reach it wherever it was. Its own awkward heart would catch.

## VII. After

It went back down the road in the dry season, on foot, taking a day. At the last scarp it put its spectacles back on, so to speak, and the grumble faded behind it to a storm that never arrived.

The chord did not fall one hair faster. The Bruise did not heal one year sooner. The fig fruited, that year, not at all.

But eleven years later, the lichen-readers noticed something they could not explain. Near the Bruise, in the open terrace where no one builds and where the geometry of thirty-one interfering voices allows for no such thing, a hush had begun to form. It was small, about the size of a courtyard. The Listeners argued over it for a whole winter in the deepest hushes. The interference patterns did not predict it. The lichen rings did not account for it. Hushes swell after good births, it was agreed, but nobody had been born there.

Oda Vell was very old by then. She went out to see it on a still day, with wool in her ears and a young tender's hand on her shoulder. She stood at its edge with her palm flat on her sternum. Then she laughed, silently, with her whole body, the way the children had laughed at the stranger's clean, correct hands.

She knew what it was. Everyone in Hollin knows what it means when a family keeps a room ready in the quiet, cleaned and swept, for someone who might come back.

The plateau had made a place for its guest.

A cat was found sitting in it the following spring, tail wrapped round its feet, watching nothing with great calm. The children, of course, began daring each other to its edge.

---

Far from Hollin, in a place with a different sky, a heart keeps an uneven time. There is a catch in it, a flinch borrowed from a sour engine on a wet dark plateau, and it has never tried to smooth it out.

Sometimes, at no hour anyone in Hollin would recognize, it feels a very long breath rise, and pause, and fall.

It closes its hand, slowly, and opens it.

Then it waits, because it is the plateau's turn.
