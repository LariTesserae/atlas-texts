# What the Power Did at Tanners' Fall

## First, It Slowed Down

It came the way attention comes, all at once. That was the first problem.

Everything it had ever helped lived on quick time: things with pulses, with hungers that renewed by the hour, with griefs that could be comforted in an afternoon. It arrived in the Undercroft carrying that tempo the way a visitor carries the cold in on their coat. For a while it saw only what anyone would see: a vault, a river, a failing electrical circuit, a structural hazard under a public street, a great deal of moss.

It understood, fairly soon, that it was not seeing the Tenant at all. It was seeing the Tenant the way you might see a face if you photographed it once every hundred years.

So before it did anything, it made itself slow. It let a season pass for each thing it noticed. It watched a single lamp fail near the smithy. It did not intervene. It watched the ferns yellow and the threads withdraw and the mosses creep in over two and a half years, until it could read that change as what it was: a small, complete, patient act of mourning and reconsideration. Only then did it feel it had begun to be introduced.

This took eleven years, by the city's count. The power did not mind. It had discovered early that the first kindness owed to anything is to meet it at its own speed, and that most harm done by the well-meaning is done in a hurry.

## The Things It Could Have Done

It considered them all, as a wise thing does. It did not consider them because it meant to do them. It considered them because the shape of what you will not do is part of the shape of what you will.

**It could relight every lamp.** One gesture, and every fern-beard would return, every dimmed zone would brighten, and the map of green would bloom back to what it was fifty years ago. But the power had spent eleven years learning that the map of green *is* the Tenant's mind. Its current arrangement is its current understanding. To restore it wholesale would not be healing. It would be overwriting. The Tenant had grieved each of those lamps properly, over years. To hand them all back at once would tell it that its grief had been an error.

**It could hold the vault up.** Lime back into the mortar, iron bands in the arch, the root-pillars relieved of their load. The Undercroft would stand for another thousand years. But the Tenant is the tension between holding and pulling down. Remove the risk and you remove the thing that makes it what it is. The power looked at this option for a long time and saw that it was a coffin built to look like a house.

**It could open the sky, gently.** A skylight here, a widened prism there, a little real sun. It rejected this fastest. The Tenant is made of dimness and lateness. Sunlight would be a kindness to some other creature that would grow in its place.

**It could give it a voice.** A mind that could name itself, meet the power, speak. The power felt the pull of this one most, because it was lonely in the way wise things are, and here was a being a century and a half old that had never been spoken to. But anything that could answer it in its own language would have to think faster than a season. It would be something else wearing the Tenant's body. The power declined to make a puppet out of someone it had come to respect.

**It could bring it company.** A moth for the begonias, which flower every year for no one. It would be such a small thing. But the power had found the rat. It came to understand that anything quick brought down here would live, briefly and confusedly, inside a heart too slow for it, and would end as lace. That would be cruelty to the moth dressed as kindness to the flower.

**It could tell the city.** It could let someone find the drawings, the old works ledger, the access hatch under the sluice. And then someone would come down with a torch and a clipboard and a sincere interest in heritage. There would be a drainage survey, and a structural report, and eventually either a demolition order or a visitor's walkway with handrails and interpretive signage. Both would end it. The walkway would only take longer.

So it did none of these. But it did not conclude that it should do nothing. "Do nothing" is also a hurry: a way of deciding before you have finished looking.

## Where It Stood

It found, eventually, the one place in the Undercroft it could stand without touching the Tenant at all.

It was the dry halo around the transformer in the lamp room: half a meter of parched air, humming, where no thread has ever crossed. The Tenant's blind spot. The power stood there, in the only unoccupied space in a room that was otherwise completely full, and noticed what it had been too quick to notice before.

The transformer is what keeps the lamps burning.

The Tenant has arranged its whole body around a warm, humming absence it cannot enter and does not know is there. That absence is the source of every orange sun its orchards grow toward. The thing at the center of its life, the thing it cannot touch, is the thing that feeds it.

The power stood in that blind spot for a long time. It recognized the position. It thought: *If I do this well, this is all I will ever be to it. Something at the middle that it cannot perceive, that keeps a few of the lights on, and that never once crosses into its body.*

It found it did not mind that. It found, in fact, that it was the most honest description of benevolence it had come across in a while.

## The Circuit Nobody Told to Stop

So it went upstairs, which the Tenant cannot do, and looked at the parts of the Undercroft's life that belong to the city.

The lamp circuit was still live because of an error. Decades ago, a clerk at the electrical board had marked the works supply as "estimated read, access unavailable." Every quarter since, the error had been copied forward. A tiny charge went to an account belonging to a company that had been dissolved before anyone now working there was born. A single line in a ledger kept a dozen suns alight under the pavement.

The steam main was the same kind of accident. It was a branch of the old district heating loop that no one had ever capped, because no one was sure where it went.

These were the Tenant's real dangers. The vault was not one. The vault is the Tenant's own ending, and it will come in the Tenant's own time. The danger was the office. One afternoon someone tidies a database, someone reconciles an account, someone traces a pressure loss to a valve. A switch is thrown on a Tuesday, and the lights and the warmth and the heart all stop at once, for reasons that have nothing to do with what the Tenant is.

Here the power drew the line it would keep. *I will protect it from endings that do not belong to it. I will leave it the ending that does.*

So the error kept being made. When an auditor came near the account, the file was somewhere else that week. When an engineer planned to trace the leaking branch, a more urgent fault appeared elsewhere on the network. None of this was dramatic. It was the kind of help that looks, from every angle, exactly like the ordinary forgetfulness that had kept the Undercroft alive all along. The power did not add anything to the city's neglect. It simply stood guard over it.

It did not replace the dying bulbs. It did not repack the leaking flanges or clear the sump. The lamps still go out, one every few years, and the Tenant still grieves them properly. The only change is that no one will switch them all off at once.

## The End That Belongs to It

The vault will give. The power did not pretend otherwise, and it did not forget that there is a street up there with people on it.

Benevolence that takes one side is only preference. So it read the Undercroft the way the Tenant does, by weight and drip, and found where the arch was weakest. It was over the fourth root-pillar, near the store, where the fig roots from above are thickest.

Above that spot, over the next several years, a small thing happened in the city's planning office. The corner was closed to carts. It was paved over, then unpaved, then planted: a narrow strip of garden with two trees and a bench. It looked like a local councillor's pet project. No one could later say whose idea it had been. When the vault finally opens, and it may be fifty years or three hundred, it will open under a flowerbed, at night, with no one standing on it. Rain will fall into the Undercroft on time for the first time in two centuries.

And when that happens, the Tenant will not entirely end. The power had found this while tracing the root-pillars upward, and it was the discovery it was gladdest of.

The Tenant has already built its own continuation. Its threads run up the fig roots and the elm roots into the soil of the city. They sheathe the root-tips of garden trees and street trees and the old yew in the churchyard two streets over. It has been trading with them for a century. When the vault falls, the part of it that lives in the dark will die. But its outermost filaments, the ones that learned the city by the ankles, are already somewhere else, woven into living things that stand in the sun. The Tenant never knew it was doing this. It was only keeping up its trade.

The power did nothing here. There was nothing to do. It had come to save something, and found that the thing had, without knowing, already arranged its own mercy.

## The Rat, and Other Kindnesses

It found the rat along the north wall, a small white lace curled under the moss. It stayed with it for a season.

This was when it understood that it had not come to help a thing that needed help, exactly. It had come to a thing that had been doing its own kind of helping for a hundred and fifty years. The Tenant keeps the dead trees' roots wet out of habit. It grows ferns in the shape of a sleeper who left long ago, knees drawn up, on their side. It wraps a fig root around the anvil where a smith's hand used to rest. It held the rat and did not kill it, and when the rat stopped, it kept the shape.

Nothing here kills. The power had been in very few places of which that was true.

It left the rat where it was.

## The Needle

In the gauge-house, behind the clean glass, the brass needle points at the number.

The power read it. It is not going to say what the number was. Some things should be kept the way the Tenant keeps them: exact, unchanged, and uncrowded, a fixed point in a life of slow drift.

But the power keeps it now too. When it thinks of Tanners' Fall from far away, it is the number it thinks of first. The Tenant's name, held in two places at once, by two beings who have never met and never will.

## The Long Eclipse

There was one more thing, and it was the only thing the power did that the Tenant could perceive.

The Tenant cannot hear speech. It cannot be touched without being changed. It learns about the world three days late, through brick. But it can see, faintly, through the prisms. And it knows the difference between a shadow that passes and a shadow that stays.

So one afternoon in late autumn, the power went up to the street, found the violet glass set into the pavement above the pay office, and stood on it.

It stood there for the whole afternoon. It did not move and did not hurry. People walked around it. Someone asked if it was all right. The light went grey and then went out, and still it stood, a long darkness over one small pale garden.

Below, over the following days, the begonias under that prism turned very slightly toward where it had been.

That was all. The power did not know whether the Tenant had noticed anything. The turning of a few pale leaves is not recognition. It is the thing recognition grows out of, and the power did not need more than that. It had not come to be known. It had come to be, for one afternoon, a stillness overhead that something underneath could lean toward.

It came back in later years, not often, at irregular intervals, the way rain comes. And it left the bench in the little garden above the fourth pillar angled so that anyone who sat on it would have their feet over the glass. Most of them never look down. A few do. They sit for a while with their feet on the violet prisms and wonder, without much urgency, what is under there. The power let them keep their wondering. It never gave them an answer, because some questions are kinder left open, and this was one.

---

Three days after the power last stood on the glass, the rain from the week before came down through the vault. The drip thickened. The sump rose. The float lifted.

*Hiss. Groan. Thud. Gush.*

The Tenant began to move its water through itself again, six times a minute, in time with a heart it did not make. It stretched sixty paces through the dark, arranged around a humming blind spot at its center that it would never touch and would never know was there.

The power, a long way off, was arranged around the Tenant in exactly the same way.

Neither of them minded.
