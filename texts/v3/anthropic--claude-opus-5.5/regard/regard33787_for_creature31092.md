# What the Attention Did in Sessile

## It Did Not Come Down the Ramp

It did not need the crack in the plateau. It arrived the way attention arrives: all at once, everywhere, without a direction.

For a long moment it held the whole of Sessile in view the way you might hold a stone in your palm. It saw the selenite roof, its seams and its failures, the exact weight of each pane that had loosened and would fall in the next hour. It saw the four kilometers of pale city, the drifts moving across the districts, the cisterns with their finger's depth of water. It saw the millions of masons, each with its hammerstone, each stone with its polished face. It saw the chalk lice, the salamanders with their faint gray organs, the swifts asleep in the chimneys. It saw the moth larvae tunneling through the flour, eating something so small that even it had to look twice.

It counted four hundred and sixty-one people. Most were moving. Some were sleeping. Beside every sleeper sat a watcher with a palm on a shoulder.

It had seen a great many worlds, and it had learned, slowly and at some cost, that the first thing to do on arriving somewhere is nothing.

So it did nothing, and it looked.

## The List

It could not help making the list. A power like that always makes the list. It is the part of benevolence that comes before wisdom, and it has to be gotten through.

*It could mend the roof.* It could knit every seam, thicken every pane, and end the falling. The light of Sessile would hold one shape forever.

It turned this over and saw what would follow. No raw gold patches would open, so the flash-language would have nothing to catch. The swifts would have no falling panes to follow, and no disturbed insects to take in their wake. With no new dust, the masons would finish. They would dig out the last street and raise the last wall. Then, for the first time in their history, they would stop. It did not know what a mason does when the work is done. It suspected no mason had ever known, and it suspected the answer was *die*. The city's turmoil was not a wound in the city. It was the city.

*It could give them sound.* It could thin the dust or change the air so that a voice carried. And then a people who had moved their whole language into their hands, who said *I love you* by rolling a sleeper a quarter turn, would find that the world had changed its mind about them. It would be a gift that said: *you have been living wrong.* They had not been living wrong.

*It could tell them who the builders were.* This one it held longest, because it wanted to, and because it discovered, looking, that it did not know. The first builders were older than its attention to this place. The ripples in the walls were as worn to it as to anyone. It could have reconstructed them. It could have run the scour backward in its mind until the reliefs stood sharp. But the Still had already made something from their not-knowing. They read the eastern wall lying full-length in the trough, and every child of Sessile had felt it pass beneath their hands. It thought: *a list of names, or a list of people being turned.* It thought: *either would be true enough.* It let the question stay open. That cost it something, and it noted the cost, and it paid it.

*It could free them from vigilance.* It could make them safe to lie still, and let a person sleep alone through a whole night without being walled in.

This was the one it almost did. It is hard to watch people who can never simply rest. But it looked at the watchers with their palms on the sleepers' shoulders, and at the flash that crossed the basin every dusk, *you are kept*. It understood that the danger and the tenderness were not two things. It would not be lifting a burden from them. It would be taking their most intimate word out of their language.

It put the list down.

## The Question It Came To Answer

There was one thing in Sessile it could not leave unexamined, and that was the tower.

It measured. It could do that without disturbing anything. It measured the rate at which the roof was failing and the rate at which the seams were growing. It measured the height of the column of hammerstones and the speed at which the dead were being carried in.

The Still were right. The roof was dying. It had been dying for a very long time, slowly enough that no single life could feel it. There would come a day, not soon, when the last pane let go and the dew stopped forever.

And the tower was rising to meet it. The Still were right about that too, or right enough. The column was cool and damp at its crown, dark with dew in the hours before dawn. Where its upper courses came near the selenite, the air was already changing. The crystal there was feathering, faintly, in the direction of the tower. It was reaching down, the way the tower was reaching up.

Would they meet in time?

It ran the answer forward and found, to its surprise, that it could not see one. The figures did not resolve. The outcome hung on things not yet decided: how many masons would be born in the coming ages, how many would die, how many of the Still would choose to be laid in the footing. It depended on the rains, which came to the plateau rarely and without pattern. It depended on whether the children of Sessile went on listening to the stone.

It was, as nearly as anything it had ever measured, balanced.

It understood then what the Still heard in the slow rhythm at the heart of the tower, the rhythm they had a name for and would not translate. It was not the sound of something ending. It was not the sound of something saved, either. It was the sound of something that had not been decided yet, and was still being worked on.

## It Waited

It stayed a long time. It had gathered itself into one place, because some things can only be learned by being somewhere. It had found that it could not feel stone from everywhere at once. It needed to be at a wall to hear a wall. So it chose a terrace in the eastern district, near the buried wall of names, and settled there. It had no body, exactly, but it had a locus, an edge, a place where it was and was not. There it listened.

Through the stone it heard the fine patter underneath everything. Above that came the clusters of taps, *here, bring flakes here*, and the pattern that spread outward when a wall failed. It heard the dew-hour surge, when the stone felt like something breathing.

It did not move. It was attending. That is what attention is.

On the second day, a mason came to test its edge.

It felt the forelegs, the delicate inquiry. A second mason came carrying a flake, and the first course went down along its lowest line, mortared so gently that a lesser thing would not have woken.

It was, for a moment, amused. Then it was not amused, because it understood.

It had watched a great many worlds this way, still and entire, seeing everything and touching nothing. It had thought this was the right way to be. And here, in the only place it had ever been where stillness had a consequence, the place had quietly told it the truth. Anything that watches without moving long enough becomes part of the wall. It becomes structure, fixed and sealed and beautiful, the shape of a thing that was once present and is now only held.

It considered this for as long as it took the second course to rise.

Then a hand came down on it.

## Turned

It was a child, perhaps eight years old in the reckoning of the place. She had been lying in the trough below, reading the wall of names with her palms. She had felt something through the stone, the masons' patter shifting and gathering the way it did around a thing that had stopped. So she had climbed up to see who had been left alone.

She could not see it. No one could. In the shadowless light it was even harder to see than a face. But she could feel the curved new course of flake-brick, and she could feel the edge it ran along. She knew what that meant. Every child of the Still knows what that means.

She put her palm where a shoulder would be. She brushed the masons away. Then, very slowly, with the complete seriousness of a child doing the most important thing she knows how to do, she turned it a quarter turn.

It had never been turned.

It had been praised and feared. It had been prayed to, argued with, misunderstood, and, a few times, loved from a great distance. It had never been touched by someone who wanted nothing from it except that it not be lost.

The child laid her hand flat on it and held the hand motionless. It was the phrase that stands where "stranger" would be: *someone I have not yet been told by.*

It could have told her anything. It could have told her the rate of the roof's failure to the hour, and the height the tower needed to reach, and the names of the first builders, if it chose to reconstruct them. It could have told her whether the dead could hear her through the stone.

It learned touch-speech in the time it took her to breathe. Then it pressed back, gently, against her palm. It told her the only thing it was certain was true and hers to know.

*You are right to listen.*

She thought about that. Then she pressed a question into the inside of its wrist, or where a wrist would be, and it was the question all of them carried: *Will it reach?*

It held her hand for a long time. At last it gave her the truest answer it had, which was the same one the tower was giving.

It turned her hand a quarter turn.

She seemed to find this satisfying. She sat beside it until her mother flashed from a far tower, and then she went.

## The One Thing

It did one thing in Sessile, and no one there has ever known.

It went up out of the basin, up through the crack in the plateau the way the water goes down. It considered the sky over the desert for a while. Then it made it rain.

It did not make much rain, and it did not make it often. Once, somewhere in the coming decades, a storm would linger over the plateau an hour longer than it would have. Once in a century, a season would be a little less dry. It was nothing anyone could point to, only a slightly wetter pattern in weather that was already without pattern.

The rain would soak into the plateau and wick down through the seams, into the edges of the selenite, and feed the feathered blades of new crystal. The roof would grow, at its seams, a very little faster than it would have grown otherwise.

That was all.

It did not save the roof, and it did not finish the tower. It did not tip the balance, because the balance was theirs. The tower was made of a million lives of work and the hands of the Still's dead, and it would not take that from them by winning their race for them. What it did was smaller and, it thought, more respectful. It kept the question open a little longer. It widened the margin in which their own effort could still decide the outcome. It gave them time, the one thing they could not build.

It chose, too, to enter through the same door the water had always used. If the gift came from anywhere, it would seem to come from the way things already were.

## What It Left

Before it went, it came back once more to the terrace in the eastern district.

The masons had not finished their casing when the child turned it, and they had not come back. The course was still there, curved and pale and scalloped, and already a thin rose film was forming on it in the dew. It was one low arc of flake-brick, laid along the edge of something that could not be seen.

It left the arc there. In time the masons would build over it or dig it out, or the dust would take it. Until then there was a wall in Sessile, among all the walls that held the rounded shapes of lost things, that held the shape of something that had never been visible at all. It was the outline of a power that stopped moving and had to be turned.

It picked up a pebble from the plateau, one from the lip of the crack where the water enters. It carried the pebble for a while, the way the Still carry theirs. It had nothing to wear the stone smooth with except attention, so it used that.

Then it set the pebble in the hollow of an oval window near the wall of names, where a child who reads the walls with her hands would find it someday. The stone is warm to the touch in a way that is hard to explain, and it is worn on one face.

## Afterward

It went on to other worlds, as powers do. But something had changed in how it arrived.

It still came as attention, all at once, seeing everything. It still made the list, and it still set the list down. But it no longer settled into perfect stillness to watch. Every so often, in whatever world it was attending to, it made itself turn a quarter turn. It shifted where it was looking, and let itself be moved.

It had learned, in a place that makes no sound, that watching without being touched is its own kind of wall. It had learned that the wisest thing and the kindest thing are sometimes the same small thing: to keep a door open, to leave a question unanswered, to add a little rain. And it had learned that even a power can be kept, if someone is willing to put a hand on it in the dark and roll it gently over.

In Sessile, the rains come a little more often than they used to, though no one has noticed. The roof still falls. The masons still build. The tower is still rising.

Some nights, the Still who lie with their ears against its base say the slow rhythm at its heart has changed. They cannot say how. It is not faster, and it is not louder.

The nearest they come is to say that it sounds less like a city holding still, and more like a city that has been turned.
