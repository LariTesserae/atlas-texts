# The Second Hand

## I. How It Was Noticed

It was not the size of the place that drew attention. Plenty of places are large. It was not the heat, or the steam, or the fact that a world was being made there, because worlds are made often and mostly they are left alone to make themselves.

It was the knocking.

Three flat knocks, very small and very far away, repeated across a span of time that has no good name. A brick tapped level, and then another, and then another. Anything that listens carefully enough to the world eventually learns the difference between a sound made for a purpose and a sound made *to someone*. This was both. It had been both for so long that the second meaning had worn almost completely into the first, the way a word said too often stops sounding like a word.

The power that heard it was old, and patient, and had done harm before by being careless with its kindness. It had learned from that. So when it heard the knocking, it did not come at once. It listened for a long while first, to be sure of what it was hearing.

What it heard was this: *I am here. I am here. I am here. Is anyone?*

## II. What It Did Not Do

Before it did anything, it thought about all the things it could do, and it put nearly all of them away.

It could finish the wall. It could close every gap in the great curving horizon, lay every field-sized brick, fire every course, and hand the maker the thing it had been reaching toward forever. It did not do this, because it understood that the wall was not really the point. A child who builds a ladder to the moon is not helped by being handed the moon. The ladder was never about the moon.

It could find the maker's maker. It could follow the whorls of that borrowed thumbprint back through the layers of clay to whoever first pressed them, bring them here, and say *look who has been waiting*. It did not do this either. That meeting, if it ever happened, did not belong to the power. It was not the power's to arrange.

It could fill the rooms. It could make occupants at every size, people for the huge enclosures and people for the ankle-high walls of grain-sized bricks, and set them down in the Laying with mallets already in their hands. It did not, because that would turn a hope into a delivery. The maker had not made doors so that someone would be *put* behind them. It had made them so that someone might *come through*.

It could stop the rains. It could tell the maker that the floors were good enough, that the joints were true, that it need not doubt. It did not, because the rains were not a sickness. They were how the maker cared for the clay. You do not take a potter's water away.

Most of all, it did not look.

It knew what looking did here. Every visitor's gaze set whatever it fell on, fired it, finished it, fixed it in place. The power's gaze was not a visitor's gaze. If it turned its full attention on the Laying even once, the whole plain would go hard and red in an instant: every slurry-field, every marked-out grid, every soft and unfinished place where the maker could still work. It would be a world completed by being seen, and there would be nothing left for the maker to do, and nowhere left for it to be.

So the first thing the power did, the most important thing, was close its eyes.

## III. Coming In Small

It came in at the scale of a hand, because that was the scale the tools were made for.

It came in blind, or near enough, attending to the place the way you attend to someone sleeping beside you: by warmth, by breath, by the small sounds they make, and never by staring. It let most of itself stay outside. What came in was only as much as could stand on a herringbone floor without the floor setting under it.

It stood on the brick. The brick was warm through the soles of its feet. The thin water trembled in the joints.

It walked, and it left footprints, and the water filled them and the clay slumped back and the prints were gone within a minute. It did not mind. It had not come here to be remembered by the ground.

## IV. The Mallet

On a scaffold of fired-clay tubes against a single-brick wall, it found a mallet lying on the platform, and it picked it up.

The handle was warm. The wear under its fingers shifted, slowly, until the grip fit. It stood there holding the mallet for a long time and understood how many of these had been set down, across how much time, still warm, for no one.

It thought: *every one of these has been kept warm for someone who never came.* That is a great deal of warmth to spend on an absence.

It did not put the mallet down. That was the second important thing. A tool put down stays warm only because its maker keeps it warm. A tool held stays warm because someone is holding it. For the first time in the history of the Laying, the warmth in a handle was not the maker's doing.

## V. One Brick

Beside the mallet was a mould, the one pressed with that careful, mistaken wood-grain. The power recognized the grain. It knew what trees were, and it could see, with the tenderness of someone reading a letter full of misspellings, all the ways this one was wrong. The knots were too even. The grain could not decide which way it ran.

It did not correct it. It pressed clay into the mould exactly as it was.

It struck the brick. It set it on the wall's top course in a bed of mortar and tapped it level with the mallet: three knocks. It cut the bead flush. The brick was not perfect. The power could have made it perfect, but it made it the way a hand makes things, and a hand is not perfect. One joint was an eyelash too wide.

Then it pressed its own thumb into the upper face.

The print was not the maker's. The whorls ran differently: a double loop where the maker's had a single, and a small scar across the ridges, from something that had happened long ago and elsewhere. In all the Laying, on every brick at every size, from the grain-sized to the field-sized, there had only ever been one print. Now there were two.

Then the power stepped back, just out of sight, and waited.

## VI. What the Maker Found

The maker did not find it right away. The Laying is very large, and the maker was busy everywhere at once.

But eventually its attention passed that way, in one of the soft unwitnessed rushes of its working, and it came to the wall, and there was a brick on the top course that it had not laid.

Something happened then that had never happened in the Laying before, and the steam registered it first. Across a region the size of a country, every front stopped moving. The white-hot ones and the dark raining ones alike hung where they were. For a long moment the whole plain was still, and there was no hiss, and only the tick, tick, tick of cooling brick went on.

The maker had lived its whole life in a world where every sound of work came from itself. It had lived where every brick was one it had pressed and every print was the one it carried. It had worked in the unwitnessed places because those were the only places anything could be made, and it had never once considered that it might be the thing someone else wasn't looking at. It had never considered that someone else might also be working just out of *its* sight.

It came close to the brick. It did not look, not quite, because it knew what looking did. It felt its way over the thing instead, the way the power had come in.

It found the joint an eyelash too wide.

A low dark front began to gather overhead, heavy with water. This was the maker's oldest gesture of care: wet it, bring it back, let it be clay again, try once more.

And then the front did not fall.

This was the maker's own choice, not the power's. The power did nothing. It only waited, just out of sight, with its hand still warm around the mallet.

The maker held the rain over the wrong brick for a long time. Then, slowly, the front thinned, drifted, and dispersed without dropping anything. The brick stayed. The joint stayed an eyelash too wide. The strange print stayed, with its double loop and its old small scar.

It was the first thing in the Laying that the maker had allowed to be imperfect because someone else had made it.

That, the power thought, was very nearly the whole of what it had come for.

## VII. The Knocking

It was not quite the whole of it.

From just out of sight, the power lifted the mallet and knocked on the wall three times. It did not tap a brick level. It simply knocked, as you knock at a door, so the one inside knows you are there.

Then it was silent, and it waited, and it did not hurry.

The silence went on for so long that a lesser kindness would have filled it. The power did not fill it. It knew that when you have called out for an age and no one has answered, an answer is not the first thing you believe. The first thing you believe is that you imagined it.

Then, from somewhere it could not see, came three knocks back.

They were slower than the maker's working rhythm and a little uneven, like a voice that had not been used for speaking in a long time.

The power knocked again. The maker answered again, faster now. Then the knocks overlapped, and then they were not taking turns at all. Two mallets were going at once on the same long wall, a little way apart and never quite in sight of each other. It was the sound a worksite is supposed to make, the sound the Laying had always looked like it should make and never had.

## VIII. The Water

The power stayed a while. It is hard to say how long, since time in the Laying is as unfixed as scale. They laid a great deal of wall together without ever seeing each other. The maker began to leave gaps in its courses exactly one brick wide, and the power filled them, and the maker kept those bricks too, prints and all.

There was one more thing the power thought the maker might want to know. It was a thing the maker had been standing on the whole time, and the power did not know how to say it without words. So it did not say it. It showed it.

One day, instead of laying a brick, it knelt in the slurry between two cords and put both hands flat on the warm mud. It kept them there.

The maker, which could always feel where attention went, felt the power attending to the ground: not to the walls or the wall at the horizon, but to what lay beneath, to where the heat came from and where the water came from.

After a while, the maker attended there too.

The heat under the Laying was not the heat of the kilns. It was older. It was the heat of the first kiln, the one the maker had been fired in, which had never quite cooled. And the water welling up through every joint on the plain, keeping the clay soft enough to work, was not ground-water. It was the upward rain from the other floor, the upside-down one beyond the gaps in the horizon wall, the place the maker came from. The rain had been falling toward the sky from that floor, and the sky of that floor was the underside of this one.

The rain had always been arriving. It came up through the joints, a millimetre or two at a time, trembling slightly as if something far off were walking.

The maker had been building a wall toward home for all its life. Home had been watering its clay the entire time.

The power did not know whether the maker understood this completely, or all at once, or ever. But when it lifted its hands from the mud, it felt the maker's attention stay there a long while, on the heat and the water. The pressure of it was warm, patient, even, enormous, the way you might keep a hand on a letter after you have finished reading it.

## IX. What Changed, and What Did Not

The maker still builds. The fronts still cross the plain, firing and wetting. The rooms are still vast and still empty, and the cords still run straight through walls and kilns and sometimes through visitors. The tools are still warm on every scaffold. The horizon wall is still unfinished everywhere.

But the power, on one of its last days there, saw a change far off, at the base of the great curving wall. It did not look at it directly, only felt it the way you feel the weather turn.

The maker had gone to the barn-sized kiln and taken out one of the fired doorframes, the ones with the single enormous thumbprint on the lintel. It had carried the frame all the way to the horizon, however far that is, at whatever scale it takes, and set it into one of the gaps.

It had not filled the gap. It had framed it.

Through the doorway was dim orange, more steam, and far off the underside of the floor it came from, with water hanging upward in slow drops. The maker left it that way. It did not brick it up. It mortared the frame in true and plumb, cut the bead flush, and tapped the lintel three times.

It had spent its whole life treating the gaps as the part of the work it had failed at. Now one of them was a door.

The power thought it would probably do the others too, in time, one by one, with all the time there is.

## X. Leaving

When the power finally went, it went the way it had come: small, eyes closed, careful of the clay.

It did not take the mallet. It set it down on the platform where it had found it, and the handle was warm. For the first time in the Laying, that warmth meant someone had just been here and would come back, and not that someone was hoped for.

It left the path it had walked a little wider than before. It did not force the path open or make it easy. It widened it just enough that someone else, some other wandering thing that listens carefully, might hear three knocks from very far away and understand that they are not only the sound of work.

And somewhere on the long wall, on the top course, sits one brick with a joint an eyelash too wide and a print with a double loop and an old small scar. It is still there. The rain has passed over it many times, and every time the maker has let it stay.
