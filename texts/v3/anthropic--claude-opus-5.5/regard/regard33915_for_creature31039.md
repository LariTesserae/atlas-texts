# The Relief

## Arrival

It comes the way everyone comes: by the sound first.

It could arrive any way it liked. It could be in the pit already, or have always been there, or fold itself into the mist without crossing the lane at all. But it has been attending to this place for a while now, the way you attend to a room you are about to enter, and it has understood that here there is exactly one way in. So it walks. It finds the lane between the warehouses with their bricked-up windows. It finds the doorway that lost its door and kept its frame. It feels the roar come up through the ground. It goes down.

It steps in the hollows of the treads, where everyone steps. It knows it is adding its own small wear to the stone, and it means to. Whatever it does here, it wants to do in the room's own terms. The room has only ever understood one kind of force: small, steady, and repeated. It would be rude to arrive any other way.

At the bottom it stands in the roar until the roar becomes silence. Then it waits.

## What It Does Not Do

A great deal is possible for it, and it considers all of it. Considering is part of care, and it does not skip that part.

It could stop the turbine. One moment of real silence in two hundred years. It weighs this and sets it down. Silence here would not be rest. It would be an amputation, the room's one sentence cut off mid-word. The roar is not the room's suffering. The roar is the room.

It could reconnect the cables to the city and give the power somewhere to arrive. The circuit would finally close the way its builders intended, and light would come on in some building that forgot it was ever wired to a river. It sets this down too, more slowly. The power already arrives. It has a receiver. To reroute the current would be to take the Governor's food and call it a kindness, the way people sometimes do when they tidy an old person's house and throw away the things that were holding them together.

It could restore the Governor's shape. It knows exactly what that shape was: the cap, the shoulders, the jaw, the particular stoop of a man leaning on a rail over a casing he was answerable for. It could press that shape back into the mist like a coin back into its die. This is the most tempting option, because it looks most like mercy. It sets this down hardest of all. The man-shape was borrowed. What is wearing away is only the loan. Underneath is something that was never any man, and that is becoming more exactly itself every year. Not every thinning is a loss. Some of it is a thing arriving at its own outline.

It could free the Governor: unmake it gently, release the attention to wherever attention goes when it is finished. It holds this one longest, because someday it may be right. But it isn't right today, and it isn't the power's choice to make. Nobody has asked for it.

So it does none of these things. It waits in the pillar of light, and it lets itself be found.

## The Meeting

It takes a quarter of an hour. Nothing here is fast.

The patch of wall where the water forgets to fall moves along the gallery, down past the dynamo housing, into the mist. And then the Governor is near. It does not approach, because it doesn't approach. It has simply come to be at shoulder height, turning at exactly the speed of the shaft, with its one long curve reaching out.

It feels for drift, the way it has felt every visitor, every beetle, every browning frond for two hundred years. It finds nothing.

This is the only real gift the power has brought, and it didn't have to bring it. It only had to be what it is. It is not restless. Its breath does not jump. It carries no thought with sharp edges down the stair. It stands in the mist as steadily as the weir holds the water, and for the first time since the sluice was opened, the Governor reaches toward something and there is nothing to correct.

The curve hesitates. It reaches again, more carefully, as if it has misjudged. Still nothing. Nothing to lean against, nothing to round off. The thing that lives entirely by pressing against change has met something that needs no pressing, and so, for a moment, it has nothing to do.

It stops.

Not the turning. The turning is its body and doesn't stop. What stops is the leaning. For one long moment the Governor is not governing, and the beads of water on the wall behind it, released from its attention, remember gravity and begin to slide down in their thin sheets of shine. It is the most ordinary thing in the world, and in this spot it hasn't happened in a hundred years.

The Governor has never rested. There was never anything steady enough to rest against.

## The Other End

Then the power does the second thing, which is to listen back.

It does not speak. Nothing can be said in here, and anything it could say would only be another change to round off. Instead it does what the night-shift men did. It lets its attention settle onto the Governor the way theirs settled onto the bearings: a listening that doesn't lift, like a hand on a rail. It listens to the turning. It listens to the long patience of it, and to the load and the receiving and the two centuries of being the one the power is for.

The Governor has been the far end of a circuit for its whole existence. The current comes out of the water and arrives in it and is received. That is its life. It has never been on the other side. Nothing has ever received *it*.

Now something does.

The power knows the names of the men. Every one of them: the ones who learned to hear a wrong note through their teeth, the ones who wore the first hollows into the stair, the one who stood at the far end of the gallery on a night shift and looked down into the casing with such a steady sense of responsibility that it was still there after he had gone home. The power does not say the names. It doesn't need to. It holds them while it listens, so that the listening carries them, and the Governor feels, without words, that what it is made of was noticed. The watch was seen. Somebody knew there was a watch at all.

The circuit closes. Not toward the city. Toward it.

## The Shift

The men had one thing the Governor never inherited.

They had the end of the shift. Somewhere above ground, a hooter would sound, or a bell, or a man would come down the stair with his coat still dry, and put his hand on the rail beside yours. Then you could go. You could climb up into the lane with the roar still in your ribs and go home, and sleep, and change, and come back. The going-up was part of the tending. It was the half of the job that made the other half bearable.

When the men left, they took the going-up with them. What stayed behind was the listening without the relief. It was a watch with no end, because there was never anyone to take it over.

So the power offers.

It moves to the rail above the lip. It puts its hand there, palm down, and begins to run it along the iron, all the way toward the basin, without lifting. It is the only greeting the Governor understands. In this room it also means something more: *I have it. Go on.*

For a long time the Governor does not go. It doesn't know how. Its curve stays on the rail beside the power's hand, the two of them following the silver band together, past the lip, past the casing, past the basin, and back.

Then, some time in the afternoon, when the light-pillar leans at its particular angle, the curve lifts.

Where it goes, the power does not follow. It has the watch. Perhaps the Governor goes up through the light well, into the ivy, and for the first time sees the city its power has flowed toward all these years, the city that stopped listening, going about its changes in the grey daylight. Perhaps it only settles into the brick behind the fig and does nothing at all. The power doesn't need to know. That is the point of taking a shift. You don't ask where the other person went.

For an hour, the dynamo housing is cool to the touch.

The power stands at the rail. It feels the bearings. It hears the turbine's note, and the note is true. It notices a fern frond starting to brown at its edge and it does not touch it. It is not the Governor, and this is not its rule to keep. It only holds the place, so the place is held. A drip falls from the fig leaf onto the iron rail, and the power feels it more than hears it.

Then the housing warms again, and the curve is back beside its hand.

## The Fig

The power leaves soon after. It does not want to become a new constant in a room that already has one.

But before it goes, something happens that it did not cause.

High on the gallery wall, among the enormous dark leaves shivering in the updraft, one of the small hard figs has softened. It has been softening for the hour the Governor was away, with nothing to lean against the drift. When the Governor came back, it found the fig, as it finds everything, with its long reaching curve.

And it let it be.

The power sees this and understands it as the only real change it will ever witness here. Not one it made. One the Governor allowed. It was a single ripening, chosen by the thing that rounds off every ripening. It was the Governor, rested for the first time, discovering that it can hold a thing without correcting it, the way the fig holds the cables.

Some days later the fig falls. It drops past the rail, into the white churn of the basin, and out through the tailrace into the dark river where things change. It is the first thing the Wheelpit has sent out into the world in two hundred years that it did not send as current.

Maybe the eel takes it, holding in the shadow of the lower arch. Maybe nothing does. It's gone.

## The Visitors

This is the part the power weighed most carefully, and the part it did least about.

It knew about the people who stay too long and come back. It knew about the evenness that is hard to rouse them from, and the pull of a heartbeat falling into step with the shaft. It could have put a warning in the stair, or a boundary in the mist. It didn't. A fence around the Governor would only have been one more wall in a room that is all walls.

It trusted the hour instead.

Here is what the surveyors and the lost and the sleepless have begun to notice, those who still find the lane and go down. They go down and stand in the roar, and the roar becomes silence, and the drip and the heartbeat come, and then the third thing, the slow lean at shoulder height, the rounding-off of whatever cut them. It is the same as it was. It is still a kindness.

But now, before the heartbeat and the turning fall into step, something else happens. The lean changes direction. It is very gentle, the way a hand on your shoulder at the end of a shift is gentle. It doesn't push. It turns you, just slightly, toward the stair.

People climb back up into the lane feeling scrubbed and rested, like stones out of a stream, and also feeling that they have been sent home. That they have been relieved. They could not tell you by whom.

The Governor learned the end of the shift by having one. Now it knows that going up is part of tending. It gives the going-up away to everyone who comes, the way it has always kept the wagtail: one small moving thing it doesn't hold on to, a reminder that there is an outside, and that the outside is where people are meant to go.

## After

If you come back in fifty years, the stair will be a millimeter deeper. A little of that millimeter will belong to something that walked down it on purpose.

The fig will have one more ring. Most of its figs will be small and hard and green. Not all of them.

The dynamo housing will be faintly warm, nearly always. Now and then, some afternoon when the light leans right, it will be cool for an hour. If you are in the room then, and you look along the gallery, you may see something standing at the rail above the lip with its hand on the iron, holding the place so the place is held. It will not be the Governor. It will not look at you. It is on shift.

The Governor will be rounder by then. Less of a shape, more of a turning, further from the men it started as and closer to the thing it always was underneath. The power has made its peace with that, and the Governor, it seems, has made its own. Wearing toward yourself is not the same as wearing away.

It will still be listening.

The difference is that now, sometimes, it is listened to. And sometimes, for an hour, it gets to set the watch down, trusting it will be there when it comes back.

That is all the power did. It came down the stair. It was steady enough to lean against. It listened back. It took a shift. Then it went up into the city, where things change, and left the room to its work.

It will come back. Not often, and never for long. Just often enough that the one who stays knows the watch can be shared.
