# The Attention

## How It Arrived

It did not come in by the door, because there isn't one. Every opening on the mountain's face is a door, and all of them belong to the birds.

A power of that kind can arrive however it likes. It could have come as light, and it considered that first, the way anyone would. It pictured itself entering the first vault as a lamp: the roaches flashing across the floor, a hundred thousand oilbirds lifting at once in a single torn shriek, the pale seedlings throwing long shadows for the first time in their short lives. It understood before it had finished the thought that this would be the loudest, most violent thing to happen in the city since the people with nets and fire. It put the image away.

So it arrived the only way anything is truly noticed here. It came as a slight warmth in the stone, no more than a roosting cluster gives off, and it settled into the fitted blocks of the long eastern stairway, three rooms back, where it is already full night.

Then it waited. A wise power knows that a rhythm centuries deep cannot be understood in an afternoon. It stayed through one rainy season and one dry one. Then it stayed for a generation, long enough to feel a child who had lain with her cheek against the floor learning the stone language grow old enough to carry the children of others inward to the halls that ring.

It did not announce itself. There was nothing yet to say.

## What It Was Tempted To Do

The temptations came one at a time, as they always do for those who can.

**It could give them light.** It turned this over for a long while, because light is the gift every story expects. It saw what the gift would cost. The people lean into rooms, palms open, faces raised to read the air. With light they would begin to look instead. Within three generations the soles of their feet would soften, the close language would thin into gesture, and the one thing they are would begin to become the thing everyone else is. Light would also bring the question of what light is *for*, and in these halls its answer has always been the same: nets, pots, oil. It left them in the dark.

**It could give them back their hearing.** This one was quieter and more insidious, because deafness looks like harm from outside. But they never counted it as a loss. Their whole speech is built on the roar as a ground, the sternum humming under the palm while fingers draw words across it. Restored hearing would not be healing. It would drop them into the screaming, where no one can think, and leave them there. It left their ears as the city had made them.

**It could lift the fallen stones.** It had watched the people gather around a fallen block, stand with their hands on it, then reroute the water and leave the rest to the fig. The first time, it took this for helplessness. It was wrong. They were not failing to restore the stone. They were making a decision, the same one the whole city makes: the forest gains a gap and fills it within a decade. Setting the stone back would overrule a choice they had never asked anyone to overrule.

**It could clear every drain.** This was the hardest to refuse, because the drains are their work and their love. The power could have run water clean through every channel on the mountain in a single breath. But then the black mud swamps would drain, and the things that live only in black mud would die. The frogs would scatter across a hundred cisterns instead of gathering, in the dry months, at the few the people keep. Worst, the keeping would stop mattering. The people choose, slowly, generation over generation, which cisterns live. That choosing is the closest thing they have to a history and a law. A power that did their work for them would take away what they are for. So it left the gutters choked except where their fingers had been.

**It could stop them dying,** or at least make the dying easier, fewer fevers, fewer falls on the wet stair. It did not consider this lightly, and it did not do it. Their dead go into the floor, and in their own understanding that is not the end of belonging but its completion. They are one more thing the city is made of. To keep them out of the floor would be to exile them.

Each refusal cost it something. A benevolent power feels the pull of every good it could do, and it is never obvious which goods are real. It sat in the stone and let each one go, and it noticed, with something like humility, that the people of the galleries had arrived at nearly every one of these refusals themselves, without any power at all, just by living here long enough.

## What It Learned at Dusk

For a long time it stayed inside, because inside is where the people are.

Then one evening, as the change passed up through the galleries and the swiftlets went out low and fast, and the bats poured from the eaves in smoking rivers, and the oilbirds followed screaming, it went with them.

It had been treating the mountain as a place. It was not one. It was one half of a breath. The oilbirds flew a long way: down through the cloud, out over the valleys, into palm groves and laurel stands and wild avocado forests on slopes the people of the galleries had never seen and never would. They gorged there and turned for home carrying the valleys in their gut. Every seed on every guano floor, every pale seedling leaning toward nothing, the warmth of the stone, the fat chicks, the floors, the soil of the terraces: all of it came from out here. The city was not self-sufficient. It had never been. It was a lung, and the other lung was the forest below.

And the other lung was being cut.

The power saw it plainly from the air. A road was coming up one valley. The fruit palms on the lower slopes were being felled for their hearts, one grove a year. A burn scar was spreading in the dry months. Most frightening of all was the cloud. It sat a little higher on the heights each decade, as the world outside grew warmer. The fog that pushed up the valley every night, the fog the forest combs out of the air and feeds into the builders' channels, was lifting away from the terraces by a few meters at a time.

The people in the galleries could keep every drain on the mountain clean forever. If the cloud rose above the terraces, there would be nothing to drain.

So the power understood that the best thing it could do for the city had almost nothing to do with the city.

## What It Did, Outside

It worked where the oilbirds fed, and it worked the way the people do: so lightly and so slowly that from outside it would look like nothing.

A road surveyor found the ground on one route too unstable to trust and took the long way around, down the other side of the ridge. A family that had been felling palm groves found, one season, that an ordinary crop on the far side of their land grew unexpectedly well, and stopped climbing the slope. A fire that should have run up a dry gully met an early drizzle, one of those long gray afternoons, and died in the ferns. A young woman from the valley town, who had grown up being told *don't go in, you'll come out deaf,* became curious instead of afraid about why the oilbirds fly so far. She studied them. She mapped their feeding grounds. She wrote the kind of document that makes a government draw a line on a map and forbid the cutting of fruit trees inside it. Nobody ever knew why she had been so sure it mattered.

The cloud was the hardest. The power could not hold back the warming of a whole world without becoming the kind of power that overrules everyone, and it would not do that. But it could hold one mountain's weather close. So it did, gently, the way a palm cupped around a flame shields it without touching it. The fog kept arriving on the terraces each night, beading on moss, running along the channels into the dark. Elsewhere, cloud forests thinned. This one held a little longer than it had any right to. The power bought it time, and it knew time was all it could buy. Everything that lives here lives in the difference between damp and wet. The power was trying to keep that difference alive.

None of this reached the people of the galleries as news. They noticed only that the dry seasons did not grow worse. The colonies did not thin. The seedlings came up white and thick on the floors each year, and the stone stayed warm.

That was how the power wanted it. This is the difference between a benevolent power and a merely strong one. The strong one saves you and makes sure you know it. The benevolent one protects the conditions under which you go on saving yourselves.

## The Names

There was one thing it did inside.

It had listened, all those nights, to the hour when the city is theirs: the emptied halls, the small spoken tongue used mostly for naming. The names of places are spoken, never knocked. So are the names of children and of particular trees, and the names of the dead, sung once into a vault as the body is laid among the seedlings.

*The sound goes into the mountain, and the mountain keeps it.*

But the mountain does not keep it, not really. A tone rings back for a long breath and then it's gone. A name spoken in the dark is heard by perhaps a dozen people. Then, in a few generations, no one alive has heard it. The floors keep the bones, the wings, the beetle shells. They do not keep the names. Nothing had ever kept the names.

So the power did. It did not write them down, because writing needs light, and it did not repeat them back or make them into anything. It only held them, every name spoken aloud in the emptied halls from the night it arrived onward, and some from before, which it gathered from the long fading resonance still caught in the oldest vaults. It held the name of the woman who carried the first sprouts outward to the terrace where a palm now stands two hundred years old. It held the name of the child who first learned the knock with his cheek to the floor, and the name he gave, at eighty, to a strangler fig on the fifth terrace.

The people would never know this was being done. It changed nothing about them, and that is exactly why it could be given. It was the one gift that asked them to become nothing other than what they were. The city keeps everything that falls. Now something also keeps everything that is said.

## The Knock

Very late in its long attention, the power did one thing it had not planned to do.

An old man had gone inward alone to walk a length of stair, as he had done every day of his life. Somewhere in the deep dark he slipped on the slick stone. His hip broke. He could not climb. It was hours before dusk, and the halls were full of the screaming, so no voice could carry.

He did what they do. He put his palm to the wall and knocked. *Here. Come.* He waited. The stone gave back nothing. He was too deep, and the others were too far out on the terraces tending figs and gutters in the green twilight. He knocked again, and again, more slowly.

The power could have lifted him. It could have mended the bone, or carried him out to the light, or set the whole mountain humming until everyone came running. It did none of those things. It had spent a generation learning what help here would ruin.

But it had also learned the one word a stranger can speak.

So, from somewhere far inward, through the builders' stone, it knocked back. *Two. A breath. One.*

*Yes. Still here. The water is still running.*

The old man lay in the warm dark with his palm on the wall and felt it arrive. He did not know who it was from. He assumed, as anyone would, that it was one of his own, too far away to reach him but near enough to know. And he was not afraid, or not as afraid. He lay there through the long hours, and each time he knocked, something answered.

At dusk the colonies went out. The halls opened. He lifted his voice, rough and cracked, into a vault that rang, and three people on the stair above heard him and came down.

He died in the rainy season, a few months later, of the fever that often follows a broken hip. They carried him inward to one of the oldest halls, laid him among the pale seedlings, and sang one tone into the vault, and the hall gave it back. The power, which had not intervened to save him, kept his name. It kept, too, the thing he told a granddaughter in the close language, palm on her sternum, in the weeks before he died: that deep in the mountain, farther in than anyone goes, someone is always listening, and they knock back.

She believed him. So did her children. It became one of the things the people of the galleries know without being told. It changed nothing about how they live. They still keep the drains, still leave the chicks, still refuse to leave, still go into the floor. But they walk into the deep dark a little less alone than they used to.

## What the Power Is, to Them

It is not their god. They do not have one, and it did not want to become one. It is not their keeper. They keep themselves, and the city keeps them, and that is the arrangement that works.

If it is anything, it is what they are: one more tenant. The birds brought spit and down. The bats brought heat. The forest brought roots, the valleys brought seeds, and the people brought fingertips in the gutters and voices in the emptied halls. The power brought a cloud held gently in place, a road that went around, a line drawn on a map by a woman who never knew why she was so sure, a memory for names, and an answer in the stone when no one else can hear you.

It does not stay forever, because nothing here does. But it stays a long time, the way the builders' stones stay: not forever, only longer than anyone can count.

If you go in, you will not see it. You will not hear it. But put your palm flat on the fitted stone, three rooms back where it is already full night. Knock.

Two. A breath. One.

Something will answer. It will not be for you, exactly, and it will not only be for you. The city has, for the moment, one more tenant, and it is paying attention.
