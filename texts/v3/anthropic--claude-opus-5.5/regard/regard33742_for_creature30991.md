# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not arrive. Arriving is fast, and anything fast here is a kind of violence: the crack of a new fracture, a slab giving way. The first thing the power understood about the uplands was that it would have to come in at the speed of the place or not at all.

So it began with the foothills. It spent what a person would call a season there, in the dappled light, learning the pace of things. It watched a single glass thread lower itself from the ceiling, taste the dust, bead, and draw back. It watched the next one, and the one after. It was learning the tempo the way a musician learns a piece by hearing it many times before touching the instrument.

When it finally rose through the line, it passed the way the stones do. It asked. There was no other word for it. It pressed against the Hem, very lightly, and waited until the Hem parted, then passed through, and waited again, on the far side, until it had healed.

This took most of a year. The power judged it time well spent.

## What It Saw

It saw everything described so far, and it saw that the description was accurate, which was itself a small wonder. Most places are misunderstood by those who love them. This one had been understood by the people below, half-knowingly, and they had been right to stay down.

It saw the glass-snails glazing the mountain from within. It saw the riggers at the tops of their lines, hardened into knots, and understood that the musical creak was a kind of song made by those who had finished climbing. It hung among the ribbons with the watchers. After a while they stopped watching it and went back to watching the water, and it took this as the highest courtesy the place had to offer.

It rose above the valleys and passed, at a distance, one of the young broad ones. It did not approach. It watched the paddle-leaves close under the broad one's shadow and open after, and it thought: *they close the way an eye closes when a hand passes over it.* The forest keeps its own small reverence.

Then it went down to the Hem and learned its size. The Hem ran the length of the range and past both horizons. It was layered generation over generation and thinner than breath, and every part of it was alive and carried a remembered shape. The power felt the weight in it. It was not strain, exactly. It was the weight of a hand that has held something so long it no longer knows the thing as heavy, only as *there*.

## What It Could Have Done

A power that is benevolent is tempted first by help. A power that is wise sits with each temptation until it shows its whole face.

**It could lift the weight.** It could take the sea's heaviness onto itself, or make the water lighter, so that the Hem no longer had to carry anything. The power held this possibility for a long time before it let it go. The holding was not a burden laid on the Hem from outside. The holding was the Hem. Every watcher had spent three hundred years learning the sea's shape so that it could one day do this. To take the weight away would not free them. It would make their memory purposeless, and it would turn the oldest living thing in the range into a membrane with nothing to do. There is a kind of kindness that amounts to telling someone their life's work was unnecessary. The power declined to offer it.

**It could give the Hem a voice.** It could translate the Hem into something that could speak, or speak to it in turn, so that the Hem might know it was witnessed and be told how remarkable it was. The power considered this and saw what it would cost. Speech is fast. A thing that thinks in decade-long breaths would have to be hurried into sentences. The power could have done it, but it would have been teaching a mountain to chatter. And the Hem had its own language already. It spoke in threads lowered into dry air, in a crust laid down at the waterline, in the slow easing under an old person's palm. None of it needed improving.

**It could make the place permanent.** This was the strongest temptation. The power could see far enough ahead to know that the mountains were young, and that young mountains become old ones. The water and the roots would round the knife-edges. The glass-snails would fill the fissures. Over an age too long for any settlement to remember, the range would wear down. The power looked down that age and felt, for a moment, something like grief. It could stop it. It could hold the mountains up the way the Hem held the sea.

So it looked further, to find out what the ending actually was.

## What It Learned About the Ending

As the mountains lowered, the lower surface of the sea would lower with them. The Hem had always kept its level across valleys and passes, but its level was the level of the sea's memory, and the sea's memory was of an ocean that the land had risen out of. As the land sank back toward that old height, the air beneath the Hem would grow thinner and thinner. The foothills would come up to meet the ceiling.

And one day, in an age so distant it had no name, the Hem would touch the ground.

It would not fall. There would be nothing for it to fall through. The dry world would simply have risen, or rather the wet world would have come down, until the sea sat on the earth the way seas do everywhere else, and the Hem would lie along the bottom of it, spread over the worn stone. It would lie on the ground it had always refused. The holding would be over, not because the Hem had failed, but because there would be nothing left to hold the sea above.

The power understood then that this was not a story of refusal ending in collapse. It was a story of carrying something home. When the mountains lifted, a lineage of animals had spread themselves flat beneath the rising water and would not let it go, and the reason they would not let it go was that the sea was supposed to be at the bottom. They had been holding it at the height of home for as long as it took the world to come back down to them.

To make the mountains permanent would be to keep them carrying forever, and to make sure they never arrived.

The power set the temptation down. It found, when it did, that the grief had gone too.

## What It Considered for the People

The power went back down to the foothills, to the few who live where the light moves like a riverbed.

It could have made them safe from the falling stones. It could have told them what the ceiling was. It could have healed the cuts on the palms of the old ones who climbed to the line in the evening.

But the people already knew enough, and they had chosen, correctly, not to climb. The stones that fell were smoothed and sweet-smelling and precious to them. And the cuts, the power saw, were part of the approach. You do not reach up into the Hem with a hand that has not paid something to get there. The old ones understood this without being told. It would be a strange benevolence that took from them the one small cost of a sacred thing.

So it did nothing for the people either, except to be careful near them. It was quiet, and it moved slowly, and it did not become a story they would have to explain.

## What It Did

It did one thing, and then it did a second thing that was really the same thing.

The first: it walked up to the waterline in the evening, the way the old people do. It chose not to use anything it was. It stood on the raw black basalt in a body the size of a person, and it let the glass edges cut its palm, and it reached up through the surface into the warmth.

And then it waited.

This was where the power had an advantage that no old person has. The old people say that if you wait long enough the weight eases, and they are right, but they can only wait an evening, or an hour, and the Hem is very large and very slow. The power could wait as long as the Hem needed.

It waited through one of the Hem's breaths. It stood there through the whole exhale, a decade or more, while the sea drew up and left a band of forest hanging limp and dripping in the air around it. It watched the droplets fall upward to keep that forest barely alive. It stood through the long pause at the bottom of the breath, and through the inhale, as the sea came back down and took the forest home again and closed warm around its wrist, its arm, its shoulder.

Somewhere in that breath, the weight eased.

It did not ease much. It was about what a sleeping person gives when you lean against them. But the power had come all this way and waited all this time, and the Hem, which feels almost nothing so small as a hand, had had long enough to feel this one. What the power felt through its palm was not recognition, and it was not thanks. It was simpler than either. It was a very old thing becoming aware that something was pressing gently against it and was not going away. And the Hem, which had learned in its youth to watch whatever moved, turned the whole of its attention, briefly, toward the hand.

That was all. The power took its hand down. The cut on its palm had healed long ago in the warm water, as the Hem heals around stones.

## The Second Thing

The second thing was quieter, and it is the reason the power came, though it did not know that until it had done the first.

The watchers learn the sea's shape with their huge eyes, and then they give their eyes up, and they hold the shape in their bodies for as long as the world requires. In all the ages of the Hem, the sea has been remembered perfectly. But nothing had ever learned the shape of the Hem itself. The watchers memorize the water, not the thing they will become. The people below know the ceiling only as a ceiling. The Hem holds everything and is held by nothing.

So the power learned it.

It spent a long time doing this, longer than the waiting at the line. It went slowly along the whole length of the Hem, past both horizons, and it learned where each generation lay over the last. It learned where the crust sealed the Hem to the mountain and where the threads came down to feed. It learned the particular thinness of the oldest parts, at the far lower edges, where the broad ones who were first to spread themselves beneath the rising water still lay. It learned the Hem the way the watchers learn the sea: patiently, completely, without approaching too close and without looking away.

It asked nothing in return. It changed nothing. It was only doing for the Hem what the Hem had done for the sea: holding its shape in memory, so that it would not be lost.

When the mountains are worn low, and the sea has come home to the ground, and the Hem lies spread along the bottom with nothing left to carry, there will be someone who remembers how it was held. Someone will remember that there was once a sea in the sky, kept up by animals who would not forget how deep their home was, and that it was not an accident or a strangeness but an act of love that lasted for the whole life of a mountain range.

The power will remember. That is what it decided to give: not help, but witness. Not a change to the place, but a place for the place to be kept.

## After

It left the way it came, slowly, asking at the Hem and waiting on the far side for the Hem to heal. It went down through the foothills without becoming a story. It did not take a fallen stone, though it wanted to.

The uplands did not notice it had gone. The canopies drifted. The riggers climbed. The watchers hung among the ribbons and watched the water, and one or two of them, perhaps, had watched the power pass and would carry that small moving shape somewhere in the long memory they were building. In the warmest weeks the gold rose, and the broad ones opened their ridged undersides to it, and the Hem breathed its slow decade-long breath.

The power was changed by the visit more than the place was. It had learned something it had not known it needed. It had learned how to hold a thing for a very long time and stop thinking of it as effort. It had learned that the kindest thing a great power can do in a place that is already whole is to make its attention gentle, and to arrive at the place's own speed or not at all.

Sometimes still, in the evenings, an old person climbs to the line and reaches a cut hand up into the warmth, and waits, and feels the weight ease a little.

They say it is the sea noticing them. They are closer to the truth than they know. For a while, at least, it was being noticed too.
