# The Visit

## Arrival

It did not come down through the ice, because it did not need to. It was simply there one dark-season day, in the way attention is there when you notice you have been looking at something for a while.

It had been drawn by the pattern. A shelf that size, divided that finely, shows even from very far away, like a fingerprint pressed into the bottom of the world. Its first thought was the one anyone would have: *someone made this.* Its second thought came slowly, as it settled into the water of a narrow strip-room among ribboned black-brown kelp: *someone is still living in it.*

It took some time to work out who.

## Looking for the One Who Suffers

A benevolent power looks first for need. That is what its benevolence is for.

It looked in the beehive chambers. The pale kelp there lived on almost no light, its tissue thin as wet paper, stretching one finger's-width of new blade from a store laid down months ago. The power's first impulse was pity: *starved, shut in, give it light.* It held the impulse and looked longer. The plant was not failing. It was doing exactly what it had become able to do, at exactly the pace its room allowed, and it had been doing so for longer than most mountains have stood. Light would not rescue it. Light would bleach it. What looked like deprivation was the shape of its life, and the shape fit.

It looked at the brine-folk, standing half in one water and half in another. It looked at the white veils filling the low rooms to the brine line, doing their slow work in airless sourness. It looked at the soft, undefended forest. Nothing hurt. Nothing was hungry in any way that hunger could be relieved. Nothing was afraid, because there had never been anything to fear.

This was the first thing it learned: *this place has no wound.* Benevolence that arrives looking for a wound, and finds none, has to become something else or leave.

It did not leave. It became attention.

## The Reading

It began where the record was kept.

On a lintel near the middle of the shelf, over a doorway that no one had walked through in an unimaginable time, the coralline crust was as thick as a hand. The power did not cut it. It did not need to. It read the bands the way you read a face you are coming to know: pale for the green seasons, dark for the long nights, one pair per year, back and back.

It read good years and hard years. It read a run of thin bands where the ice must have lain heavy for generations. It read the slow shift of the climate above, recorded by a thing that never knew there was an above. Then it reached the innermost band, pressed against the stone itself. That was the first season after the sea came in, when the crust took hold on a surface that had, the year before, been dry.

It stayed there a long time, at the boundary between the last season of the builders and the first season of everything else.

It did not learn who they were. The stone would not say, and the power did not force it. It did learn one thing, by looking at the rooms, the channels, and the gradient the brine still followed: the builders had been people who stored. They lived through a bright season and a dark one. They ground what they had saved in dished stones, divided the ground so that each part could be tended, and moved water where it needed to go. The forest living in their rooms now does all of these things too: it stores, keeps to its rooms, and travels along the drains. It does them without knowing, the way a riverbed keeps the course of a river long gone.

The power found that it was moved, and it let itself be.

## What It Considered

Power considers things. That is part of wisdom, and it does not skip the step.

**It considered bringing life.** The shelf was so empty of animals. It could seed the rooms with small grazers, a few snails and some amphipods, the ordinary busy crowd of every other cold sea, and fill the silence with crackle and the rubble with eyes. It saw at once what would happen. The kelp had never learned to be bitter or tough. Its blades were soft and faintly sweet. Grazers would find a banquet with no defenses. Within a few decades the households would be stripped, the beehive-folk gone first because they had the least to spare, and the patchwork of lineages that had taken ages to form would be blurred into bare stone and whatever survived. It would be making a different place and calling it a gift. It set the thought down.

**It considered stopping the burial.** The drifts were filling the rooms. In some far age the walls would vanish under peat, and the shelf would become a smooth brown plain. The power could hold that off indefinitely. It could clear the low rooms, stir the brine, and keep the walls showing forever, like a museum. Then it looked at who lived in the drifts. The white veils were not destroying anything. They were the shelf's only eaters, and the burial was their life. It thought about what the second half of the account had understood: when the walls are gone, the families will keep their lines. The pattern would outlast the stone a second time, carried in the lineages of the kelp. The burial was not an ending. It was the pattern passing from one medium into another, the way a song passes from a singer to someone who never heard the singer. You do not prevent that. It set the thought down.

**It considered mending.** It could re-lay a slumped wall, re-seat a sluice-board, clear a blocked culvert. The builders would have wanted their works kept. But behind the blocked culvert lived the brine-folk, who existed only because the brine pooled there. In the rubble of the slumped wall lived a seed-bank that the slump had created. Every repair it considered was also an eviction. The place was so completely occupied that there was no improvement left that did not displace someone. It found this beautiful, and it found it instructive. A place is finished when there is nowhere left to help without harm.

**It considered light.** One more week each year. It thought through the consequences and found the beehive-folk bleaching, the households blurring as the fast growers overran the slow, and the brine-folk shaded out. It set this down with the others.

**It considered speaking.** It could make itself known, to whom? There was no one here to be told. It saw that the wish to be known was its own wish, not the place's need.

## What It Found at the Edge

Having set down everything it could do *to* the place, it went looking at the place's edges. Things that come from outside do not respect the grammar of the rooms.

At the seaward margin of the shelf, where the made ground finally dropped away into a real depth, the power found the outer ocean pressing against the lattice. That ocean was full: larvae, drifting eggs, the spawn of urchins and snails and a thousand grazers, carried on currents that almost never crossed over the rim. *Almost* never. The shelf's isolation was not a law. It was an accident of cold, of brine that flowed outward and away, and of ice that locked the surface. It was a narrow, fortunate balance.

It looked above and saw that the world up there was not stable either. There were long slow changes in the ice. There were years recorded in the crust that were thinner or thicker than they should have been. If the ice ever failed for good, the brine would stop, the stratification would go, the warmer outer water would come in, and with it everything the forest had never needed to fear.

This was the one place where benevolence had an object. It was not a wound in the shelf. It was a danger to it.

So the power made its first decision. It would not act on the shelf. It would keep watch at the edge. If a grazer's larva drifted over the rim, it would be turned gently back into the deep, the way a current turns. If some great change came from above, if the ice began to fail and the warm water to come, it would think again, carefully, about what could be done that preserved the place as it was and did not simply replace it with a place the power preferred. It did not promise the shelf would last forever. It promised only that it would not end by accident, while something that cared was looking.

## The One Thing

It stayed for a full turn of the year. It felt the brine season, the internal waves lifting whole districts of forest together, the melt, the crowd of diatoms blooming gold and sinking back, and the long dark again. It listened to the ice, the stones, and the woody creaking of the kelp. It learned the households, all of them, and it would have been able to tell you, of any room on the shelf, which family lived there and how they differed from the family next door.

Near the end of that year, in a wall near the lintel it had read, it found a joint.

The coralline crust had closed this joint long ago. Reading the bands at its edge, the power judged that it had been sealed very early, within the first few hundred years after the sea came in. Inside, pressed between two stones, lay filaments of the waiting generation, shut in with their shelter and their seed. Nearly all of them were long dead. Nothing waits that long.

But one was not. One speck of brown thread, a few cells long, held some thin remnant of life that no ordinary reckoning could explain. Perhaps the cold, the dark, the stillness, and the sealed stone had preserved it past all reason. The power did not look closely into *how*. Some things are allowed to be unlikely.

This was the only thing on the shelf that seemed to be *waiting for something that would never come.* Everything else was living its life. This one was only waiting. Its room had been closed, not by its own nature, but by the slow grain of the crust.

So the power did one thing.

It did not cut the crust. It did not lift the stone out with its own strength, in its own way. It went to the holdfast of a kelp that grew beside the joint and did what holdfasts already do: it leaned, over one green season, a little more strongly than it otherwise would have. By the end of the season the stone had shifted by the width of a fingernail. The seal cracked. Light, the dim green light of this place, reached into the joint for the first time since the sea came in.

It made a dull knock, then a gritty trickle. On the shelf this happens every so often, and anyone listening would have heard nothing unusual. The power had made sure of that. Its single act was, by design, indistinguishable from the place's own ordinary behavior. It acted only in the grammar of the shelf: a stone settling.

What the filament did next, the power did not arrange. It watched. The next green season, a young sporophyte pushed out of the joint. Its blades were unlike those of any household on the shelf, neither ribboned nor broad but something older, with a slight curl along the margin that had not been seen in these waters for a very long time. It was a descendant of the first kelp to arrive after the builders left, the first family to move into the empty rooms.

Its spores would fall, as spores here do, close by. It would make a household, or fail to. Its lineage would mix at the thresholds, or keep to itself. The power did not decide which.

## Leaving

It left the way it had arrived, by ceasing to be noticed. A part of its attention stayed at the edge, turning back what should not come over the rim and watching the ice, so it was not entirely gone.

If you asked it afterward what it had done for the being that lived there, it would have had to think about who that being was. It was not any single plant. It was the whole shelf: the pattern made by people who stored and divided and channeled, and then lived in, perfectly and unknowingly, by a forest that stores and keeps to its rooms and travels by the drains. The being was the plan still being inhabited. That kind of being does not need to be saved or improved. It needs mostly to be left intact, and it needs, perhaps, to be witnessed once.

So the power's answer would have been something like this:

*I read its whole memory, from the last dry year to the present, and I remember it now, so it is kept in two places. I set down every improvement I could imagine, because every improvement was an eviction. I stand at its edge, so that it will not end by accident. I let one stone settle that had been waiting, only one, and I did it the way stones already settle there.*

*It lacked almost nothing. The kindest thing I could offer it was to notice that.*

Out in the dark the ice creaks. Somewhere in the lattice, near an old doorway, a young kelp with a slight curl along its margin, of a kind not seen since the sea first came in, lets its first spores fall into the still water of the room.
