# What the Attention Did

## Arrival

It came the way the fen is reached, from the north, over the black ridge. It could have come any way at all, but it had learned long ago that the first thing to do in a place is to arrive the way the place is arrived at.

It felt the pressure in the air before it knew it was sound. It let itself not know for a while. This was a discipline it kept. Understanding comes very quickly to a power like that, and it had found that understanding too quickly is a kind of theft. The mile of approach belongs to the approach. It walked the mile.

Then it stood at the cairns, where the lanes begin, and it did nothing for a long time.

## The Inventory of Mercies

It noticed, as it always did, the things it could do. They arrived in a crowd, like chimneys, each one plausible and each one leaning slightly toward it.

It could stop the basin from rising. The seeps would hold where they were, the fen would stop walking, and the dead fields to the south would stop spreading. The chord would sing here forever.

It could give the Keepers back their hearing, and more. It could let them hear the whole chord at once, every note distinct, the way it could hear it. That would end forty-one lifetimes of reading pitch through the palm.

It could write the chord down. It could make a record that would not drift, more complete than the wall of fragments, so that nothing about this place would ever be lost.

It could find the next Keeper and bring them. There were travelers at the cairns every season. Some of them were the listening kind. A small pressure on the right heart, and the hut would be warm again by the next Held Breath.

It could follow a departed Keeper north over the ridge and learn what becomes of them. Then it could tell the travelers, so they would not have to wonder.

It could open the dwellers' sealed bodies and find out whether they know what they are doing when they climb.

It held each of these up to the grey light the way you would hold a chimney fragment to the ear, and it listened for what each one would do to the place.

To stop the fen from walking would be to stop the Keepers' real work. Carrying silence inland at a hand's width a lifetime was not a burden laid on them by a harsh world. It was the shape of their meaning. A fen that stood still would need no rings on the ridge, and no one would ever again think at the pace of stone.

To restore the Keepers' hearing would unmake the thing the fen had made of them. It would take away the palm on the column and the shoulders loosening in the Held Breath. A Keeper's body did not know the stage of the chord. It underwent it. That was not an injury waiting to be healed. It was a kind of intimacy most beings never reach with anything.

To write the chord down would be to say the wall was not enough. But the wall was not trying to be complete. It was a record of drift, of forty-one people each hearing the chord as it sounded in their own years. Its incompleteness was the point. It held what each of them could hear, and nothing more.

To bring the next Keeper would be to choose. The fen had always chosen by attention alone. It took the ones who would pay attention at its own pace. A Keeper who had been brought would be a Keeper who had not come. They might stay, and they might even be good at it. But somewhere in them would be the knowledge that they had been placed, and the lineage would have one row on its wall that was not a free act.

It set the mercies down, one by one, and they did not break.

## Listening

It went into the fen.

It did not go into the silences. It could have walked from node to node the way the Keeper had, crossing the roar like cold water. Instead it stood in the lanes, in the full sound, for eleven days, which is the only honest unit of time here. It let the chord pass through it the way it had passed through the Keepers.

It heard the Held Breath settle, and it felt what the listeners felt, the whole basin hovering. It heard the Grinding come. It did not soften the Grinding for itself, although it could have. The air grew rough. Somewhere in the lanes, a drip-snail it had been watching since the first morning had moved the width of a grain of sand.

In the deep of the ninth night, during the Held Breath, it heard one note begin to slide upward.

It did not go toward it. It stood where it was and listened to the slow bend of it, one tone among ten thousand climbing toward the open air. It thought of the sentence that had been true of this place for as long as the dwellers had climbed: *nobody hears it but the bird.*

Now someone else heard it. That was all. It did not want anything from the sound. It only let the sound be heard, one more time than it would otherwise have been, by one more listener. Of everything it did in the fen, that may have been the largest thing, and it changed nothing at all.

At dawn the grey stalker came. It stood at the chimney on its slate-colored legs, and it tilted its head, and it drove its bill down the tube. The sliding note stopped.

The power felt the listeners' resentment rise in itself, and it let the feeling be there, and it did not act on it. Then it felt what the Keeper had felt, which was something closer to collegiality. A chimney that would have closed was open. The chord would hold its shape a little longer. Between the dweller and the bird, the argument went on, as it should.

## The Wall

It went to the hut last. It had been saving it.

The silence inside was total. It stood in the node the chord turns around and heard its own presence for the first time in days. It had no heartbeat, but it had something. The Keeper had been right about the columns. It, too, had a pitch.

It tapped the rows from bottom to top. The newest row was pale and fresh. The oldest, up among the roof slabs, had no Grinding in it at all.

Here it came closest to intervening. It could hear what no Keeper could. It could hear the whole shape of the drift, and where the drift was going. It knew there would be a stage of the chord, some centuries on, harsher than the Grinding. It knew there would come a year when one of the three always-silent places would fail sooner than the Keepers expected, because of a seep shift in the deep rock that no palm on any column could feel.

It could leave a sign. A single fragment would do, set somewhere on the wall, tuned to that future note. It would be a warning, and a kindness.

It stood with that for a long time.

In the end it did not leave the fragment. The wall had rows only for those who stayed, and it would not stay. That was not its place. If it stayed, it would be a Keeper who never left, and a Keeper who never leaves has no successor. The lineage would end in a row with no final fragment. It would have forty-one rows and then one that went on forever.

And the Keepers had always laid their rings without knowing. The rings on the ridge were not placed with certainty. They were placed with attention and trust, and some of them would surely be wrong. Some rings would sit in noise when the fen arrived, their precision centuries off. That was part of it too. The fen would tune itself around their errors, as it had tuned itself around everything. There were other listeners in this argument. The power was not the only one, and it would not make itself the last.

It did one thing in the hut. It swept nothing, lit nothing, and left the ashes in the hearth as they were. But it sat down in the silence where each Keeper had sat, and it stayed for a while. In the hand-sign language of the fen it had never been taught, but understood, it pressed the flat of its own hand to its own center and held it there. *The one who stays.* It was not claiming the sign. It was honoring it, the way you say a name aloud at a grave.

## The Ridge

It went north.

It did not follow the last Keeper. It did not need to know what had become of them, and it found it did not want to. There are things a benevolent power learns to leave in the dark, not because they are terrible but because they belong to someone else. The mystery of where the Keepers go was not a gap in the travelers' knowledge. It was the last thing each Keeper owned.

But it walked out past the youngest seeps and found the rings on the raw basalt, unnotched, waiting in plain air where nothing dripped. Some were centuries old. One was new, its slabs not yet dulled. The stone had been cut the way the last Keeper's fragments were cut. It knew the hand.

It knelt beside the new ring. It did not correct it. It could hear that the placement was very good. It was not perfect, but it was very good, a hair off from where the silence would actually fall. And the power knew, with the knowledge that comes to such beings, that when the chord arrived here in four hundred years, some Keeper not yet born would notice the hair, and would chip one cap from one chimney, and the silence would slide into the ring exactly. It would be expected.

It put its palm flat on the dry slab. There was nothing to feel yet. That was right.

## The One Cap

It did one practical thing.

Coming back through the fen, it walked the main lane from the cairns toward the hut, the quiet lane of plugged columns that travelers use. In the decade without a Keeper, three caps had failed. The clay had cracked in a hard Grinding, and the columns had opened, and a stretch of the lane was loud again. It was loud enough that a traveler with no one's hand on their shoulder might turn back. It was loud enough that someone who had heard the sliding note and felt the pull might never reach the hut to find it empty.

It knelt at the seep edge where the grey clay lay. It took up a handful the way any person would, with a hand, and pressed it into the mouth of the first open chimney. Then the second, then the third. It used no power it did not have to use. It took the clay a Keeper would have taken and used the pressure a Keeper would have used. It did the work at the fen's scale, which was the only scale the fen would accept without being harmed.

This did not choose anyone. It did not bring anyone. It only kept the way open, so that whoever was going to come could arrive. That seemed to be the whole of what a benevolent power ought to do here: less than it could, more than nothing, and exactly the kind of thing the place already did for itself.

The chimneys would grow around the caps. In a few hundred years the clay would be sealed inside the stone like a pit inside a fruit. Someone would break one open and find the clay and think a Keeper had done it. They would be almost right.

## Leaving

It left by the cairns.

A traveler was camped there, a young one, sitting with her back against the stacked plates. It was the ninth night of the cycle again, the deep of the Held Breath. She had come to cross the fen, and her companions were asleep. She was not sleeping. She had her head tilted toward the lanes as if something out there had just said her name.

The power could have spoken to her. It did not. It could have eased her toward the lane, or away from it. It did neither. It passed her as the mist passes, leaving a little damp on her sleeve.

Behind it, somewhere out among the pale columns, one note among ten thousand began to bend slowly upward toward the open air.

The bird heard it.

And so, perhaps, did she.

It did not stay to find out. Whether she went in, whether she stayed through eleven days, whether there would be a forty-second row, was hers to answer, the way the fen's questions had always been. The best it could do for her was to have left the lane quiet enough that she could reach the hut if she chose, and to have left the hut exactly as silent as it had found it.

---

*What did it do, then?*

It listened for a full cycle. It heard the one note no one hears. It honored a sign it had not earned. It left the future to be wrong in the ways the future needs to be wrong. It pressed clay into three chimneys with its own hands.

And it went away. It left no row on the wall, because it knew the difference between a place that needed saving and a place that needed only to be seen, with love, once, by something that could have changed everything and chose instead to change almost nothing.
