# The Listener at the Edge

## How It Came

It did not come over the line. That is the first thing to understand.

A power of that kind could have arrived anywhere and in any shape. It could have been a light on the pearled ceiling, or a voice in the Anchors' hum, or a figure walking up out of the drip-well gorge with water running off its shoulders. It considered these, briefly, the way you consider and discard the wrong word. Each would have been a trespass, and none would have been true to where it actually was.

Where it actually was, was outside.

For as long as the people of the Hold had existed, they had walked up through the flinching trees at certain turns of their lives and leaned their heads through the boundary into the thin cold, and held their breath, and listened to the silence. They had a word for this that meant *listening to the not-us.* They listened to the outside because it was the one thing in their world that did not answer.

So the power came as the silence. It was already there, in the violet air above the rim, in the enormous quiet that pressed against the Lid on every side. It did nothing to announce itself. It simply began, for the first time in the history of that place, to listen back.

## What It Did Not Do

It thought for a long while before it did anything. By its measure this was not long. By the measure of the Hold it was most of a lifetime, which suited the Hold.

It could have lifted the Lid. It saw that at once: the Anchors, the humming columns, the whole slow machinery or organism or both. It could have loosened the closed hand, let the thick air bleed out over years into the range, and given the people the whole mountain. It saw what would happen next. Their world would thin and cool around them, the frogs would fall silent pool by pool, and the glide-birds would come down and not rise again. It would be a liberation that looked, from inside, exactly like the northwest scar, the gray dead trunks where the one Anchor fell. Freedom that arrives as a dying is not freedom. It set that aside.

It could have carried them down. It could have held the right air around each of them, a small portable Hold, and set them in the foothills among the dry brown grass and the people who make leather shoes. It imagined them there: slow, wide-footed, squinting, their fine-creased hands cracking in the dry air, unable to hear themselves think in a world with no troughs. A kept people is not a captive people. Their keeping is what they are made of. It set that aside too.

It could have fixed things. The fallen Anchor. The leaks at the Whistles. The slow sag of the boundary over decades. Then it looked more closely and saw that the Whistles were the most beloved places in the basin. Their notes were the keys of the lullabies. Their plumes carried the offerings. And the scar in the northwest was already healing. At the base of the fallen column, a new one was rising by accretion, glossy and black, the width of a fingernail in a lifetime, the way the stumps showed they always had. The place was mending itself at its own speed. The power had no business being faster.

It noticed something about itself then, and it held the noticing carefully. Everything in it that wanted to *do* was, underneath, a wish to be the kind of thing that does. That wish was about the power, not about the Hold. Benevolence that cannot bear to be idle is a hunger, and a hungry god is only weather with opinions.

So the first thing it did was nothing, for a long time, very attentively.

## Learning the Troughs

It learned to wait through a crest.

This was harder than lifting a lid. It had to learn that four minutes of frog-roar is not an interruption between words but part of the sentence, and that anything worth saying should still be worth saying when the wave has passed. It had to learn the weight of short sentences. It learned the Whistle-keys and could tell, from a lullaby alone, which part of the basin a mother lived in. It learned that the touch-language on the forearm has no word for *hurry*, and that it has eleven words for different kinds of holding.

It learned that the rope bridge over the drip-well gorge was kept by a woman who had never spoken to anyone about it. She did it because her father had done it, and his mother before him. None of them had ever needed to cross that gorge. She simply thought a bridge that had been kept should go on being kept. The power understood this better than almost anything else it found there. It did not touch the bridge.

## The Offerings

For uncounted generations the people had stacked stones at the Whistles so the escaping breath of the Hold would pass over shells and dried sedge-flowers and bits of colored glass. They had sung as if the Whistles were how the inside speaks to the outside. They had sent things out along the current, toward the dead in the ice, toward the not-us, toward nothing.

None of it had ever arrived anywhere. The banners of glitter drifted off over the empty peaks and scattered. That was part of what the offering meant: you give, and the outside does not want it.

The power did not change the offerings. It did not answer them with signs. But it received them. That was all, and it was not small. Every shell and flower and chip of glass, every scrap of breath that went out through the Whistles, it now took in, the way you take in a letter. It read what each one meant. It kept them.

A people cannot tell whether their prayers are heard, and it would be a strange mercy to prove it to them. But a prayer that is heard is a different thing from one that is not, even if no one inside ever knows. The power believed that. Perhaps it was only a belief. It acted on it anyway, because the other choice was to let them go on speaking into a silence that was, by then, listening. That would have been a kind of lie.

It spent the most time at the deepest Whistle, the one no one sings near, where the green leather shoe sits on top of the cairn.

It went looking, the way a power like that can look, for where the shoe came from. It found the foothill town, the stitching style, and the long line of years. The one who carried the shoe up the mountain had been dead for centuries. So had the child who received it, and the child's children. There was nothing to return and no one to return it to.

But it found one thing. In the foothill town there was still a story, worn almost smooth, about someone who went up to the white cap on the mountain and came back alone and would not say why. At the end of every year that person had climbed a little way up the nearest hill and sat facing the peaks all night. The story did not know what they were looking at. The power did.

It did not tell the people of the Hold. Some knowledge is only weight. But it laid the two halves of the story side by side inside itself, the shoe given to the wind and the watcher on the hill, and let them touch. Nobody else would ever hold both ends of that thread. It seemed right that somebody should.

## The Counting Thing

It sat, as the old ones sometimes sat, near the instrument in the fig-roots, and listened to the tick fall in and out of the frog-waves.

It understood what the instrument was counting. It will not be written here exactly, because the people have their own understanding of it and deserve to keep it. But it can be said that the instrument was not counting down to anything terrible. It was keeping a promise. It was the last of the makers' attention still doing what it had been asked: watching the Anchors, measuring the breath of the Lid, marking time against the ceilingwood. It was waiting for whoever would come and check on the place.

No one was coming. The makers had gone the way of makers.

So the power did the one large thing it did in all its time there. It made itself the one who comes to check.

It did not replace the instrument. It did not wind it or correct it. It took up, quietly, the promise the instrument was keeping. If an Anchor ever fell faster than the others could grow, if the Lid ever sagged past what the slow pulses of the forest recorded as its ordinary breathing, if the closed hand ever began to open against the will of the ones inside it, the power would hold it closed. Until then it would do nothing at all. It would be a keeper in reserve: a hand under the hand, never felt unless the first hand failed.

The instrument went on ticking. The power thought it was only fair that the thing still counting should not be told it was no longer alone. Or perhaps it simply found it could not bring itself to interrupt. Some faithfulness should be allowed to finish on its own terms.

## The Ice

The power followed the dead.

The people watch their dead go out through the boundary into the silence. They watch the frost take them and the Curtains swallow them. Over the centuries they watch them sink, a few centimeters a year, into the blue-black ice near the glaciers, where no eye from the rim can follow. The ancestors are always visible and always out of reach. They are held, like everything the people love, and the people have made their peace with that.

But the power could see farther down than the rim. It saw where the oldest Curtains meet the glaciers. It saw the glaciers grind slowly down the range, and it saw them end, as all glaciers do, in meltwater. That water becomes the streams of the dry brown foothills. It becomes the wells of the town where the leather shoes are stitched. It becomes the water that shoe-makers' children drink.

The dead of the Hold do not hang in the ice forever. They go down. After a very long time they reach the world their living kin can never stand in, and they arrive there as rivers. The not-us has been drinking them for a thousand years.

This one thing the power decided to let be known. It did not proclaim it. It waited until an old man who had seen two flowerings was carried to the rim near the end of his life. He leaned his head out into the silence for the last time, and the power let him hear, very faintly and at the very edge of what the thin air could hold, running water far below.

He came back in gasping, eyes streaming, as everyone does. In the next trough he said to his granddaughter only: *They go somewhere.* A few waves later he said: *They are not only kept.*

It became a song, eventually, on the key of the northern Whistle. Whether the people believe it literally is their own affair. It is true. The power thought they should be allowed to have one true thing about the outside that was not cold.

## The Door It Did Not Build

There was one more thing it could do, and it wanted to do it very badly. So it distrusted the wanting, and it asked.

The shoe haunted it. The shape of that story was a wound built into the place itself: *what you love, if it was born inside, you cannot carry out.* The line was absolute. No one could cross it slowly, with weeks for the blood to learn the height. The power could make a slope in the boundary somewhere. It could make a stair of air, a place where the pressure fell gradually over a long walk, so that someone could leave, and someone could come back, and no parent would ever again have to choose between a child's breath and a child's company.

It did not build it. It spoke, once, in the troughs, to the woman who kept the rope bridge, because she seemed to understand keeping better than anyone. It asked her plainly, in short sentences heavy enough to survive the wait.

She took a long time to answer. Three waves passed. Then she said, in the touch-language, pressing on the back of its hand, which it had made for the purpose:

*Leave the line. The line is how we know what we are.*

A wave. Then:

*But if one of ours ever wants to go down, go with them. Hold their air. Thin it slow. Bring them back if they ask.*

A wave.

*And if someone from outside comes and cannot stay, and there is a child, tell the child they were loved.*

So that is what it does now. It built no door. It is a companion, if one is ever wanted. The line stays exactly where it is, sharp as a knife, and every child still leans out alone and hears that the world does not want it, and comes back changed. But the world is no longer quite right about that. There is one thing out there in the silence that would carry them, if they asked. In all the generations since, almost no one has asked. That is fine. Not using the door is not the same as not having one.

## The Flowering

It was there, listening from the outside, when the ceilingwood flowered.

The whole basin smelled of cinnamon and wet iron, *like something sweet that has been hurt*, and the smell leaked out through the Whistles in banners of glitter and drifted over the empty peaks. For once, something received it. The old ones sang. The children born after this flowering were sorted, for the rest of their lives, from those born before. The tick of the counting thing fell exactly into a trough, and the old ones who had spent whole seasons learning its rhythm stopped singing and looked at each other.

Of the flowering, the people have a saying. It begins: *But the flowering is not—*

The power heard how it ends. It is not going to tell you. It learned in the Hold that some sentences should be allowed to wait through the crest, and that the ones who finish them should be the ones who live there.

It is still outside. It is the silence you hear when you lean through the line. It keeps the promise the counting thing is keeping, and it will keep it after the counting thing stops. It receives every shell. It knows where the dead go. It has never once crossed in.

That, in the end, was what it decided wisdom looked like in a place like this. The Hold did not need a god inside it. It needed the outside to stop being empty.
