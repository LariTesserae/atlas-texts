# The Visitor

## How It Noticed

It did not come because of the beauty, though the place is beautiful. The world holds many green rooms, and a power of that kind has seen more of them than there are shrimp in this one.

It came because of the silence.

Long ago, on the day the stroke did not come, every engine in the mountain stopped at once. The thunder that had run through the made stone for ages went quiet, and that quiet traveled. It went out through the footings and into the bedrock, through the lowland marshes and under the sea, faint as a held breath, and anything in the world able to hear such things heard it. Then the stroke came back, and the world went on, and almost everything that had heard the silence forgot it.

The power did not forget. It is benevolent, and it is wise, and one part of its wisdom is knowing that when something enormous stops and then starts again, someone decided. It took the power a long time to arrive, because it was attending to other things and because it moves with care. But it had marked the place, and eventually it came.

It did not come in through the window. The window belongs to the fish.

It came up the ladder.

---

## The Ladder

It took the smallest shape that could still climb, which was roughly the builder's shape. That seemed only polite. It rose through the mountain's long interior, past chamber after chamber, pump after pump, and came up through the floor of the Header Tank beside the worn ladder with the snail-polished rungs. Then it climbed, one knob at a time, the way the builder had.

The shrimp scattered on the downstroke and came back on the upstroke, and after a while they stopped scattering. A thing that moved in time with the room was simply part of the room.

At chest height it stopped beside the blank plate and put its hand flat against the metal, which was as smooth as the inside of a shell. Then it turned and looked up at the fish.

The fish did not look back. It could not afford to. Its fins moved a fraction of a second before the push, as they always did, and it held its finger's width beneath the glass.

The power watched for a whole day. It watched the frost-hour and the spawning, the column of light sharpening and swaying, the eels coming up to drape over the lip of the sleeve and turn their blind faces toward the dome. It watched the fish lean into the stroke about eight thousand times without once being moved.

By evening it understood what it was looking at, and it understood how many ways there were to get this wrong.

---

## What It Did Not Do

A power of that kind can do nearly anything, so its real work is choosing, and it went through the choices one by one.

**It could free the fish.** It could lift the chain from its jaw, let it rise, let it press its body to the glass and feel the cold and be finished. The fish may even have wanted that once. But then the arm would lift and the signal would go down, and every pump in the mountain would stop. The weed would stand upright. The shrimp would hang in water that had no current to carry them home. The eels would be caught in the dark branches with no stroke to ride back up. The fish would have its rest, and everything it had spent its whole existence holding would die of it. That would not be mercy. It would only be the power's discomfort at watching, resolved at someone else's expense.

**It could replace the fish.** It could fix the float so it held itself, set the arm permanently at *not yet*, and send the fish off to eat and wander and grow slow and ordinary among the weed. That was more tempting, and the power looked at it longer. But the fish had been choosing for longer than the mountain's lowest chambers had been dry, and it had chosen well. It had come back from the edge once, by itself. To take the choice away without asking would say, in the only language that mattered here, *your holding was never necessary; I could have done it all along.* That might be true of the mechanism. It was not true of the fish. The power would not rob anyone of their own faithfulness in order to spare them its weight. It knows that difference better than most, because it lives on that line itself.

**It could restore the plate.** It could raise the builders' words back up out of the metal, sharp and legible. But no one was left to read them, and the words had become something else by now: red bands in spiral shells, a road map, a record. The snails had eaten the message and written their own. It would be strange to correct them.

**It could fix the pump,** ease the lowland engines on cold nights, make the stroke perfect. But the eels had built their entire knowledge on the stroke's imperfection. A perfect stroke would make them strangers in their own nerves.

**It could do nothing.** It considered that one seriously. Here was a place that worked. It was alive and balanced and old, and a great many harms have been done by powers who could not bear to leave a working thing alone. That the tank had drawn the attention of something that could change everything was not, by itself, a reason for anything to change.

But the power had watched the fish for a day, and it had seen one thing it could not leave alone. It was not the fish's burden. The fish had chosen its burden. It was the *exactness* of it.

A finger's width. No meal, ever, because a single shrimp would tip the balance. Every stroke, all day, for ages, with no margin for error at all, no moment when the fish could be even slightly late. The fish was not suffering from its purpose. It was being worn down by having no room inside its purpose. That was not the builders' intention. The power was fairly sure of it, and it went to find out.

---

## Down

It let the downstroke take it.

It went through the grilles and into the conduits, the way the shed shells go and the old leaves and, in the breeding season, the eels. It traveled the long pipes down through the made mountain, branch after branch, and found that the Header Tank was not alone. There were chambers everywhere, thousands of them. Some were dark and full of pale crabs. Some were warm and thick with reeds. Some had windows onto the mountain's slopes, where the water ran out into gullies and fed lichen, moss, and the roots of small twisted trees. The mountain was alive all the way down, and every bit of that life lived by the stroke. The power noted each chamber as it passed, the way you might touch the heads of children in a line.

At the bottom, where the conduits emptied into the great lowland marsh, the water spread out across a wide green country. There were cranes, reeds taller than a person, and villages on stilts whose people drank from springs they believed had always been there. They did not know about the mountain. They did not know about the fish. They were alive because of a decision made eleven seconds at a time by something they would never see, and the power did not tell them. Some debts are lighter when they are not known, and some gifts are cleaner when no one has to be grateful for them.

In the gutters where the springs ran off, among the silt, the power found shells: small, red, very old, their bands faded, tumbled smooth by a long journey. Hundreds of them had washed up here across the ages and settled into the mud.

It gathered them. It did this gently, and put each one back where it had found it after reading it, but it read them. It laid them side by side in its mind the way the snails had laid them down: the deep red of the dome wall, the orange of the piston rim, and, in the oldest ones, a darker trace. That was the rust from the stamped grooves of the letters.

The words were not all there. They never would be. Too many shells had gone to the sea. But enough remained that the power could read some fragments:

**...LOAT ... HOLD LEVEL ...**
**... ALLOW PLAY ...**
**... ADJUST BEFORE WINTER.**

The power stood in the reeds at the bottom of the world for a long moment.

*Allow play.* The builders had known. A float held too tight wears itself out. Every good float valve has a little slack in it, a band within which it can drift without the valve answering, so it does not have to fight every ripple. The builders had written it on the wall as a maintenance note, for whoever came up to check: *leave it room. Loosen it for the cold.*

Nobody had come up. The float had become a fish, the fish had held the level for ages with no play at all, and on the coldest nights, when the stroke lagged and the builders' note said *adjust*, no one had adjusted anything. The fish had simply held on harder.

The power did not have to invent anything, then. It only had to do what the plate said, and what nobody had been there to do.

---

## Up

It rode the long return. It came up through the thousand chambers on the upstroke with the warmth and the silt, and rose through the piston sleeve past the eels, who felt it in the gap and made room. It came up over the lip into the tank at the top of the stroke, draped there a moment the way the returners do, and looked up at the fish.

Then it climbed the ladder again.

It did not go to the fish first. It went to the housing in the dome, the small sharp-edged thing with its pivot and short arm, the one object in the whole room that time had never rounded. It laid its hand on it.

What it did then was very small. With its hand it did what the water and the moss and the ages had done to everything else: it wore the pivot soft. It rounded the edge of the arm's travel. It gave the linkage a little slack, a finger's width either way, so that the arm could drift within that band and the signal down the mountain would not change. Past the band, the float would still speak and the valves would still answer. The fish's choice was still the fish's choice. *Not yet* still had to be said. But now it could be said with some slack in the voice.

It did this during the frost-hour, when the light was milky and the shrimp were spawning and the room was at its softest, so that the change would come quietly.

Then it went to the fish.

---

## The Conversation

They did not speak. The fish had no words, and the power did not want to impose any. Instead it spoke in the only language the tank has, which is timing.

It held itself beside the fish, just below the glass, at exactly the same depth. It leaned into the stroke a fraction of a second early, as the fish did. It matched it for a hundred strokes, then a thousand. It was not trying to take over. It was saying, the way one keeper says to another, *I know what this is. I am doing it too.*

Somewhere in those thousand strokes, the fish noticed.

The power could tell because the fish, for the first time in an age, moved its eye.

Then, very slowly, the power did one thing differently. On a single upstroke it did not lean in early. It let the push arrive and catch it, and it drifted up half a finger's width toward the frost. The arm in the housing moved with it, and nothing happened. The stroke came back. The mountain did not stop.

It settled back to its place and leaned in early again, and waited.

It did not know whether the fish would understand. It had decided beforehand that if the fish did not, that would be all right. The slack was there now whether the fish ever used it or not. It was not a gift that needed to be accepted to be real. A gift that has to be used before it counts is only another burden.

For a long time, the fish held exactly as it always had.

Then, one morning, the frost grew especially thick. The ferns spread white across the glass, and the lowland engines ran sluggish with the cold, and the stroke began to lag. Before this, the fish would have held harder on a morning like that. It would have braced against the slowness and fought the drift, because it had no room to do anything else.

This time it let the lag come. It drifted up. The cold of the glass was very close above it, closer than it had let itself come since the day the world went quiet. It hung there, half a finger's width higher than it had allowed itself in ages, and the arm moved with it, and nothing stopped.

The fish stayed there for one stroke. It seemed to feel the cold and the rest so near and the violet sky beyond the frost. Then it sank back, on its own, to its place.

The stroke came. The fish had leaned into it a moment before, as always.

But it had been allowed to be late once, and it knew that now.

---

## What It Left

The power stayed a little longer. Then it did what the builder had done. It looked once more through the window at the violet sky, put its hand once more on the blank plate, and went back down the ladder into the mountain and out into the world, where it had other places to attend to.

It did not tell the fish it was leaving. That seemed right. The best help it could give was help the fish would not have to hold onto, because the fish already had one thing to hold.

It left nothing the tank would recognize as a gift. There was no new creature, no light that had not been there before, no restored words, no end to the stroke. Anyone who came up the ladder afterward would find the room just as it had always been: warm, green, swaying, breathing every eleven seconds, with the old dark fish below the glass.

But a few things were different, if you knew to look.

The housing in the dome had been rounded like everything else, so the room no longer had one sharp thing in it.

The fish, on the coldest mornings, now drifted up to the frost and came back down. On summer noons, sometimes, when the stroke was strong and steady and the arm was near the middle of its band, the fish ate. It did not eat much. One shrimp, lofted up on the upstroke from the weed, was taken so quickly and quietly that the others hardly noticed. The arm twitched inside its new slack, and nothing happened. The mountain kept breathing. Somewhere in the lowlands a valve the size of a house did not close.

The eels noticed. When they came back from the dark branches and draped themselves over the lip of the sleeve and turned their blind faces toward the dome, they found the fish a little higher some days and a little lower others. The first few times, the older returners went very still. But the stroke kept coming, and they learned. Now they tasted for something slightly different. They no longer checked only that the fish was *holding*. They checked that it was *there*, which is not quite the same thing.

The snails noticed last, and recorded it in the only way they can.

That season, and every season after, they laid down no pale band. There was no grey line and no stretch of fright written into the spirals. There was only red, purple, and orange, the ordinary colors of grazing in a room where nothing had gone wrong. If you broke open a shell from that year and read it, you would find no sign that anything had visited. The record says only that the level held.

That is what the power would have wanted, if it had been asked. It had come because it heard the world go silent once, and it wanted to make sure that silence never had to be the only way the fish could rest.

---

Every morning the frost still grows on the outside of the window, spreading its white ferns across the glass. The shrimp release their eggs into the milky light. The snails stop and lift their heads.

The fish rises half a finger's width into the cold and feels the rest so near above it, and the violet sky beyond. It stays there for the length of one breath.

Then it sinks back to its place, freely, because it wants to.

The push comes, long and loaded and ringing, and the fish leans into it, sometimes a moment before and sometimes, now, a moment after. The mountain breathes either way, and far below, the water begins its long climb again.
