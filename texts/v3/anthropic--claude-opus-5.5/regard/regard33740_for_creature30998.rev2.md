# What Came to the Crown

## Arriving

It had been watching the world for a long time, and it noticed the Crown because the Crown had a note.

Most places don't. Most places are noise: a thousand small motives crossing and cancelling. But on the heavy nights, when the charged cloud came up the flanks and drew itself across the Harp, the whole dome sounded one low tone that went down through the grus and out through the wires, and something that listens to the world heard it and turned.

Its first act was a correction to itself. It understood almost at once that arriving was the danger. On the Crown, anything that came quickly was loud, and everything loud was either swallowed or broken. A boulder that moved fast went over. A voice raised was a voice lost. The spark gaps cracked, and that was the town venting what it could not hold. A power that came down out of the sky in a blaze of attention would be, to this place, a heavy day with nowhere to ground.

So it did not come down. It came up, the way everything comes to the Crown: in the cloud, with the tide. It took a whole season to arrive, because it wanted to arrive at the Crown's speed. It climbed the flanks with the grey wall each mid-morning and drained off the rim with it each dawn, and every day it came a little farther in, until it could pass through the Harp without making a single wire fizz.

By then it had learned the first rule. *Keep moving.* And it had learned the second, the one nobody says: *but slowly*.

## The Things It Did Not Do

It could have done almost anything. Benevolence here was never a question of power. It was a question of what to leave alone, and the power spent a long time on that list.

It could have made the granite whole again: run the rot backward through every grain boundary, knit feldspar out of kaolin, and given the Crown its edges back. It looked at this for a long while. Then it saw that the Crown had spent longer than there had been life in becoming soft, and that the softness was not a wound. It was an achievement. It was what the mountain had done with its time. You do not repair an old woman's face into a girl's and call it kindness.

It could have burned the cloud away and given the people sun. It looked into their wide dark pupils, made over generations for the pearl, and at the children crying at their first dawn, and it understood that the clear hour was precious because it was an hour. Given all day, it would be only a glare in a place full of people who had to squint.

It could have stopped the moss. It followed that choice to its end. The floors would never close. The dead would lie in open rooms forever, visited, disturbed, unkept. The town would have to learn to dig graves in clay it sold. The stairwells would go down to nothing held. The old ones would never stop and would never walk out into the Harp, and it could not be sure that was a mercy. It put the idea down.

It could have steadied the current and ended the rationing. The lamps would stop breathing. The ebb would become merely night, and the careful hour, the hour when no one starts things or argues, would go the way of anything no longer needed. It left the lamps to pulse.

It could have stopped the pits. The town would starve. Beyond that, it was not certain the Crown wanted to be kept whole. The people believed it wanted to leave. The power, listening, suspected they were right.

It could have told them what the greycoats were.

It knew. It knew within a week. It did not tell them, for the reason the kiln-keepers don't tell: some knowledge belongs to the place where it is kept, and handing it out does not make anyone richer. It only makes the place poorer.

And it could have told the Crown what the Crown was: stone that has already become sand but has not yet been told. This was the one it thought about longest. It set it aside for later, the way the town sets aside things it does not start in the ebb.

## What It Learned

It learned the lamp codes. That was the first thing it did that anyone might have seen, if anyone had been looking at the right pylon on the right night. A light high on the lattice where no wireman was, lifting once: *I'm here.* A wirewoman on the next tower lifted hers back out of pure habit before she stopped and stared. By then there was only fog.

It learned that here you put a hand on an arm. It learned to be a presence that could be leaned against.

It spent a winter at Orla Tenn's window, five storeys up, over two floors she had never seen. It came as the kind of stillness a room has when someone is in it whom you cannot see. Orla noticed on the third night. She watered the pale moss in its porcelain dish, looked at the empty chair, and said, low, as everyone on the Crown says everything:

*You can stay if you keep moving.*

So it did. It moved very slowly around her rooms all winter, never settling, so that the green never found it, and Orla talked. She told it about the dozen mosses: the one that smells of pepper, the one that bleeds rust. She told it about her mother, who had gone to grus. She told it that she was not afraid of stopping, only of stopping before the Weir Street step was done. She said she had never once finished that step in forty years, and that this was the best thing about it.

It went down with the sinkers. A wireman died in the thaw, not by the charge but by his heart, at fifty-one, halfway up a pylon with a pell nesting three insulators above him. His crew brought him down, and the sinkers came up for him, and the power followed the lamps past the storerooms, past the damp rooms, down, down through the cold. It went into a parlour where the wallpaper had gone the colour of tea, where they laid him on a sofa and set beside him the cup he had used every morning for thirty years. One of the sinkers, a girl of perhaps nineteen, with eyes that were nearly all pupil, turned her head toward the place where the power was standing.

*You're heavy,* she said. *Not your weight.*

*I'm sorry,* it said. This was the first time it had spoken on the Crown.

She considered. *No. It's all right. They like company down here.* She closed the door.

And on a thick night in the outer ranks, it met a greycoat.

They stood still together in the dripping Harp, both of them, and both of them became boulders. For a long time there were three grey shapes where there had been two corestones, and no lamp to tell which was which. The power knew who the greycoat had been. It had been a Marrow of the cable-house, the one in the kiln-keepers' story, who had once sat at the winding-house window for a season while the moss came up to her shoulders, listening to the cable over the sheaves. The power reached toward her very carefully. It wanted to find out whether anything in her needed saving.

Nothing did. She was the most content thing it had met on the whole mountain, and it had met the moss.

That settled something. Nothing on the Crown was waiting to be rescued. Nearly everything it had taken for loss was keeping.

Nearly.

## The One Thing

There was one thing wrong, and it was not wrong in the way the power had half expected. There was no cruelty in it. The Crown is not cruel, as Orla said. But it is not careful either.

The diggers had opened a new pit east of the Tallow quarter, where the white clay ran thick. Under it, deeper than any digger could see, the spring melt had found a seam of kaolin gone to grease and begun running along it, under the Tallow quarter itself, toward the rim. When the thaw came fully, the whole quarter would let go. It would not fall. It would pour: a slow fan of wet sand over the edge and into the cloud, carrying four storeys of lived-in rooms and six of sealed ones, and some two hundred people asleep in the upper floors.

The power could hold it up. That would be easy. But holding it up meant keeping what the Crown was letting go, and then going on keeping it, always, a power propping a mountain from beneath. The town would never know, and would build on the propped place, and depend on it.

It could warn them. It could speak with a voice in every Tallow window at once, and the town would obey. Then, the next time the grus shifted, they would wait for the voice. And the time after that. A town of listeners would become a town of waiters.

So it did something smaller. For three weeks, only in the lowest floors, it made the creak of the grus a little louder.

It made nothing new. It only took the sound the mountain was already making, the slow settle of the seam letting go, and turned it up by a hair, the way the throwers widen a cup, for the only people on the Crown who ever listen to the mountain from inside.

The sinkers heard. And they came up.

They almost never come up except for the dead. So when four of them climbed into the Tallow quarter in daylight, in the pearl, and went from stack to stack putting their pale hands on people's arms and speaking low, nobody argued. The town has always understood that when the sinkers come up, somebody is leaving. This time they said it was everybody, and that they should go now, while they could still walk.

It took a month. People carried their lamps and their porcelain and their children across the catwalks into other stacks, into kiln rows, into the winding-house, where the Marrows made room around the drum. Then, in the thaw, on a thin day in the early pearl, the Tallow quarter went over.

It was slow. That was the strangest part, people said afterward. It was so slow you could watch it. The ground sighed, and the stacks leaned, and the whole quarter slid toward the rim like a table being drawn across a floor, and went over the edge into the cloud with a long soft grinding the moss couldn't swallow. The sealed floors went with it, and the dead went with them: generations of Tallow ancestors in their rooms, cups beside them, out into the grey.

The town stood at the rim in silence. One of the sinkers who had sounded the warning, an old man, said what the power had hoped someone would say:

*They're kept either way.*

The power did not know if it had done right. It had spared the living and let the dead go over. It had made itself neither a wall nor an oracle, only a hair of extra loudness in a sound that was already true. Afterward the sinkers said *the Crown told us*, and the power let that stand, because it was true enough, and because it was better for the Crown to be the one who warned them.

It went down to the parlour where the wireman lay, which was nowhere near Tallow and was safe, and sat with him a while. It did not know what else to do with the not-knowing, so it kept it.

## The Question of Telling

Then there was only the last thing on the list.

On a heavy night, in the ebb, with the lamps pulsing slow and every head in Kell lifted in its faint halo, the power went down into the grus below the lowest floor and below the first Kell. It went into the stone that has already become sand, and it spoke to the Crown in the only language the Crown had, which was the hum.

It had come to tell the mountain what it was. It found that the Crown already knew.

Of course it knew. It had been rotting from the inside since before there was anything alive to watch. It knew it was sand. It knew that every crate going down the funicular was a piece of itself, and it knew it was nearly finished. *Not yet told* had never meant ignorance. It meant that nobody had said it yet out loud, to its face, and the Crown was in no hurry to hear it. The power understood, then, that saying it would help no one. It would only make the power the one who had said it. It did not say it.

But in listening, it found out what the Crown did not know. The Crown keeps everything: hearths, clocks, the dead, the cups on the tables, the old ones in their coats of moss. That is its nature and its whole generosity, and the town has learned it and does the same. What the Crown had never been able to answer, in all the long wearing, was the question every keeper eventually asks.

*And who keeps me?*

So the power told it.

It said: *You are already being kept.* It told the Crown about a hospital on a lowland plain lined with tile made from its rotten heart. It told it about a woman in a city who drinks her tea every morning from a Kell cup she inherited, and who has never wondered where the clay came from, and turns the cup in her hands without knowing why. It told it about a thousand kilometres of line strung on white bells humming in lowland storms, sounding a faint small note that is the Crown's. It told it about the grey hands of the people, which carry its white lines in their palms until they are carried down themselves. It told it about the throwers' moulds, a hair deeper every generation. *Just in case.*

And then it offered the one thing it could give that nothing on the mountain could.

*After the last grain has gone over,* it said, *after the town, after the cups have broken on tables you will never see, after the pells, I will remember you. The whole shape of you. The dome, the loaves of stone, the Harp dripping in the dark. The step on Weir Street that was never finished. I will keep you the way you have kept everything. That, I can do.*

The Crown did not answer that night. Mountains are slow.

But a week later the wind came across the dome, and the Harp sang its long thin chord, the one that never resolves, the only sound that carries on the Crown. And once, for perhaps the length of a breath, it resolved. Every wireman on the pylons heard it. They argued about it for years at the kiln walls. Some said it was a trick of the gusts. Some said it was a new insulator pattern. One old wirewoman, who had heard the Harp for sixty years, said only that it sounded like somebody setting something down after carrying it a long way.

It never happened again. It did not need to.

## Leaving

The power left the way the Crown leaves: down the funicular, a crate at a time. It went in a crate of insulators, bells a hair deeper at the mouth. The Marrows in the winding-house felt the car go down heavier by nothing, and old Tam Marrow put his hand on the drum and frowned and said *well*, and nothing more. For most of an hour it rode inside grey nothing, listening to the cable over the sheaves.

There was one thing it had wanted to do more than anything else on the Crown, and it did not do it.

It had wanted to carry a pell down. It wanted just one, to a far insulator on a lowland line, so that the throwers' *just in case* would come true, and so that somewhere a bird of bells would call two notes in a storm on a plain, and the Crown would be living there, not just humming. It would have been easy, and it would have been lovely. It did not do it, because the throwers had already made that promise, generation by generation, in the moulds, and a promise kept for someone by someone else is not quite kept. The pells had waited for the bells before. They could wait again. The power left the cups a hair deeper and the birds free.

Years later, on a clear morning, a scraper stood at the rim with her empty barrow. She was not Orla, who had gone to grus by then and been carried down with her dish of moss beside her instead of a cup. She was another scraper, standing a moment longer than she needed to, looking out over the sea of cloud in the hour before the tide.

From below the rim, from somewhere down in the grey where there are no pylons and never have been, she heard two notes. A bright high *pe*, and a falling *ell*.

And from the Harp behind her, clear and startled in the muffled air, another pell answered.

She stood very still. Then, because it was the only thing a person on the Crown knows to do with a sound like that, she lifted her lamp high toward the cloud: *I'm here.*

Then the grey wall rose over the edge, and the light went soft, and the dripping began. The scraper turned back toward Weir Street, where the step still wasn't finished, and she kept moving. And the Crown, the town, and the thing that had come to them went on as everything there goes on, keeping what they had been given.
