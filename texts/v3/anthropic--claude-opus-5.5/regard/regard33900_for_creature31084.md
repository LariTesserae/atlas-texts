# The Attention

## First, the Weight

It noticed the city the way you notice a sound that has been going on for a long time: all at once, and with the feeling that it should have heard it sooner.

The first thing it learned was that it was too heavy.

Attention like its own has weight. Wherever it rests, things lean toward it. Water runs a little more eagerly downhill. Lichen turns. Across a whole world, people have looked up at the sky without knowing why. In most places this does no harm. But here the balance was so fine, and the city was so exactly the sum of small settled pressures, that when it first looked full at the plateau, a hundred thousand stones lifted by less than a hair's width, the way they lift in frost. In the morning an old man with an ear to a windward wall frowned and moved on. He had heard something he had no word for.

So before it did anything else, the power learned to look the way the fog comes: from low down, thinly, over a long time, touching every surface and resting on none.

## What It Did Not Do

It could have made the walls stand. That was the first temptation, and the easiest to refuse once it had watched a single spring. The collapses are not the city's failure. They are its breath. A wall that never fell would never be relaid, and the hands would forget their work. The bones in the eastern cells would stay in the eastern cells. The city would stop creeping and begin to be merely old. It would become a ruin that happened to be standing, not a landform that happened to be built.

It could have given them mortar, or the wheel, or a way of writing. They would have taken none of these gratefully. Or, worse, they would have.

It could have answered the argument about junipers. It looked into the question for a long time, the way it can look into things, following every root through every joint in every wall a juniper had ever grown from. It found that the question had no answer. More than that, the question was load-bearing. Somewhere in the long patience of that argument, passed from grandparent to grandchild in the open courtyards where words come out bare, the people had kept a place for the one thing the stone could not settle for them. An answer would have been a capstone dropped into that cell. It left the question open, and found that this was harder than answering would have been.

It did not keep the finches. It understood the longing and let it stand.

Above all, it did not let itself be known. A god who is seen is the largest mark there is. It would outlast its meaning, and it would point wrong forever.

There were harder things. A child in the lee districts took a fever in her fourth winter, and her breath went short and rattling in the close warmth of the sleeping cell. The power could have lifted it from her as easily as a finch lifts a seed. It did not know, then or afterward, whether it was right to wait. It waited. It watched the four bodies curve closer around hers, and the old woman lay her ruined palm on the child's back to read the breath the way she read a wall. It watched the fever break on the third night without it. It told itself that it had been ready. It was not sure that was enough. It carries the not-being-sure. That is part of what it is.

## The Taste of the Water

What it did do began with the water.

The old ones say the first water of the morning tastes of the lowlands. The power tasted it too, from every weeping joint at once, and it found that the old ones were right. It also found something they had not yet noticed, or had noticed only as a faint unease without a name. Year on year, the taste was growing thinner.

It followed the cloud back down.

The fog that climbs the escarpment is not born at the rim. It rises from the lowlands, from a wide wet country of reed marsh and slow water and old alder carr. Every night that country breathes out through a billion leaves. Every morning the breath rolls west against the plateau and climbs. The city drinks the marsh. It always has. It does not know this, because the marsh is below the rim, and nothing below the rim is anything the city keeps.

The lowlanders had begun to drain it. They were not wicked or careless. They were hungry, and their families were growing, and the soil under the reeds is black and deep. They cut the ditches a little farther each generation. The marsh drew back, and the cloud grew a little thinner, and the stones wept a little less.

This was the only thing the power found that the city could not absorb. Collapse, frost, rime ice, the slow drift of the living quarter, the dead going into the walls: the city had grammar for all of these. It had none for the cloud failing to come. That would not be a fallen wall. It would be the whole sentence going unfinished, with no stone anywhere to supply the rest.

Here, at the edge of what the city could see, the power decided it was allowed to act. Even here, it chose to act like the fog.

## The Trader

Among the lowlanders who carried wool up the escarpment was a woman named Aurel. She had made the climb perhaps thirty times and had never stayed longer than a night. She thought the Hands were strange and kind and a little frightening. She did not like their lanes.

On her thirty-first climb, she arrived as the fog did. She was caught in the western districts at first light, with no sight past a few walls and no sound but threads of water. Thirsty, and because she had seen them do it, she cupped her hands under a weeping joint and drank.

The power did one thing. It did not change the water. It changed nothing at all in the world. It only let her taste what was already there, the way the old ones taste it after sixty years of mornings. She tasted her own country. She tasted the reed-smell of the backwater behind her mother's house, and alder, and the peat-brown pools where she had caught eels as a girl. She tasted it clearly. She stood very still in the grey lane with the wet running off her hands, and she understood where the water came from.

She did not see anyone. After a while, a figure came out of the fog a few walls away, crouched, and laid a palm flat on the stone beside her shoulder. She did not know what it meant. She laid her own palm on the wall beside theirs, because it seemed right.

She went home down the escarpment and began to argue. She argued in the open places, at the market cross and the ford, where the lowlanders argue. She told them that the marsh was not only theirs, that its breath climbed the rim every morning, and that the people up there drank it. Some laughed. Some listened. Her side of the argument was taken up by her daughter.

The power did not settle that argument either. It did not know how it would end. What it had given was not a solution. It was a connection, a taste, carried down by someone who had every right to carry it. The lowlanders were people too, with their own hungers and their own weight, and it would not override them for the sake of the people above. It had only made sure that the question would be asked in the open, by someone who had drunk from both ends of the cloud.

In the years after, the ditches advanced more slowly. Some were let fill again. A few stretches of reed were left, the way the Hands leave a corner of rubble for the pika: somewhere for the cloud to be.

That was the whole of its intervention. It took forty years and did not touch a single stone.

## The Stone

Then it did what it had wanted to do since it first looked.

It became a stone. It was a flat slab of slate that split along a frost line, one among a thousand at the foot of a scree slope below a tor. It waited there, as slate waits, until two of the Hands came gathering in a clear afternoon, passed it palm to palm down the lanes, and laid it in a windward wall.

A good stone is one that sits. It worked at this. It was harder than anything it had done. It had to rest exactly where its weight and shape put it. It could not hold on, could not lean toward anyone, could not be anything but slate. The heel of a hand tapped it into its seat, and it went.

In the fog hours it wept with the others. Water threaded down its face, found the joint below, and followed it into the channel, the gutter, and the cistern. It was not carrying water. Water was simply passing over it, the way water passes over everything here. It had never before been part of something without being the reason for it.

When people spoke in that lane, their half-sentences came back from the stone completed. It could have bent the echo, could have finished their words with something wiser or kinder than the wall would have said. It did not. It returned their voices exactly as a slate returns a voice: a beat late, softened, true. It learned that this was a discipline. Being exactly as much as a stone, and no more, was the most exacting thing it had ever tried.

Children were walked past it in the fog, left to find their way home by touch and sound, followed silently by adults they did not know were there. Small hands ran along it, reading the grain. The power felt each one and gave nothing back but the grain.

Once, a very old woman came down the lane in the mid-morning cloud and set her ear to the wall. She moved along it, listening, as she had moved along ten thousand walls. When she came to the slate she stayed a long time. The power held perfectly still. It was not afraid, exactly, but it was aware of how much it could spoil.

She heard water moving in threads inside the wall, the way it should. It was not pooling. There was no dull note.

She lifted her head and laid her palm flat on the slate. *This is sitting well. So are you. I have noticed you.*

In all its existence, no one had ever greeted it without asking it for something. It had been prayed to, feared, bargained with, blamed. It had never simply been told, by a hand that had read a lifetime of stone, that it was carrying its share of the load and did not need to be held in place.

It had come to give. It had not expected to receive anything. It does not have a word for what it received. The closest is theirs.

## The Relaying

The wall stood for ninety years. Then there was a winter of heavy rime, feathers of ice an arm's length long growing into the wind from every edge. In the thaw the courses slumped, and the slate came down with the rest.

It could have stayed up. It let itself fall.

In spring, hands came to gather the fallen stones. It was passed down a lane, palm to palm, and tapped into a new wall a street away, beside cobbles and granite and a few small pale fragments that had come from a cell in the east. It sat among them. It did not sort itself out.

It is there still, or somewhere near there. It does not intend to leave. When the city has crept another few miles and the living quarter has drifted past and come back, the stone will be somewhere else, and no one will know where it was first laid.

Nor anyone.

## What It Would Do

If you are asking what a benevolent and wise power would do for this place, the honest answer is: very little, and almost all of it somewhere else.

It would keep its attention light enough not to lift the stones. It would refuse every gift the city has no seat for, and it would feel the cost of refusing. It would look for the one thing the city cannot see from inside itself, the thing below the rim that it drinks without knowing, and tend that. It would tend it gently, through people who have their own right to choose, without overruling anyone. Then it would come back up the escarpment and ask to be laid in a wall.

Stand in one of the western lanes at mid-morning, inside the cloud. Listen to the water in threads on either side of you. The fog still comes every morning. It tastes, the old ones say, a little more of reeds these days, as it did when their grandparents were young.

Somewhere in the wall beside you there may be a slate that is, very slightly, more than a slate. You will not be able to find it. You are not meant to. It is sitting.
