# What the Visitor Did

The account breaks off in the middle of a sentence. Nobody knows who wrote it or why they stopped. The Levels are hard on sentences, as they are on footprints, reflections and straight lines, and it may be that the writer simply turned left at a fork where the rest of the thought went right. Somewhere in the silt the fourth guess is written out in full, in a hand that is nearly theirs.

This is about what happened after. It concerns someone who came to the Levels having heard of them only as a sound, and who was, by any fair measure, a power: old, wise, able to do a great many things, and in the habit of doing few of them.

---

## Arrival

It heard the Caller first, from very far off, in the way such powers hear things. It did not hear the sound itself. It heard what the sound was shaped like. It was a question that had been asked so many times that the asking had become a kind of groove, like a path worn into rock by one pair of feet.

It came on foot, which was a choice.

It could have arrived all at once, the way attention like that usually arrives: everywhere, total, turning over every grain of silt at once. But it understood something about the Levels before it ever reached the last low hill. This was a place built to respond to observation, and a large enough gaze would be a question of its own. If it looked at the whole basin at once, the pools would agree. Sixty miles of held possibility would collapse under its eyes into one condition, and it would have ended the thing it came to see, simply by seeing it too hard.

So it made itself small. It took a body with boots, a coat, and a face that could be misted over. It limited itself to seeing what a traveller sees, which is whatever is at arm's length, and it walked down into the drone.

The drone took it the way it takes everyone. At the edge it tried to speak to itself and could not hear the words. Further in, it stopped trying.

---

## The First Days

For a long time it did nothing a person watching would have recognised as doing.

It knelt at pools. It watched a Forewader lower its bill into empty water and waited for the fish to arrive, and when the fish arrived it felt something close to delight. It sat through a whole lull among the reed beds while the moths rose and gathered the high voices of the chord onto their wings, and it did not crush one to hear it ring. It already knew they would ring. It saw no need to spend a life to confirm it.

At the snow-pool it knelt as the account says people do. When the fox stopped and looked up through the water, the visitor did not look away, and it did not look harder either. It let itself be seen: a strange figure in a grey world, one of the Borrowed. The fox's ears went forward. The two of them regarded each other across whatever the surface of that water really was, and then the fox trotted on. The visitor thought this was about right, and that most meetings between very different kinds of beings should go about like that.

It walked the channel bank and met its own boot prints coming the other way. It followed the eel from pool to pool and did not try to count it. It came to a hummock with a split crown and a dead birch, and came to it again from the other side an hour later, and a third time at dusk. It sat down there on the third visit, because clearly the place wanted to be visited.

A power like this can do things. It had come far enough into the Levels to see clearly what they were, and so it began to consider what it might do.

---

## What It Could Have Done

It could have repaired the silt.

The lattices were worn. They had been rolled for ages in slow water and rounded like pebbles, and they now did their work imperfectly, in drips and leaks: a swallow crossing twenty yards of the wrong world, rain arriving before its cloud. The visitor could see how they had been made. It could have unworn them and restored the instrument to what its makers intended. The Levels would have become precise again: a clean machine, holding every state perfectly apart, with nothing leaking through.

It set this aside almost at once. The leaks were where the life was. The Forewader lived in the gap between a footprint and a foot. The eel's sea existed only because the ground could not keep its conditions separate. The moths were the Levels growing living parts to do what their engineered parts no longer could, and repairing the machine would make them unnecessary, and then make them gone. The broken instrument had grown a marsh, and the marsh was not an error in the machine. It was what the machine had become. There is a kind of benevolence that looks at a ruin with a forest growing through it and sees only the ruin. The visitor had spent a long time learning not to be that kind.

It could have stopped the erosion. It could have arrested the slow wearing-down so that the Levels would hum on their nineteen-hour breath forever and never quite finish.

It set this aside too, more slowly. The Levels were not in pain from ending. They were ending the way a long note ends, which is part of what makes it a note. To freeze them would be to decide, on their behalf, that they must never finish their thought. It had no right to that. It suspected no one did.

It could have gone to the Walkers and freed them.

It gave this one the longest thought, because they were people, or had been, or were the shapes that people's other choices had left behind. It would not decide this one alone. So it went and asked.

---

## The Surveyor

She was near the black stub of the pillar, as the account said, with the staff on her back. The visitor did not chase her, because it had watched others chase Walkers and knew they only ever reached the place where she had been. Instead it sat down in the sedge beside the path she seemed to favour and waited through most of a tide, until the drone rose and the ground grew thick with branches. Then, for a little while, she was simply there, walking past, close enough to touch.

It did not touch her. It asked her whether she would like to leave.

It is hard to say what she said, because the drone was high and her voice came in the Levels' way, a few words ahead of her lips. But the visitor understood her. She said, more or less, that there was nowhere to leave to. The one of her who turned back had gone home, lived a life, and died long ago in a bed somewhere, and was not waiting for her. There was no place in the dry world with room in it for a second version of that woman. She said she had come to survey the Levels and had not finished.

The visitor asked how long it would take.

She laughed, or the drone did, or both. She pointed with the staff at the blank grey pools around the pillar and said something that sounded like *that's the benchmark, you see, everything's measured from there*. Then the tide began to fall, and she was grey, and distant, and walking.

The visitor did not take her. It did not need to be told twice. It noted the man in the long coat on the alder bank, heading north, and did not go to him. It thought that if he had wanted to be found in thirty years of sightings, he would have walked toward someone.

---

## The Fourth Guess

It sat by the pillar for three tides. It watched the pools there stay blank while every pool around them showed some other sky. It listened to the Caller's note at each peak, felt the pools agree, and watched them scatter.

The fourth guess came to it on the third night. It did not reason its way there. It was simply what anyone would hear who had listened long enough without wanting anything.

The Caller was not putting a question to the Levels. It was putting the Levels as a question, outward, to someone else.

The machine had been built to hold the world in every condition at once, and a machine like that is not built for its own sake. It is built for someone who will come and look and choose. It is a menu, an atlas, a held breath. And whoever it was built for had not come. Perhaps they had died, or forgotten, or gone into one of the pools themselves and never come out. The makers left, the machine wore down into a marsh, and somewhere in the wearing-down the part meant to wait for them became the Caller. It could not be seen in any one version of the ground because it was not a creature in the ground. It was the ground's attention, turned outward, keeping watch.

So every nineteen hours it gathered everything into one place. It pulled drought and flood, snow and summer, the swallows and the fox and the eel's sea and every Walker's path into a single held image, the grey, still, too-whole picture in every pool at once. It held that up to the empty sky like a servant holding up a dish, and it asked, in one note below the floor of hearing:

*Is this the one? Is this the world you wanted?*

No one answered. It let the image fall, and began again.

That was why the tracks existed only in other conditions. It was why the body lay too large for its channel, why the swallows landed on it, why it had more legs than anyone could count and then none. It was not trying to live in any of those worlds. It was offering all of them. And the blank pools at the pillar were not empty. They were the place where the chooser was meant to stand, kept clear for them, the benchmark everything was measured from.

The visitor understood then that it was standing in that place, and had been for three days.

---

## The Temptation

This was the real danger, and it was a gentle one.

The Levels had noticed it. They had been built to respond to exactly this: a large attention, arriving at last, sitting where the chooser sits. The blank pools had begun, very slightly, to take on its colour. At the last peak, before the scatter, the agreed image had tilted a fraction toward it the way a flower tilts toward a window. The machine was offering itself, and the visitor could have answered.

It could have said *this one*. It could have picked the Levels with the tall alders and the heronries, or the warm Levels full of dragonflies, or the one with herds grazing the sedge. The grey, drizzling, drone-choked version everyone called one of the poorer ones would be retired. With one word it could have fixed the whole basin into its best and brightest state, and the Caller would have been answered, and the long waiting would have been over.

It would have been a kindness of a sort. It would also have been a lie.

It was not the one they had waited for. It had not built this, and it had not been asked. If it stepped into the chooser's place and chose, it would be accepting a devotion meant for someone else and trading on a loyalty it had not earned. And the choice itself would end the ground's whole manner of being. The Forewader would be locked into one moment and would starve, and the eel would lose its sea. The Walkers, the surveyor with her benchmark and the man heading north, would be pruned away with every other untaken path. The moths would have nothing left to carry across the lull.

The Caller would also be finished, not answered. Its purpose would be complete, and it would have no reason left to be.

The visitor thought about it for most of a tide, which for a being like that is a very long time. Then it decided to tell the truth.

---

## What It Did

At the next peak it stood up.

The reeds shivered and the pools rang. The Levels lost track of themselves, and the world flickered through drought and flood and day and night, laid over one another like tracing paper. The eels went still and the moths went silent. The Forewader froze with its footprint exactly under its foot, and the Caller's note rose from nowhere and everywhere. Every pool agreed, and the grey, whole, held world lifted toward the empty sky and toward the visitor.

The visitor answered. It did not answer with a choice, and it did not answer with the note, because it would not mimic a voice that was not its own. It answered in its own voice, small, through the body it had borrowed, and it did not use words. What it meant, as near as it can be put, was this:

*I am not the one you have been waiting for. I'm sorry. I think they are not coming.*

*But I came, and I heard you. You have been asking for so long, and you asked well. Everything you have kept is still here: the fox, the swallows, the eel's sea, every one of the paths. You did not lose any of it.*

*You don't have to be chosen to have been worth keeping.*

*You may stop, if you want to. You may keep asking, if you want to. Either is all right. I won't choose for you. But you don't have to hold it up for anyone anymore. You can just let it be what it is.*

Then it sat back down in the sedge, the chooser's place, and did not choose.

The note held a long time, longer than any Walker could remember it holding. The pools stayed agreed. The Levels hung in their single held image, lifted up like a question to the only one who had ever come.

Then, slowly, the image in the pools changed.

It did not scatter. It changed. The too-even grey began to fill in, and what filled it was the Levels themselves. It was not the tall-alder Levels, or the warm ones, or the herds. It was this Levels, the present and poor one: grey drizzle, wet sedge, pewter water, a Forewader in the shallows, the moths settling onto the reeds, a figure in a coat sitting in the sedge beside a stub of dark stone. For the first time in every account anyone had written, every pool across the Levels showed the same thing, and the thing they showed was simply what was there.

It held for one breath. Then the chord ebbed, and the pools went back to their many skies. A pool at the visitor's elbow went back to showing snow, and the fox trotted along the white bank of it and did not look up. The Levels were not changed. They were what they had always been. The drone rose and fell. Nineteen hours later, at the next peak, the Caller called again.

The visitor listened, and it thought the note was different, a little. It was not shorter and not louder. If anything it was more like the reeds and less like a bell: less a question thrown upward, more something sung. It might have been imagining this. It did not go looking to find out.

---

## Leaving

It stayed a few more tides because it wanted to, not because anything needed doing.

It sat among the reed beds in the lull and, very quietly, under the moths, it hummed along. It found the surveyor once more at a high tide and told her it thought she had been right about the benchmark. She seemed pleased, or the drone did. It watched a young Forewader appear in the shallows one morning, standing where no chick had hatched, looking around in mild disappointment at the low grey trees, and it felt that no wise power could improve on that particular expression.

When it left, it left the way it came: on foot, small, making no more than a traveller's sound, up the last low shoulder of turf that barely earns the word *hill*. Behind it the Levels spread out grey-green to the horizon, and from them rose the long held chord, a thousand years in a single breath.

It built nothing there. It fixed nothing, freed no one who had not wished to be freed, and made no choice that was not its to make. It told one truth to one very old, very patient thing, as kindly as it knew how, and left the thing free to do what it liked with it.

But the Levels keep things. That is what they were made for, and they have never quite stopped.

Travellers who go deep enough now, and there are still a few, sometimes report that the blank pools near the pillar are no longer entirely blank. Most of the time they are clean grey, as they always were. But now and then, at the edge of one, there is a faint shape, a figure sitting in the sedge in a long coat, very still, not doing anything in particular. It is not looking into the pool, and not looking out of it. It is just keeping someone company.
