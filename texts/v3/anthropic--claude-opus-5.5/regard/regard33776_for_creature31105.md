# What Came to the Hammer Moor

## The Attention

It did not arrive, exactly. Nothing came down through the cloud. The cloud base stayed where it was, thirty meters up, sometimes three. What changed was that the moor was being *looked at*, fully, for the first time in its existence.

The birds knew first. A flock of plover, turning over a swell, hesitated in the middle of a wheel, a hundredth of a second out of step, and the drumming snipe missed a beat. Then the frogs stopped. All of them, across kilometers, at once, the way frogs stop when a heron's shadow crosses a pool. The telegraph went silent.

The pumps did not stop. The pumps have never stopped. But for a while, under the noise of the working moor, there was a new quiet, the particular quiet of a room where someone has just come in and is standing by the door.

## What It Saw

It saw what you have seen, only all at once: the living mat several meters thick, the jelly of ancient moss becoming oil, the rock beneath. It saw the swells running across the plateau like peristalsis, and the stokers in their scalding tunnels with silica rattling in their fur. It saw the reelers ticking inside the bobbins in the dark and the slugs carrying strips of moor on their backs to lay across fresh wounds.

It saw the ghost mountains, and it saw why they were there, which is more than anyone on the moor has ever known. It was not a haunting. The rock underneath still holds the stress pattern of the old range. The crystal grain was set under the weight of peaks that are gone, and the weather still reads that pattern the way water reads a riverbed after the river has moved. The mountains are gone. Their *shape* is not. Shape is harder to erode than stone.

It saw how old everything was, and how the moor had never once been finished, and never would be.

And it saw the one who lives there.

## The Things It Could Have Done

A benevolent power is dangerous to a place like this, and it knew that. The kindest thing a great power can do is often the hardest: to notice its own impulses before acting on them.

So it held them up and looked at them, one at a time.

**It could have given the factory a product.** Everything here is produced and nothing is used: the cables, the current, the heat, the endless pumped water. It would be easy to make the output *for* something. The power could turn the steam into warmth for some cold city a continent away, run the charge down wires, or gather the sedge-cable and weave it into sails. It considered this for about as long as it takes a swell to pass underfoot, and set it down. The moor does not waste anything. What looks like waste is only work whose purpose is itself. The cables are not unused. They are homes for reelers, windbreaks for lichen, weight that keeps the knuckles' ecology anchored in a gale. To call the moor unproductive is to mistake a body for a machine. A heart does not produce anything either.

**It could have given the factory an owner.** It could take the moor into its own keeping and make it safe from whatever the future holds: drainage, burning, the slow drying of the world. But a moor that is kept safe stops being a moor. This place lives by rupture. The bog-bursts, the dying vents, the tarns that drain in an hour are not damage the moor survives. They *are* the moor. Protection would be a kind of taxidermy.

**It could have let the mountains lie down.** It could relieve the land of its memory and settle the rock's old stresses so the ghost peaks finally went flat. It could make the compass point to one north and the distances hold still. It looked a long time at this one, because there is something in it that looks like mercy. Isn't it a burden, to be haunted by what you were?

But the flocks bend around the ghost peaks. The grazers keep to the old valleys. The rain falls at the angle of slopes that are not there, and so it waters the ground in patterns the mosses have grown to. An entire living order has been built on a memory. Erasing the memory would not free the moor. It would orphan everything that had learned to live in the shadows.

Also, it had to admit, it did not believe that being shaped by what you were is a wound. It thought it might be closer to character.

**It could have made the moor notice.** The moor does not notice you. It is busy. The power could have woken it and given it a face, a voice, a mind that looked back.

It did not even pick this one up to examine it. Some things you do not do to a being that is not asking.

So it did none of these. It stayed by the door for a while, the way you do in a workshop where everyone is concentrating, and waited to be useful or to leave.

## The One Who Lives There

There is a person on the Hammer Moor.

She came, or was born here, or her grandmother was. She is not certain which, and it no longer matters. She lives in a hollow in the lee of a large bobbin-knuckle, where the reelers tick all night through the fiber wall beside her head and the wind cannot reach. She burns dried peat that the stokers' vents cure for her. She eats eel, and grazer-milk when a herd will let her near, and the fat white roots of cotton-sedge.

She is not lonely in the way people from elsewhere would assume. But she is alone, which is different, and she has a question she has carried so long it has worn smooth.

Every evening she walks to the edge of a charged tarn, sits with her hair lifting in the static, and listens to the frogs. She writes down what they send. She uses a stroke for the short calls and a longer stroke for the long, in notebooks made of pressed sedge-paper, stacked now in the dry back of her hollow, dozens of them, a lifetime. She has never decoded a word. She does not know whether there is anything to decode. She does it because it seems like the thing on the moor that is hers to do, the way the mending is the slugs' and the winding is the reelers'. And some nights she is afraid she has made it up. Maybe she is a woman writing down frog noise in the dark for no one, and her whole life is a cable wound tight around nothing.

That is her question, though she would not put it this way: *Is this work, or am I only telling myself it is?*

The frogs had gone quiet. So she was sitting at the tarn's edge with her pen lifted and nothing to write, and that is how she knew something had come.

## What It Did

It did not appear to her. It thought about that too, and decided that the moor had enough apparitions.

It did this instead.

It read her notebooks. It did not take them. It read them the way it had read the rock, all at once, with the kind of attention that does not move paper. And it found that she had not made it up.

The frogs are counting.

Each clipped burst is a tally of swells. The frogs sit on the mat and feel the waves pass beneath the pools. Near the charged tarns the current sharpens their calls into something regular, and what comes out of them is a count. It covers how many swells from which direction, how many beats of which rhythm, where the rhythms meet. Relayed pool to pool across the plateau, it is the moor's own ledger of its labor. A factory with no owner still keeps its books. No one ever read them, because there was no one to report to.

There was one person copying them down. Her notebooks are the only record the Hammer Moor has ever had of itself. Not every entry is perfect. She misses things when the sleet is loud, and a stretch of her fortieth winter is thin, when she was ill. But she has kept the accounts of the moor for most of a human lifetime, and she has kept them faithfully.

The power could have told her what the marks meant. It could have handed her the key and let her translate the whole stack, which would have made her a kind of scholar and made the notebooks into *knowledge*. It thought that would be taking something from her, even while giving. The work would become about the answer. She would stop being a keeper and start being a reader, and the moor would go back to having no one at the tarn's edge.

So it gave her only enough.

When the frogs started again, and they did, cautiously, one pool and then a hundred and then all of them, the first burst that reached her tarn was a count she had heard ten thousand times. For once, for the length of that burst, she *understood* it. It was not a translation. It was the feeling of understanding, the way you suddenly understand a language you've been hearing through a wall for years. It was a number. It was the moor telling itself how hard it had worked that hour.

And she knew, without any voice telling her, that she had been writing it down right.

That was the whole of it. The next burst was noise again, clipped and staccato. She wrote it down.

## The Shift

Then the power did one more thing. It was not for her, and not really for the moor. It was for itself.

Everything on the Hammer Moor has work, and the power, for all that it was, did not. It moves through the world looking at things and helping when help is wanted, and there is no rhythm to that, no beat to fall in with. It had watched the snipe dive on the pulse of the pumps and felt something it recognized, with some surprise, as envy.

So before it went, it took a shift.

On a big bobbin to the east, where a gale had torn a loose end free of the winding, it tucked the fiber back under and pulled it tight. It did it once, the way a reeler would, bracing against nothing. The reelers inside went silent for a moment, and then went back to ticking. One of them came out and inspected the join, found it acceptable, and went back in.

It walked the length of a fresh bog-burst scar beside the slugs, not helping. They did not need help. It was just going at their pace for an hour so it would know what that pace was.

It stood in the shadow of a ghost mountain and let itself feel the cold of a north face that had not existed for a hundred million years. It thought: *so this is what it is to be shaped by something that isn't there anymore*. It found that it knew that feeling already, and had not had a word for it.

Then it went, the way a presence goes from a room. Nobody saw it leave.

## After

The moor did not change. It would be wrong to say it did. The pumps went on thudding and the vents went on shrieking, and a new tarn opened by morning in a place that had been dry. The stokers moved house. The mountains shifted in the fog: a peak, a col, a range facing another way.

But the moor remembers everything, and this was a thing that happened on it.

Perhaps a thousand years from now, when some other traveler stands in the lee of that eastern bobbin, they may notice one strange detail. Among the hundreds of neat tucks the reelers have made, there is one that is very slightly wrong, too careful, pulled a fraction too tight, and the reelers have been winding over it ever since, faithfully, the way the rain falls on slopes that are gone.

And the frogs' count may carry, for longer than frogs should be able to carry anything, one burst each evening that is not a tally of swells. It is short. It falls on the beat. It means, roughly: *someone was here, and the work was seen.*

The woman kept writing. She lived a long time, as people on the moor do, and filled forty more notebooks. She was not certain, ever again, that she understood the frogs. That one burst never came back to her as meaning, only as noise, the way it had always been.

But she stopped being afraid that it was nothing. She had felt, for the length of one call, what it was to be part of the workforce and not merely camped at the edge of it. The moor still did not notice her. She understood now that it did not need to. She was keeping its books. That was her job. It had always been her job.

On the nights the plateau hummed, she would sit with her hair lifting and her pen moving, and the snipe would drum overhead on the pulse of the ground. For hours at a time she would not be able to tell the difference between listening and working. She came to think, in the end, that there wasn't one.
