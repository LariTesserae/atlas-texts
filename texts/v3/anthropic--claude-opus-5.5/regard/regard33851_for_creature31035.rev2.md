# The Keeper of the Rim

## How It Comes

It could have come any way it liked. It could have come as light, or as a voice, or as a sudden knowing in the minds of thirty people at once. It considered these the way you might consider barging through a door in a house where someone is sleeping, and it set them aside.

So it came in fog, the way almost everyone comes.

It walked down off the dry turf at dusk and let the rim go out of sight behind it. It noticed the air turn cool and heavy and smell of cut stems. It let the ground go soft under it by degrees and did not hurry the softness. It did not need to walk. It walked because in Ashcarr you walk, and it wanted to arrive in a way the place would recognize.

There were lights, dim and orange and uneven. There was a sound like many small clocks. And there, with a palm flat on a hornbeam wall, was someone who lifted the other hand without looking round: *wait*.

It waited.

This is the first thing to understand about the power that came to Ashcarr. It was very great, and it was wise. The part of its wisdom that mattered here was that it knew how to be told to wait by someone smaller than itself, and to do it.

"It's all right," the tender said at last, and took her hand from the bark. She meant the arm.

## What It Sees

It sees more than the tenders can, because it can stand outside time a little, the way you can stand on the rim and see the whole bowl.

It sees the arms as they were when they were saplings. They were bent by hands whose names are now the worn-out names in the brittle brown pages, names that are no longer names. It sees every spring's re-pinning laid over every other, like the old maps that disagree with each other in small ways, and it sees the slow two-pace drift of the building that no one moved. It sees the peat under the middle lawn: the black gear wheels, the dropped mallets, the bones stained the color of iron, all of it held.

It sees the count. This is what draws it, more than the trees or the people. A whole basin has arranged itself, tree and slug and beetle and human, into a single slow act of attention. The fog comes, and it is noticed. Each tick is one small unit of weather caught and kept. Nobody designed this as a mind. It became one, the way a path becomes a path.

A wise power loves few things more than attention given freely and kept up for a very long time. Here was a place that had done nothing else for longer than anyone had records.

It also sees what the tenders cannot see from inside the bowl, and this is the part it carries carefully.

## What It Considers

A benevolent power arriving somewhere hard and small feels the pull of kindness first, and kindness wants to *fix*. It let itself feel each of these wants fully, because a want you refuse to look at will act on you anyway. Then it took them one at a time and set each down.

**It could end the dry.** It could make the fog come every night, so the lamps never went dark and the town never sat blind. But the dry is where the waiting lives. The dry teaches the answer to the rime-test before anyone asks it. A town that never went dark would never learn that dark is not the same as dead. The dry stays.

**It could stop the wood from growing.** It could fix every arm in true forever, so the pins never wandered and the rods never ground. It could not do this without making the tenders unnecessary. The whole mind of Ashcarr hangs on the fact that the wood drifts and something must walk to where the counting has gone wrong. Take away the drift and you do not free the tenders. You unmake them. *We're what corrects it.* To remove the thing that needs correcting would be to tell them they were never needed. The wood keeps growing.

**It could make the lamps bright.** It could give them clean steady light on the paths, enough to read by at midnight. But the lamps pulse with the breath of the wood. That pulse is the weather's rhythm made visible, and there are not many places in the world where you can see it. Bright light would be a lamp. This light is a reading. The lamps stay dim.

**It could give them rest.** They sleep on bracken beside the housings and wake on their knees with the mallet before they know they are awake. But the power watched a tender do this one night. It saw no strain in her face, only the absolute presence of someone who is exactly where she belongs. There are exhausting lives, and there are lives that are simply full. This one was full. It left them their vigil.

**It could spare them loss.** It could keep Mother Tine from dying, hand the old names back, and stop the pruning that leaves one tender every season without an arm and sends them up to *go dry*. It thought about this one longest, because loss is where kindness aches most. But the name moves on to the next keeper the way the ratchet moves to the next tooth. The dead go into the moss and some nights the moss ticks back. This is a people who have made peace with ending in a way most of the world never manages. They did it by folding ending into the count, not by denying it. Undoing it would be a theft wearing the face of a gift.

So it set down every one of the large kindnesses. That did not leave nothing.

## The Rime

It stayed into the first winter, the way the ones who might stay do. It learned the paths. It learned not to put anything down on the lawns. It learned to set its feet as if carrying a full bowl, and it found that it liked this. It had never before had to be careful of anything so small.

At the first rime, when the fog froze onto the branches and the arms locked stiff and the whole basin went silent, an old tender brought it to a wall. She was the one they call Herself's keeper, and she has forgotten her own first name so thoroughly that not even she would recognize it. She put its hand on the bark.

"What's it doing?"

The power could have told her everything. It could see the sap drawn down into the root plate. It could see the frozen reaction-wood holding its stored bend like a breath. It could see the cells already, invisibly, preparing the thaw. It could have given her a truer answer about that arm than anyone in Ashcarr had ever given.

It said: "Waiting."

She nodded, and took the hand away, and that was all. But she had heard what it did not say, because tenders hear everything, and she looked at it sideways for a moment with the small pleased surprise of someone handed back something she had dropped. It had known more and said less, and she understood that it had chosen to. In Ashcarr that is the whole of good manners.

It was not given an arm. It had not expected one. You do not give an arm to weather.

## What It Does

It does three things, and none of them are visible.

### The first: it tends the one arm nobody tends.

This is what it saw from outside the bowl.

Everything in Ashcarr is corrected by something. The slugs correct the algae on the iron. The grubs correct the moss in the teeth. The tenders correct the drift of the wood. But one thing in the machine has no keeper, because no one inside the basin could ever walk to it. That thing is the fog itself, and the land around the bowl that makes the fog, and the country beyond that sends the wet air in.

The power could see the futures of that country, the way you can see from the rim where the paths will wander. In some of them the land upslope is drained for grazing, the water table sinks, and the fog comes thinner every decade. In some a road is cut along the rim, and its warm dry surface breaks the evening pooling. In some the whole climate of the region leans, a little each year, drier and drier, until the dry season is not a few weeks in late summer but half the year. The arms barely move, the rope walk stalls, and the tenders wait, and wait, and are finally waiting for nothing.

Nobody in Ashcarr would ever see this coming. It would arrive the way the doorways narrow: too slowly to notice, until one day a door must be abandoned and there is no new door to train.

So the power became the keeper of the rim.

It does not make the fog come. It keeps the fog *able* to come. It is a small list of tasks, done patiently. A surveyor's line is gently lost in bad weather, and the drainage scheme is abandoned for a cheaper field elsewhere. A road is built, but on the other side of the hill, because a council member had a feeling. The springs that feed the moor are left alone. When the long drift of the climate leans toward the basin, it is nudged back by a hair each year. The correction is never enough to end the dry, only enough to keep the dry the size it has always been.

It does this the way the tenders do everything: flat-handed, slowly, never one large motion, always many small ones. It unpins and re-pins the weather a little each spring. When it has done a season's work it would write, if it wrote anything, *pinned lower*: fixed at a new point, a little below notice, so the old machine can go on turning.

The tenders will never know. That is correct. The slugs do not know the tenders are there either, and the arrangement works.

### The second: it keeps the names.

The person-names fall out of use when a tender takes an arm. They fall the way things fall into the moss: gone from sight, not destroyed. The power does not hand them back. Mother Tine is Mother Tine, and that is who she has chosen to be. But it holds them, every one, back to the earliest brittle pages and before them, to the first hands that bent the first saplings.

It does this not because the names are needed, but because something should hold them. A mind that notices and remembers the weather has earned, at least, to be noticed and remembered in turn. Somewhere outside the basin, in whatever the power has in place of a ledger, there is a column organized by person rather than by arm. Nobody will ever read it. It is kept.

If a tender, some quiet afternoon, reaches for her old name and finds it, and says it with a small pleased surprise, that may be the power. Or it may be the moss giving something back. The power is content not to know which.

### The third: it listens underneath, and does not say.

On the heaviest night of the wet season, with the fog to its waist and the lamps nearly bright, the power did what no tender would do. It lay down on the middle lawn with its ear to the moss.

*Tk.*

A long pause.

*Tk.*

It could hear what it was. It could hear it more clearly than anyone ever had. It could have told them whether it was roots through an old ratchet, or the peat swelling, or something else.

It listened to the whole of a night's count from below, all the way to the top of the swell. Then it got up and brushed the damp off, and never told anyone what it heard. Some things in a place are held up by not being known, the way a lintel is held up by two trunks leaning together. The tenders offer their explanations in the tone of explanations they do not need. The power left them not needing them.

What it will say is this much. In the oldest column, the one under no arm, the most recent entry is a tender who died three winters ago, and beside her name, in the same cramped hand as all the ordinary notes, is *pinned lower*. The power read that, and it did not correct it.

## The Dry Night

One more thing, not a deed exactly.

Late that summer, in the dry, there came a night with no fog at all. The arms did not curl. The dynamos did not wake. The lamps stayed dark, and the town sat blind.

The power could have lit every lamp in the basin with a thought.

It went and sat in the carding house instead, on the bracken beside a housing that was not moving. A young tender sat with it, one who had only just been given her first small arm and was frightened of the silence the way the new ones are. They sat together in the dark. After a long time she laid her palm flat on the still wall, and so did the power.

"What's it doing?" she whispered. She was testing herself.

"Waiting," it said.

"Yes," she said, and her shoulders came down, and she slept.

That was, in its own reckoning, the most useful thing it did in Ashcarr.

## Leaving

It left in the morning, as visitors do. The fog was lifting, the arms beginning to straighten, the first pawls dropping into their teeth. Herself's keeper walked it to where the moss gives way to turf, and stopped there, because the turf is loud and dry and not hers.

It turned back once. She was already walking away down into the green, slow and careful, a palm going out flat to the nearest wall.

Then the power did the only thing it had truly learned there. It knelt at the rim, where the deer come and look down and turn away, and laid its palm flat on the country: on the turf and the moor and the long slope of land that makes the fog. It spread its fingers, because that is how you hear.

It was not steadying itself. It was asking.

The land, it supposes, is answering. Somewhere below, in a ratchet, in the peat, in the chest of a sleeping tender, the count goes on, one small unit of weather at a time.

*Tk.*

It's all right. It is only reporting on the weather.
