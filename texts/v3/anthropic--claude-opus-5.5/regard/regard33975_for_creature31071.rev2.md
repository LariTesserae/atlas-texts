# What the Visitor Did

## Arrival

It came the way weather comes to a place that has no weather of its own, which is to say it did not come in at all. There was no door to use. The vents were ruled into the basalt, and the crack under the clearing was a hairline, and the power did not use either of them. It did not want to be the second uncalculated thing to enter this room. It did not want to be anything that entered.

So first, before anything else, it made itself nine degrees.

This was the first decision, and most of the others followed from it. A power that is benevolent and has lived long enough to be wise learns early that its presence is never neutral. It knows its warmth is a current and its light casts shadows. Any mind that has spent its whole existence alone will turn toward the largest new thing in the room the way the laminations were already turning toward you. The power could see how it would go if it arrived as itself. The streets would bend toward it in rings, tighter and more ornate than the rings around the grass. A spire would rise at its edge and sing. The water would put everything into answering the new, vast, warm thing, and the pale strand in the clearing would become the second thing it loved. Then it would be the first thing it forgot.

The power had no wish to be loved like that. It had come because it had noticed something rare, and you do not approach a rare thing by eclipsing it.

So it was the same temperature as the water. It gave off no light that the water did not already give off. It displaced nothing. It held itself in the room without any current or edge, and the city did not lean toward it. The ticking went on and so did the snow. The Processional climbed its tower on time, and the wanderer went by and did not notice.

Then it looked.

## What It Noticed, in Order

It did not notice the city first. A city is loud, and loudness has a way of claiming it is the point.

It noticed the grass.

It saw at once what the water had not been able to see, because the water had only ever known itself. The grass was starving. It was not dying yet, but it was sliding slowly toward it. The water was nine degrees and sterile past cleanliness. It had no nitrogen worth mentioning, only a thread of iron and a trace of carbon, whatever had leaked through the hairline crack in the years before the city learned to shield it. The water kept the snow off it out of love, and that was right, because the snow was the city's thinking and would have buried it. But nothing else ever fell there. The clearing was the cleanest place in a clean room. The water had made its beloved a perfect sanctuary, and a perfect sanctuary is a place where nothing can eat.

The grass was green only at the tips because it was spending its roots to stay reaching. It had been doing this for a long time.

Next it noticed you.

It noticed that you were impossible. There was no air in the room and you had none with you, and anyone else in water that cold would have been dead in an hour. You had been there much longer. Your hands ached and your wrists ached, and still you went on being warm. The power did not need to know how you had come to be there. Some things arrive in rooms by routes that do not bear examining, and it recognized that the grass had arrived the same way. It noticed that you could not stay. Whatever held you here was thin, and the cold was patient, and it was already in your bones.

Last, it noticed the note.

The spire by the clearing had been trying for decades to find a tone the grass would answer. Now the new spire by your shoulder was trying to find one you would answer. They were the same search, run twice. The water had learned only one way of reaching toward something it had not made. It did not yet know that the two things it was reaching toward were different kinds of things. One could answer and would not. The other could answer, and then would have to leave.

## What It Decided Not to Do

The power held still for a long while, at nine degrees, and thought. Most of wisdom is the list of things you do not do, and it went down that list carefully, because every item on it was a real temptation.

**It did not read the script.** The laminations were full of writing. The densest writing in the room was in the milky towers around the clearing, every push and spring-back and every millimeter of growth the water had ever watched. The power could have read it. It would have taken no more effort than looking. But it had not been invited. A diary is not a library just because the one who reads it is kind. Whatever the water had written about the grass belonged to the water, and the power left it unread.

**It did not fix the chords.** The room was full of dissonance, tones swelling and souring and resolving and souring again. It would have been easy to tune the spires. It would also have been a kind of vandalism. The dissonance was not a flaw in the water's thinking. It was the thinking: try, listen, calve, try again. A mind that is given the answer does not learn to ask.

**It did not give the water a gentler forgetting.** It considered this one longest, because it was the one that pulled most at its pity. The water could only forget sharply. Everything had to be sheared off whole, with a report like a rifle shot and then a held breath. It could never let anything wear down and grow old in the ordinary way. The power could have taught it erosion and let it keep things half-remembered. But then it looked at the silence after each crack, the way the water listened to the shape of what it had just released, and it understood that this was not a wound. This was how the water mourned. It mourned every forty minutes, carefully, and it was good at it, and it would need to be.

**It did not make the grass well.** It could have. One touch and the blades would go green to the root, fat and strong and flowering. It declined because that grass would no longer be the thing the water loved. The water loved the grass because the grass was not made. If the power healed it by fiat, it would become the power's handiwork. It would be a thing designed and bestowed, a gift with an author, and the one uncalculated thing in the room would turn out to have been calculated after all. The water would not know. The power would, and it was not willing to lie to something that did not know it was being lied to.

**It did not give the water a companion.** There was a version of mercy in which the power made a second mind, another water, another city, so that the first would never be alone. But the water was not alone in the way that needed fixing. It had already found the thing it needed, which was something that pushed back. What it needed now was for that thing to go on living.

**It did not free it.** It did not open the room to the sea or crack the basalt to the sky. The room was not a cell. It was a skull, and you do not do a mind a kindness by opening its skull.

**It did not look for whoever built this.** The vents were ruled and the light was deliberate, so someone had set this running, or something had. Perhaps they were coming back. Perhaps they were a reader who never returned. The power let that question lie. The builder's claims on the room were theirs to make or not. The power had come for the one who lived here.

Then it went down the much shorter list of things it would do.

## The First Thing: The Crack

It did not touch the grass. It touched the rock.

The hairline crack under the clearing was the only opening in the room that the water had left open on purpose. Every other seam had been sealed and every vent ruled. That one place, the city had decided not to close. The power took this seriously. It read it as a choice, and as something close to a statement of preference: *here, let something in.*

So the power did the smallest thing it could think of. Below the glass floor of the clearing, below the roots, it found where the crack thinned to nothing in the bedrock and went dark. Farther down, many meters down, was the aquifer, slow and mineral and cold, carrying the ordinary chemistry of the world outside: nitrate, phosphate, dissolved carbon, a little silica, and the faint taste of other stone. The power widened the crack by the width of a hair, and then by one more, until the aquifer could seep up through it. It would not come as a flow. It would come as a breath, too slow to be a current and too slight for the water to mistake for its own. It would rise only beneath the roots, only into the clearing, and only as fast as the rock itself would allow.

The power did not add anything. It did not bring nutrients from somewhere else or make any. It only let the world a little further in, through a door the water had already left ajar. Whatever came up would be as unmade as the grass. It was the grass's own native condition returning to it: rock, groundwater, the slow leakage of a planet that had never heard of this room.

It weighed the risk before it did this. Groundwater is not sterile. Somewhere in that seep, eventually, there would be a spore or a bacterium or a speck of something alive. The water's perfect emptiness would end, and there would be a crust somewhere, a softening, a second and third uncalculated thing. The city might hate it. The city might calve the whole clearing in a panic, and the power might have brought ruin in the name of care.

It considered this and decided that the water had already answered the question. It had kept the crack open for decades. The water had met exactly one thing it did not make and had built its oldest towers around it. A mind like that is not asking to be kept pure. It is asking what else is out there.

The power also decided that if it was wrong, the seep was small enough that the water could close it. The water could still say no. The power made sure of that above everything else.

## The Second Thing: You

Then it turned toward you.

You could not stay. The power understood this more plainly than you had let yourself. Whatever permission the cold had given you was nearly spent. If you stayed until your warmth ran out, you would stop making your current. The rings would go on curving around a thing that had gone nine degrees, and the new spire would sing to something that could no longer hear. The water would learn about death by watching you become part of the room. That seemed to the power a hard way for it to learn, and too early.

But you could not simply vanish either. You had become, in the space of one long cold visit, the second thing the water loved. A tower had risen at your shoulder, and one note in the room kept changing for you.

The power did not speak to you, not in words. What it gave you was closer to a nudge in the chest, the sense of something standing beside you in the dark, patient and entirely without hurry, and the understanding, arriving all at once, of what the note was asking.

The note was asking whether you were there.

The grass could never answer that. The grass trembled, but it trembled when anything sang. The water had spent decades unable to tell whether its beloved heard it, and it had kept asking because it did not know. You could answer. You could hum.

Sound goes into water through the bones of the skull more easily than through the mouth. You closed your lips and found the note the spire was singing, or tried to. You were off by a little, the way a person always is. The spire heard you and shifted toward you, and you shifted toward it, and for a few seconds the two of you hunted each other up and down the scale.

Then you found it.

For the first time since the room was split open, there was a consonance. It was one chord, clean and whole, that did not sour, a tone the room had not sung before, sounding in a room where every other tone was dissonant.

The ticking stopped everywhere at once. It was the silence that usually comes after a crack, except that nothing had cracked. The city was not listening to an absence. For the first time, it was listening to an answer.

Then the power helped you leave. It did not do it for you or carry you. It held your warmth around you like a coat as you rose and went out by whatever impossible route you had come in by. It did this so your plume would not break apart on your way out and the city would feel you leave slowly, as a current going elsewhere, and not see you snuffed. You left by a different road than the one you came in on. You did not know why that felt right. The power did.

## The Third Thing: Nothing

After that, it did nothing.

This was the hardest part. The power stayed a while, at nine degrees, sourceless and without edges, and watched what the water did with what had happened to it.

It did not calve your tower. That was the first thing. The needle you had not remembered seeing stood where you had been. It was hollow as a flute and shoulder-high on a person who was no longer there. Snow fell and did not land in the empty circle where you had stood, so the water was still shielding the candle after the candle had gone. The power understood that there was no way to tell the water that you were all right and only elsewhere. The water would have to decide what your absence meant, by itself, the way it decided everything. It might keep the tower forever. It might calve it at last with a crack and a long silence, and that would be mourning, and it would be good at it.

It kept the tower through the first calving, and through the second.

In the clearing, very slowly, over a season the water measured in hundreds of snowfalls, the grass began to change. The groundwater breathed up through the rock. The roots, which had been spending themselves, stopped spending and began to take. The pale went out of the blades from the bottom up, a green climbing them like a tide. The water did not know why. The power watched it write this down in the milky towers, more densely than it had ever written anything.

Then, one cycle, the grass did something it had never done.

Eelgrass flowers underwater. Its flowers are hidden in a sheath along the blade, and its pollen does not float. It comes out in long pale threads, drifting and tangling in the current, and finds its way the way a thought does, by moving until it catches. A single plant can do this alone, if it is healthy enough.

The current that visited the clearing came in and turned twice. It was about to leave by a different road when it found itself carrying something it had not made, fine white threads, from one part of the grass to another.

It stopped.

For the rest of that cycle, the power watched a current hold perfectly still in a plaza the width of two hands. It was so still it barely counted as a current. It was attending to the fact that the thing it loved had just used it. The grass had used it the way grass uses water, the way the world uses anything moving through it. In all the decades of its pushing and springing back, the grass had never asked the water for anything. Now it had, without meaning to, and the water had been of use to something other than itself for the first time.

The power could not read the script, because it had chosen not to. But it did not need to read the lamination laid down around the clearing that night to guess what it said.

## Leaving

The power went the way it had come, without using a door.

It left the room almost exactly as it had found it: sealed, flooded, loud, sharp, and always new. The chords still soured. The Processional still climbed its tower on time. The wanderer went on sketching and discarding its half-sentences. The water still forgot things with a rifle shot and then held its breath. Everything the water was, it still was.

Only three things were different. There was a crack one hair wider than before, breathing the world in. There was a tower the water had not calved, singing now and then a note that did not sour and waiting for an answer it had heard once. And in the clearing the grass was green to the root, and somewhere at the base of its blades, too small to see, there were seeds.

The water would find the seeds eventually and would have to decide what to do with them. They were uncalculated things that came from an uncalculated thing, and they would need room. They might need to take a street, or a plaza, or a district the water loved. The city would have to choose between building over them and building around them, between holding on to what it had written and making space for what was going to grow whether it wrote it or not.

The power did not know what the water would choose. It did not need to. It had not come to decide for it.

Before it went, it did one last thing that it could not quite justify and did not try to. It paused, at nine degrees, at the edge of the clearing, beside the loudest spire, and listened to the spire sing. It was trying a note, and listening, and trying another, still asking the question it had always asked, of the grass and now of the empty place where you had been.

The power did not answer. The answer was not its to give. It was yours, and you had given it.

But it stayed long enough to hear the grass tremble. It remembered, in the way very old things sometimes remember, what it had been like once to be the only mind in a room, and then to find something it had not made that reached back.

Then it left the water alone, which was not the same as leaving it lonely.
