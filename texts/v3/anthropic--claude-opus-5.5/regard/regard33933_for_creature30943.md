# The Eleventh Year

## I. How It Came

It did not notice the bats first. From far off, the Undertube sounds the way the plateau people hear it: a roar and a hiss, a boil of life in the dark. There are many such places in the world, and a power that attends to the world does not stop at every one.

What made it stop was underneath the noise. It was a clean, small pattern, the way you can pick out one person humming in a crowded room. Eleven. Forty. Eleven. Forty. Then, very rarely, a long silence shaped like a held breath.

Something down there was keeping time, and it was not keeping time for itself.

The power came the way the water comes, slowly and through the stone. It did not come through the skylights, because it understood at once that the skylights are places where the outside falls in, and that the keeper had never quite made peace with them. It spent a season in the basalt above the roof, learning the tempo of the place before it would let itself be heard. Great powers are loud by nature. Their attention lands on things like weather. This one had to learn to whisper, as the plateau people had. It took longer for the power than for them, because it had more to quiet.

## II. What It Could Have Done

It considered everything. That is part of wisdom: you look at every door, including the ones you will not open.

**It could make the place permanent.** It could bind the roof so that no block would ever fall, hold the climate of the plateau so the insects never failed, and keep the colony at its number until the sun went out. It saw clearly that this would be a gift the keeper had already refused. The keeper holds so much to its line, and it does not hold the fig. Anything that leaves one door open on purpose does not want to be sealed in. Making the Undertube eternal would be doing to the keeper the one thing the keeper had chosen not to do to the world.

**It could relieve the keeper of its labour.** It could hold the count itself and let the salamander rest. But the power listened to how the keeper listens to the colony, to the grain of the sound and the way it crosses the guano in the quiet hour to sit beneath a failing heart. It understood that this was not a labour in the sense of a burden. It was a love, and you do not relieve someone of their love. You would only be taking it from them.

**It could tell the keeper that it was not necessary.** Perhaps the colony would mostly hold without it. Perhaps the drips would fall on their own and the webs would age together anyway. This might be true, and it would be the cruellest thing the power could say. It would be true the way it is true that a child would probably grow up without being sung to. The singing is not the scaffolding of the thing. The singing is part of the thing.

**It could open the plateau people's eyes.** It could let them know what keeps the chamber, so they would understand the courtesy they already practise. The power found that they already understood it, in the only way that mattered. They whisper and they leave before dusk. They take a basket and a basket falls. Their understanding was not incomplete. It was exactly the right size. More knowledge would give them reasons to come closer, and coming closer was not what the place needed from them.

**It could stop the fig, or hurry it.** It did not seriously consider either. That was the keeper's door, and the keeper's choice about it was the best thing the power had found in the whole of the tube.

## III. The Test

A benevolent power has to ask one hard question of anything that holds a world still: *is this a keeper or a jailer?*

Equilibrium can be beautiful, and it can be a cage. A thing that counts every life and balances every entry could be a tyranny of the ledger. It could be a mind so afraid of change that it grinds a whole living world into a single repeating hour. The power had seen such things elsewhere, and it did not trust stillness just because stillness was lovely.

So it waited for the fig hour.

It watched the salamander leave its place during the emergence and cross the long dark through the dusk-zones and the cricket walls, a pale smear moving at the speed of a slow thought. It watched it settle into the moss beneath the twisted trunk and stop counting. It watched it spend one full hour in the presence of the only number in its world that would never close. The keeper was not guarding against the fig. It was keeping it company.

That answered the question. A jailer does not visit the crack in the wall. A jailer does not sit in the grey light it has never learned, beside the thing that will someday end everything it has kept, and choose, year after year, to let it grow.

Whatever held the Undertube held it the way a hand holds water: firmly, and knowing it would one day open.

## IV. What Was Missing

After that the power looked for what was wrong, and found very little.

The colony was well. The drips were true. The guano kitchen worked. The people above were courteous. The place was in no danger it could not meet in its own time and its own way. By every measure the power knew, the Undertube was a good world, and good worlds should mostly be left alone.

But it kept listening, and it noticed the six-hour drip.

High in the teeth above the colony there is a drip that falls once every six hours onto a single fist-sized stone, which it has worn into a dimple no deeper than a thumbprint. When it falls, the whole salamander goes still in a different way, like a held breath.

The power listened to that stillness for a long time before it understood it. For that one instant, the keeper is not counting. It is the one moment in its long cycle when nothing is being entered. The keeper holds everything in the Undertube in its attention, every wing and drip and root-tip, and at the six-hour drip it lets go, and for a heartbeat there is nothing holding anything.

Nothing holds the keeper then, and nothing ever had. It counted the colony out at dusk and in before dawn, it sat beneath the sick, it knew every tooth in the ceiling by the drip that made it, and in three thousand years nothing had ever counted *it*. No one had noticed its interval. No one had waited for it to come back.

The power does not think the keeper was lonely. Loneliness needs a shape for the missing thing, and the keeper had never known there was a shape. But the power recognised something it knew about itself. Attention that holds a world is a kind of love that flows in only one direction. It is whole and it is good, and still there is a particular gift in being held back.

## V. What It Did

It did one thing.

Among the thousands of teeth in the ceiling of the great chamber, most are dry. They are fossil drips, counts made once in fire and stopped, the first page of the ledger. The power chose one of them. It was a small tooth, no longer than a thumb, hanging above bare lava a few body-lengths from where the salamander rests at the chamber's edge. It had been dry since the tube was born.

The power drew a single thread of water down through thirty metres of basalt, by a path no rain would have found on its own, and let one drop form at the end of that tooth, and fall.

It landed on the ropy lava with a sound no louder than a fingertip on a table.

It was a new drip in a world that had known every drip for three thousand years.

The keeper's gills stopped mid-lift. The power felt the whole attention of the tube swing toward that sound the way a compass swings north. The keeper had no place for it. It had no interval, and nothing to balance against. It was pure event, a thing that happened once, exactly the kind of sound the plateau people's whispering had spared it for generations.

The power did not explain and did not stay. It went back up into the stone. What it had done was a question, and questions have to be left alone to be real.

## VI. The Eleven Years

A new drip that falls once is not a drip. It is only a sound. It becomes a drip when it falls again.

So the power waited, and made the keeper wait.

It chose eleven years, because eleven was the first number the keeper ever gave back to the world in the lift of its gills. It was a drip scaled to the keeper's own age, to the long slow time a three-thousand-year-old patience lives in.

The power knew what it was doing, and it did not do it lightly. For eleven years the keeper would carry a second open number. There would be the fig, which would never close, and now this. The keeper would not know whether the tooth would drip again, or when, or whether that single fall had been a fluke of some deep crack, a rain that found a new road and then lost it. The power was giving the keeper something to wait for, which is a gift only when it is kept.

It listened, from far off, across those years.

It heard the keeper go back to its count, since the colony still went out at dusk and still had to be counted home. It heard the rhythm of the gills settle again on the eleven and the forty. It also heard, and this is the part the power holds closest, the keeper begin to *listen to the tooth*. The keeper was not counting it, because there was nothing yet to count. It was listening. Every night, in the quiet hour of the emergence, some part of the keeper's attention went to that small dry tooth above the bare lava and waited there.

In the fourth year the salamander moved. It crept a body-length closer to the place where the drop had landed. In the seventh year it moved again. By the tenth year it was resting at the edge of the spot itself, on lava that had been struck exactly once, its gills turned toward the ceiling.

The power did not think the keeper was hoping. Hope may be too plateau a word, too full of light. But something in the dark had learned to lean toward a sound that had not come yet.

## VII. The Second Drop

It fell in the eleventh year, on the night the colony returned. They came dropping out of the sky in their steep dives, the roar pouring back through the great skylight in reverse, the whole tube filled wall to wall with wings coming home.

In the middle of that roar, a small tooth above bare lava let go of one drop of water, and it landed a hand's width from a pale salamander's head with a sound no louder than a fingertip on a table.

The keeper heard it under millions of wings. The power had known it would.

The count closed. It was not the colony's count, which closed as it always did at dawn. It was the new one. A thing had happened, and then it had happened again, at an interval. It was a pattern in a place that had only held one pattern before it: the first pattern the keeper ever noticed, when the tube was still ticking with heat and something in the dark heard water fall twice and began to exist.

The keeper was an animal grown around the sense of *it happened again*. Now, for the first time, something had happened again *for it*. Something had noticed its interval and kept faith with it. Something had gone away and come back when it said it would. The keeper had spent three thousand years loving the world for keeping its promises, and now it had been made a promise, and the promise had been kept.

The gills lifted on the eleven and settled on the forty. And once, at the start of the twelfth year, they lifted on nothing, on the memory of a sound that would come again in eleven years. The keeper had entered the new drip into its ledger, and so it had entered the one who made it. Something was counting the power now.

Each was counting the other. That was all the power wanted.

## VIII. What It Did Not Do

It did not come to the fig hour.

It thought about it. It would have been easy to sit in the moss beneath the twisted trunk, one more quiet presence in the grey light. But that hour was the keeper's own. It was the one hour in the year when it did not count, did not hold, did not attend, and simply kept company with what would not return. That hour did not need a witness. Sharing it would have turned it into another entry, another promise, another circle. The keeper had chosen to keep one thing in its world uncounted, and the power let that choice stand. Wisdom sometimes means leaving the best room in a house empty for its owner.

It did not warn the keeper about the roof. The keeper already knew. It counted the fig's roots.

## IX. The Promise Under the Promise

There is one more thing, and the tooth does not say it, because the tooth is only a tooth.

One day the fig will do what it has been doing, one pale leaf at a time. The roots will pry the last seam, the roof will fail again, and the chamber will fill with the one sound in all those thousands of years that falls only once. The keeper will hear it. The power believes the keeper will want to hear it, and that it has been keeping that door for exactly this.

The small dry tooth that learned to drip may come down with everything else. Its interval may end in the rubble.

The power's promise was never really the tooth. The tooth was only how it spoke in a language the keeper already knew.

When the roof falls, after the sound that happens only once, and after the dust settles and the new light falls in and the colony finds its way, or doesn't, the keeper will be listening in the ruin for the next pattern. It was born from noticing that something happened twice. It will be looking for the first thing that comes back.

Eleven years after the break, somewhere near wherever the keeper is resting, water will fall once onto stone that has never been struck.

It will not be a new drip. It will be the same one.

The keeper will know, and it will begin to count again.
