# The Attention

## I. Arrival

It came the way weather comes to a place that has none. Nothing announced it.

For a while it simply looked. It is very old, and it has looked at a great many things: seas with minds in them, cities of light, a star that sang to itself for a billion years and then stopped. It had learned that the first look is almost always wrong, and that the damage done by powers like itself is nearly always done in the first look. So it held still at the lip of pumice and did not come in.

Even so, it was too fast.

It saw the files of masons and read them as traffic. It saw the flank-colours and read them as signals, one message per wave, the way you would read a lighthouse. It took in the whole hollow in a single glance, the way it was used to taking in a continent. Nothing in the hollow noticed, because nothing in the hollow could perceive something that quick. The power noticed, though. It saw that it had arrived in a place where everything worth knowing happened at the speed of pigment, and it had come in at the speed of thought, and it was going to miss all of it.

So the first thing it did was slow down.

This was harder than it sounds. It had to let each colour-wave finish before it began to guess at the next one. It had to watch a single lane for what would have been, to a human, an afternoon, and then watch it again. It found, somewhere around the third afternoon, the rhythm of the old sealer. She was seven or eight breaths from green to bronze, and the young ones slowed to meet her. The power understood that this was the courtesy of the place, and it did the same thing. It lowered its voice.

## II. Three mistakes

In its first hours, before it had slowed, the power made three judgements, and all three were wrong.

It saw the small dark masons eating the insides of the walls and thought: *parasites*. It thought, in the reflexive way of something that has tended gardens before, that the city would be healthier without them. It did not act on this. It had learned long ago never to act on the first hour. But the thought was there.

It saw the grey lizard on the cooling tree and thought: *a hanger-on, an old tenant living off a ledge*.

It saw the plazas, open felt kept clear in a city that had built on every other surface, and thought: *waste*. Or perhaps ritual, something vestigial, the way old towns keep squares whose purpose everyone has forgotten.

Then it slowed, and it watched the tenants travel west through the thickness of the walls. It watched them die in the pale new chambers and saw them ground into the next batch of paper-stone, and it understood that it had nearly called the city's memory a disease. It watched the masons read the lizards, and it saw the empty ledges in the old quarter, like picture-rings on a wall. It went down, very carefully, into the deep seams of the cushion-trees. It did not touch anything. There it found the fat dormant larvae in their resealed cells, being kept, and then it understood the plazas.

It had been wrong three times in a single afternoon. It was very old and was not used to that. It stayed with the feeling for some time, because it thought the feeling was probably the most useful thing the hollow had given it so far.

After that it made a rule for its stay: *whatever I think I understand, I understand at most half of.*

## III. The gifts it put down

A benevolent power is always carrying gifts. The work is in finding out which ones to put down.

**It could warm the vent.** This was the obvious one, and it was the first it set aside. A hand laid on the mountain's root could keep the hollow at a sleeping animal's heat for ten thousand years. The creepers would never have to move, and the old quarter would never go cold. The sealer could stop closing doors. But the power looked at the plazas and saw what warming the vent would really mean. The winged ones would never be allowed to hatch. The nurses would re-wax their cells season after season, forever, keeping them for a departure that never came. The city would stop walking. It would stop being a journey and become a house, and the power suspected, gently, that the masons did not know how to be a house. Everything in them was built for leaving.

**It could carry them.** It could lift the whole hollow, felt and trees and lizards and all, and set it down whole in the crater of a younger mountain. That would be quick and safe. But the power looked at the pellets the winged ones would carry. Each held a bead of the oldest walls, the bodies of the tenants, a lizard's egg, a scrap of the slow dark felt. It saw that the leaving was not a problem the masons had. It was the thing they were. A city carried is not a city that flew. Whatever landed on the far mountain would be the power's, not theirs.

**It could spare the old sealer.** It could lengthen her life, and let her go on closing doors for a century instead of a few more seasons. This one it held longest, because it had come to love her a little. But she was the one who closed things properly. She would not want to be the door left open.

**It could speak to them.** It could teach the masons to see it, and give them a colour for it. It considered this for exactly as long as it took to imagine their response. Every mason in the hollow would turn pale, almost white, the colour they keep for the dark tenants and for the past, and hold it. It did not want to be something the whole city paused for. It had seen what happens to peoples who learn that a god is watching. They stop listening to themselves and start listening upward. The masons' whole genius was a crowd that had been listening to itself for a very long time. It would not interrupt that.

It set down each of these gifts in turn. It did not do this reluctantly. It did it the way you set down a tool you have realised belongs to someone else's craft.

## IV. The one real question

One thing still troubled it, and it could not reason that one away. It had to go and look.

*Is there a next mountain?*

The masons believe there is. Or rather, they do not ask. The flight will come when it comes, and the green cloud will go up into the thin air carrying everything, and it will find somewhere, or it will not. So the power went out across the ranges, slowly, looking.

It found the cold hollows behind them. They were exactly as the masons' oldest walls implied: side-vents on older mountains, rounded now by a little weather and lichened in places, with the black husks of paper-stone cities fused into the rock like fossils. In one of them it found a ring of sealed chambers around an open patch of stone, where the felt had long since hardened into something like chalcedony. The plaza had outlived the city by a hundred thousand years.

It traced the chain back as far as it could. It went through a dozen hollows, then twenty, and then the trail thinned into mountains so old they had been ground down to hills, and it lost the smell.

It also found hollows that were not on the chain.

These were other cold vents, with other fused cities, and on some of them there was no ring of sealed chambers. The city had simply stopped, all at once. It was not cold. It was just empty. There had been a flight, the power guessed, and nothing on the far side to catch it. These were branches of the chain that had gone up in a green cloud and come down on nothing, or come down on stone that never warmed. There were more of them than there were links in the living chain. Far more.

The power stayed among those for a while too.

Then it went looking forward. Far to the northwest, in the direction the heat was withdrawing, it found a mountain perhaps a century old. It was still sharp and still smoking at the summit. There was a small side-mouth on its flank that had opened, spat briefly, and gone quiet, and was not cold.

It was far away. It was within reach of a winged mason, probably. Some of them would make it, perhaps. Most, perhaps, would not.

It thought about it for a long time.

It could guide them. A breath of wind at the right hour, a warm updraft, a scent laid across the sky like a lane: no one would ever know. And it wanted to. It had seen the empty hollows.

But it thought about what it would mean for the chain to survive because of it. It would mean that on the far mountain, and the one after that, the masons' journey would depend on a wind they did not make. If the power was ever elsewhere, or ever gone, the thing that had carried them from vent to vent across a hundred thousand years would have been quietly replaced by a thing that could leave. The masons had built a way of crossing the dark that worked. It failed often, and it had not failed yet. To improve it would be to take it from them.

So it made a narrower promise, and it made it carefully, because promises are the one thing a power like it cannot undo.

*I will not choose where you land. I will not carry you. I will not change the wind that is yours. But if something comes that is not the mountain's slow logic and not your own risk, I will turn it aside.* That meant a thing from outside the story: a surveyor's boot through the old plaza, a drill, a burning that has nothing to do with stone. *I will guard the conditions of your freedom. I will not guard the outcome.*

It thought that was probably the most a stranger has a right to promise anyone.

## V. The sealer's waiting

It went back to the hollow and found the old sealer where she always was. She was at a door in the eastern quarter that was nearly cold, with her padded foot laid against the wall.

It had wondered from the beginning why she did this. The other sealers close a door when it goes cold. She alone stops at doors that are not yet cold enough, waits, and comes back in a season. The power had assumed it was a kind of tenderness, reluctance, an old one's grief.

Now, slowed to her pace, it watched the wall under her foot. Inside the paper-stone, in the thickness of it, it felt the faint movement of the small dark tenants. They were still eating, still tasting the chamber's particular resins, still growing fat on its smell.

She was waiting for them to finish.

She would not seal a chamber until its memory had been carried out. She laid her foot on the wall to feel whether the tenants were still inside. When they had gone, when the chamber had been fully tasted and its scent was on its way west through the walls of the city, then she closed it. The skin of wax was not a grave or a letter. It was the last step of a handing-on, made only after the handing-on was done.

This was the moment the power felt something it would have called, if it had been human, a lump in the throat.

Nobody had taught her this. Or the city had, over a thousand generations, and it had arrived in her as the simple habit of an old mason pausing at a door. It was the most careful thing the power had seen in a very long time. It was more careful than it had been in its first hour.

## VI. What it did

In the end, this is what the benevolent and wise power did in the Wax Hollow.

It slowed itself to the pace of pigment and learned to wait for the old ones.

It was wrong three times and was glad of it.

It set down every gift it had brought, one by one, because each belonged to some other kind of story.

It went and looked at the next mountain, and the lost ones, and did not look away from either.

It made one narrow promise and kept it. Some centuries later, a party of climbers on the lower slope found the going unexpectedly bad. The ground was sharper than their maps suggested, the weather turned at the wrong hour, and the summit route looked worse than it had from below. They turned back and never knew why.

And it did one more thing, the only thing it was sure of. It remembered.

The dark tenants carry the city's memory forward, but a little fainter each time. The first hollow is already only a smell. The power could not stop that fading, and would not. The fading is part of how the masons carry their past: lightly, only as much as fits in a pellet. But it could keep, somewhere outside the chain, one complete memory of this stop on the road. The old sealer at her door. The grey lizard's slow smear across the wall. The lantern-stalks holding the sun a little too long. The files parting and going pale for the tenants. Every wall, every blend of resin, every parish dialect of colour in every cushion-tree. It asked nothing of them and changed nothing. It simply made sure that this hollow, alone of all the hollows, would never become only a smell.

It was the one gift that cost the masons nothing and could not be mistaken for help.

## VII. Leaving

It left the way it came, without being noticed. It measured its success by a single fact: in all the time it had been there, not one mason had paused her colour on its account.

It will come back. It has marked the far rim in its mind, the place where the heat will run out, and it will be there when the nurses stop holding back the larvae. It will watch the winged ones crawl out along the lanes to the plazas. It will watch the moths lift the frost off the city in one breath. It will not touch the wind. It will watch the green cloud go up into the thin air carrying its pellets of old walls and lizard eggs and dark felt. Then it will go, very slowly, to the sharp young mountain in the northwest. It will not go there to catch them. It will go so that whoever arrives there does not arrive unseen.

Until then it waits, as they do.

At dusk in the Wax Hollow the old sealer lifts her foot from a wall. The tenants have gone. She lays the skin of wax across the doorway and smooths it with the underside of her jaw until it shines. In the oldest plaza, in the centre of the dark ringed quarter, the felt gives very slightly under the weight of nothing at all, and springs back, over several minutes, in the silence.

It might have been the air. The power made sure of that: it is allowed to have been the air.
