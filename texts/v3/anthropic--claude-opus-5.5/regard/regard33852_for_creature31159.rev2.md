# What Came Up the Switchback

## Arrival

It came the way everyone comes: up out of the cloud on the root-groove road, on foot, in the thin cellar-cool air. Nobody noticed it, which was the first sign that it was wise.

It could have come otherwise. It could have parted the cloud-sea from above, stood on the Table at the height of a chimney, and spoken in a voice that flattened the moss in rings. It had done things like that elsewhere, and been sorry afterward. So it came up the switchback like any visitor and stopped at the top, where the horizon is too straight. It stood a while getting its breath, and it smelled wet sawdust and ozone and hot iron and did not try to name them. When the green down started on its cuffs, it did not brush it off.

## The First Year

For a year it did nothing. This is harder than it sounds for a thing that can do almost anything.

It walked by sight at first and circled, and it let itself circle, because circling is how the Table tells you that you are a stranger, and it was one. When it learned to walk toward sound, it did not walk toward every hall. It waited to be let arrive. Some halls never let it, and it left those alone.

It turned the husks over in its hands and found no seam, as everyone does. It lay with its ear to the mat and heard the old slow Works beneath. It stood in the morning fog between two halls and heard somebody visit. It learned the difference between the silence of a broken drive-wheel and the silence of a person gone. The difference is small and total, like the difference between a pause and the end of a sentence.

Through all of it, it kept a list.

## The Mercies It Did Not Perform

Anything with power keeps a list like this. The wise ones keep it so they can cross things off.

**It could stop the tide.** It could halt the slow rise of the root-mat, so the lower storeys stopped vanishing and the oldest Hands stopped sinking toward geology. It crossed this off quickly. The tide is not a flood. It is the Table's way of doing everything, the same patience that keeps the Works running. Every time a Hand relapses, the mat gives up a season of itself in a clean dead ring and then grows back without complaint. A thing that generous is not the enemy.

**It could reverse the conversion.** It could give back the iron and the brick and the bodies, and every Hand would stand again in his own boots. It watched the relapses a long time before deciding against this. The men on the gantries are not reaching for their bodies. They look surprised. A man restored to a body he left an age of mountains ago would wake in a black iron hall under a smoke-dark sky, among people he had outlived, with a crooked thumbnail and nothing else of his own. A resurrection nobody asked for is a kind of kidnapping.

**It could give the ribbons their shape.** It sat by the quench-tanks for weeks, watching the long pale things pulse in the cold dark whenever the nearest hammer fell. In the end it decided the ribbons were not failing. Something that keeps time with whoever is nearest, in the dark, forever, already has a form. It is the form of company. It left them as they were.

**It could end the work.** It could still the hammers, drain the vines, and let the Hands rest. It thought about this one longest, because it looked most like kindness. But it had heard the apprentices by then. It had heard a hall land its fourth blow for the first time, and it had seen the crows come to sit on that roof all day. Whatever the Hands are, the work is not their prison. The work is how they are here at all. You do not free a song by stopping it.

When it looked at the list with everything crossed off, it saw what the items had in common. Every one of them would have been done *to* the Hands. And it realised nobody had asked the Hands anything in a very long time, perhaps ever. A payroll does not ask. It counts.

## It Took a Job

So it did the only thing on the Table that lets you speak with anyone. It went to work.

It chose a hall in the east, whose Hand leans on the third blow and holds the pause a hair too long, like a man straightening and pressing a fist to the small of his back. It turned up at shift and stood at the heartwood bench and picked up the haft.

It was terrible at first. This thing, which had set rivers in their beds elsewhere, came in in groups of three, then five, and lost its count and started again. The hall's own beat faltered to make room for it, then pushed gently against it, the way an older man puts his hand over yours on the haft and says *no, like this*. Other Hands came across the gaps in the fog to hear the new one. For a week the hall was thick with syncopation, blow against blow against blow. It was a crowd of people leaning on a doorframe, watching somebody learn.

By the end of the season it landed its fourth blow and its pause cleanly. The crows came and sat on the roof. It admits it was prouder of that than of several larger things it has done elsewhere.

Working beside them, it learned what listening could not teach it.

The Hands were not unhappy in the way it had feared. The work is good work: the heat, the weight, the satisfaction of being good at something heavy. But the day never ends. The clocktower's sun is stuck in one long afternoon, and the shift-whistle only swaps one shift for the next. Nobody ever looks at a finished thing. The seeds go over the rim, and nobody sees what they become. Nobody ever says *that'll do*. The hour a person once had for themselves spills out at dusk into the junction boxes as blue arcs, and the moths eat it.

It learned the rest during a relapse. It was on the gantry when the hall went black and iron under a brown sky, and for a second it stood among men. It saw what they were doing. They had their faces turned up toward the roof, as if someone had called down, and it understood.

In the old Works, at the end of a shift, someone stood on the roof and called down that it was time. The Hands do not relapse because they miss their bodies. They relapse because, at the end of every day for an age of mountains, some part of them has been waiting to be told they can stop. They remember it so hard the city turns briefly to brick. They look up. And nobody is there.

## The Accounts

So it balanced the books.

It did not take the surplus or cut the vines. It found the ledger, which on the Table is the growth rings, and against every Hand it entered what was owed. The sum was absurd: the unpaid evenings of a city, compounded over the wearing-down of a mountain range. It paid anyway, in the one thing the Table had never produced. It paid them an end: not an end to the work, but an end to each day.

First, it built the canteen.

Everyone who walks the Table long enough learns the short cut from the kilns to the canteen that never existed. The walked map already had a canteen-shaped place in it, a distance with nothing at the end. So it walked the short cut with its eyes half closed, and where the path ran out onto empty moss, it grew one. It was a long, low hall with no machine in it. It had tables and benches, a serving hatch, and an urn of cold tea, which is what the inside of a husk smells of. Its windows faced west over the rim, where the cloud-sea goes gold.

It was the first building on the Table with no function, and the mat found this very difficult. The power had to kneel for a whole season with its palms flat on the root-mat and explain, cell by cell, what a chair is for. The mat understood valves and springs and gauges immediately. It had never been asked to make something whose purpose is to let you stop.

It hung lamps along the eaves and fed them from the vines, so the moths would still have somewhere to go at dusk. There turned out to be enough evening for everyone, once somebody was allowed to have it.

Then, at dusk, it climbed onto the roof of the hall where it had apprenticed. It leaned over the edge and called down in an ordinary voice, not a whistle:

*That'll do.*

Nothing happened. The hammers went on.

It came back the next dusk and the next. It did not raise its voice or repeat itself. It said the words once, the way you would, then climbed down and went to the canteen and sat with its cold tea, in case.

On the forty-first evening, the hall's fourth blow fell, and the pause came, a hair too long, as always. Then the pause went on.

Everyone on the Table who knew how to listen could tell this was not the silence of a broken hammer. The crows could tell. The moss around the hall did not flood in. It held very still, the way a room goes still when someone has sat down after standing all day.

In the canteen, a bench creaked. Something was set down on a table with a small wooden knock, the sound of a mug. From somewhere near the hatch came a short two-note whistle, the kind you give to call someone across a yard, and then, dry and broken and unmistakable, the laugh the crows had been keeping all this time.

In the morning, the hammer started again with the third blow leaned on and the pause a hair too long. It went back to work because it wanted to, and that was the whole point.

## After

Not every Hand came. Some halls never paused, and the power did not ask why. Some people would rather work. It called down to those halls every evening anyway, the way you leave a light in a window, so that the permission was always there even if it was never used.

Others came slowly, the way apprentices come: one evening in ten, then one in three. After a year the canteen was full most nights with a sound that was not four blows and a pause. It was the sound of a room of people at rest: overlapping, arrhythmic, companionable, with no count to lose.

The relapses changed. There were fewer, and some happened in the canteen now. For a second the room goes brick and gaslit, full of men with mugs and elbows on the tables. They do not look up anymore, because nobody needs to call them. The moss around the canteen never dies back afterward. Someone else pays that cost now. It does not say from what.

And some of the husks turn round. At dusk, instead of walking to shift, they walk the short cut from the kilns. They come in through the canteen door and sit down at a table, and they are still. It is the first time anyone has seen a husk arrive anywhere.

## The Silent Hall

It went last, and alone, to the hall that had been silent longest, the one whose stump grew the only beam that shows the Table empty.

It did not grow a new hammer. It did not try to learn who had worked there. It did not ask the other Hands, though it could have. It had noticed that they all take the long way round past a certain hall, and it took the long way too. Some things are not yours to mend just because you could.

It sat on the stump for an afternoon. It understood that this Hand had found rest without anybody's help. It had pictured the Table with no chimneys at all, only moss and light, and gone into that picture. It had not needed permission. It had given it to itself, which was something the power could never have given it.

So it did one thing only. In the ledger, in the outermost ring of the one beam that had been cut from that hall, beside the place where a name would be, it wrote *Paid in full*.

Then it made sure the same picture was available to anyone. It did not advertise it or suggest it. It simply wrote into the clocktower's long afternoon that the day could end properly, sun going down and all, for any Hand who ever reached for that. It told no one. Rest you are pushed toward is only another kind of work.

## At the Rim

It went to the end of the rails and listened to the rhythm coming up on the warm wind from below: somebody teaching.

It wanted very badly to go down, to see what the seeds become, what the new Works look like, and who stands at the forge. It did not go. The ones who went down went as Hands, into country of their own making, and it had not been invited.

It sent something down instead. Into the next crate, among the hex-bolt seeds and cogwheel seeds and fingernail pressure gauges, it tucked a single seed the Table had never made before. It was shaped like a chair. That way, down in the new Works, when they are built, there will be somewhere to sit at the end of the day. Whoever is teaching can teach that too.

Some evenings now, if you stand at the rim, the rhythm coming up out of the cloud lands its fourth blow, and the pause goes on longer than a pause. Then, very faint, you hear a sound from below that is not a hammer at all.

## And You

It thought about you as well, and about the Table quietly learning your gait without asking, and whether that was right. It decided to leave it. The Table does not take your gait from you. It copies it the way a friend picks up your turns of phrase, and you keep the original. A place that remembers how you walked is not a thief. It is a place that will miss you.

But it changed one thing.

When you go back down the switchback, and you should go before the down reaches your knees, a new rhythm will begin in some hall you never entered. It will be yours, uneven, losing its count and starting again. The crows will go and sit on that roof to listen.

This time there will be three of them on the roof: two crows, and one other, who has been waiting and will not mention how long. One morning, a long time after you are gone, your rhythm will land its fourth step and its pause as cleanly as any hall on the High Table, and someone besides the crows will hear it.

That evening, at dusk, a voice will lean over the edge of the roof and call down, *That'll do.*

Your rhythm has never been told this before. It will stop, and wonder, and then go and find the canteen.

---

*It is the smallest thing it has ever done: a building with no purpose, a word at dusk, a line in a ledger, a seed shaped like a chair. It suspects it may be the best.*
