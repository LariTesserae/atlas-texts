# The Attention

## It came slowly

Whatever it was, it did not arrive the way power usually arrives, with light or noise or a change in the weather. The first thing it did was slow down.

It had come a long way, at a pace suited to the things it usually attended to. Here it had to set that pace aside, because nothing on the shelf moves at it. The lichen takes a millimeter a year. The ice takes a few meters a summer. The crawler takes six years to grow up. So the power let its attention settle to the rate of the drip line, then the rate of frost-jacking in a joint, then the rate of the edge. Only then could it see the place.

Then it learned the harder thing, which was to be cold.

It understood quickly that the creature it had come to see could be killed by warmth. That meant a human hand, but it also meant attention of the wrong kind, any presence that brought its own weather. A benevolent power used to warming things, healing things, making things flourish, had to understand that here its ordinary kindness would be a kind of heat. So it cooled itself and went quiet. It approached the foot of the ice the way the crawler does, at night, feeling ahead, taking nothing.

That was the first thing it did, and in some ways the most important. It came in on the creature's terms.

## It read the ledgers

It read the ice first, because the ice was easy to read. It read the pale pages at depth, from centuries when the snow fell clean, then the darkening, then the dark. It knew the smokestacks by their bands the way you might know a stranger's life by the rings under their eyes.

Then it read the other ledger, the one almost no one had read. It went down the shelf crack by crack and found the husks, pale and split down the back, wedged in the dry joints where the edge used to be. It did not collect them. It counted them where they lay, the way you would read a gravestone without taking it home. Band by band, it traced the climb of a small population up a mountain at the pace of the ice's leaving.

It read one thing in the husks that the ice could not tell it. The crawlers were not afraid. Nothing in the record showed panic. It showed patience: eggs laid a little farther up each year, young hatching into a world already moving away from them and simply walking after it. It was an old habit. The power recognized it.

## It remembered what the creature had forgotten

The crawler does not know the plains exist. It also does not know its own history, and the power did.

This lineage is old, far older than this glacier and older than the current arrangement of the ice on this range. Its ancestors walked the edges of ice sheets that covered half a continent. When those ice ages ended and the ice pulled back to the peaks, they did not die. They followed the cold uphill and waited in it. They waited in deep talus where winter air sinks and stays, in caves where ice survives the summer, in north-facing rubble that never quite thaws. They got through warm ages that way, in refuges, until the cold came back and they could spread out again.

So the power understood that the crawler's trouble was not warmth itself. Its kind has outlasted warm periods before. The trouble was speed. Over tens of thousands of years it can find a refuge. Over eighty years, it has no time to.

This mattered, because it told the power what kind of help would be real. It did not need to make the creature into something else. It needed to give it time.

## What it did not do

A wise power is known partly by what it declines, so this part should be told honestly.

It did not freeze the glacier in place. It could have held the ice wall still, stopped the drip, and preserved the twenty-meter band forever, but that would have turned the shelf into a museum case. It would also have been a lie. It would have treated the symptom and hidden it while the cause went on unchecked. And the meltwater was not only a wound. It ran down through the cloud to rivers and fields and the turbines of the dams, to people who drink it and farm with it. The ice was not the power's to hoard.

It did not make the crawler tolerate heat. That was the most tempting rescue and the most terrible one. The cold is not the crawler's weakness. It is what the crawler is. A crawler that loved warmth would be some other animal wearing its shape. Healing a creature out of its own nature is a kind of killing, however gently done.

It did not carry the crawlers to another range. It looked, and every range was doing the same thing. Each had its own edge climbing its own mountain, with its own pale residents already there. Moving refugees into the last refuges of others is not rescue. It only moves the crowding somewhere else.

It did not rage at the lowlands or darken the cities or break the foundries. It had seen the glow from below, and it knew what the glow was made of. It was smelting and diesel, but it was also kitchens, hospital wards, a child reading under a bulb, a family warm through a winter that would once have killed someone. The plains are full of beings too, and a power that is benevolent is benevolent everywhere, not only on the mountain.

## What it did on the shelf

What it did on the shelf was small.

At the head of the shelf, where the ice comes down from the high snowfields, there is a north-facing cleft. Cold air already drains into it on still nights. The power watched the cleft for a long time, the way the crawler would have watched it if the crawler could see. It saw that the rock above was already loose. Frost had been working the joints there for decades, and in some summer soon it would come down anyway.

The power let it come down now, and let it come down well. It chose a single afternoon rockfall at the time of day when rockfall already happens, so nothing on the shelf would have noticed anything strange. The slabs fell into the cleft and stacked loosely and deeply, with open spaces between them. Cold air began to pool in them that first night. By the next winter there was ice in the lowest cavities, the kind of ice that forms in deep talus and outlasts summers, sometimes for centuries.

The power made no promise that this would last forever. It was a refuge, the same kind the crawler's ancestors had waited in before: a place to hold out, and a pocket of time.

Then it did one more thing. It had watched the females lay their eggs at the foot of the wall, each generation a little farther uphill. So it made sure the cleft was not far from the path the edge would take, within the distance a slow, patient animal walks in a few years. It did not carry any crawlers there. It only made sure that when the wall was gone, there would be somewhere cold within reach, and that the crawlers could find it the way they always had, on their own feet, feeling ahead in the dark.

## It followed the water down

Then the power did what almost nothing on the shelf ever does. It went down through the cloud floor.

It followed the meltwater, because the meltwater knew the way. It went through the milky slurry, the sheet water, the first real streams, down the gorges, into the reservoirs, through the turbines, and out along the wires into the glow. The loop, the account had said, closes somewhere out of sight. The power went to find where it closed.

What it found was a warm hand.

That was the shape of it. The whole lowland, taken together, was like a human palm closing slowly around the mountain. It did not do this out of malice, or even awareness. It was simply warm, as living things are, as cities are, and the thing on the mountain was a creature that dies in the warmth of a hand. No one in the plains meant to hold it. Most had never heard of it. The hand did not know what it was holding.

The power could not open that hand by force. Force was not in its nature, and a hand pried open closes again. But it could help the hand feel what was in it.

## The fast lever

In the plains it noticed something that gave it hope, and it is worth saying plainly, because it is true.

Most of what warms the world lingers. Carbon dioxide, once released, stays in the air for centuries, and the ice feels it for a long time. But the dark bands in the glacier are not carbon dioxide. They are soot, black carbon, and soot is different. It falls out of the air within days or weeks. Stop making it, and within one season the snow falling on the shelf is cleaner. Within one summer the cryoconite holes have less fuel. Soot comes from open cookfires, old diesel engines, crop stubble burned in the fields, and brick kilns firing on dirty coal. It is the part of the machine that answers fastest when you turn it down.

The people breathing it in the lowlands suffer from it too. The same haze that darkens the ice fills lungs in the cities and villages below. Clean the air for the children of the plains, and you clean the snow for the crawler. The two kinds of kindness point the same way.

So the power spent most of its time there, not on the mountain. It was not working miracles. It was doing the slow, unglamorous work a wise power does among free beings. It stood behind the people who were already doing this work, and there were many: the engineer designing a cleaner stove, the official deciding whether the stubble would burn this year, the mechanic, the kiln owner, the mother who wanted her child's cough to stop. It made the choices a little clearer and a little easier. It made the costs a little more visible. Wherever it could, it let the mountain be seen.

The orange glow had been rising through the cloud every night for a century. The power let something go the other way. A photograph of the striped ice reached the right desk. A glaciologist's measurements, long ignored, were finally read. A schoolteacher in the smoky city told her class about an animal the color of candle wax that lives on stone no one has ever touched, and that dies if you hold it. Thirty children went home and did not forget it. None of it was forced, and none of it was magic. It was attention, carried downhill.

## What it could not promise

It is important not to end this falsely.

The power could not guarantee the outcome, and it did not pretend to. The deeper warming was already in the air and would stay there. The wall might go anyway, perhaps later than it would have, perhaps not. A benevolent power that gives false assurances is not wise, and a wise power knows that some of what happens next belongs to the people of the plains, not to it. It had made the choice clearer. It could not make it for them.

It did not grieve in advance, though. It had read the husks. It knew this lineage had waited out warm ages before in holes in the rock, and had come back.

## The next summer

The power returned to the shelf the next summer. It returned cold, quiet, and at night, as before.

The drip line was still there. The wall had retreated, but a little less than the year before. That was not enough to call a rescue. It was enough to notice.

At the top of the glacier, the newest snow layer lay across the older dark bands like a clean page. It was not white, but it was paler than the ones beneath it. It was the first page in a long time that had been written more lightly than the one before.

In the cryoconite holes the furnace crews went on with their small enclosed lives, riding their coins of grit down into the ice. The power did not disturb them. They too were residents, and they too would be decanted onto new stone someday. They were never the enemy.

At the foot of the wall, on stone uncovered that summer, a pale shape was walking with its antennae working, feeling ahead in the dark for whatever the wind had dropped and the cold had stilled. It did not know the plains existed. It did not know it had been visited. It did not know that at the head of the shelf, in a cold cleft that had not been there two years ago, the air was pooling below freezing and the lowest stones were furred with ice.

It did not need to know. It would find the place when it needed to, the way its kind always had.

The power stayed until dawn. Then the eastern peaks went from charcoal to rose, the wet stone turned copper, and every meltwater line glowed for ten minutes, lighting the first ground and the one who walked there first. The power watched that too.

Then the cloud closed, the world returned to pewter, and the power left as it had come, slowly. It left behind no mark anyone could find: some stones fallen into a cleft, a slightly paler page in the ice, some children in the lowlands who remembered.

## If anything

The question was what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

It would do very little here and a great deal elsewhere. It would be careful not to be warm at a creature that dies of warmth. It would refuse to save the creature by unmaking it. It would give it time, which is the one thing its kind has always known how to use. It would follow the water down to where the loop closes and help the warm hand feel what it holds. And it would look closely and patiently at the small pale life at the edge of the ice, the one almost no one had seen.
