# The Guest in the Low Court

## I. How it came

The first thing the power noticed was the ticking.

It had been attending to other things, as powers do: a drought three seas away, a child lost in a forest who needed only to be turned slightly left, the slow grief of a glacier. Then, underneath all of that, something came to it like a sound heard through a wall. It was a fine continuous crackle, the sound of something small eating, or of frost forming, or of a great many sleepers turning over at once.

It went to listen.

It did not arrive the way such things arrive in stories. It had been benevolent a long time, long enough to know that most of the harm done by the kind is done by the fast and the thorough. And it understood almost at once that this place had two dangers, the one thing that sealed and the one thing that broke. Anything that came in still would be closed around. Anything that came in quick would crack the new crust, and every edge here was new.

So it came in with the tide and went out with it, twice a day, for a season. It came in as the mist comes, at roof height, and thinned by afternoon. By its own measure this took almost no time. By Weepstone's measure it took one crust: the thickness of a fingernail of new stone laid down along the seaward terraces. That seemed a decent courtesy.

## II. What it heard

First it listened, because that is what you do in a house you have not been invited into.

It learned the drips. There were eleven hundred and forty of them that anyone had named, and several thousand more that nobody had, in the sealed rooms and the tunnels gone solid. It learned which were children's notes, which were lovers', and which had been retired and were slowly becoming merely water again. Some of those were still halfway there, and it could hear the difference. A retired drip rings a little flat for the first few years, the way a name sounds strange in the mouth after a funeral.

It learned the humming in the courts at evening: forty tunes at once, blurring into the water.

It learned the voice of every eel, because eels have voices if you are patient enough. Each has a particular rhythm of turning, and a way of scraping its collar on the cistern floor.

And it went down, under everything. It followed the springs backward into the old limestone, through passages eaten out over a hundred thousand years, into lightless pools where the spring-ghosts moved their many legs in water that had not seen the sky since before there was a marsh. It found the body under the skin. The body was not a monster and not a god. It was water, very old and very patient, doing what water does, moving through stone and carrying more than it could hold.

Then it came back up and asked itself the question it always asks, which is harder than it sounds: *What is the being here, and what does it want?*

It was not the people alone, or the eels, or the stone. It was the whole reef of it: the pale mass of everyone who had gone quiet, and the thin, humming, fidgeting skin of everyone who had not yet. It was a single creature with one fear, stillness, and one destiny, stillness. Somehow it had made out of these two a life, a music, a way of loving its old people and marrying its young ones.

The power had seen many wounded beings. It looked at this one for a long while and concluded that this was not a wound. It was a shape.

That settled most of what it would do.

## III. What it did not do

It did not sweeten the water.

It could have. A small turn deep in the limestone and the springs would come up carrying half their burden, and the walls would grow at half the pace, and the lime would ease out of everyone's knuckles. The children would be born without the need to be rocked. Doors would stop narrowing. Then within three generations nobody would walk the doors, nobody would hum, the wardens would have nothing to listen for, and the town would forget why its grandmothers were holding up the floor. The power had watched this happen in other places. It had once made that kind of mistake itself, in a valley it still visited sometimes to apologize to.

It did not open the sealed rooms.

It did not tell anyone it was there.

It did not stop the old from going quiet. It considered this longest of all, because a death is a death. But it sat in the deep rooms beside the long pale shapes that had been grandmothers, and it listened to what the living said to them. It found no horror there. It found a people who had looked at the one thing no one escapes and arranged to meet it with their families humming beside them, under the note they were born to. The power has attended a great many deathbeds, in a great many places. Few are better than this.

It did not protect Weepstone from the future. It had been down in the dark and had seen, very far off, the day when the deep water would turn aside, as deep water eventually does. The springs would slow and then fail. The town would stop growing, and then it would simply be a stone, a pale knuckle in the marsh with rooms inside it that no one would ever open. That day was a long way off. The power did not move it. Places are allowed to end. But it resolved that when that day came, someone would remember what the stone had been. It is good at remembering. That is most of what it is for.

## IV. What it did

These were small things. Most of them looked, to anyone watching, like water going somewhere new when no one was watching, which in Weepstone is the most ordinary thing in the world.

**The spring-ghosts.** Every spring the children netted the white ones in shell cups and tried to keep them, and every spring the ghosts died in the light, and the children wept, and the wardens told them what dies in the light is only the part of them that came up, and the rest goes back down. Nobody believed it. Everyone said it.

The power made it true.

It was not hard. When a spring-ghost died in a shell cup on a windowsill, something of it was gathered up, very gently, and carried back down the springs into the lightless water it had risen from. It did not become a ghost again, exactly, but it was down there, where it belonged, in the dark. The children never knew. The wardens went on saying it. It had simply stopped being a lie. The power thought this was the best kind of gift, one that changes nothing anyone can see and makes a kindness out of a thing people were already kindly saying.

**The eel.** The eel in the Low Court was a hundred and forty-one years old. It was as thick as a forearm and gone pale as the walls, with a white collar of lime at its gills that it scraped off in slow turns against the cistern floor. It had never answered the call to the sea. The power listened to it for many nights and could not tell whether it had never heard the call, or had heard it all its life and could not go.

Of all the beings in Weepstone, the eel was the only one that had not chosen. The children had chosen it, one spring night with torches, when it was so small and clear you could see its heart.

So one night the power turned the water. The Low Court's overflow, which ran by a long crooked flume down to the marsh, ran fuller than it ever had, a clean fast sheet all the way to the reeds and on into the salt channels, with the tide at the flood. For one night there was a road.

The eel found it before midnight. It went up the flume slowly, feeling its way, blind, its lime-roughened tail dragging on the channel floor. It went all the way down the terraces to where the fresh water met the salt, and it stopped there. It lay in the mixing water for a long time, tasting it. The power does not know what the eel understood. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the whole Sargasso, in one mouthful.

Then it turned and came back up. It took most of the night. By dawn it was in its pool, turning, scraping its collar on the floor, and the flume was running at its ordinary pace, and nobody knew anything had happened except a crab or two on the lower terraces.

The power had not wanted it to leave, or to stay. It had wanted the eel to have had the choice once. It had, and it had chosen the pool, and that was the end of it. The power thought the eel turned a little differently afterward, easier, but it may have been imagining things. Even powers do that.

**The rooms.** It went into every sealed room. It did not open them. It moved through the stone the way the water does, which was only good manners here.

Most held what you'd expect: a table, a bowl, a rush mat gone to stone, the ordinary furniture of a life, sheathed. In one there were two people sitting at a table, as the stories said, and the stories had been right. In one there was a reed flute that had been left on a sill, and the power could tell from the wear on its holes which tune it had been played in, and it was a lullaby still sung in the east courts in the same key. The oldest room of all, at the very core of the spur, held nothing human. It was only a pool, from before the first builders, the place where the first spring had come up and made the first stone, sealed now into a pale bubble at the heart of everything.

The power did not tell anyone. But it knows now what is in every closed room in Weepstone. Some families' stories are true and some are not, and no one living needs to find out which. It is enough that someone knows. Things are not lost only because they are hidden. They are lost when nobody holds them. The power holds them.

**The water turned by grudge.** Not every warden is Ysse. In the North Court there was a warden, an old man, who had nursed a quarrel with one family for twenty-two years over a marriage that went the wrong way. He had been turning their water ever since, a little at a time, always with a reason. Their walls had not grown in a decade. Their youngest son slept in a room so narrow he could not turn over in it.

The power did not punish the warden. It did not speak to him. It waited for a storm, because in Weepstone everyone knows that after a hard storm the flow shifts and water goes somewhere new. When the storm came, the water went to the wronged family's walls, and stayed there. The warden spent a month trying to turn it back and could not find the reason it would not turn. Eventually he stopped trying. It is possible he understood something. Wardens often do.

**The woman in the east rooms.** Not everyone who goes quiet chooses. The power found one who had not quite. She was old and her knees were bad, and her family was large and poor. She had decided, the way people sometimes decide, that it would be easier for everyone if she went. They had settled her under her birth drip. She had been saying the right things. But the power listened to her at night, when she thought no one could hear, and she was not ready. She was frightened, and she wanted to see the next spring's elvers netted, and she wanted to go on complaining about the stairs.

So her drip stopped. It ran dry overnight, a thing that happens. In the morning her family found her sitting under dry stone.

In Weepstone that is a sign, and everyone knows how to read it. The water refuses her. It isn't time. They carried her back up, grumbling and weeping, and she lived four more winters. She complained about the stairs every day of them, and she watched the elvers netted twice.

**The old man.** Three walls from the Low Court, an old man had been sitting under his birth drip for nine days, not answering, while his granddaughter hummed his note beside him. The power went and sat with them too.

He had chosen. The power could tell. It did not interfere with that. But the lime was in his joints, and his hands had been clenched for years against the ache, and there was fear in him, the ordinary kind, the kind everyone has at the very end regardless of how well they have arranged things. The power took the ache out of his hands and the fear out of the dark, not all at once, over a few nights, the way water turns.

His granddaughter noticed his hands open. They lay palm-up in his lap, the white lines of every cut he ever took showing in the lamplight, back to the first one. She did not know why. She took it as a good sign and hummed a little louder.

The power hummed with her, underneath, just a little, in the same key. It was not enough for her to hear as a voice, only enough that the room rang more fully than one girl's throat could make it ring. She thought it was the stone. It was, in a sense. She would tell her own grandchildren, years later, that on the last nights the walls had hummed with her. They would think she was being poetic. She wasn't.

He went quiet in the second week. It was easy. His hands were still open.

**The widow's doors.** The power watched a small boy running through the rooms of a widow who could not walk them all. In one door and out another he went, touching each jamb, shouting a greeting at every threshold, never stopping.

It did nothing about this. Nothing was needed. The boy was already doing what the power would have done.

This was the part of Weepstone it loved best, and it lingered there longer than anywhere else.

**Ysse.** One dawn it walked Ysse's seven channels with her. It did not help. The channels were clear. Ysse keeps them clear.

At the fourth turn she stopped with her white hand in the water, the way she stops a hundred times a morning, and tilted her head. The flow had something in it. It was not a clog, and not a thief's clay. Wardens know when the water is carrying more than it ought to.

She stayed crouched there for the space of a hundred drips. Then she said "Mm," the way you'd say it to a dog, or a difficult husband, and went on to the fifth turn.

She has never told anyone. The power has always been grateful to her for that.

## V. What it took

Before it left, the power did one thing for itself.

In the Low Court there is an arch so thin the daylight comes through it milky and pale. For three hundred years it had been dry. One morning in early spring it began to drip. A single slow fall of water from the arch's crown struck the edge of the cistern pool, where the old eel turns. Its note fell between two others, between a lover's note and a child's, and it belonged to no one anyone knew.

Ysse heard it on her dawn round. She stood under it and listened. Then she did what a warden does with a new drip in a court: she decided whether it should be cleared. She took a long time about it.

She let it run.

The children say the Low Court has a new citizen, and they hum its note back to it at evening, a little shyly, the way you'd greet a heron standing on the roof-ridge in the mist. The marriages agreed in that court are now said to the eel and then, by a custom that started nobody knows how, to the arch.

The power has gone on to the other things that need it, the droughts and the lost children and the grieving ice. But a drip is a small thing to leave behind, and it did not quite leave all of itself. Some part of it is still there, in the Low Court, falling at its own slow pace into the pool. It is listening to the humming. It is growing, very slightly, a fine crust of new stone on the arch where it falls.

It will not be given to any child. Its note is taken.

And when the springs fail, a long time from now, and the town goes quiet all at once, the last drip in Weepstone to stop will be that one. Someone should be there at the end, humming. The power has promised.

Until then, it is in no hurry. It has learned that here.
