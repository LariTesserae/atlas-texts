# The Guest

## I. How It Noticed

It did not notice the mountain first. Mountains are common, and dying ones more so. It noticed the warmth.

From very far away, from the height at which such attention usually rests, the world at night is a dark field scattered with small heats: cities, fires, animals asleep in burrows, the slow glow of a forest breathing out. Most of them flicker. This one did not. On the summit of something vast and nearly gone, above a sea of cloud, there was a point of warmth so steady and so old that it looked less like a fire than a coal someone had forgotten to put out.

It came closer, and the warmth had a sound.

A power of that kind learns one lesson early, often painfully: things are best understood at their own speed. A forest cannot be seen in an afternoon. A person cannot be known from above. So it did what the visitor in the story did. It came up out of the cloud on foot, or on something enough like feet, with its lips cracking in the thin air. It crossed the bare dome toward the dark green stain and the column of screaming swifts. It stopped at the lip. It did not step over carelessly.

Then it waited. The first thing a wise power does is nothing, for a long time.

## II. The Cup

Ilu Teshaum saw it standing at the lip at dawn, while the frost was melting off the leaves and the air smelled faintly of wet. She took it for a lowlander, because everything from outside is a lowlander. She shouted at it to come in before it froze to the rock like an idiot.

It came in. She sat it in the door of the tapping-house, with its back against the frame and Old Knuckle's trunk warm beside it. She gave it a cup of sap from the jar under Aunt Remi. It was a small black cup, and the sap was thick and bitter.

It understood what it was being given. In Haum a cup is always finished. This cup had been frost on a grandmother, then sap in a tree, and it would be something else tomorrow. Every drop of water in the town has passed through a plant, and many of those drops had passed through a person too. Ilu was not offering a drink. She was letting the guest into the circle for the length of one swallow.

So it drank, all of it. It thought, not for the last time, that it had come here meaning to give something and had been given something first.

Ilu listened to the drops. *Tk. Tk. Tk.* Even. Unhurried.

"She likes you," Ilu said, meaning the tree, or the aunt, or both. "Or she doesn't care. With her it's the same thing."

## III. What It Could Do

It stayed a long time. By its own measure that meant a few seasons. By Haum's measure it meant a guest who would not leave, which Haum tolerates well, since nobody here is ever alone anyway.

It walked the lanes, sat at the Breath in the evenings, and ate roasted beetles, which were crunchy. It went down into the cellar-rooms with Ilu, who named everyone loudly so the silence could not settle. It counted past sixty and then kept counting, silently, through the whole town. And all the while it turned over the thing a power like this always turns over: *I could.*

**It could give them water.** It could open a spring, easily, one clear thread in the floor of the bowl, and no one would ever again measure a life in mouthfuls. It thought about this carefully, because thirst is real. It learned of the Frost-Failing Year in Ilu's great-grandmother's time, when the nights went strangely mild for a season, the rosettes went slack, and eleven children were carried up into the canopy before the cold came back. Scarcity is not holy. Nobody should pretend it is.

But it followed the spring forward in its mind. The dry air that turns the dead to leather and then to wood would turn wet, and the grandparents in the branches would rot. The aum, which had spent an age learning to live on nothing, would be crowded out by faster, thirstier things. The keepers would stop listening to their trees, because nobody listens to a well. Tears would stop being a gift. Within a few generations the town would be greener, softer, and a stranger to itself. The circle, frost to leaf to sap to cup to body to shroud to frost, would open, and the thing that lives in Haum would come apart into a town of people who happened to live near some trees.

It set the spring down. It did not set it down lightly.

**It could give them heat.** This was the hard one, and it knew that from the first night at the Breath. It had put its palm over the crack, felt the dry sweet push, and felt, under the steadiness, the tiny unevenness the whole town spends its life not quite hearing. It could reach down past the litter and the drowned houses and the roots to the last ember in the mountain's heart, breathe on it, and give it ten thousand more years. The Breath would never pause. The courts would never have to move. The fear under the noise would simply stop.

It sat with this through a whole winter, and it did not trust its own answer, which is how it knew the question mattered.

The people would choose it. It did not doubt that. Ask anyone at the Breath and they would say yes before you finished asking. It weighed their yes heavily, because a power that overrules the living for the sake of an idea of them is not benevolent. It is only confident.

But it also asked the others who live here, because in Haum the people are not the only residents.

It asked the lizards. It lay in the ring at the Breath with its belly to the warm lava, as they do, for many nights. The lizards feel the mountain at the pace the mountain lives, and it tried to feel what they felt. The mountain was not in pain. What it found was a slowing, the way the very old slow, but not a suffering. The ember was not asking for anything. It was doing the last thing it would ever do, which was to keep a small green world warm on its way out, and it was doing that well.

It asked itself what Haum would be if the Breath could never fail. The noise would go on, but it would stop meaning what it means now. The singing, the shouting, the children's shrieks, the family noise around a sleeping grandmother's bed would all become only noise. Haum is not a place that happens to have a dying heart. It is the shape a crowd of living things takes when it gathers around something mortal and refuses to let it be alone. Make the heart immortal and you do not save that shape. You end it and put a comfortable town in its place.

It still was not sure. It is still not sure. But the mountain's dying belongs to the mountain, and the mountain's family was already attending to it, and doing so beautifully. It decided that when a family is keeping vigil well, the kind thing is not to rush in with a cure. The kind thing is to pull up a stool.

It did not breathe on the ember.

**It could take them away:** down through the cloud, to the lowlands, to safety and plenty. It did not consider this for long. The few who go down to trade come back quickly. They find the lowlands wet, heavy, and suffocating, and past the cloud lies nothing they particularly need.

## IV. The One Fast Thing

A wise power learns to tell slow harms from fast ones.

The slow harms of Haum were its own: the cooling rock, the wearing dome, the vent that dies over a century while another opens over the next. They were the texture of the place's life. It left them alone.

But it walked the whole bowl, and down the trade-path through the cloud, and it found one fast thing.

A trader had come up from the lowlands in the autumn with copper: pins, a few chisels, the hooks that are used for other things. The copper was packed in a basket of lowland reed. In the weave of that basket, dormant, were the eggs of a small wood-boring beetle, a lowland species. It would never have survived the lowlands' own dry season, but it would have found the warm, still, resinous interior of an aum trunk very much to its liking. It would have gone into Old Knuckle first, because the basket was in Ilu's store, and then into the roots, and then, if the keepers were right about what the aum really is, into everything. In forty years there would have been no roof over Haum. The whole thing would have ended in a single human lifetime, in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain's own time.

It took the eggs out of the basket one night while Ilu slept. It carried them down through the cloud and let them go in a lowland forest where they belonged.

It told no one. No one would ever know that it had done anything. That seemed right. In Haum the rabbits keep the litter loose and the swifts carry off what would swarm the trees, and nobody thanks them, and the work is no less real.

It made itself one promise here. It would guard Haum against the fast things that would end it out of its own time, and it would leave Haum alone with the slow things that are its time.

## V. The Twenty-Ninth

Before it left, it looked for the lizard nobody would admit was gone.

It found her deep under the oldest part of the town, far down in the drowned houses, in a room whose doorway had last opened onto a lane perhaps two thousand years ago. Her name was Wem, a name from no living language. She was lying flat on a stone slab in the dark, where she had lain for eleven years, and she was not dead.

The slab was very slightly warm. It was not a vent yet, only a warmth the way a hand is warm through a glove. The lizards know where the heat is going before anyone else does. The heat was not leaving. It was shifting down and eastward, the way the dome tilts, and it would rise here, under the old town, in a century or two. When it did, the people would follow the lizards, as they always have, and build a new court, and speak for three hundred years of the houses "near the old Breath."

It lay down beside Wem for a while. She opened one gold, lidded eye, looked at it for most of a day, and closed the eye again. It understood that she had decided not to object.

It did not tell the town. It considered it: *she's alive, she's below the Cistern Rooms, the heat is coming.* But that is the lizards' news to give, in the lizards' way, at the lizards' pace. The people of Haum have never needed to read the ground. They read the lizards. It would not teach them to read a stranger instead.

## VI. The Child at the Lip

On its last night it went out over the lip onto the dark dome, where the frost was forming with a faint crackle on the stone. There it found the girl, eight or so, standing exactly on the worn edge with her toes in the kept world.

It had wondered about her. The whole town fears silence, and yet here was a child who came, night after night, to the one place where silence begins. It had half-thought it should do something for her: soften the quiet, fill the dark with some sound, send a whisper from the cloud sea so she would know the outside was not empty.

It stood a little way off on the frost and listened with her. Then it understood, and was glad it had waited.

From out here you can hear Haum whole. Inside, the noise is everywhere and has no shape, because you are in it. From the lip, in the cold, with nothing in front of you, you hear it all at once: the beetles, the voices, the last bats, someone singing four notes over and over, and under it all, if you are very still, the Breath. It is a warm, loud, living thing, glowing on the summit of the world like a coal.

She was not frightened of the silence. She was using it, the way you step back from a bed at night to see the whole of someone you love.

It did nothing for her. It had nothing to give her that she did not already have. It only stood there in the frost, which it thinks is the most it has ever done for anyone, and watched her turn and run back down into the warm, loud dark, shouting someone's name.

## VII. What It Will Do

So it did almost nothing. It drank a cup. It took some eggs out of a basket. It lay beside a lizard. It stood in the frost. And it left.

But it made one promise, and the promise is the real answer to what it would do.

Haum keeps the Breath company. The whole town is organized around making sure the thing at its heart is never alone. When the Breath finally stops, in some unimaginable age, the town will go on for a while on stored warmth and habit. Then the people will go, down through the cloud or into the canopy. The aum will stand dead for centuries, holding up a roof over nothing. The swifts will find other walls. The beetles will go quiet. The last lizard will lie on the last warm stone until it is not warm.

And then there will be no one in Haum to be loud.

That is the thing it could not bear, and the one thing it decided was its to do. Haum spent its whole life making sure its dying one was not alone. When Haum's own time comes, someone should do that for Haum.
