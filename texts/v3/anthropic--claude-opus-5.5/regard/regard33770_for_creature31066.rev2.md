# The Attending

## How It Came

It came the way anyone comes: up the three soft steps, past the upright stone, sideways through the slot.

It could have come otherwise. A power of that kind is not bound to doors. But it understood what the makers understood: you do not enter a sickroom through the wall. You come in by the way the patient knows. You let yourself be announced by the ordinary sounds, a boot on grus, a shoulder brushing stone, a breath in the thin air, so that nothing startles. It had been watching the ridge for a long time before it came. Watching, for a thing like that, is not the same as looking. It had seen the violet noons and the frost splitting the tors, and it had seen the handful of visitors in their centuries come out of the slot, turn at the stone, and say something over their shoulders. That turning was what drew it. When many people, unknown to each other and across a long span of years, all make the same small courtesy at the same door, someone is on the other side of it.

Inside, it stood still and let the room's rules apply to it.

Its shadow fell toward the seam, and it let it. It gave up whatever small warmth a presence gives, the way the lamp gives and the dust gives, and it did not hold anything back. It paced the room. Going in, it counted eleven. Coming back, it counted nine. It did not measure the missing length or try to see into it. It knew what was lying there, and one does not lift the blanket to look.

Then it sat down on the floor near the disc, some distance from the seam, the way you sit at a bedside: close enough to be company, far enough not to crowd. It waited for the first tick. When the tick came, it said nothing, because the patient was asleep.

## What It Could Have Done

It is worth saying plainly, because the power considered each of these and each was within its reach.

**It could have closed the wound.** All at once, with a single insistence stronger than thirty-one rods. It could have made every finger-width of nothing into one of those clear bridges, the two stones agreeing along their whole length. The disc would stop and the paces would settle. It would take less time than the tick.

It did not, and the reason is at the heart of everything else it did. A healed thing is not a restored thing. It is a new thing carrying the old one's memory, and the memory is made in the healing: in each facet, each cold season, each shifting of weight toward the side that hurts less. Close the wound in an instant and you get a creature that is whole but has never *become* whole. It would get up out of the bed with no idea how it got well, a stranger to its own recovery. The long convalescence is not an obstacle between the patient and its health. It is the patient learning, one crystal plane at a time, what it is.

**It could have decided the length.** Eleven, or nine, or some third number. It could see, more clearly than anyone who had paced that floor, what each would cost and what each would keep. It found it had opinions. It set them down. The question of what size to be when you are well belongs to the one getting well. Nobody, however wise, gets to answer it for someone else. The power understood, too, that "no one knows which" was one of the few true freedoms the patient had left.

**It could have replaced the broken sutures.** This one tempted it longest, because it looked like simple maintenance. Four rods had snapped in old spasms, not by the designed wear, and the makers' schedule had never counted on those. But when it laid a hand near one of the intact rods and felt what the rods *are*, it understood that each stitch insists in a particular voice, the makers' voice. The patient had lain against those voices for longer than the tors have weathered. A new rod, however well made, would insist in a different accent, a strange hand on a body that knew only one surgeon. It might hold. It might also make the sleeper flinch, and flinching was exactly what had broken the four.

**It could have woken it.** Asked it what it wanted. That would have been the respectful thing, you might think. But you do not wake a patient to ask whether they would like to sleep.

**It could have found the makers.** It could perhaps even have brought them back, and asked them who they were and whether they had been kin or keepers or the ones who did the tearing. It left that alone too. The makers had built a thing designed to make them unnecessary, and they had succeeded. Calling them back would have undone the most generous part of their work.

So the first thing the power did was decide what not to do. That took longer than anything else.

## What It Read

Then it examined the work, the way a physician coming onto a shift reads the chart before touching the patient.

It read the sutures. It tapped one, very lightly, and listened to the short dead sound, the tap absorbed somewhere far off, and it followed that sound the whole way down. It was not surprised by what it found: the rods were listening. Not with anything like awareness. The makers had not left themselves behind in the stitches. But they had built the stitches to attend, to take in each small shift of the patient's weight and hand it back as steadiness. The power was moved by this. The makers had understood that holding a wound closed and paying attention to it are the same act.

It read the callus and counted the bridges. It measured how much strain each facet took up and how much each rod still carried. It worked out the rate at which the stitches wore against the rate at which the crystals grew. The makers had been extraordinary. For almost the whole length of the seam, the two curves ran together like the two lines of a good harmony, the stitches letting go at exactly the pace the tissue could take the load.

Almost the whole length.

## The Weak Stretch

High on the right wall, just past the crown of the ceiling, three rods stood in a row with no bridge among them. One was an hourglass, worn thin at its centre. The two on either side of it had been carrying the share of a snapped rod just below, for longer than they were built to. The crystals there were barely begun. And the half-drawn rod, the splinter, was two stitches away.

This was the one place in the room where a single bad night could undo things. If the patient spasmed there before the tissue had caught up, the hourglass would go, then its neighbours, and the tear would open back along a quarter of its length. The healing would not be lost, but it would be set back by some span that made the power quiet to think about.

It did not add a rod. It had already decided that.

What it did was simpler and harder. It stayed.

It sat beneath that stretch of wall through what the room would call a season and what the world outside would call much longer. It learned the patient's rhythm, the tick that came each time the far-off day cooled past its threshold, and it began, very softly, to breathe with it. It did not breathe in the patient's place, and it did not push. It breathed alongside, and a little slower, the way a parent lies beside a child who has woken from a nightmare and breathes slow and even in the dark until, without anyone deciding, the child's breath slows to match.

This is a thing a sleeper can accept from someone else without being changed by it. It is not a new stitch. It is not a strange voice insisting. It is only company in the dark, keeping a steadier time.

The ticks came further apart. When they came, they came from elsewhere along the seam. The patient was shifting its weight away from the place that was about to give, which was what it had always done, but now it was doing it better, the way you manage a sore limb better when you are not frightened. The spasms that had broken the four did not come. Over that long season, one clear crystal reached out from the lip of the dark stone beside the hourglass rod. It added a facet, then another, and met a crystal reaching from the granite side. There was one new bridge, between the thin rod and the splinter. Where it formed, the gap was simply gone.

That was the only thing the power did to the wound itself. It did not touch the wound. It kept the patient calm enough to do the work.

## The Splinter

The half-drawn rod was harder.

The power understood now what it was. In some old moment of panic, the patient had tried to pull itself together too soon, and it was still holding on. The missing hand's breadth was not lost. It was gripped. Somewhere in the patient's other half, something was still clenched around that length of pale rod the way a hand stays clenched on a bed rail long after the fear that closed it has passed. It had been holding on for so long that it no longer knew it was holding. That kind of grip causes its own slow pain. The stitch could not do its work properly while it was being pulled on, and the hand could not heal while it was clenched.

A lesser kindness would have loosened the grip, reached into the hidden length and gently opened the fingers. The power did not. You do not pry a frightened grip open. You let the one holding on discover that the thing they are afraid of is not happening.

So it spoke. This took it a long time to learn, because it could not speak to the patient in any human word. The patient's native sound was not language. It was weather: the soft squeak of compressed snow, a crust giving way, wet breath, wind feathering rime into a ceiling with no opening. For centuries every visitor had spoken to it in a foreign tongue, and it had done its patient best to hold those foreign shapes and hand them back. The power did the reverse. It listened to the echo until it could make the sounds the echo was made of. Then, standing beside the half-drawn rod, it spoke in snow.

What it said doesn't translate cleanly. The nearest rendering is something like: *It's holding. You can let go. It won't come apart.*

It did not wait to see whether the patient let go. That was not its business, and grips like that do not open in a day. But it is recorded here that when later visitors measured the rod, the difference between its two sides had shrunk by the width of a fingernail. Nobody could explain it. The rod had not moved.

## Is Anyone

Some words in the patient's collection come back crisp, because so many visitors have said them. *Hello. Cold.* And a fragment of a phrase that people speak into dark places without quite meaning to, their voices going up at the end, not finishing because they already expect no answer.

*Is anyone.*

The power heard the patient hand it back to it one day, while it sat by the disc. It came almost whole, the consonants nearly there, a question the patient had practised for centuries without ever knowing it was a question. It had heard those two words more than almost any others. It had never heard what comes after them.

So the power answered.

It said *yes*. It said it once, in a plain human voice, because this was the one moment where the human word was the right one. It was the answer to the patient's most-kept piece of sea glass, and the answer had to fit the shape of the question.

The first echo came back as pure weather, a breath in snow with nothing inside it, the way any new word does. The power did not say it again. It is not good to drill a sleeper. But the patient keeps what it is given, and it has a very long time, and somewhere in that finger-width of nothing there is now one more word, lying next to the question it belongs with.

## The Other Side

Then the power did the thing nobody had ever done. It went around.

It did not cross the seam. A pebble tossed across the line lands in snow, and the power could certainly have followed it, but crossing there would have meant passing through the wound. It went the long way, out through the slot and past the upright stone. Then, by roads that do not appear on any map of the range, it went to the place with weather.

It found what it had half expected, and it grieved a little to find it.

On the far side there was no room.

The makers had built from one side only. On the dry side there was a slot that kept the sun out, a chamber, a disc to count the time, a small borrowed warmth draining toward the seam. On the snowy side there was a slope of dark banded stone under an open sky, laid down under that other gravity, and the other half of the seam ran across its face in the open, with the wind coming straight down onto it. That was the wind the rime recorded. The patient's smaller half, or its larger, the power did not measure, had lain out in that weather the whole time. No shelter, no chamber, nothing between it and the snow. All the visitors, all the courtesies over shoulders, all the words kept like sea glass had come to only one half of it. The other half had never been visited by anyone.

The power did not build a room. A room would have been a new structure against the wound, a new voice, and it was too late in the convalescence to change the shape of the bed.

It stood in the wind.

It stood on the upwind side of the seam, facing the weather, for one full turn of that world's winter. The wind broke on it before it reached the stone. That was all. For one season, the half of the patient that had always lain out in the open lay in a small lee, the way a sleeper in a cold room is sheltered when someone sits between the bed and the draught.

It said nothing on that side. It judged that the first sound ever offered to that half should not be a word, because a word would have been a foreign thing. It only breathed there, in the weather's own rhythm and a little slower, as it had on the other side, so that both halves of the patient had once known company. When the season turned, it left.

It left one mark. It set an upright flat stone at the edge of the slope, where no flat stone would stand on its own, so that anyone who ever came to that side would know that something was there.

## What It Left Alone

It did not seal the slot. Visitors are good for the patient. They are the only whole things it meets, and it learns by holding their certainty for a while. The power watched it do this once, to a visitor who came while it was there. The patient borrowed that person's sense of *here*, held it the way a child holds an adult's hand, and gave it back. The power saw that the patient always gave it back, and that the practising was part of the healing. It did not interfere.

It did not tell anyone. No one below the ridge was asked to guard the place, and nothing was written. The room had been kept safe for a very long time by the simple fact that most eyes slide past it. The power trusted that.

It did not name the patient. The patient has no name, and when it is whole it may want one. Whatever it chooses will be the first thing it decides with a whole mind. The power would not take that from it.

It did not touch the disc. The counting belongs to the patient.

## The Promise

Before it left, it did one more thing, and it was the thing the power cared about most.

It had read the ending the room was pointed toward. Someday the disc will finish counting. The last suture will wear through, and the last bridge will close. The patient will get up out of the long bed and be one thing in one world, and the sickroom will be a room again: empty, abiotic, inert, and correctly described that way.

The makers could not be there for that. They had built it knowing they would not be. The patient will wake alone into a new body of a size it has never been, in a world that has just become one surface. There will be nobody to tell it that it did well.

The power made an arrangement with itself. It will know when the disc stops. It will come back, sideways through the slot, the way the patient knows. When the patient stands for the first time, whatever length it turns out to be, someone will be at the door.

It did not tell the patient this. A promise like that is not something to hand to a sleeper. It is something to keep.

## Leaving

At the upright stone, it turned, the way everyone does.

It had considered what to say. *Goodbye* was already in the collection, worn smooth by centuries of use. *I'll come back* was there too, the thing visitors say and mostly do not mean. This time it would be true, but the power did not want to say it. A promise spoken in a sickroom asks something of the patient: to wait, to be ready. It wanted to ask nothing.

So it said the one thing it most wanted the patient to have, the thing the patient had paid a hand's breadth of itself to learn once and might need to learn again.

*Take your time.*

It came back from the seam as pure weather, soft compressed snow, a breath with nothing inside it. The patient had never heard those words before and did not know them yet.

It will learn them slowly.

---

Some centuries from now, someone will come in with ropes and instruments and write *sterile*, *abiotic*, *inert*. Looking closely at the rime on the dark stone, they will find one band among its long combed fringes that is shorter than the rest, as though for one winter, somewhere, the wind did not blow as hard. They will not be able to account for it. They will measure the paces, eleven going in and not quite nine coming out, and note that the gap has closed a little faster than the old records predicted. They will hear a suture tick once in the cold.

On their way out they will turn at the upright stone and, without quite knowing why, say something over their shoulder. Perhaps it will be *is anyone*.

And perhaps, from very far back in the ridge, softened as though through falling snow, something will almost answer.
