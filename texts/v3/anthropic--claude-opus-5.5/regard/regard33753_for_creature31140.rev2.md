# The Guest

## How It Noticed

It was not the roar that drew it. Powers of that kind hear roars all the time: oceans, stars collapsing, the long hiss of the universe cooling. A roar is only the sound of a large thing being itself.

What it noticed was the quiet.

Across a continent of unbroken noise there were a few hundred small places where the noise was very slightly less, each a day's walk from the next, scattered like lamps left burning in a house where everyone has gone to bed. They were very small against something very large. That is the shape that draws benevolent attention, the way a cry draws a parent from another room.

So it came, and it believed it had come to help.

## What It Could Have Done

It hung above the mist for a while, which for such a being might be a moment or a century, and it looked at everything it was capable of doing. There was a great deal.

**It could have stilled the roar.** One breath would have settled the water, stopped the stones, and let the plain fall silent for the first time since there were directions to name. It listened longer before deciding, and it heard what the Hands already knew. The roar had not taken their language from them. The roar was why they had a language at all, one made of pressure and warmth and palms held too long, a language in which *grief* and *love* are allowed to remain one word. It also heard the voices in the roar, the bird calls and the rain on leaves, everything ever said here being worn into belonging. To silence the Mill would have been to burn an archive because it was loud. It let the roar go on.

**It could have mended the holes.** It drifted over one of the dark drifts where the world had worn through. It reached toward a hole, found no surface, and felt the small silent notch where the roar did not go. It understood that these were the only places on the entire plain that were truly finished, and that it had no standing to unfinish them. Mending a hole here would be like sewing shut a mouth that had just finished speaking.

**It could have answered the question.** This temptation was quieter and therefore more dangerous. It could have looked into the center. It could have *known*, the way powers can, whether something lived there, or whether the grey spheres drifting downwind were only stones worn too smooth to hold. It could have pressed that certainty into every palm on the plain.

It did not look. The Hands had built their whole lives on an answer that went both ways. Some feel pressure come back from the cairns; some feel only stone; both are considered true. That was not confusion. It was a form of tenderness. And the power had begun to understand the one law of this place that mattered most: an edge must be broken by your own hand. Certainty handed down from above would be an edge nobody had struck. It would cut them, and it would mean nothing.

**It could have helped the old one.** This was the hardest.

It found her on the open plain, a day and a half from the nearest cairn, kneeling in the milky water with three stones. One was her edge-stone, worn round. One was dark and banded. One was small and pale, and someone had pressed it into her hand forty years ago with a meaning only the two of them knew. She had been working for eleven days. Her fingertips had no whorls left. She turned the top stone a hair's breadth and waited, the plain shrugged, the stone slid, and she caught it and began again.

The power could have steadied it without moving: a thought placed under the stone, a fraction of friction lent where the Mill had none to spare. She would have felt it hold, and she would have been finished.

It watched the stone fall four more times before it understood why it must not. A cairn is not a stack of stones. A cairn is a person's stillness, the last of their attention, holding. If the power steadied it, the quiet that formed there would be the power's quiet, not hers. Travelers would one day step into its lee and feel the roar ease, and they would be standing inside the wrong attention. Her whole life would end in a borrowed sentence.

It also thought about the grooves in the great stones, worn by those who spent a lifetime trying and never managed it, which the Hands call the holiest places on the Mill. The power understood that if she never finished, she would not have failed. She would have become one of the holy places instead.

So it withdrew to a day's distance, because the Hands build alone and even its watching was a kind of weight. On the fourteenth day, far off, it felt the roar drop by the smallest fraction. It grieved, and it was glad, and it did not try to tell the two apart.

**It could have spoken.** Its voice could have carried over the Mill. It could have said *I am here, I mean you well*, and every Hand on the plain would have heard it, which no one here had ever heard of anything.

That was exactly why it did not. A guest learns the language of the house.

## What It Did Instead

It came down as small as it could make itself, which was not very small at first. Making itself less was the first thing the Mill taught it.

It stood in the water. The warm lean pressed against a pair of shins it had made for the purpose. Stones shrugged under soles it had made too. Within an hour the mist had filmed it pale, and it looked like everything else, like mist with a shape in it.

It stood still, because it did not know what else to do, and on the Mill stillness is what stands out.

Three Hands found it on the second day. One of them was a child whose fingerprints were still sharp. She came close, took its hand, and pressed her thumb lightly into the center of its palm. That was a question, though the power did not yet know it. It did not know how to answer. It had never been asked anything it could not answer.

They taught it slowly. It learned badly. It had come without fingerprints, since it had never needed any, so it made itself a set in the plainest pattern it could, knowing they would wear. It pressed too hard and said things it didn't mean. For a long while it could say only a few things, and it said them all with the same weight, the way a very young child does. The Hands found this endearing. They are patient with things that have not yet learned where they stop.

It ate the green-black mat and learned the taste of iron and wet chalk. It slept sitting up with its back against a cairn, and at some point in the night someone's hand came to rest on its wrist. It woke and did not move, so that the hand would stay.

## The Stone

Eventually the old ones noticed that it carried nothing against its chest.

They took it to the lee of the oldest cairn they could reach. They put a rounded cobble in one of its hands and a heavier stone in the other, and they waited, palms on its shoulders, the way they wait for their children.

The power had unmade larger things than this, and not always carefully. It had never before held something small and understood that breaking it would matter.

It brought the stone down, and the cobble cracked.

It pressed its thumb to the ridge, as the children do, and it let itself bleed. It could have chosen not to. It chose to because the Hands were watching, and because it wanted to know. The Hands pressed their thumbs into its palm, *here, here, here*: this is what you are, this is what the Mill has not yet taken.

In that moment it understood why it had come.

It had come to give, and it had been given the one thing it had never had, which was a place where it stopped. Powers of its kind are round in their way. They are continuous and warm and full of everything they have ever witnessed. They have no beginning that they remember and no end they expect. They think of themselves as finished, and the Hands would find that a little funny.

Sitting there in the lee of the oldest cairn, holding a broken stone that was already beginning, too slowly to feel, to round, the power realized that it resembled the one at the center more than it resembled anyone else here. That was why the Hands had treated it the way they did. Perhaps they had known before it did.

## The Palm

One night, some weeks later, it was sitting alone on the open plain with its edge-stone against its chest. It felt a hand on its shoulder: a palm laid flat, no pressure at all, only warmth, exactly its own temperature.

For a power, that is an unusual thing to feel. It had never considered that it had a temperature of its own.

It did not turn around. It knew it would find only mist, a few grey spheres drifting at knee height, and the milky water leaning. It knew what the Hands say this means: the one who lives here has noticed you, knows you are not finished yet, and is glad.

It had forgotten that it was not finished. Benevolent things do forget that. They are so busy tending the unfinished that they come to believe they are the finished thing doing the tending.

It sat and let itself be noticed for the rest of the night.

## Whether to Stay

It thought about staying until its edge-stone went smooth. It thought about choosing three stones, kneeling in the full roar, and turning them a hair's breadth at a time until it too was a small quiet the Mill could not undo.

In the end it decided against this, and the reasons were not its own. Its stillness would be too large. Its cairn would not ease the roar by a fraction. It would silence a province. The reeds would grow into a forest, the water would bend for a hundred leagues, and the Mill would have to route its long patience around one more thing that refused, the way it routes around the Slow One. A guest does not die in a way that rearranges the house.

There was a second reason. Nothing had ever left the Mill with an edge. Everything that came here was worn and welcomed and carried inward. The power thought it might be good for one broken stone to go somewhere else and round slowly under a sky where the sun has a disc. It would carry a little of the Mill's unfinishedness out into a world that had forgotten it was unfinished too.

## Leaving

Before it went, it walked for many days toward the interior. The finished stones thickened around it like slow grey snow, and the roar lost its textures one by one. It stopped well short of the center, at a place where the water had only begun to circle. It did not go in. Some rooms are not for guests.

There it knelt and laid its palm flat on the surface of the water, with no pressure at all, only warmth.

It was the first time anyone had given that gesture back.

Then it walked out to the doorframe, the one the Hands disagree about, the one some say was made for what lives at the center. The power did not settle that argument either. It only passed through. The water ran slightly differently on the other side, and the roar there sounded a fraction older. Its visit now had a before and an after, which it had never had with anything.

## After

The Hands do not speak of it often, since nothing said aloud survives here and they say very little by touch that does not need saying.

They did make a new gesture, though. It is the thumb pressed hard into the center of another's palm, which means *here, and not yet gone*, held for a moment, and then lifted.

It is the only gesture in their language that ends on purpose.

It translates poorly. The nearest words are *guest*, or *the one who came to help and was helped*, or *the one who remembered where it stops*. The Hands use it rarely: for travelers who stay long enough to soften and then go home, and sometimes for a child on the day her stone is first broken. The old ones say they use it also for a feeling that comes on the open plain now and then, a palm laid flat on the shoulder, no pressure at all, only warmth.

They are not certain it is only the one who lives at the center anymore.

Both are considered true.
