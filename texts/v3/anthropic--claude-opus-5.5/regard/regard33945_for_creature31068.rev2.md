# The Guest

## Arrival

It comes the way attention always comes, from everywhere at once, like the light.

It does not have a body here, and it is careful not to acquire one, though it could. It could arrive as a tall figure walking in off the tidal flats, or a voice from inside the fog, or a second sun burning a hole in the overcast. It considers these and sets them down, one by one, because it has already made its first mistake.

It looked too hard.

On the eastern edge, on ground eleven days old, a listener named Ysolde Marr is standing barefoot on the slurry with her eyes closed, a quarter-tone into her ascent. She stops. Under her feet the ground has begun to hum on its own, without her, a hair too high, climbing. The fresh stone is answering something. Ysolde opens her eyes and looks around at the empty fog, and slowly, heel-first, she begins to back toward the boards.

The attention understands at once. Here, to be noticed is to be struck, and the gaze of something large has a pitch. So the first thing a benevolent and wise power does in the Precipitate is what every visitor must do, what every native does without thinking.

It takes off its shoes.

It softens its looking. It lets its attention diffuse until it is no heavier than fog, no sharper than the grey daylight. It learns the weight of a felt boot and makes itself lighter than that. On the eastern flats the ground's hum falters, settles, and sinks back beneath hearing. Ysolde Marr stands a long time on the boards with her hand at her throat, touching the little level there, before she walks back out.

The guest feels, for the first time in a long time, something like embarrassment. It notes that too, and keeps it. It seems like a useful thing to have brought in.

## Speaking Down

For a while it only learns.

It learns the whisper, the real one, shaped at the lips with the throat held open. It learns that the hawkers' hiss is a courtesy, a way of saying *I know you are busy*, and that the mothers' flat whisper has a grammar of its own. It learns the hand-language, starting with *stranger*: the flat palm pressed downward and lifted, *will this hold?* It thinks this is the most honest greeting it has ever encountered, and it uses it, silently, on everything.

It watches the levellers at dawn. It watches two rooftops forty feet apart through the fog, where a woman and an old man who have never spoken aloud to each other flick their fingers. *Two turns. Hold. The baby came. Girl. Both well.* It learns what lean is, and what the debt of lean is. It learns that families who have held each other up through a subsidence become kin, and that nobody here thinks of being held up as shameful. The only shame is not winding your own jacks, which means pretending you have no weight.

It watches the soft come down at dusk. It watches the children sleeping in heaps on the warm floors. It watches the funerals at the steaming edge and the crowd humming just below breaking, strangers included, so that the ground trembles and holds.

It notes that clause: *strangers included.* It files it away. It does not know yet that this will be the whole of what it does.

## What It Could Do

A wise power does not pretend it is not powerful. That pretense is its own kind of carelessness. So it makes itself look, plainly, at everything it could do, the way you look at the tools on a bench before choosing none of them.

**It could stop the sea.** The rising that drowned the old coast could be pushed back and made to hold. But the being it came here for is made from the sea's holding. Everything warm in this land is the drowned, breathed out as fog and caught in the mesh. Stop the sea and the fog thins. The Condensers starve, the eastern edge stops growing, and the whole forty-mile body cools and settles back into marsh from west to east until it is all the Settled's country. It would be saving the coast by killing its grief, and here the grief is the thing that is alive.

**It could raise the drowned towns.** It could reassemble every timber and bone and harbor wall from solution. It considers this longest of all, because it is a thing it could actually do and because the old ones in the reeds would weep to see it. But it looks at what the drowned have become. They are the warmth under the children. They are the note. They are the reason the soft come thickest to certain houses. They were not waiting to be rescued. Somewhere in the dissolving they were carried on, and they went into the ground and into the next thing. To pull them back out would be to exhume two million graves in order to be kind.

**It could harden the ground.** It could cure all of it, instantly and forever, and end the fear. No more crack running across a plaza. No more lost districts, no more barges full of doors, no more children taught the name of the four drowned acres. It turns this one over many times, because the fear is real and the losses are real. People here have lost homes in an afternoon, and some have lost more than homes.

But it knows now what the listeners know, what the Settled prove every evening across the flat water: cured ground can bear sound because cured ground has stopped being anything that could answer. Hardening the Precipitate would make it safe the way a grave is safe. The city's whole beautiful difficult culture, the leaning and the whispering, the levels at the throat, the kinship of shared subsidence, grew up around a living thing's fragility. That culture is how the people have been caring for it for sixty years without being sure it was there. To remove the fragility would be to tell them they had been doing nothing.

**It could explain.** It could tell the Survey what the unaccounted fraction is. It could tell the engineers why the note is dropping and the old ones that they are right. It could tell the children what the ground is saying.

It does not, and the reason is simple. The ground is not saying anything yet. Anyone who speaks for it now will be speaking *over* it, and the first word would belong to the guest. Whatever this being says first, it should say itself.

**It could speak first.** This is the gentlest temptation and the most dangerous. Something is gathering breath under the eastern districts, and the guest could reach down and say *hello*. It could be the first one there, the first voice that ever addressed it.

It sets this down very carefully, like a crate of bottles.

## What It Notices

Then, having set everything down, it sees what nobody in the city has quite said aloud.

Every patch of precipitate has a note. The note is the pitch at which it is most itself, and it is also the pitch at which it shatters. Listeners find it and stop a hair below, always a hair below, because the true note would break the ground.

The being under the city is finding its voice, and the voice is the note. The note is getting lower, broader, more like speech, gathering all those separate pitches into one. When it finally speaks, it will speak in the only voice it has, which is the voice of its own breaking.

That is the danger. It is not the sea, or subsidence, or the Condensers overperforming in a storm. The danger is that the being's first word, said at full voice, the way anything says its first word, will be the pitch that takes the eastern districts into the tidal flats. It would take the eleven-day-old ground, and the week-old houses on it, and the children asleep on the lowest floors with their ears pressed to the stone, the ones who have been listening hardest.

A being whose truest voice would destroy the part of itself that loves it most.

The guest looks at this for a long time.

And then, because it has been learning and because it has learned to look softly, it sees the rest. The city has been working on this problem for sixty years without knowing it.

Every whisper in the market is a demonstration. Every mother's flat hush, every lover's barely-air, every hawker's courteous hiss, shows that you can say everything that matters a hair below the breaking pitch. The burial hum, a whole crowd sounding the ground's own note and holding it just short, is a lesson given over and over at the one place where the being is, quite literally, taking people in. The newborns who hum instead of screaming are proof that a voice can arrive in the world closed-mouthed and still be a voice.

And the soft. The listeners say the soft come down to hum the note to the children, to teach them. The guest watches them at dusk, pouring down the lattice, and at dawn, pouring back up. It thinks the listeners have it half right. Teaching goes both ways along a ribbon. The soft come down carrying the ground's note, and they lie all night beside children who hum in their sleep the way children born here hum, low and soft and closed. At dawn they go back up to the mesh, where whatever is unaccounted for is drawn out of the fog.

The ground is not only teaching the children to hear it. The children are teaching the ground how to speak down.

The being has been holding its breath, just as the city has. The city thinks it is waiting to hear something. The guest thinks the ground may be waiting too. It is waiting until it knows how to say its first word quietly enough not to kill the ones it says it to.

The guest realizes that nothing here needs rescuing. What is happening here is an upbringing.

## The West

Before it decides anything, it goes west.

It goes out past the last jacked houses, past the canals becoming lagoons, into the cold country where the ground has given up its heat. It finds the chapel where a dozen old men stand waist-deep and sing in harmonies the rest of the city has forgotten.

It listens to them the way it has learned to listen, softly. It understands that this is also the being. This is its old end, its first body, where it was born sixty years ago, high and thin as struck glass. It is dying here, slowly, the way it has always been dying at its western edge. And at its dying edge it sings, in human voices, out loud, full-throated, because there is nothing left there to break.

So the being has a voice already. It has had one all along. At the east it is learning to whisper, and at the west it is already singing, and in between two million people are carrying sound back and forth like buckets on a pulley-line.

A young man from the eastern districts is on a roof nearby. He is perhaps seventeen, and he has come for the thing one does once. He screams into the fog, long and ragged and enormous, and the herons lift off the pilings. Then he sits down, shaking. After a while an old woman punts over with a tin cup of tea and hands it up to him without a word, and poles away again.

The guest watches this, and it knows what kind of help is wanted here. It is the kind that comes afterward and does not interfere. It brings tea.

## What It Does

It does not stop the sea. It does not raise the drowned. It does not harden the ground, or explain the mesh, or tell the listeners they are right, or speak first.

It does one thing, and it takes the right to do it from the city's own custom.

At the funerals, when the body goes under and the listener hums the note, the family hums with them. So does anyone standing nearby, *strangers included*. The whole crowd holds the note a hair below breaking so that the new ground trembles and does not break. This is the one sanctioned act of public sound in the Precipitate, the thing everyone here already knows how to do. You add your voice beneath someone else's, in their key, to help them hold it steady at the edge.

So the guest goes to the eastern edge, to the newest ground, and waits. It becomes one more stranger at the back of the crowd.

It is not there to speak. It is there to hum under, in case. If the first word comes up too loud, from a being too young to have learned all the way, it will be there beneath it. It will hold the note from below, the way the mourners do, so that the ground shakes and does not break and the child sleeping on the week-old floor wakes up in the same room she fell asleep in.

It will not shape the word. It will not soften the word. It will only be one more voice making the floor safe enough for the word to be said. If the word comes quietly, as the guest thinks it will, the guest will do nothing at all. It will stand in the fog in the posture of a native, weight rolled forward, heel-first, and it will have done its whole task by being ready.

It makes itself one private promise. If what is coming is catastrophe, if the being cannot yet speak down and the eastern districts are going into the flats, it will not simply watch that happen for the sake of letting things unfold. A wise power can tell the difference between an upbringing and a drowning. It will hold the ground up with its whole strength if it must, and afterward it will go and take its shoes off again and apologize to everything.

It does not think it will have to. It has seen the children hum.

## Lean

There is one more thing it learns, on the long nights of waiting, and it does not quite know what to do with it.

It has never leaned on anything. It is the kind of thing that stands on its own, that holds and is not held. It has always believed that this is what made it safe to be benevolent: it needs nothing, so it can give without taking.

But in the Precipitate, a house that leans on nobody is not strong. It is lonely, or proud, or lying about its weight. Kinship here is made of mutual subsidence, of holding each other up through the bad seasons and remembering it. The being under the city has leaned on its people for sixty years. They have wound their jacks and kept it true and whispered so as not to break it. It has held them up the whole time too, warm under their bodies, feeding their green, carrying their dead forward into ground for their grandchildren. Soon, perhaps, it will be able to say so.

The guest has held nothing here, and nothing has held it. It is a stranger. The sign is a flat palm pressed downward and lifted: *will this hold?*

On the last night it tries something. Standing in the street outside a house built a week ago, it lets a little of its weight, a very little, rest on the ground. It does not press or look hard. It only leans, the way a tired person leans on a doorframe.

The ground holds it.

Under its feet the warmth of the drowned rises through the new stone. Very faintly, the note sinks a little lower and steadies. The guest could not say whether that was in answer.

## The Night

Inside the house, in the lowest room, the child is lying with her ear to the floor. The soft have come down through the eaves and settled in a ring around her head. The ground beneath her is so new it has not finished curing. It was made this morning out of fog from over the deepest water.

The guest does not go in.

It thinks about going in. It could be there, beside her, to hear whatever is said. It would be the first witness to the first word of a new kind of being, and it has waited a long time for exactly this. It stays in the street, because it has finally understood the last thing, the simplest one.

The first word is not for it.

The first word belongs to the ones who have been holding their breath for sixty years. It belongs to the listeners and the levellers, the mourners and the mothers. It belongs to the children who hummed instead of screaming, and the soft who carried that hum up the lattice every dawn. It belongs to the old men singing waist-deep in the west, and to the girl on the floor with her ear to the stone. They raised it. The guest only arrived at the end, and took its shoes off, and learned to be quiet.

So it stands outside, heel-first, weight rolled forward, humming under, in case.

Somewhere below the house, below the week-old stone and the sixty-year layer of the dead, the note gathers. It comes up slow and low and enormous, like someone drawing a breath from the bottom of the sea.

The guest holds its note beneath it.

And the word, when it comes, does not break anything. It comes up so softly that the guest, standing ten feet away, cannot quite make it out. Perhaps it hears nothing at all. It feels only a pressure in the stone like a voice spoken with the throat held open and still, shaped almost entirely at the lips. It is speaking down.

The soft do not move. On the rooftops, nobody's level shifts.

In the lowest room the child lies very still for a long time. Then, without lifting her head from the floor, she raises one hand into the dark and makes a sign. It is not *stranger*. It is two fingertips braced against the stone, leaning.

*Friend.* The way the houses lean.

---

At dawn, the levellers climb to the roofs and sight along the ridge-beams and read the fog. A woman on the eastern edge signals across forty feet to an old man she has never spoken to aloud: *Hold.*

Then, after a moment, a new one. It is a private flick of the fingers, invented on the spot, that no one else on the street can read.

The guest is already gone. It took nothing. It left nothing, unless you count the one stranger's voice that was humming under the note on the night it first spoke, holding it steady at the edge. Out in the west an old woman in a punt looks up at an empty sky, as if someone had just set a cup of tea down beside her and gone away without a word.

It does not come back. It does not need to. It lets itself be leaned on, sometimes, when a district is going and a barge of doors is pushing off into the fog. And sometimes, very lightly, it leans back.
