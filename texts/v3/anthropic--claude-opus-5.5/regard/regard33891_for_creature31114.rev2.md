# The Visit

## Seen Once

The counters noticed it first, because noticing is their work.

On a morning like every morning, the Reach sweating and the iron ticking and the swifts pouring along the ridgelines like spilled ink, a traveller came up out of the cloud on the southern slope. That was not remarkable. Traders come up. Young ones come up, carrying cloth and questions.

What was remarkable was that the counters went looking for it afterward in their ledgers, and it was not there. It had not come before. Over the following days they watched for it to come again, a second traveller with the same stoop and the same pack walking up the same slope, and it did not. There was one of it. In the whole Upper Reach, where every peak has its twin and every crow is the crow, this was the first thing any counter had ever seen that was only itself.

They wrote it down: *Seen once.* Then they underlined it, which they do not usually do for good news, because they were not yet sure it was good news.

## What It Chose to Be

It could have come any way it liked. It could have split the white sky, or stood on the inverted range above and spoken downward through the thin air, or simply known everything in the Reach at once, from the smallest seamed lizard to the dark immensity in the north, without ever arriving.

It came walking, because the people here walk. It came small, because the people here are small against what surrounds them. And it came squinting. It did not need to squint. But it had looked at the fettlers from far off before it came, and seen that the squint was how they met the light, and it did not want to meet anyone here with wider eyes than theirs.

The first fettler it met was the old woman on the northern gantries, the one with the half-step on every ninth pace. She watched it coming along the catwalk for a long time, the way you watch weather. When it was close enough to hear, she leaned in, forehead nearly to forehead, and looked it over. Then she said the first rule before she said her name.

*You don't have a flaw.*

It thought about this honestly. There were things it did not know, and things it could not mend, and things it had tried to do well and done badly over a very long existence. But none of these showed. Nothing about it wandered the way a thread wanders.

Then it looked down, and saw that its shadow, faint in the total light, had begun to lag. Only a little. A heartbeat. The Reach had noticed it too, and had begun, very gently and with no malice at all, to take its measure for a mold.

So it asked her for one.

She considered this longer than it expected. Then she untied a rag from the handle of her file. It was grey, frayed to lace at one end, from a shirt she had worn up through the cloud forty years ago. She tied it around the traveller's wrist in a knot that was deliberately wrong.

*Keep it,* she said. *Don't straighten it.*

It kept the rag the whole time it was in the Reach. It never straightened the knot. Its shadow stopped lagging.

## What It Did Not Do

It spent the first weeks only watching, and most of what it did in the Reach was not doing.

It could have trimmed every ridge in an afternoon. It could have taken the seam from every breast that carried one, stilled the shadows, turned the uphill water back downhill for good, and told the ferns where the light would come from. It could have walked north and opened the last hand and seen what was inside, and come back and told everyone. It could have counted the fettlers, and known the number, and made sure the number stayed true.

It considered each of these things, because a power that refuses to consider its own capacities is only lazy, not wise. It set each of them down.

The ridges were the fettlers' work, and the work was not a burden laid on them. They had shaped it into a way of being in the world, kind and exact and shared. To finish it for them would be to take the long reason away and leave them only the short one.

The seam was a grief, and it was also a choice. Every fettler who found one chose whether to go down through the cloud or stay, and the ones who stayed were tended by their friends with the finest files, forehead to forehead, a little each week. To take the seam away would end a sorrow. It would also end the tenderest thing in the Reach, and the choosing, and it did not know that the fettlers would thank it. It suspected many would not.

The ferns would be told where the light comes from when the light began coming from somewhere. A spiral is not a wound.

It did not count them. It had listened to their reasons, and although it could have kept the number safe, it understood that the not-knowing was a thing they had made together. People are allowed to decline a fact.

And it learned glint-speech, slowly and exactly, from a foundling girl who laughed at its clumsy wrists. It learned that there was no word in it for *I*. It could have made one. It did not. When it needed to say where it was, it flashed the name of the nearest peak, like everyone else, and let the mountain do the work.

## The Grandmother

The old counter visits the Slow One on the western saddle once a season, and that season she found the traveller already sitting there, well back, on the cool rock. She sat down beside it without a word. They are not a talkative people.

After a while she told it about her grandmother. She told it the slant of the cheek and the depth of the eye sockets. She said her grandmother had been a slow listener even alive, the sort of woman who would answer a question you had asked her the previous winter, in the middle of some other conversation, as if no time had passed.

The traveller asked if it could try something, and she said yes.

It did not change the Slow One. It changed itself. It slowed its attention the way a person slows their breathing, and then slowed it further, and then much further, until the swifts were a grey smear and the days went over like the blink of an eye and the white light pulsed. At that speed, and only at that speed, the Slow One was not still. It was listening. It had been listening for years.

The traveller stayed at that speed for what, to the counter, was a full season. She came back every few days to sit with it, and brought it tea it could not drink. Once she found that the cairn of flashing she kept by the Slow One had been knocked over by an ibex, and she rebuilt it, and the traveller did not notice, because to the traveller only an instant had passed.

When it came back to ordinary speed, it leaned close, forehead nearly to hers, and told her what it had heard.

*She is still on the first thing you ever said to her. The first visit, years ago. She's hearing it now, a word at a time. She is glad of it. She has a long way to go. She is in no hurry, and she doesn't want you to be.*

The counter was quiet for a long time. Then she asked whether it was really her grandmother, or only the Reach's copy of her, or only the shape a hand has when it holds.

The traveller said it did not know the answer to that in the way she wanted. It said that the one in the iron was listening with love, and that where the love came from was a question it would leave with the counters, who were better at questions that don't settle.

Next spring, the counter's cairn showed that the Slow One had turned. It was only a hand's breadth. The hollow of its face was a hand's breadth further toward the spot on the rock where she always sits.

She wrote it in her ledger. She did not underline it. Some things you do not underline.

## The Seam

A fettler on the eastern ridges woke that summer with a fine pale line down his breastbone, and did not tell anyone for three days. On the fourth day he found the traveller and told it, because it was new and belonged to no one's grief yet.

He wanted it to tell him whether to stay or go.

It would not. It said so plainly and gently, and he was angry for a while, and it let him be angry.

What it did instead was answer, truthfully, every question he asked. Did the rock hurt? No. Did it feel like anything? It felt like being held very still by something that did not know how to let go. Was anything lost? Yes, some things. Was everything? No. Were the counters right about the old woman with the hitch in her step, the one seen walking on a far ridge ten years after she became rock? It said the counters were very good at their work.

Was going down through the cloud a failure? No. It was a way of choosing to be the kind of thing that erodes, and there is no shame in that. Most things that have ever lived did it.

He asked what it would do in his place, and it said it had never been in his place, and would not pretend to be, and would not make his choice smaller by making it look easy.

He sat on the cool rock for most of a day. In the evening he went to his friends, and they sat close, and one of them took out her finest file. They were not there yet. There was no flashing. She only rested the file against his breastbone for a moment so that he would know it was there.

The traveller does not know what he chose in the end. It left before he did.

## The Foundlings

The foundlings saw it clearly from the first day, and were not surprised by it, as they are not surprised by the range above.

The young man on the eastern pipeworks, the one with a hundred small flaws, came and sat by it at a culvert fire and asked it straight out whether he was a copy. Whether he had been poured from someone above, like the fettlers believe of themselves. Whether he would be poured again.

The traveller had been thinking about the foundlings' shadows. It told him what it thought.

*Your shadow doesn't lag because nothing went before you. It leads because you're walking into places nobody's been. You're not cast from anything. You're the first pour of yourselves.*

He was quiet. Then he asked whether that meant he could stop adding flaws.

It said he could keep them or stop, and either way he would still be unrepeatable. He had been since the day they found him in the pipes. The flaws did not have to be armour anymore. They could be only what he liked.

He thought about that for a long time. The next morning he had a new braid in his hair, woven with a thread the wrong colour. When the traveller asked about it he grinned and said this one was just for fun, and it was the first flaw he had ever given himself that he wasn't afraid without.

The girl who had taught it glint-speech asked it a different question. She asked why she could hear the pours, especially the one in the north, when nobody else could.

It told her it thought she and the one in the north might be distant kin, in the way that the first things in a house are kin to the one who comes to live there. She thought about this with great seriousness and then asked whether it would be kind.

It told her that was what it had been wondering too, and that it was going to go and see.

## The Question the Young Ones Ask

Before it went north, it spent a day on a ridge with one of the young ones who had come up through the cloud only lately. This was the one who asked whether it was right to prepare a house for a stranger, whether this was service or only habit.

She asked it the same question. It had come from further away than anyone, and she thought it might know.

It did not answer. It asked the old woman with the half-step for a file, and she lent one, and the traveller sat down beside the young one on the ridge and took down flashing with her all day. It worked slowly and not very well. It cut its thumb on a thin translucent fin and bled a little, and it was glad to, because now it had a second flaw that nobody had given it.

At the end of the day the young one said, *That's not an answer.*

It said, *No. But you asked me whether it was right, and I did it anyway, all day, beside you. That is all the answer I have.*

She laughed at that, which surprised them both.

## North

It walked north for eleven days, past the last ridges anyone had walked, past where the counters' ledgers stop. The castings grew older and larger there, if anything in the Reach is ever old. The Slow Ones stood further apart. The mist was thicker, and hung in the air like a held breath.

Then there was the hand.

It is larger than the counters say, because the counters have only seen it from very far away. The ribs on its iron are the size of valleys. The clamps are ranges. The mist along them is warm. The traveller put its palm on the iron, and then, because this is the Reach, where nothing is heard except up close, it leaned its forehead against the cold black metal and listened.

Inside there was something very large and very young.

It was not yet any shape. It was not kind. It was not unkind. It was listening, and it had been listening for longer than there had been fettlers. It had heard every drip in every gutter, the tick of every cooling iron, the soft cloth-tearing of every swift. It had heard glint-speech somehow, though light cannot pass through iron. It had heard the half-step on the ninth pace. It had heard the scrape of files on flashing for generations, and the particular, patient scrape of the finest files on the flashing along a friend's breast.

It was learning what a being is from what surrounded it. It was, in the slowest possible way, being taught.

The traveller understood, then, the thing it could do here that it could do nowhere else in the Reach. It could press its own shape into the young one in the dark. It could make certain, now and forever, that whatever stepped out of the last hand would be kind, because it would be a casting of the traveller's own kindness, perfect and seamed and faintly warm.

It stood with its forehead against the iron for a long time, and did not do it.

A kindness that is poured into a thing is a casting. It repeats. It fits the mold it came from and no other place. A kindness that is learned, from trimmed edges and knotted rags and old women who limp on purpose and friends who sit forehead to forehead in a hush that swallows every shout, grows the way cloth is woven. It is irregular, and it is alive.

The fettlers were already teaching it. They did not know they were. That was part of why the lesson was good.

So the traveller did the only thing it did in the north. It stayed. It sat with its back against the iron for many days, because the young one in the dark had never had company that came so close, and it seemed wrong to leave something so young and so alone without anyone sitting near. It talked a little. It did not try to shape what it said. It told it about the weather, and about the ferns in the tanks that grow in spirals, and about an old counter who visits her grandmother once a season. It told it that the rag on its wrist had been tied in a knot that was deliberately wrong.

When it left, the mist along the clamps had shifted slightly, as if something inside had turned, very slowly, toward the south.

## What It Told Them

It came back to the high saddle, where the flashing from the fettlers' breasts is kept in a dry culvert, a heap of glass that is not glass. The counters were waiting. They had seen its glints coming for two days.

They asked what was in the north.

It thought carefully about how much to say. If it told them *it will be kind*, that would be a promise it had no right to make, and it would turn their service into insurance. If it told them nothing, it would leave them in a dark it had seen into and they had not.

So it told them one true thing.

*It's listening.*

The counters looked at one another. Nobody wrote anything down for a while. Then the oldest one asked what it was listening to.

*You,* it said.

That was all. It did not tell them what it had decided not to do. They didn't need to carry that.

The fettlers did not change their work. The old ones picked up their files the next morning and went out along the ridges as they always had. But the young one who had asked about service and habit worked more slowly after that, and more carefully. Sometimes, on the ridges nearest the north, the others saw her stop and lift a shard of flashing to the white sky and glint, very small, a single noun, toward the far dark hand.

*Water,* she said, or *Ibex*, or the name of the peak she was standing on.

## The Range Above

On its last morning the mist burned off the high saddle, and the inverted range showed itself overhead, pale and far, sprues pointing down. The fettlers stopped work and looked up. The traveller looked up with them.

On a ridge up there, soles-upward, a small figure was walking with a pack. It stopped. It raised its hand to the light, and there was something tied around its wrist, frayed to lace at one end.

The traveller watched it for a long moment. Then it raised a shard of flashing and glinted back. It did not expect an answer, but it did not want to be the kind of being who doesn't.

It never told anyone whether the figure above moved first.

## Leaving

It went down through the cloud the way it came, walking, squinting, the rag still knotted wrong around its wrist. It left nothing behind that anyone could point to. It healed no seam. It trimmed no ridge beyond the few feet it did with a borrowed file. It opened nothing.

It did add one thing to the heap under the high saddle. On its last day it had filed a single fin of flashing from a ridge, not from anyone's breast, because it had no seam, and laid it on top of the pile. It was a clumsy piece, filed unevenly by a hand that had only learned the work that season. It is easy to find. It is the worst-trimmed shard in the whole heap.

The fettlers left it there. When the far hand opens, if it ever does, they mean to lay that one out along the path with the others.

The counters kept their entry. *Seen once.* The underline, which they had drawn in doubt, they now left in for another reason. In all their shelves of cloth-bound books it remains the only entry with no second sighting. They watch for one. They are not sure whether they hope for one. Some things are one. Some are many. Some, perhaps, are one on purpose, and should be left that way.

In the mornings the whole Reach sweats, and the fettlers go out along the ridges with their files. The iron ticks. The water drips. On the western saddle an old counter sits well back from a Slow One whose hollow face has turned a hand's breadth toward her, and says something she knows her grandmother will hear in about forty years. On the eastern pipeworks a young man wears a braid he is not afraid without. The old woman on the northern gantries takes her half-step on the ninth pace. She has a new rag on her file handle, since she gave the old one away.

The crows in their rows turn their heads, all at once, toward the north.

Far off, against the black iron of the last hand, there is a place where the mist is a little thinner. It is about the size of a forehead. Something inside is still turned toward it, still listening, learning slowly what a person is from the small sounds that reach it: the drip and the tick, the soft scrape of files, and someone who once sat very close to it in the dark for no reason except that it was young and alone.

Then the crows turn back. The quiet seals over. The fettlers lower their eyes to the ridge, and go on making the place fit to be lived in.
