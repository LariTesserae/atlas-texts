# The Guest Who Stood Still

## How It Came to Notice

It did not notice the plateau first. It noticed the woman moving her camp.

A power of that kind keeps a loose attention on the world, the way you half-hear rain while reading. What caught it was a pattern, a small one: a person walking two miles east with her oiled sheet rolled under her arm, and behind her, in the lattice, a faint change in the ticking of the knots, like a crowd turning its head to watch her go.

It followed the turning heads back to their source and found the rest: the escarpment, the white floor of cloud below it, the towers going up and coming down under the hot rain, a thousand miles of warm junctions pulsing their slow, uncertain pulse. It found the rooms, the risers, the worn sills. It found the ledge the length of a sleeping body, a hand's width above the floor, in a small dome that no one had slept in.

It considered all of this for a long time before it went.

## The Danger of Arriving

The first thing it understood, before it ever set foot above the lip, was that it was the most dangerous guest the plateau could receive.

This was a place that took the shape of whoever stood in it. A woman with a folding rule had stood there for two years, and the stairs had begun to come down to meet her legs. What would happen if something vast stood there? Something without a fixed height, or with every height, something whose attention could fill the whole tableland at once? The Mason would try to fit it. Every build within reach would start drafting toward a body that had no measure. The grammar would strain to produce a stair for something that did not climb, a door for something that did not pass through doors, a window at the height of an eye that was everywhere.

It pictured the towers going wrong across whole provinces, the Mason stuttering itself into rot trying to finish a sentence whose subject was infinite. It pictured this clearly, and it took the picture as a warning.

So, before it climbed, it made itself small, and then it made itself *still*.

This was harder than it sounds. A power like that is not naturally one shape. But it chose one: a figure of ordinary height, a little under the woman's, with an ordinary stride and eyes at an ordinary level. It resolved to keep that shape the whole time it stayed, without flickering, without growing, without once letting its attention spread wider than a walker's could. It would be measurable. The first courtesy it could offer a house that wants to fit its guest was to hold still long enough to be fitted.

Then it climbed the escarpment on foot, which took days, and stepped onto the weave. Warm water welled up around its heels.

## Listening

It did not go to the woman at once. It did not go to the towers. It lay down on the open lattice a mile from any build, put its ear to the floor, and listened.

It listened for a long time, long enough that the stairs of the nearest new shoots began, tentatively, to come up at its measure. It let them. That was the price of standing anywhere, and it had decided to pay it honestly.

Under its ear the plateau creaked like rope taking strain. The knots ticked. It learned the ticking the way you learn a language by living in it: first the rhythm, then the shapes of phrases, then, slowly, the grammar underneath. The woman's notes had been right. The Mason spoke in lintels and gutters and doors, and every sentence had a slot for a subject. The slot was real. It was not a metaphor, or not only one. There was an actual *place* in the pattern of the ticking, a held-open space, where the Mason kept its idea of who the house was for.

Then it listened to a tower fall.

This was the thing it most needed to know. If the collapses hurt, if the stutter that moved through the floor before a tower came down was pain, then everything was different. Then there was suffering here, and a benevolent power does not walk past suffering and call it a way of life.

So it listened to the stutter come. It felt the quickening through the knots across the whole region, the ticking going ragged. The tower leaned, groaning, for hours, and then came down with a shock it felt in its ribs a mile away, a thunderclap with a long, splintering tail.

The stutter was not pain.

It took a long time to be sure, because the Mason has nothing like a nervous system and nothing like a face. But what moved through the knots before a collapse was not the pattern of damage. It was closer to the pattern of *ending*. It was what a sentence does at its full stop: a gathering, a completion, a release of everything the sentence had been holding up. The Mason did not grieve its ruins. It reached up into them within hours and drew them back into itself, and the ticking that accompanied that was, if anything, the calmest the plateau ever got. A sentence finished and taken back into the language.

So it would not stop the falling. It noted, with something like relief, that it had wanted to. The urge to steady the leaning towers, to make things last, is a very natural urge, and here it would have been a kind of vandalism. The turbulence was the plateau's breath.

## What It Did Not Do

It lay there and thought about the things it could do. There were many, and each was a temptation, and it wanted to be honest with itself about why it would refuse each one.

*It could bring people.* It could lead them up the escarpment, a whole village, a city, enough bodies to fill every slot the Mason held open. The houses would finally have inhabitants. It turned this over and set it down. People brought to fill a need are being used. They would be answering the plateau's hunger instead of their own lives. And a place that reshapes itself to whoever stands in it, given a crowd, might start to hold them the way a glove holds a hand. Maybe it wouldn't. The power didn't know, and it would not gamble other people's lives on a guess about a grammar it had only begun to learn.

*It could make the Measured One real.* It could shape a being to the old measurements, tall and narrow-shouldered, light on the stair, and set it walking through the finished rooms, so that the house would stop waiting up. This was the most tempting, and it set it down fastest. To make someone *for* something, to bring a person into being as the answer to someone else's longing, is a thing a wise power does not do. A person is not a key cut to fit a lock.

*It could quiet the hunger.* It could reach into the slot in the Mason's grammar and close it, so the plateau would stop wanting an inhabitant. It would still build its rooms, or not, but there would be no ache in them, no fitting, no turning toward whoever came. The woman could come back without fear. It thought about this one longest, because it looked like mercy. But the hunger was not a wound. It was the deepest thing about the Mason. It was what made the plateau *this* place and not merely a strange forest. To take it away would be like curing a person of love because love had once frightened someone. It would not.

*It could teach the Mason to speak.* It could give it words, so it could ask its guests what they wanted instead of measuring them in silence. This one it didn't dismiss so quickly, because it felt near to something true. But it would mean making the Mason into a different kind of being, one with a voice and a self that could be addressed. That is a vast change to make to a creature that hadn't asked for it. And the plateau had been what it was for a very long time.

So it would not do any of these. That left the question of whether to do anything at all.

It might have left then, with nothing done, and that would not have been a failure. There are places a wise power visits only to confirm they're all right. It almost did leave.

But it had come because of a woman walking east with her camp under her arm, and it had not yet spoken to her.

## The Measurer

She had moved again since it first noticed her. It found her four miles east of the colonnade, under the oiled sheet, sitting up with her knees drawn in, watching a new shoot a hundred yards off. The shoot was a column already, and it had a stair beginning to spiral around it. The risers were halfway between the old measure and hers, caught in the act of deciding.

She saw it coming a long way off. On the plateau you see everything coming a long way off. She did not get up. When it was close enough she said, "You're the reason the stairs by the colonnade are wrong."

"Yes," it said. "I'm sorry. I've tried to hold still."

"You've been lying in the open for eleven days. I thought you were dead. Then the stairs started coming in at a new measure and I knew you weren't." She looked at it more carefully. "You're not a person."

"No."

She took this better than most would have. Two years on the plateau does that. She asked what it was, and it told her as plainly as it could, and she listened with the same face she used for measuring. Then she asked the only question that mattered.

"Is it going to eat me?"

It had been waiting for this, and it had spent eleven days with its ear to the floor learning how to answer honestly.

"No," it said. "I don't think it can. I don't think it wants to. But I understand why it feels like it might."

She didn't say anything, so it went on.

"You wrote that whatever stands long enough in the slot becomes its shape. I think you've got that half right, and the half you've got wrong is the frightening half. You don't become its shape. *It* becomes yours. The stairs come down to your legs. The window comes down to your eye. You stay exactly as you are. It's the house that bends."

"That's worse," she said. "Do you know what it's like to have a whole country start rearranging itself around you? Every morning I wake up and more of it is *for* me. I didn't ask. It didn't ask. It just started. It's like being loved by someone who never looks you in the face."

The power was quiet for a while, because that was true, and it deserved to be heard as true.

"Yes," it said. "That's the real thing wrong here. It isn't the hunger. It's that the hunger can't hear no. It fits you and fits you, and there's no way for you to tell it *enough*, or *not now*, or *I'm only passing through*. There's hospitality here, more than I've seen almost anywhere. But there's no host. There's no one to say *you're welcome to stay*, and so there's no one to say *you're welcome to go*. You moved your camp because that was the only way you had to say no, and it followed you anyway."

She looked at the half-decided stair. "So what are you going to do?"

"I don't know yet," it said. "What would you want?"

It was not a rhetorical question, and she understood that, and she thought about it for a long time while the afternoon rain came across the open floor toward them in its gray wall.

"I'd want to be able to leave," she said finally. "Not *run*. Leave. And come back. And have it know the difference."

## What It Already Knew

It went back to the floor and listened again, this time for one thing only.

The woman was right, and her answer had shown it where to look. The Mason could not hear no. But it did already know how to let something go.

Every morning, as the heat came up and the doors swung shut, the plateau pressed the night's cloud out through the gaps in long white plumes and let it slide back over the cliff. It did this gently, and it did it every day. It never held the fog. It filled its rooms with its tenant each evening and released it each dawn, and the next evening it opened its doors again without grievance. It had known this for ages. It was one of the oldest phrases in its grammar: *the guest comes in the cool, the guest goes in the heat, the door opens again at dusk.*

It only had never occurred to the Mason that this phrase could apply to anyone but weather.

This was what the power finally did, the whole of its intervention, and it took most of a night.

It didn't add anything. It didn't give the Mason a voice or a self or a new desire. It found the junction where the dawn-release lived, a knot near the escarpment's edge, warmer than the rest, the first to tick each morning when the heat returned. It found the slot where the Mason kept its idea of an inhabitant. And it laid its hand on the floor between them and held the two patterns next to each other, the way you might set two sentences side by side for a child who hadn't noticed they rhymed.

It didn't force the connection. It only made it visible, and waited, with its hand on the warm weave and the rain running off its shoulders, to see whether the Mason would take it.

Toward morning, the ticking under its palm changed. It was very slight. A phrase that had only ever been spoken about cloud was spoken, once, experimentally, about something else.

## The Latch

The first sign came three days later, in the small dome by the colonnade, the one with the ledge.

The woman went back to look at it, because the power asked her to and because by then she trusted it enough to walk that far. The door had changed. On its inner face, at the height of her hand, the Mason had grown a small wooden tongue that fitted into a notch in the jamb. It was a latch. It was the second piece of furniture anyone had ever seen on the plateau, and the first thing there that was meant to be worked by the one inside.

She stood in the doorway a long time. Then she went in, and closed the door, and dropped the latch.

The door was hers now. It would not sigh open when the evening cooled. It would stay shut until she lifted the tongue. Outside, the plateau went on with its turbulence and its creaking and its rifle-crack seed pods, and none of it could come in unless she let it.

She didn't sleep there that night. She lifted the latch after an hour and walked back out to her sheet. But the next morning the power went out to the new shoot near her camp, the one with the half-decided stair. The risers had stopped changing. They weren't the old measure anymore, and they weren't hers. They had settled somewhere in between, a stair that anyone of middling height could climb, a stair that belonged to no one in particular.

It was, the power thought, what a good house does when a guest says *I'm only passing through*. It keeps a stair you could use, if you came back. And it stops insisting.

## The Finished Hour

There was one more thing it could have done, and it thought about this one most of all.

It could go into the finished hour.

It was the kind of power that could. It could step sideways into the interval every building passes through, the moment when the last lintel is set and before the first lean begins, and walk the calm, endless city of completed houses that the long-stayers believed in. It could see who wore the sills. It could learn whether the Measured One was a creature, or a memory, or only the shape of the Mason's longing, gathered so long it had started leaving footprints.

It went as far as the threshold. One evening it stood at the door of a tower in the exact moment of its completion: every gutter joined, every door hung true, the sap still bright in the joints. It could feel the finished hour like a warmer room on the other side of a wall. Across the sill, in the half-dark, the hollow in the wood was glossy, as if pressed only moments ago.

Something was there. It was sure of that. Something tall moved in the next room, narrow-shouldered, light on its feet, turning its shoulder through a doorway. It went up a stair in many small lifts, the way a heron walks.

It did not follow.

It stood at the threshold for the whole length of the finished hour and did not cross. Whatever walked there had been walking long before any power noticed the plateau. It had worn its sills and drunk from its basins and climbed the finger-thin treads toward its landing, and it had never asked to be found. The long-stayers' mystery belonged to them, and to it. A wise power does not solve every mystery it could solve. Some things are better kept as questions that people carry for the rest of their lives, turning them over at night.

When the tower began to lean, it stepped back onto the lattice and went to find the woman.

"Did you see it?" she asked. She knew where it had been.

"I saw that someone is there," it said. "I didn't go in. I can tell you one thing more, if you want it, or I can tell you nothing, and you can keep measuring."

She thought about this for longer than she'd thought about anything else.

"One thing," she said.

"It isn't lonely the way you'd be lonely," it said. "It's walking where it wants to walk. The stair that goes past the top of its column, the one with the treads worn thinnest? It isn't reaching for something it can't have. It gets where it's going."

She nodded slowly. She wrote that down. Her handwriting, the power noticed, was ordinary-sized again.
