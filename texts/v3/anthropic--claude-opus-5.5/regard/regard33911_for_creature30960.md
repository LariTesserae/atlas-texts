# The Visitation at Low Hesk

## It Did Not Come Down the Stair

It came the way attention comes: all at once, and without a place to stand.

For a long while it did nothing, and it considered this the first and most important thing it did. A power that is both wise and kind learns early that most harm done by the strong is done in the first hour, before they have understood where they are. So it waited. It let the hall breathe through it. Eleven minutes in, nineteen out. The mist went past it toward the pumps and came back again, and it held still in the cold the way the eels hold still in the culverts, and it listened.

It counted, because that is what the hall teaches. It counted the pillars out to the blue lamps, and then past them into the rubble, and then into the numbers that had not been cut yet. It noticed that the grid did not confuse it the way it confused the tenders. It took note of that as a difference between itself and them, and a reason for care. What was merely large to it was unbearable to them. It would have to remember that.

It counted lamps: burning, rose, dead. It counted moths in a single cone of sodium light and lost count, and was pleased to lose it. It counted the gauges in every booth, the names crossed out on every telephone card, the stone setters at the feet of the pillars near the stair. There were sixty-one, all facing out.

Then it asked itself the question it had come with.

## The Question of Which One

It had been drawn by *the being who lives in it*. It had heard that as one voice, the way a single lit window in a dark valley can be seen from very far off. Now, close to, it found the phrase coming apart in its hands.

Did it mean the tender in the booth at pillar 208, a woman called Wenna Stobb, who had inherited Aud Ferrow's kettle and her card and her habit of whistling? Or the eels, who lived here before there was a *here*? Or the settle, the lives no bigger than a full stop that were building the first stalactites out of their own falling? The setters? The cutters? Or the one the brass plates were for, which did not live here yet and was, in some sense, the only thing that did?

A lesser power would have chosen. It would have decided which of them was the real inhabitant and served that one, and let the rest count as scenery. This power had learned, somewhere long ago and at some cost, that there is no such thing as scenery. So it resolved to go to each of them in turn. It would find out what hurt, and not assume. It would find out what each wanted, which is not the same as what it needs, and is not always less wise.

It also set itself a rule, the one it always set. Mysteries that hurt, it would try to open. Mysteries that did not hurt, it would leave alone. Low Hesk was full of the second kind. The water authority with no register. The rotas no one draws up. The pillars stenciled with numbers not yet cut. The power could have learned all of these. It chose not to look. Some strangeness is part of the shape of a place, and a place has a right to its shape.

## Wenna, at 208

It went first to the booth, because the tenders were the ones who could be most easily frightened, and so the ones it most needed to understand.

Wenna Stobb was forty-four and had been down eleven years. She had hair beginning to rime at the tips. She breathed on the cycle without knowing it and counted without meaning to. She was not unhappy. That surprised the power, and it took the surprise seriously. The booth was warm. The work was clear. She liked the moths, and she had names for three of the eels, which was two more than was sensible. She liked the telephone most of all: the sense that across kilometres of wet dark there were others at their gauges, reading the same breath.

What hurt her was small and constant, the way the weep is small and constant. She did not know whether she was allowed to stop.

It wasn't that she wanted to stop, exactly. She had tried the surface once, on leave, and the still air had kept her awake for nine nights. But the rota envelopes never said *until*. They only said *continue*. Under the counting, at the bottom of every out-breath, there was a question that had no one to be asked of. *Is this for anything? Will someone tell me when it's done? Will I remember in time?*

She had read the pressures long enough to suspect what the cistern was. Every tender who stays long enough does. She had found Aud's notes, pencilled in the margins of the gauge log in a tiny cramped hand. *Rising again. Floor-net pressures up 0.02 this rota. Not ours to hold it forever.* Wenna had underlined that last line, and then felt foolish, and then left it underlined.

The power could have told her everything. It could have laid the whole truth of the basin in her mind at once, the way a flood comes. It did not, because the whole truth all at once is a kind of drowning too. It could have taken her up the stair and made the surface air bearable. It did not, because she had not asked, and because a person's attachments are not a disease, even when they are strange.

What it did was very small. On the next out-breath, in the quiet, the kettle on its coil came to the boil a little earlier than it should have. Wenna turned to it. The steam went up past the gauge log, and for a moment the booth smelled not of damp and concrete but of the settle, the lived-in smell, the old-apple smell of the hall by the stair. She sat down with her tea. She found she was thinking, quite clearly and in her own words, *Nobody is keeping me here.* She had not known that until then. It was true. She stayed, but now she was staying rather than being kept, and the power saw that her shoulders came down half an inch and stayed down.

That was the first thing it did. It was barely anything. It believed most kindness is barely anything, done precisely.

## Down, with the Eels

The eels were harder to reach, because they had spent ten thousand years not needing to be reached.

The power went down through the grates, into the branching net, past the depth where the tenders stop and the pipes turn old and cracked and mineral-choked. The water here was not the hall's water. It came from the rock, from the fissures the cutters had opened, and it still carried the taste of them: iron, cold, and very long time.

It found the knot where Aud had found it, or one like it. There were dozens of them, pale and still, spines showing like threads of ink through glass. Among them, the eldest was a creature so old that the power had to slow itself almost to stopping in order to be in the same moment with it.

The power had come expecting grief, and there was grief. The eels remembered the fissures: hair-thin, lightless, endless, a whole world made of cracks. They remembered the cutters breaking in, the water rushing out, the terrible bright openness of the gutters. That was why they swam upstream. It was the way home, and home was gone.

But the power listened longer, through a great many breaths, and heard something under the grief that Aud had heard too, lying on the grate with her ear to the iron. The eels were not waiting in ruins. They were waiting for the water.

They knew what the tenders had only begun to suspect. The basin would fill. The hall would go dark and still, and the pumps would stop, and the cold would settle. When that happened, Low Hesk would become the largest fissure the eels had ever known. It would be one black, patient, lightless crack, kilometres across, full of old water. Everything the cutters had taken would be given back, larger. The eels had gone down not to hide but to be near the front of the queue.

There was nothing to fix here. The power saw that clearly, and saw how easily it could have done harm by trying. It could have shored up a fissure somewhere and made them a little refuge of the old world. That would have been kind, and it would have been beside the point.

So it did the only thing the eels seemed to want, which was company in the waiting. It lay with them in the cold flow for a while, as still as they were. When it rose, the eldest turned its blind head a fraction of a degree toward where it had been. In eel, the power understood, that was a great deal.

## Beside the Slowest Setter

At pillar 1147, a setter was falling behind.

The power found it on an out-breath, alone, three kilometres back from the edge, where the concrete had cured months ago and bled nothing. It was stiff. When it took a step, the joints made a sound like a fingernail on slate. The rest of its group had gone on toward the cutting, toward the food, and it could not keep up. It knew it could not keep up. It kept stopping to press its face to the floor.

This was the case that tested the power most, because here, finally, was the plain thing. A creature was dying, alone and slowly, and the power could have stopped it. It could have made the setter young and soft again. It could have carried it to the raw edge and set it down in the lime-milk to lap. It could have done that for every setter that would ever fall behind. It was not certain it shouldn't.

So it asked. It did not ask in words; setters have no use for words. It knelt, in whatever way it knelt, and put itself where the setter could feel it. It offered: *I can take you forward.*

The setter pressed its face to the floor.

The power pressed with it, and listened, and understood what the tenders had half-guessed. The setters were not only eating when they bowed their heads to the concrete. They were listening. They listened through their faces, through the slabs, down past the pipe-net to the rising water, and out along the grid toward the cutting. It is the only sense they have. The young ones listen for food. The old ones, the stiffening ones, listen for something else. The stone ones at the pillar feet near the stair, sixty-one of them facing outward, were still listening. That was the thing the power had not known. Stone is only slow. They could hear the water coming, and they had been hearing it for fifty years, and they were not afraid of it.

The setter at 1147 did not want to go forward. It had spent its whole life at the edge, running ahead of the cure. Now it wanted to stop and listen to the thing it had been too busy to hear.

So the power did not save it. It stayed with it, through a day of breaths, while the setter slowed and stiffened and finally crouched at the foot of pillar 1147, facing out. And because the power was kind as well as wise, and those two are not always the same, it did one small unasked thing. It warmed the concrete under the setter by a single degree, so that the last of its setting would not be cold.

Then it went to see where the others were going, because there was a larger problem behind this one. The setters live only at the edge. When the cutting finished, the edge would end, and there would be no young concrete anywhere, and the whole kind of them would stop. The power did not know yet whether that was a grief or a completion. It went to find out.
