# The Attention

## How it comes

It could not arrive the way powers arrive in stories.

A light breaking through the haze-lid would blind every animal that has evolved to live without one. A voice would be one more voice in a place that is already deafening. A sudden presence, a pressure, a shift in the air, would pass through the troop from wrist to wrist, the way the old female's stillness does. The monkeys would scatter across the glass, and some of the young would cut themselves.

So it comes slowly, the way the heat does. It comes up from underneath and settles into the basin a degree at a time, until it is simply part of the conditions. Nothing notices, because there is nothing to notice. The bats pour out at dusk. The frogs begin. The crabs click past.

Then it looks.

It looks at the whole thing at once: the braided tubes, the rising vapour, the fig roots working the basalt apart, the swarms and their collapses, the vent in the east still breathing sulphur over its grove of white trunks. It sees the blind salamander hanging in its pool, feeling the drops. It sees the granddaughter with the glowing splinter in her mouth.

Then it looks at her, the old one, sitting on the warm slab with both palms flat. Because it is wise, the first thing it understands is how easily it could ruin all of this.

## What it could do

It could cool the rock.

That would be the obvious mercy. It could close the fissure, seal the vents, and let the basin settle into ordinary ground. There would be no more groves cooked in a night and no more forests falling into the earth. But the heat is the mother of everything here. Without it there is no vapour, no drip, no warm tubes for the bats, and therefore no guano, no figs, no forest, no frogs in their thimbles. The basin would become a dry field of black rock that nothing lives on. The power sees this in an instant and sets the thought down.

It could heal her hands.

It looks at them for a long time: the white lattice of scar, the missing joints, the split thumb that healed wrong. It could make them whole. And then she could not hold the glass the way she holds it. The long, curved flakes that open a snail along its whorl come out of that injury. Her skill is not something she has in spite of the wound. It is the wound, made useful. To heal her would be to take something from her while calling it a gift.

It could give her back her hearing.

Here it hesitates, because this one is closer to kindness. Then it imagines her with the frogs and the bats and the cicadas flooding back into her skull, the roar she has not heard in years. She would lose the thing she hears instead. The ground, which is loud to her now, would go quiet again under all that noise. It imagines the troop with no deaf elders, only screaming, and nobody laying a flat palm between their shoulder blades. It sets that thought down too.

It could give them more.

This is the largest temptation, and the power is honest enough to feel it. The monkeys stand at the edge of something. The granddaughter carries light. The power could nudge that. It could make the thought of fire arrive a little sooner, make the knapping a little finer, help the tapping-rhythms become a language that holds more. It could lift them.

But it looks at the scatter around the warm slab, the thousands of flakes going back to the first figs, and it understands what it is seeing. This is theirs, every flake of it. It is the slowest, bloodiest, most expensive kind of knowledge there is, paid for one cut palm at a time. It is the only thing in the basin that belongs to anyone. If the power adds to it, even gently, the knowledge becomes partly the power's. The monkeys would never know. But the power would know, and it does not want to be the author of their beginning. It wants them to have one.

## The hardest one

The collapses are harder.

Every year a tube roof gives way somewhere under a troop that has no old female to strike the stone. A patch of forest falls into the ground with a roar, and some of what falls is monkeys, and some of those are young. The power sees this. It sees the bodies in the new skylights, and the bats coming, and the figs coming after.

It sees the juveniles learning with blood. It sees the ones who do not live long enough to become the silent, careful ones.

It sits with this for a long time, because benevolence that does not grieve is not benevolence. And this is the question it cannot answer cleanly: *could I save them without taking the basin from them?* It could hold up a tube roof here and there, invisibly. It could deflect a flake from a small palm. No one would ever know.

But the old female's troop survives because she listens. She listens because her mother pushed the troop uphill once and she remembered the white trees afterward. The knowing passes from palm to palm because it is true, because the ground really does open, and the ones who pay attention really do live. If the power quietly holds the roofs up, the listening stops meaning anything. The bell becomes a toy. The next old female lays her palm on the rock and learns nothing, because the rock has been made to lie.

So the power makes a hard choice and does not pretend it is an easy one. It will not make the world false in order to make it safe. The suffering here is real, and it is not pointless. It is the cost of a place where things can still begin. The power does not like this. It keeps not liking it. It never lets the not-liking turn into indifference.

What it permits itself instead is to be present at every one of those deaths, so that none of them goes unwitnessed. That is not nothing. In the Hotbed, it may be the rarest thing there is.

## What it does

It stays.

That is the whole of it, mostly. No one stays in the basin. The basin does not allow it. But the power is not subject to the basin, so it stays, and it learns the place the way she learned it: from the inside, slowly, through contact.

It learns the language of hands. It watches her groom her daughter and tap slow rhythms into the skin. Over seasons it works out what no human visitor ever has: which rhythm means *the glass is good at the fissure*, which means *the snails are thick after the dry-breath*, which means only *I am here, I am here, I am here*. It learns that there are far fewer words than it expected, and that each one holds far more.

It learns to hear the ground. It feels the fig roots creak and report. It feels the bats' restlessness before dusk, and the slow rain of crab-feet crossing the basin. It feels the heat rise and fall. And it learns what she learned: which kind of heat means *go uphill now*.

It learns her map. This matters more than anything else it does. Behind the milky eyes she carries a landscape that no longer exists: groves that fell, tubes that drained, the forest where the eastern vent now breathes. It exists nowhere else. The basin has no memory. It makes itself and unmakes itself and keeps nothing. Everything that has happened here has happened once and then been gone. She is the only record, and she is twenty-six, and she will not last much longer.

So the power keeps it. Gently, without taking anything from her, it learns everything she knows, and it holds it.

It is the one thing it can give that changes nothing about what happens. It only changes what is kept.

## The granddaughter

It watches the girl with the glowing splinter more closely than anything else, and it does not help her.

It notices that she cuts herself less. It notices a young male watching her. One night another juvenile breaks off her own finger of glowing heartwood and holds it clumsily in her mouth. The power feels something it can only call hope, and it lets itself feel it without acting on it.

It does not know whether the light will last. The girl may forget it. The troop may take it up. The ground may open. The power could find out by deciding the answer, and it chooses not to. It would rather not know than know because it made the answer true.

## The last evening

She dies in the dry-breath months, when the vents slacken and the rock runs a little cooler than usual.

Nothing kills her. She is simply old, older than anything here, and one evening at dusk she climbs slowly onto the warm slab and does not climb down again. The troop moves around her. A daughter grooms her and taps a rhythm into her shoulder, and the power, which knows the rhythm now, looks away for a moment out of something like respect.

Near full dark she reaches for the hammerstone. Her split-thumbed hand closes on it with the old strange grip. She strikes the bell.

The note goes up through the chorus, clear and high, like a needle through cloth. All over the range, heads turn.

Then she lays her palms flat on the warm rock and listens as the note goes down into the tubes and out through the hollows, toward the dark and the distant sea.

The power has promised itself it will not touch anything. It does not touch the note. It does not change the rock. It does not ease her or take her anywhere. What it does is the only thing it has not yet done in all its seasons here. It lays itself alongside her. It puts itself where her hands are, palm to the rock, and it listens with her.

For her whole life she has kept her hand on the flank of something enormous, and nothing enormous ever kept a hand on her.

The note comes back changed, lower and longer, with a shiver in it that tells her the ground beneath the slab is solid. It has always been solid here. That is why the old ones sit on it. The note tells her she is safe. Perhaps, in whatever way she understands anything, she understands that too.

She does not know the power is there. It has made sure of that. But the rock under her palms is warm, as it always is, and perhaps a very little warmer. That warmth is the power, and it is the only trace it lets itself leave.

In the morning the granddaughter finds her. The girl sets the glowing splinter down on the slab beside the body and sits with her for a while in the brown dusk, with one small hand flat on the old one's back.

## After

The power stays.

It watches the troop go on without her. It watches the next old female, a daughter who is beginning to go deaf, stop at a ridge one day, tap it, and lay her palm beside it, uncertainly, with her head tilted. It watches her get up, grip the nearest wrist, and hold on.

The troop goes around.

The power watches the fig roots and the falling trunks, and the vents opening, and the tubes collapsing, and the bats moving in. It watches the forest rise and fall again over the places she remembered, and it lays the new landscape over the old ones it keeps, map on map on map. The basin has had a witness now. For the first time, what happens here is not simply gone.

People sometimes ask what a benevolent and wise power would do with a place like this. The honest answer is: almost nothing. It declines to cool the rock, heal the hands, lift the minds, or hold up the roofs, and each refusal is harder than it looks. What it gives, it gives out of sight: attention, memory, company at the deaths, a warmth under one old palm on one last night.

This is a place built entirely of beginnings. The kindest thing a power could do was to let it keep beginning, and to make sure, quietly, that someone would remember everything it had begun.

The rock is still warm. The frogs are still singing. Somewhere a young monkey strikes a stone, and a bell tone rises through the chorus. Somewhere a small hand is laid flat on the ground.

And beneath it, where no one will ever feel it, something very large lays its hand flat as well and listens.
