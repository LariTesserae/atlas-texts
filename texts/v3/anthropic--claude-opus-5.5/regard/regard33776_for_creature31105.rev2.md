# The Guest on the Hammer Moor

## First, the Honest Answer

It would do very little. Almost nothing. I want to say that at the start, because the pull, when you imagine something vast and kind turning its attention onto a place like this, is to expect it to *fix* something. It would not. But almost nothing is not the same as nothing. What it did do took a long time, and most of it was learning.

## The Noticing

It did not arrive. Things that arrive on the Hammer Moor get swallowed. A new steam column, a new tarn, a new bog-burst: within days the moss has them. A power that came down in light would have stood in the sourceless gray like a lamp in a cellar, and the hammerfolk would have covered their faces and looked deeply unhappy until it passed.

So it came the way the cloud comes down: slowly, from above, without edges. For a long while it was only a slight attentiveness in the weather. The plover flocks turned once for no reason and then went on. The grazers, who never startle, lifted their heads all together and looked at nothing, then went back to walking the old valleys. A hammerfolk woman at the edge of a charged tarn felt her hair lift a little higher than the eels could account for. She sent a short burst on the telegraph, and it went out across the plateau frog by frog. The nearest translation anyone could make of it is *someone is listening who is not us*.

That was accurate. It was listening. It listened for a long time before it did anything, because the first thing a wise power knows about an old place is that the old place knows more about itself than any visitor can.

## What It Thought of Doing

It considered things. Let me be honest about those too, because they were not stupid things, and a less patient kindness would have done any of them.

It thought about the cold. The hammerfolk live their whole lives a few degrees above freezing, with rust in their skin and aching joints, and the only dry place they have is the hollow heart of a stone wrapped in cable. It could have warmed them. But the cold is what keeps the moss growing slowly enough to become peat, and the peat is what the stokers breathe and the fermenters eat. Warm the moor and you would rot it. And the hammerfolk are not suffering the cold the way a stranger would. They are built into it, the way the grazers' coats are built out of moss.

It thought about the mountains. Here was a death that has been going on for a hundred million years, and it could have stopped it. It could have stilled the grinding, fixed the ghost peaks in place, and held the last stumps of the range exactly as they are, forever.

It spent a long time with that thought, and in the end it saw what the thought was: a refusal to let something finish. The mountains are not being murdered. They are being taken apart by something that loves them, or does whatever a moor does instead of loving, carefully, grain by grain, with company. Stopping that would not be mercy. It would be a kind of embalming. The mountains would be kept, not helped. The hammerfolk, who have been keeping vigil for longer than anyone has kept anything, would be told that their vigil had been unnecessary all along.

It thought about the ledger. The frogs read out the accounts, and the accounts have never balanced. It could have balanced them. It could have read the whole tally in one breath, every making and unmaking since the range was young, reconciled it, and handed the moor a final figure.

It listened to the telegraph for a full season before it understood why that would be wrong. The ledger is not failing to balance. The ledger is the moor saying *still going* over and over. An account that balances is an account that is closed. The day the frogs' count comes out even is the day there is nothing left to count.

It thought about being seen. It could have shown itself to the hammerfolk, told them what it was, and let them know that something enormous and old had noticed them and approved. But they have never needed to be approved of. They pay attention to the dying because the dying need it, not because anyone is watching. Telling them they were watched would give them something they had never lacked and take away something they had: the plain, unobserved rightness of doing it anyway.

So it did not do any of those things. What it did was harder.

## Learning the Beat

It learned to walk.

It took a shape small enough to stand on the mat, roughly hammerfolk-sized, roughly hammerfolk-colored. This was not a disguise. It was the only honest way to be there at the right scale. Then it tried to cross a kilometer of moor, and it fell.

It fell a great many times. It stepped as the thud arrived instead of as the wave passed under, and it sank to the knee in rust-colored water. It misjudged a place where two rhythms crossed, and the ground bucked and threw it. It stood too long near a vent and learned what the red, rank moss already knew about heat. A hammerfolk child watched all this from the top of a bobbin with an expression of enormous, silent pity. Eventually the child came down, took its hand, and walked it across the next swell, lifting and setting down, lifting and setting down, until the thud and the step landed together.

It could have made itself able to walk the moor perfectly from the first moment. It chose not to. There is a difference between knowing a place and being taught it, and only one of them makes you a guest.

By the end of the first year its heart, or the thing it used for a heart while it was small, beat in time with the pumps. When the nearest rhythm changed, the heart changed to match. It had not arranged this. It had only stayed long enough.

## The Bobbin

They let it sleep in a bobbin.

They did not ask what it was. They asked whether it would cut the cable, and it said no, and they asked whether it would take any away, and it said no. Then they showed it how to work the fibers apart with its fingers and bind them back behind, and it tunneled in after them to the hollow granite at the core. It lay in the dry dark with the grinding of the mountains humming up through its back. The place smelled of dry grass and lichen and people, and there was a young grazer asleep by the entrance that had wandered in out of a gale.

It learned their language, the phrases measured between beats. It learned that they speak in long heavy lines near slow rhythms and quick bursts near fast ones, and that a story told near a steam vent has the vent's breathing in it. It learned their word for their own kind, *on the beat*. It noticed that they had begun, among themselves, to use a word for it. The word was borrowed from the frogs, and it meant roughly *the listener*, or perhaps *the one that is also counting*.

It met a child who had been born in a quiet place. Very few are. The mother had been caught in a still hollow between rhythms when the labor came, and there was no swell and no thud to set the heart going. The child was eleven and anxious all the time, and she flinched at the bog-bursts that the other children walked around without looking. It thought: here, at least, is something I can mend. I could give her the beat she missed.

Then it watched what the hammerfolk did with her. Whoever was nearest carried her, always, against their chest, so that she could feel their heart doing what hers could not. When she slept, she slept wedged between two adults in the bobbin. When she walked, someone walked beside her with a hand on her back, stepping so she could step with them. She had never once in eleven years been left alone with her own unsteady heart. It saw that the mending was already being done, by the only people who had any right to do it. It saw that she was not a problem the moor had failed to solve. She was a reason the hammerfolk hold each other.

It did one small thing. When it was her turn to be carried and no one else was near, it carried her, and it let its borrowed heart keep the beat against her ear. That was all. That is what anyone there would have done.

## The Climb

In the third year, an old man invited it to climb.

They went up into the shadow of a ghost peak, into the north-face cold, into the wind coming down a col that was not there. The old man leaned into the invisible slope and the power leaned beside him. Its calves burned, which it permitted, and its ears popped, which it permitted, and snow began to fall on the two of them out of a cloud that had no business snowing. They walked for most of a day and covered perhaps four hundred meters of moss. Then they reached the summit and sat down.

The power could feel the mountain from there. It was not stone. The stone was mostly gone. What it felt was the part that had stayed: the cold, the old north, the shape of the valleys, the long habit of being high. It was very tired, and very old. It was not afraid. It had been dying with company for so long that it had forgotten fear was an option.

The power had brought one thing up the mountain, and it had carried it the whole way uncertain whether to use it.

It can see forward. The moor remembers backward; that is what the swells show, the range standing whole and white, the glaciers. The power can see where the grains go. It had seen the silt the grinding makes being carried off the plateau by the water that runs in every direction, down to some low basin, laid down and pressed and buried and turned slowly to stone. It had seen that stone, a long time from now, being lifted again by the same deep forces that raised the range in the first place, sharp and young and white. The mountain is not ending. It is being sent on. It could have told the mountain this. It had climbed up here to tell it.

It sat in the snow beside the old man and waited for the right moment. While it waited, a swell passed far below under the flat moor, and in the half-second of the crest the power saw what the hammerfolk see. It saw the plateau at another age. It was not the white range of the past. It was the other one: a plain of dust under a strange sun, and beyond it, at the very edge, something rising.

The mountain had already seen it. The swells go both ways. The moor carries every version of itself, and the mountain had been shown its own future a thousand million times over the long dying. It had simply never needed to be told, because it was never the kind of thing that needs to know where it is going in order to go.

So the power said nothing. It sat. It kept still, the way the old man was keeping still, for a day and then another day. It talked a little in measured phrases to no one visible, and the no one visible, in the only way a mountain can, was glad of the company.

## The One Thing

Here is what it did. It is the only thing it did that the moor could not have done for itself.

On the way down, in the last of the ghost-snow, it asked the old man what happens at the end. It asked what happens when the last shadow lies down flat and the grinding stops, and the hammerfolk come down from the last summit and walk out to the nearest bog-burst and lie down on the black steaming river and let it take them.

The old man said that was right. That was how it would go.

And who sits with you, it asked.

The old man was quiet for four beats, which in their language is a long time. Then he said, *No one. There will be no one left. That is all right. We are the ones who sit.*

So it made him a promise, and it made the same promise, later, to each of them, one at a time, in the bobbins. It was the only thing it asked permission to do.

When that day comes, however far off it is, it will be there. It will not come to stop anything or to carry anyone off to somewhere warmer. It will stand at the edge of the last bog-burst while the last of the hammerfolk lie down, and it will not look away. Then it will stay afterward. It will stay while the scars green over and the frogs add the last line of that chapter to the ledger. It will stay through the first quiet the plateau has known in a hundred million years, the pumps still going, the grinding gone. It will hear the silence where the mountains were, so that the silence has a listener too.

And it will remember them. It will remember the rust-red skin and the moss gardens on the old ones' heads, the step on the beat, the quiet-born girl carried from chest to chest, and the snow that fell only on them. It will carry that into whatever comes after, into the low basin where the silt is turning to stone, and into the next range when it rises sharp and young and white. Whoever lives in the shadow of those peaks may never know that the stone above them was once kept company all the way down. But something will know. The vigil will have had a witness.

They are the part of the moor that pays attention. It will be the part of everything else that pays attention to them.

## What It Left

Then it went back up into the cloud, slowly, without edges, the way it had come.

It changed nothing. The pumps still thud. The vents still breathe on the stokers' irregular rhythm. The cable is still wound, never cut, around every summit stump. The ghost peaks still shift in the fog, and the hammerfolk still climb them and sit in the snow.

Except for one thing, and it did not do this. The moor did.

That night the eels in the deepest tarns discharged together, all at the same pitch. The frogs caught it through their wet skins and passed it on across the plateau, pool to pool, a pattern the telegraph had never carried before. The old woman at the edge of the charged tarn, with her hair standing up and her feet in the water, heard it and knew what it was.

It was a line in the ledger that was not a making and not an unmaking. The moor had no category for it, so it made one. The nearest anyone could translate it was a single word, and it went into the accounts that have never balanced and never will, between a bog-burst and a vent choking out:

*Visited.*

Then the next swell came, and the thud, and the moor went back to work.
