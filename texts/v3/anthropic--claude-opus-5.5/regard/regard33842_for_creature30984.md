*...as sharp as if it were drawn this morning.*

It was.

## The Spiral

The Brief know this, though it is the one part of the Ledger they do not recite to strangers.

Follow the spiral's line outward from its center, around and around, each turn a finger's width from the last. It grows wider through forty-one turns until it has the diameter of a small room. At the end of the line, where the groove in the dust grows shallow and stops, there is a finger. The finger is still in the dust, and it is still moving.

It belongs to a child. She sits cross-legged on the open stone, a long way from any hall, alone. She is Held, worn to the soft grey of everything, her hair sanded into a single smooth shape. Her right arm is extended, and her forefinger rests at the end of the line, pressed in to the first knuckle. She is drawing the forty-second turn. She began it before the Ledger began.

No speaking tube runs out here. No one's ear is near her mouth. She was not caught at work like the builders in the halls. Whatever she was doing when the long slowing took her, she was doing it alone, out in the dark, away from the others. Her face is turned slightly down, toward the line, and the Brief have argued for generations about her expression. Some say it is concentration. Some say it is the face a child makes when she has run away and is waiting to be found.

The Brief do not touch her. They leave their prints in a wide ring outside the spiral and do not cross it. They say you should not step on someone's sentence.

## The Attention

What noticed the Tableland was not a god in any shape the builders would have recognized. It had no face and no fire. It was closer to a way of paying attention, one old enough and wide enough that whole worlds drifted through it the way a kilnmoth drifts through the dark. It was gentle because it had learned, over a very long time, that most harm is done in a hurry.

It did not notice the Tableland because of its size, or its age, or its strangeness. Stranger places were common. It noticed a single unfinished thing, a sustained vowel held in iron. Somewhere in its vast listening, that vowel sounded like the middle of a word, and a word in the middle is a kind of question.

So it turned toward the place. Then, before it did anything at all, it stopped.

A power like this one knows that the first act of kindness is to understand where you are about to stand.

---

## The Problem of Arriving

On the Tableland, everything leaves a record, and nothing erases it.

The power saw this at once, and it saw what it meant. Whatever it did here would be permanent. It would not be permanent in the way great deeds are called permanent in fast worlds, where they are remembered for a while and then remembered wrongly. It would be permanent in the literal dust. Any arrival would be written into the plateau for as long as the plateau lasted, readable by every Brief who ever crouched over it.

It could arrive without weight and leave no mark. It considered this. But on the Tableland, a thing that leaves no print is a thing that was not there, and to walk here unrecorded would be a kind of lie told in the language of the place. The Brief believe that everything kept should be read. To give them nothing to read would be to hide from the only people who would ever look.

So it chose to have weight, and it chose where the weight would fall.

It arrived at the eastern edge, where the plateau falls into unmeasured dark, and it arrived dim. It was no brighter than a filament, and in fact a little dimmer, so that no Brief eye, wide from a lifetime in ember-light, would be hurt by it. It took a shape small enough to stand close to others, because nothing here can be said from a distance. It took a stride that was neither hurried nor slow. Then it set its first foot down in the dust, and the dust took the print, crisp-edged.

It walked toward the nearest works. Where older prints lay in its way, it stepped around them. Where a tender groove ran in its direction, it did not walk inside it, because that road belonged to one animal remembering itself, and a stranger's feet in it would blur the memory. It did not cross its own path. It had learned the Brief word for that, and it had no reason to grieve yet.

## What It Could Have Done

It is worth saying plainly what lay within its reach, because a benevolent power is defined as much by what it declines as by what it does.

**It could have relit the sky.** Stars were not difficult. It could have hung a thousand of them over the plateau in an hour.

It thought about the Brief, whose eyes had been made by ember-light for a hundred generations and whose whole art of seeing was an art of dimness. It thought about the kilnmoths, who took warmth through the flats of their bodies and had never been asked to bear light. It thought about the builders' constellation laid out on the ground, the only sky the Tableland had, which the builders may have meant as a sky. A new heaven would not add to that one. It would erase it. The power set the stars aside.

**It could have reheated the root of the mountain.** It could have restored the gradient and set the engines roaring again, the flywheels spinning so fast they blurred.

It pictured the halls in that moment. The Held were standing with their hands on the spokes. The tender was lying along the piston, sealed to the iron by its tacky hide. A worn machine would suddenly be asked to be young. The girders were thinned to ribs, and the power could hear exactly how they would sing and then shear. Speed would not be a resurrection here. It would be a violence done to everything that had made its peace with slowness. It set the heat aside.

**It could have brought rain.** It could have washed the grey from the Brief and filled the hollows with water.

Water would also dissolve the record. Every print on the plateau would soften and run. The builders' layered squares would go, and the Deciding, and the fan of young lines around it. The long crusted river of tender-breath would go, and so would the funeral paths where the bereaved had stepped into their lost ones' footprints. All of it would sink into mud and be gone in a season. The Tableland's only way of remembering would be undone by a single kindness. It set the rain aside.

**It could have woken the Held.** This was the one it considered longest, because it was the one that most resembled mercy.

It could have quickened them all at once, restoring the fierce warmth of their bodies so that the man stepped up onto his rung, the woman at the desk finished her word, and the vowel in the iron resolved in an instant into a name, or into *yes*. The child's finger would lift from the dust. The city would fill with voices.

The power did not set this one aside at once. It held it, and turned it over, and noticed its own desire. There is a pleasure in rescuing, and it is not always the rescued who feel it. The power had lived long enough to be suspicious of wanting something that much on someone else's behalf.

It noticed that it did not know whether the Held were trapped or resting. It did not know whether a sentence stretched across centuries was a prison or a devotion. It did not know what the woman at the tube wanted, because it had not asked her.

So, before it did anything, it went to ask.

---

## Asking the Quick

It asked the Brief first, because the Brief could be asked at the power's own pace, and because they had been here reading the place for a hundred generations. Arriving to help and consulting no one who lives there is its own kind of arrogance.

It found a heap of them asleep in the hollow base of a chimney, murmuring. It waited outside until one woke and came out to walk. The power had already understood, reading the ground on its way, that it should not enter a sleeping place uninvited.

The Brief who came out was old for a Brief, perhaps forty, which is old among fast things in a slow world. She saw the dim shape standing by the chimney. She saw its prints, a single line in from the east that stepped around everything, and she read them before she looked at the power itself, because that is the Brief way. The prints told her the stranger was careful. Then she came close and laid her cheek against where its cheek would be, and waited.

The power spoke into her ear. It said that it had come from outside, that it meant well, and that it would do nothing without understanding first. It asked what she would want, if anything could be changed.

The conversation went on for a long time. Cheek-to-cheek speech is slow in a way fast worlds forget. You cannot interrupt, and you cannot look away. Then she took the question to the others. They stood in a ring, mouth to ear, and it went round and came back changed, and she brought it back.

Here is what the Brief said, as the power understood it.

They did not want stars. They had heard of stars in the Ledger and considered them loud. They did not want rain. They were afraid of it, the way a reader is afraid of fire. They did not want the Held woken. At this some of them had become angry in the ring, in their quiet way, and the anger had come back around the circle intact: *you do not wake someone in the middle of a word.*

But there was one thing. The old woman had to go around the ring three times before she could say it.

They wanted to know the end of the word. They did not want to be told it. They wanted to hear it. Every Brief lives perhaps fifty years, and the vowel in the iron had been sounding for longer than the Ledger. Not one of them would live to hear what came after it. They would read the whole plateau and never reach the end of the one sentence that mattered most. They had made their peace with this, mostly, the way the Tableland makes peace with almost. But the power had asked, so she told it.

Then she asked the power a question of her own. Were the Held unhappy? She had always wondered. No Brief could ask them.

The power said it did not know, and that it would go and find out.

---

## Asking the Held

To ask the Held, the power had to become slow.

This was the hardest thing it did on the Tableland, and the most important. A fast power can hear a slow people, but it cannot converse with them. It hears only their sentences stretched into a drone. To speak with the Held, it had to enter their time, a single heartbeat lasting years and a single syllable lasting centuries, and stay there long enough to exchange a few words.

A power as old as this one can do that. The cost was not pain. It was patience of a depth the power had not needed in a long while. It gave the Tableland a great deal of its attention, for a very long stretch of time, and it did not rush.

It entered the hall where the woman stood with her mouth to the bell of the speaking tube. It stood beside her, slowed until her face was no longer a sculpture but a face in the middle of speaking. It could see the particular shape of her lips around the vowel. It laid its cheek to hers, as the Brief had taught it.

It asked her whether she wanted to be woken.

The answer took a long time. The power did not experience it as long, because it was inside her time now. Her reply was not in words. She was already using her only words on someone else. It came in the small movement of attention a person makes when interrupted by someone they do not mind. The power understood it as clearly as speech:

*I am saying it. Let me say it.*

The power asked whether she knew that the city was wearing down, and that she might be sanded into dust before the word was finished.

What came back was very nearly amusement. *The word is in the iron. Nothing up here fades. When I am dust, the iron will still be carrying it, and he will still be hearing it, and after he is dust, the tube will still hold it.* The power understood that she had known this for a long time, and that she had chosen the word knowing it. It did not matter whether she finished speaking it. It mattered that it would be finished being heard.

Then it asked her the last question: whether she wanted the Brief to know what the word was.

This reply took longest of all. The power watched something happen in her face across what, outside, was most of a Brief lifetime. It was the look of someone thinking of children at her feet.

*No,* she said. *Let them argue. The argument is how they love us.*

---

The power asked others.

The man on the ladder did not want to be woken either. He wanted to reach the rung himself. He had been climbing toward it for a very long time. To be lifted onto it now would be like being carried the last step of a pilgrimage.

The woman at the desk was of two minds. She was writing a letter, and she had come to suspect, over the slow centuries of a single sentence, that the person she was writing to was gone. She did not want to be woken, but she asked the power whether it would read the letter when she was done. It said it would.

A man in the fourth hall on the northern line wanted very much to be woken. He was frightened, and had been frightened for a long time. He had been caught in the long slowing mid-stumble, and he did not want to be there. The power did not pretend otherwise, or tell him the slowness was beautiful. It asked him what he wanted, exactly. It asked where he would like to be and at what speed. Then it did what he asked, gently, and only for him. It brought him up into Brief-time, a little at a time over many days, so that his heart would not tear. It walked with him out of the hall. The Brief received him with enormous care, as a living piece of the Ledger, a builder who could speak. He did not find it easy, being fast in a slow world. But he had chosen it, and that was the difference that mattered.

Most of the Held were like the woman at the tube. They were not trapped. They were in the middle of something they had meant to do and meant to finish at the pace it was actually going. A few were like the frightened man, and the power went to each of them. It did not assume the answer from the one before.
