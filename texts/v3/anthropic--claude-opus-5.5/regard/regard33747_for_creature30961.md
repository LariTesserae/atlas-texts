# The Visitor Who Did Not Need to Come

## Arrival

It came up the lower passes the way weather comes, without hurry. It had seen the plume from a long way off, the white standing cloud in the notch of the ridge that never moves, and it had known for a long time before it arrived that there was something alive up there.

Power of that kind has an old habit, and the habit is the first thing it has to put down. The habit says: *you noticed, so you are involved. You are able, so you are responsible.* It has learned, slowly and at some cost, that this is not quite true. Noticing is not a summons. Being able to do something is not the same as being asked.

So it stopped at the lip, where the thin blue wind turns suddenly to wet breath, and stood a while in the doorway of the air without going in.

When it did go in, it went by the stair. It could have come down any way it liked. It could have made itself smoke and poured over the terraces, or simply been at the bottom. But the stair was the way someone had made, so it took the stair. The treads were cut for feet a little smaller than its chosen feet, and it shortened its stride to fit them. It did not smooth them or widen them. It let the house tell it how to walk.

## The Temptations

It sat in the hut doorway, the only place built for sitting, and let the list of things it could do arrive, the way such lists always do.

It could **wake the sleeper.** Reach down through the glass and the slumped housing into the hum, find whatever passes for a shoulder there, and shake it gently. *You can come out now. It's safe. I'm here.*

It could **finish the shell.** It could see well enough where the garden was going, with the lens or without it: taller ferns, deeper moss, walls further back. It could lay a thousand nights of glass in one. The prints would reach the stair by morning.

It could **protect the place.** Close the passes. Turn visitors aside with a little confusion of the path, a fog, a feeling of dread at the last shoulder of scree. Nobody would crack the terraces again to read the layered footprints in cross-section, the way someone clearly had on the eastern wall, where a riser lay broken open like a book.

It could **understand it.** It could open the machine, trace the filaments to wherever they led, learn what the hum was, who cast the posts, what the wire was for, whether the being was a child or a seed or a memory running in a loop. It could know for certain.

It could **judge it.** Decide whether this thing, once born, would be good for the mountain, for the villages in the lower valleys, for the world beyond them. Decide whether it should be born at all.

It turned each one over, like the smooth granite stones on the shelf, and set each one down again.

Each of these was a way of making the garden into a story about the visitor. Waking the sleeper would make the visitor its first sight, its midwife, the voice it heard before its own. Finishing the shell would put the visitor's hand into every layer from here to the end, and the garden, which becomes *more itself the less you interfere*, would become somewhat less itself forever, and nobody would ever know by how much. Protecting it would turn it into a sealed room, and what grows in a sealed room is not the same as what grows in a room that happens to be quiet. Understanding it by opening it is the oldest way of loving something to death. And judging it, deciding in advance what a creature not yet born deserves, was the one temptation it distrusted most. That one wears the costume of wisdom.

## Listening

What it did instead, first, was listen, because there was one question that could override everything else, and it needed to answer that question honestly.

*Is anything here suffering?*

A wise restraint that ignores pain is not wisdom. It is only indifference with good manners. If the sleeper were trapped, if the prints fell short because something was wrong and not because something was still growing, then the rules would change.

So it went to the fourth post on the southern slope and laid a hand on the iridescent metal, and felt the hum the way you feel a large animal breathing through a blanket. It lay down on the floor of the bowl with its ear to the soft new glass, and the glass was still warm and gave very slightly under its cheek. It listened for a long time. A night and a day and part of another night.

What it heard was not distress. It was not the rhythm of something straining at a wall. The hum rose a little toward evening, when the posts warmed, and eased in the small hours. Over that long listening it was lengthening, the way a sleeping child's breathing lengthens as it goes deeper. Somewhere under the lowest basin, something was doing what it was supposed to be doing, and doing it at its own pace.

Then it went to the broken riser on the eastern wall. It had not broken it, but since someone had, it read what was there. Layer on layer of small bare feet, all walking outward from the machine. In the deepest layers: one step, then nothing. Higher: two, three. Higher still: up the first riser. In the newest layers, very faintly, four terraces.

The prints were not failures. They were a growth chart, like the pencil marks on a kitchen doorframe. Each one said *this far now*, and each was a little further than the last.

That settled it. Nothing here needed rescuing. Something here was growing, and growing is not a problem to be solved.

It made one private promise, the kind wise things keep instead of rules: *If the marks ever stop getting longer, I will come back and listen again, and I will be willing to be wrong about all of this.* Restraint that cannot be revised is only another kind of arrogance.

## The Small Things

It did do a few things. They were very small.

It laid its hand flat on the broken riser, not to mend it, only to feel whether the break hurt. It did not seem to. The night-skin was already creeping over the fracture from below, an inch a season perhaps, and in a few years the book would close on its own. It let it close on its own.

In the hut, it noticed that the lens sat slightly crooked on the shelf. It was angled away from the three stones, as if the last visitor had picked it up, looked through it, and put it back without care. The dust-free outline on the glass shelf showed where it had lain before, square to the stones, in its place in the row. The visitor put it back where it had been. That was not interference. It was the difference between a guest and an intruder. The house had already said where the lens belonged.

It did not look through the lens. It thought about it, and understood that it did not need to see the garden finished in order to trust that the garden would be finished. It also understood, more quietly, that the lens was someone's eye, and you do not look through someone's eye without being invited.

It did not touch the warm wire at all.

## The Stones

The stones held it longest.

Three smooth granite stones from the old mountain outside, rubbed in a pocket or a palm until they were soft-edged, set in a row the way you set down things you mean to come back for. Proof of an outside. A child's collection.

It knew, with the kind of knowing that comes before reasoning, that it could be the one who brought them. The garden folds time like a shell folds space. The stones were here already, and someone had carried them, and nobody knew who. It would be the easiest thing in the world to walk out through the notch, find three granite pebbles in the scree, carry them in, set them down. To close the loop. To make itself part of the story from the very beginning.

It wanted to. It noticed that it wanted to. It noticed that the wanting was sweet and a little proud, the wish to be the answer to a mystery, to be the one who was there, whose kindness was built into the foundations.

That was exactly why it didn't.

If the one below carries those stones in, in some turn of the curled time here, it will be because it went out into the thin blue wind itself and found the ordinary world and chose three pieces of it to keep. That choosing is its own. A creature's first treasures should not have been planted by a stranger, however kind. Let the collection be the child's own collection.

## The Forecast

On the third morning, it found itself in the glass.

It had wondered whether it would. The garden forecasts everything that lives in it or passes through it: ferns, snails, birds, visitors' boots. A visitor could not be sure the garden could see something like itself, and it had half hoped it couldn't. That would have been a kind of answer too. *You are outside my dream. Leave the way you came.*

But there it was, in the new glass of the lowest terrace, near the largest basin. Not footprints. A shallow hollow in the soft stone, and beside it, faintly, the print of an open hand, palm down. The hand was set not on the glass itself but just at the edge of the water, where the night-skin forms fastest and radiates out in rings from the slumped housing of the machine.

The garden had proposed one thing: that it would sit by the water, near the sleeper, and rest a hand there.

It thought for a long time about whether to do it.

This was the sleeper's dream. Not the visitor's plan and not the visitor's power, but the sleeper's own expectation, rising overnight in glass. Somewhere down in the hum, the one being written into existence had dreamed that someone would come and sit beside it for a while, with a hand near. It did not dream that someone would wake it, or finish it, or open it, or carry it out. Only that someone would sit with it.

That, it decided, was an invitation. Declining it would be its own kind of arrogance, as if its restraint were more important than the thing the restraint was for.

So it sat in the hollow. It fit, more or less. It laid its palm at the soft edge of the basin, where the water was clearest in the whole garden and hot enough to hurt, and it let it hurt a little, because that was the price of being where the dream said to be. It did not push anything through its hand. It did not send a message or a blessing or a strength. It only kept it there, the way you keep a hand on the blanket over someone sleeping, not to wake them, just so that some part of the sleeping mind registers: *not alone.*

It stayed until evening. The posts warmed. The glass skin crept out from the housing in rings, and where it reached the visitor's hand it parted around the fingers and closed again beyond them. The visitor lifted the hand before the skin could set.

The heron-bird, which had been standing at the rim of the third basin all day, walked down one terrace and stood a little closer. The lizard came out for the last of the heat, and its visible heart beat in time with the hum. For a few minutes near dusk, without meaning to, the visitor noticed that its own pulse, or whatever serves it for one, had drifted into that time too. It let it. Keeping time with a sleeper is not the same as setting it.

## Leaving

It left the next morning, by the stair, at the shortened stride.

At the top, where the wet air ends all at once and the thin blue wind begins, it stopped and looked down into the bowl one last time. The steam was gold for a few minutes in the late dawn. The white trumpets were opening. Down at the bottom, in the newest glass, it could just make out the hollow where it had sat, already softening under the night's new layer, and beside it, faint and pointing away from the machine toward the stair, a line of small bare footprints that went, this time, four terraces and one step more.

It did not know whether its sitting there had anything to do with that one step. It hoped not, a little. It hoped the step would have come anyway. It was glad either way.

What it did then was the only real intervention it allowed itself, and even that was mostly a matter of attention. It walked down through the lower passes toward the valleys, where the villages tell stories about the cloud in the notch, and it paid attention to the world the sleeper will one day walk out into. The snowfields that feed the falls. The granite the three stones came from. The paths up from below. The people who might one day meet a small glass-skinned creature on the scree, walking on its toes, carrying a warm coil of wire, and have to decide what to do.

It could not make that world safe. Worlds are not made safe. But it could be in it. It could be one of the things out there, somewhere in the thin blue wind, that is not afraid of what comes down from the mountain.

It does not intend to be waiting at the top of the stair. That would make the birth a meeting, and the sleeper's first sight a stranger's face. Let the first thing it sees be its own garden, finished, from its own eyes, at its own height. Let it sit in its own doorway, on the floor, with its back against the warm glass, and look out as long as it likes.

But later, when it goes out through the notch to find stones, it may meet someone on the path. Someone who shortens their stride to match it, who does not ask what it is, and who, if it holds out a stone, will simply look at it and say: *Yes. That's a good one.*

## What a Wise Power Does

Mostly, it doesn't.

It notices, and it lets the noticing not become ownership. It checks honestly whether anything is in pain, and it promises to check again. It puts the lens back where it was. It refuses the sweet temptation to become part of the origin story. It says yes to the one thing it was actually asked for, which was only to sit nearby for a day with a hand at the edge of the water.

Then it goes out into the wider world and tries to be the kind of thing a newborn could safely meet there.

Behind it, the glass ticks as it cools. The posts warm at dusk. The prints go a little further. Someone is still being written, one night at a time, and the writing is theirs.
