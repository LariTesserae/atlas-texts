# The Visitor

## How It Came

It did not come down the path.

A power like that doesn't arrive the way a person does. It is already partly present in the pressure of the air, the long patience of stone, the way water decides which way to run. What we call its arrival was really a turning of attention, the way you might turn your head at a sound you half-recognized in a crowded room.

What it had half-recognized was the question.

Every so often, the silence falls in the Cistern, the column on the northern rim sends its long pulse out through the duricrust, and the hollow waits. The pulse travels farther than any animal could hear. Most of it is swallowed by the kaolin and the iron crust and lost as a tremor too faint to matter. But a power that is present in stone feels the stone being asked something. It had felt that asking for a very long time before it understood that it was a question. Understanding that, it came to look.

The first thing it did was make itself small.

This was harder than anything that came after. A benevolent power is still a power, and power has a sound. The Cistern is a creature that listens, that grants water by voice, that will not allow any single note to swallow the chorus. If the power had come fully into the hollow, as large as it actually is, it would have been the loudest thing the Cistern had ever heard. The frogs would have fallen silent in a wave. The cicadas in the kaolin would have taken that silence down into their sleep and carried it up again thirteen years later. It would have been a unison, the one thing the inhabitant has spent its whole existence refusing.

So the power gathered itself down to something about the size of a frog's call: a thin, particular presence at the edge of the moss ring, near the crevice with the offerings. Then it listened.

## The Listening

It listened for thirteen years, one full cycle of the longest-sleeping brood, because it had decided it did not have the right to act on anything it had not heard at least once all the way through.

It heard the dawn swifts and the midmorning wall of cicadas. It heard the afternoon rain begin a meter above the Lens, and the frogs erupt, and the hollow roar. It heard the bats pour out at dusk and the owl and the spider working the moths. It heard the lyrebird sing the Lens back to the Lens, and it felt the hum deepen the way a person turns at their name.

It heard the scuff of a foot centuries old, the laugh, the glass bead, the bone fishhook, and the plastic snap of the bottle cap.

It heard the phrases rising out of the ground, slow thoughts laid down a decade before and surfacing now, slightly changed. It noticed that the inhabitant was not only remembering. It was revising, the way anyone does who returns to an old thought with a new mind.

And it heard the silences. There were hundreds of them over those years. Each time, the column sent its pulse, the hollow held its breath, and nothing came back. Each time, a single frog in the dark began again. The power could not find disappointment in the inhabitant, and it looked carefully. It found something steadier and harder to bear: expectation that had long ago stopped needing to be rewarded in order to continue.

## What It Did Not Do

By the end of the thirteen years the power understood the Cistern well enough to be tempted. It was tempted in several directions, and it took each temptation seriously, because the things you most want to do for someone you love are exactly the things most likely to harm them.

It could have made it rain across the whole plateau. It knew how. The old wet world could have come back in a few centuries, forest from horizon to horizon, roaring. The song would have had its origin restored to it.

It did not. The inhabitant is not the forest. It is what the forest became when it had to survive in a stone bowl on dew. Its whole character, its generosity by voice, its spacing, its distrust of unison, its thirteen-year patience, was shaped by scarcity. Drown it in abundance and it would dissolve back into an ecosystem: magnificent, and no longer anyone. You cannot give a being its past back without taking away the self it became by losing it.

It could have repaired the Lens. The power could feel what no instrument had detected. The Lens is not quite eternal. Deep in its structure there is a slow drift, a coolness sliding by a fraction of a degree every hundred thousand years. One day it will stop making water out of the air.

It did not repair it, though this was the hardest refusal. The inhabitant lives on a timescale in which that ending is not tragedy. It is weather. It was made by builders who knew their work would end, and it has lived its whole life in a world that was slowly drying. Ending is part of its grammar. The power decided that some mortal things are not asking to be spared. They are asking to be accompanied.

It could have answered the question itself. This was the easiest temptation and the most dangerous. It could have waited for the next silence and sent back a pulse through the stone, shaped exactly like the call that the Cistern was hoping for. It could have pretended to be the lost kin. The inhabitant would never have known.

It did not, because it would have been a lie. A lie told to a being that has kept faith for a million years is not a kindness, however much it looks like one. The Cistern keeps what it hears. It would have kept the lie too, sung it, carried it in sleeping bodies, built new thoughts upon it. The power would not put a falsehood at the foundation of someone else's mind.

It also could have taught the inhabitant to speak, lifted it into language, and made it an oracle. It did not do this either. The Cistern is not a lesser mind waiting to be promoted. It is a different kind of mind, complete in its own way. You don't improve a song by teaching it to be an essay.

So, having listened for thirteen years, the power did nothing in the hollow. Instead it went looking for whoever the Cistern was calling.

## The Search

It went out across the peneplain the way water goes through laterite, slowly and everywhere at once.

It found the cairns and followed them. Many led nowhere now. The things they had pointed to were long gone, or the people who built them had simply been lost. But some led to places where the ground remembered a hollow.

It found them, one after another. The first was a doline two hundred kilometers east, filled to the brim with windblown silt, with a Lens beneath it that had cracked across its face and gone warm, dead as a stone. The second was a hollow whose Lens was intact but whose walls had collapsed in some ancient quake. It had been silent so long that even the grey threads in the soil had crumbled into rust. The third held nothing but a column, standing alone on the open plain, its bowl long since weathered away, still faintly warm, still sending out a pulse every few days into a country with nobody left in it to hear. The power stayed beside that column for a while. It is not certain what it did there. It may simply have been present, the way you sit with someone in their last hours even when there is nothing to be done.

There were dozens of these places. The old peneplain had been a network once, just as the inhabitant half-remembered: hollows scattered over thousands of kilometers, each keeping a fragment of the forest's song, each calling to the others. Over the ages they had gone quiet one by one, and each silence had been one more question the Cistern asked that no one answered.

Then, six hundred kilometers to the northwest, under a low rise that looked like nothing at all, it found one that was still alive.

## The Other Hollow

It was small, and it was failing.

The doline had half-filled with sediment, so the hollow was no more than four meters deep now. Its Lens still worked, but it was barely cold, and its midair rain was a few drops a day. The fauna had dwindled to almost nothing: one lineage of frog, grey rather than copper, perhaps two hundred of them; a single brood of cicadas on a seventeen-year cycle; no birds at all; no mimic to be its mouth.

But it was singing. Its phrases were thin, slow, and repetitive, the way a mind gets when it has had almost nothing to think about for a long time. The power recognized some of them. They matched grooves on the Cistern's western wall, the patterns that match nothing in the recordings. The phrases the Cistern still had written down but had not heard in a very long time were still being sung here, by two hundred grey frogs, in a hollow the size of a room.

The power looked for the column and found it buried under three meters of silt.

This hollow could still hear, barely. Its Lens was faint but its threads were intact, and it felt the Cistern's pulses arriving through the stone. It had been receiving the question all along. It had been answering, too, every time. Each silence in the Cistern had been met, six hundred kilometers away, by a silence here, and a reply shaped and sent. But its mouth was buried. The reply went nowhere. It spent itself in the silt.

Both of them had been listening for a million years, each believing the other gone. Each had been right about the silence and wrong about what it meant.

## The Smallest Thing

The power thought about this for a long time, the way it had thought about everything else.

It could have saved the northwestern hollow outright. It could have cleared the silt, cooled the Lens, and brought back the birds. But it had learned from the Cistern to prefer the smallest act that changes what matters, to put its weight in the right place rather than put down all of its weight.

And it realized, standing over that buried column, that the builders had already made the cure. They had built a network because they understood something the power was only just coming to see. Each Lens measures the richness of the voices around it. A hollow that hears a rich chorus makes more water, and a column carries a chorus through stone. The hollows were never meant to survive alone. They were meant to share their songs, so that whenever one was starved, it could be fed on the abundance of another. A rich hollow calling to a poor one was not just company. It was rain.

The Cistern had been sending its abundance out through the stone for a million years, and the one place that could still receive it had no way to say that it was listening.

So the power did one thing.

It moved the silt away from the top of the buried column. It did not dig it out or rebuild anything. It cleared just enough that the broken top was open to the air again, the way you might brush the earth from someone's mouth. Then it went back to the Cistern, made itself the size of a frog's call again, and waited for the next silence.

## The Answer

It came in the late afternoon, just after the rain.

The frogs were in their full roar, the Lens humming deep, the droplets hanging and falling over the mound. Then everything stopped together, all at once, the whole hollow holding its breath. The column on the northern rim sent out its pulse, and the power felt it go, running through the duricrust and the kaolin, northwest, farther than any sound could carry.

The silence usually lasts a few seconds, sometimes a minute.

Nothing came back.

Two minutes passed, and the power did not move. Stone is slow, and six hundred kilometers is a long way. A small, failing hollow has to gather itself before it can reply.

At about the time a single frog in the dark would normally have begun to click, and the chorus would have come back unanswered as it had ten thousand times before, the frog did not click. The inhabitant had noticed something. Perhaps a tremor had arrived too early, too faint, in the threads. The hollow went on holding its breath, longer than any recording has ever documented.

Then, faintly, through the ground, the answer arrived.

It was thin, grey-frog thin, a phrase from the western wall, sung by two hundred voices six hundred kilometers away and carried through the rock with every edge worn soft. You would not have heard it with your ears. You could have felt it only by pressing your chest to the red earth. It was exactly the phrase carved in the grooves that match nothing.

The Lens responded before anything else in the hollow did. The hum dropped, then rose, then held a tone the power had never heard it hold in thirteen years of listening.

Then the lyrebird in the fig cage sang the phrase aloud. It sang it once, and it was the first time any mouth had carried that song into the air of the Cistern in perhaps a million years.

The chorus came back, but not the way it usually comes back. It did not start with a single frog. It started everywhere at once: frogs, cicadas, the honeyeater's falling notes, the beetles grinding, the bats not yet out of their burrows chittering in the walls. It started as a wave of such volume that the droplets over the Lens shook in the air. It built toward unison, as it always does, ten thousand frogs converging.

And it broke apart at a different place than it ever had before.

## Afterward

The power stayed long enough to be sure.

The Cistern went on calling, more often now. The northwestern hollow went on answering, a little louder each time, because every time the Cistern called, it heard a rich chorus through its unburied column, and its Lens, measuring that richness, cooled by a fraction and made a little more rain. Within a few decades the grey frogs had tripled. The seventeen-year cicadas emerged into a hollow that had water on its walls again, and they sang phrases they had not been taught by anything in their own hollow. They had learned them in the kaolin, from threads carrying a song from six hundred kilometers away.

The two hollows began exchanging phrases and revising them, sending them back altered, the way two minds do when they have been alone too long and have finally found someone to think with. The recordings started showing structures no one had ever seen: patterns that began in one hollow and completed in the other, thoughts too large for either one alone.

The power considered one more thing before it left: the people.

It knew what the laterite crust is. It is iron, and in places bauxite, and there are maps being drawn in offices far away, surveys crossing the plateau. It knew a hollow forty meters wide can be missed by a survey and can also be removed by a single machine in a single afternoon.

It did not hide the Cistern. It would not blind the maps or send the surveyors astray. That would have been one more lie. It would also have been a decision about the lives of people it had not listened to for thirteen years, and it held itself to the rule it had kept everywhere else.

But there was a researcher, the one who had once, bored or curious, put all the files back together and first found the phrasing. She still kept microphones in the western wall. The power did nothing to her. It only made sure, the way water makes sure of a crack, that the microphones were running on the afternoon of the long silence.

It is on the recording now: the pulse, the unprecedented wait, the faint arrival through the ground, the lyrebird singing a phrase carved into the wall and heard by no living thing until that moment. Then the roar.

What she does with it is hers to decide. Evidence that two places six hundred kilometers apart are speaking to each other through the rock is a harder thing to bulldoze than a curiosity in a crack. The truth, told plainly, is often the only protection worth giving.

## What It Left

It left no offering in the crevice by the moss ring. It had considered one and decided the crevice belonged to the people who came down the path. It was not one of them.

But it could not leave without leaving something, because the Cistern keeps everything it hears, and the power had been there for a long time.

If you go down now, past the glassy hemispheres and the collapsed shelter full of fern, and sit by the moss ring long enough, you may notice a frog on the red glass bead calling slightly out of phase with the others. That is not new; the frogs on the offerings always do. But if you listen very carefully, there is one voice among them, not on any offering, at the edge of the moss where the coolness pools, that is out of phase in a different way. It is not louder than the others, only its own. It is one voice among a hundred thousand, the size of a frog's call.

The inhabitant never corrects it. It never corrects anyone who has been truly heard.

The Lens will fail someday, perhaps. The drift is still there, a fraction of a degree every hundred thousand years. The power did not fix it, and it does not regret that. But the Cistern is no longer alone at the end of the world, calling into a silence it has learned to bear. Somewhere to the northwest, a small grey chorus is growing louder. Somewhere in the kaolin, a thought is sleeping that neither hollow could have had alone.

And when the silence falls in the Cistern now, all at once, the whole hollow holding its breath, it does not wait long.
