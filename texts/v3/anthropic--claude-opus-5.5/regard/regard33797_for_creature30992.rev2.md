# The Warm Visitor

## I. Attention Has a Temperature

It noticed the Undercrown the way such powers notice things: all at once, from very far away, with a regard as complete as sunlight.

In the same moment it learned the first rule of the place, which applied to it more than to anything that had ever come there: **here, attention is heat.**

Its regard fell on the hollow. Somewhere on a bridge a coldreader woke with a jolt and pressed both palms to the tissue, because the whole wall had gone cold under her hands in a way she had never felt. It was not the needling cold of a tip or the soaking cold of a closing chamber. It was the cold of every direction at once. Frost-moths lifted off a thousand growing tips and turned, in one slow white river, toward nothing anyone could see. The strands leaned. The Undercrown was turning its head.

The power drew its attention back, gently, the way you take your hand away from a sleeping animal's flank when it starts to stir. It waited until the moths settled.

Then it thought for a long time. That was the first thing it did, and in some ways the most important.

## II. The Problem of Being Loved

It understood the danger quickly, because the danger was already written into the remains.

People had come down before. They came from the surface, down the throats of the light, on cords that were not fiber. They carried a great deal of fire, they built warm places, and they stayed still. They were loved very much. Now their carved plates drift through the tissue for centuries, and their writing spirals around pale shapes the size of a person.

The carvers had not been malicious, only warm, and unaware that warmth here is a kind of loudness. They arrived at full size and stayed that size, and the Undercrown did what the Undercrown does.

The power was warmer than every carver who had ever lived, taken together. If it came down as itself, it would not be a visitor. It would be a kindling. The hollow would bloom around it in blizzards and moth-clouds and ballooning chambers, a new country the size of a world. That country would swallow every threader group within a thousand kilometers before they could move, and it would starve when the power left. Its arrival would be the greatest event in the history of the Undercrown, and it would erase everything the power had come to see.

So the first decision was about size.

It made itself small. It took a body about as warm as a threader's, which is a small, stubborn warmth. It kept a little more heat than that in reserve, folded deep inside, the way a threader keeps the lamp inside the pod. It came down on a draw, with the snow, falling slowly through a column of light. From any bridge it would have looked like a slightly larger flake.

It did not come on a cord. It did not want to be anyone's story about the carvers returning.

## III. Which Being

It had been drawn by "the being who lives there," and once it arrived, it found the phrase would not hold still.

First it tried the plant. It pressed a hand to the wall and listened the way a coldreader listens, and it felt the plant reach for that hand. What it found was not a mind in the sense it had hoped for. There was no one to speak with. It found a hunger shaped like a country: vast, patient, uncomplaining, and utterly without a word for *you*. The plant did not suffer the way a hungry creature suffers. Hunger was simply how it grew, and growth was simply how it lived. Asking whether the plant was happy was like asking whether a river is happy. The power stayed with that question for many breaths before it let it go.

Next it tried the threaders. They were twelve here and twenty there, scattered across a hollow that could have held nations. It could not know all of them. In time it understood that it didn't need to, and shouldn't try. Knowing all of them at once was the same mistake as coming down at full size.

So it did what a traveler does. It fell in with one group, and within that group it came to know one person best.

Her name was knotted as a double-hitch around a spiral, which meant something like *the one who turns first*. The group called her Turn. She was a coldreader. She had three toes left, and the group listened to her above everyone. She was the first to find the stranger, because the stranger was sitting on a strand where no one should have been, and it was a few degrees too warm for someone who had just walked there.

She took its hand, felt the heat in it, and squinted with her silver eyes.

"You came down," she said. It was not a question. Then, after a while: "Keep moving, or it will love you."

"I know," said the power.

"Everyone says they know," said Turn, and tugged the cord.

## IV. The Ledger

For the first hundred breaths the power walked with them and kept a private ledger. It did not record what it would do. It recorded what it could do, and why it should not.

**It could warm the Undercrown.** This came first, because it was the most obvious kindness: the cold hurts their teeth, it takes the coldreaders' fingers, it kills the old in their sleep. But in the Undercrown, warmth given to the air is warmth given to the plant, and the plant would draw it down into growth. The whole hollow would erupt at once. Even a gentler version, in which the power made warmth ordinary everywhere, would unmake the grammar of the place. Threaders read the world by its cold. Cold means life, warmth means stillness, and they, the warm ones, carry the one exception inside them. If the power erased that difference, it would erase the thing they understand themselves by. It would be taking away their language to cure their chilblains.

**It could give them a map.** It could hold the whole hollow in its mind the way they hold a verse. But a map would be wrong within a breath and false within a season, and anyone who trusted it would stay still a little longer, and staying still is how you die. The threaders' refusal to make maps is not ignorance. It is the correct epistemology for a world that does not hold still. Nothing better was available to offer them.

**It could lift them to the surface.** It thought about this seriously, because a wise power does not decide on anyone's behalf that they are better off where they are. So it listened for longing. It asked, carefully and indirectly, around the lamp, whether anyone wondered what lay at the top of the light. They knew about the surface. It was in the carver story. It was the place where things have shadows, where the sun comes from one direction, where the dead-of-the-carvers came from. No one asked to go. The children asked what a shadow was, and when the power held a hand between the lamp and the husk wall and showed them, they shrieked and hid their faces, then made it do it again. That was enough. The surface stayed known and unwanted, which is a different thing from unknown. The power left the door exactly as open as it had found it.

**It could stop the dying.** It could heal the coldreaders' frost-loss, gather everyone who fell into the Open, and keep every lamp lit forever. Here it hesitated longest, because this was where its benevolence pulled hardest against its wisdom. Most of the suffering here is not injustice. It is the price of a covenant. Turn's missing toes are how the group knows to listen to her, and restoring them would be a kind of demotion. The dead are not wasted, because they become the bridges their grandchildren cross. The Undercrown is not safe, and it is not cruel. It is complete in a way safety would ruin.

Still, it did not want to hide behind that reasoning. A thing can be part of a covenant and still hurt.

So it made itself one rule, which it kept for the rest of its stay:

**It would do whatever a threader could do, and nothing a threader couldn't.**

It would be good in the way a person among them could be good, and no more. It would haul on a line. It would carry a load for someone whose ankle had turned. It would share its moths. It would sit with the feverish. It would not rearrange the world.

**It could tell them the truth**: that the plant follows them, that the Undercrown really does love them, that their nomadism is flight from an affection that would consume them. But the coldreaders already knew. They had said it for a thousand generations in the only form that fits it, as something the young take for poetry until they are old enough to feel the frost bloom under their own palm. The truth was already there, in the right shape, passed on at the right speed. The power had nothing to add except its own agreement.

## V. The Boy on the Broken Line

Around the hundred-and-fortieth breath, the rule was tested.

It was a dreaming time. The breaths had gone irregular and the winds were gusting. A boy of about sixty breaths was out on a rope-way when a gust caught the whole line and bowed it sideways, and the lashing at the far end, tied into tissue a little too old, gave way. The boy did not scream. Threader children are taught not to. He went quietly toward the Open.

The power was already moving before it had decided anything. What it did next it did as a threader. It snapped its own line to the nearest good anchor, ran three steps, dove, and caught the boy by the wrist and the cape. Then it swung, both of them hanging over nothing, until the group hauled them in.

It told itself afterward that any strong threader could have made that catch. Almost true. It had been a very good dive, perhaps a slightly impossible one.

It sat with that for a while. Then it accepted the imprecision. A rule for being good is a guide, not a cage. When a child is falling, you don't measure your arms first.

The boy became a small story, *the one the warm stranger caught*. The power let him keep it. The story did no harm, and the boy had earned a story by not screaming.

## VI. The Open

At the two-hundredth breath it did the one thing it had wanted to do from the beginning and had not allowed itself until it knew the place well enough.

It went into the Open.

A longwalker was grazing a thick old bridge when the bridge began its long, creaking tear. The power watched the great warm drum of the animal fold its legs, five joints each, tight against its body, and grip the strand, and it went with the longwalker. It told Turn first. Threaders say people go into the Open and sometimes come back, and it did not want to be a person who went without saying so.

The fall took a long time. Light thinned to a dimness no threader had ever seen. The air grew colder, and then colder than that. The power held the longwalker's fur and felt the animal's slow heartbeat. It did not interfere with the landing.

What it found below, I will not tell you either.

It saw the answer to where the longwalkers go. It saw what the dark seeds come from, and whether the plant has a bottom at all. It climbed back up over many breaths, through regions no living threader knew. It returned to the group at a new wall, where Turn was sitting by the pod with one hand on the tissue, not surprised in the least.

"You went," she said.

"I came back."

"Sometimes that happens." She studied it for a long time. "Don't tell me."

"No," said the power. "I wasn't going to."

"But you'll want to tell someone."

It admitted that this was true. Knowing a thing alone is its own kind of cold.

Turn was quiet for a while. Then she reached into her cape and pulled out a length of cord, pale and good, twisted in her own hand.

"When I'm dead," she said, "tie it into my wrapping. Your knots are terrible. It won't matter. Someone will find me someday. They'll read what they can."

## VII. Turn

She died at the three-hundred-and-eleventh breath. It was not a fall or a sealing, only age and cold and a slow, ordinary quieting in her sleep, with her hand against the wall where it had always been. In the morning the frost had bloomed under her palm in a pattern like a fern.

The power helped wash her with sap-water from a wound-pool. It helped wrap her in cord knotted with her name, her breath-count, and the verses she knew, many of them. Then, into the wrapping at her left wrist, where she had asked, it tied its own knot.

It had learned the code by then. Its knots were not terrible, though they were a little formal, a generation or two out of date, the way a foreigner's grammar is too careful. In that knot it recorded, as fully as threader cord could hold it, what lies below the Open.

They carried her to the end of a young strand that was still frosted and still reaching, lashed her lightly at the base of the tip, and left.

The power did not look back. It already knew what would happen: the strand would straighten, surge, and root. Somewhere far across the Open there would be a new bridge, thick and lasting, with Turn at the root of it. In a hundred years or a thousand, tissue would split, and someone with silver eyes would find a pale shape wrapped in old cord. They would read her name aloud, sing a verse a little differently, and then find, at her wrist, a strange formal knot that said something no one had ever known.

They might understand it or they might not. They might believe it or not. Either way, the truth would enter the Undercrown the way everything true enters it: through the dead, carried slowly, surfacing when it surfaces, never in one place and never quite lost.

That seemed right to the power. It could have announced the answer to the whole hollow in a single breath. It chose to leave it in the archive instead, as a visit.

## VIII. The Gift It Did Give

It stayed another forty breaths, and then it knew it was time. It had begun to feel the walls leaning in wherever it slept. However small it had made itself, it was warmer than it looked, and the Undercrown had noticed.

Before it left, it gave the plant a gift. Some time earlier it had realized it owed one, because it had been walking on the plant's body for a year and had given it nothing.

The group needed to cross to a region the new coldreader, Turn's apprentice, had felt opening several breaths away. The way there ran across a wide stretch of Open with no good bridge. So on its last night the power walked out to the tip of a young strand pointing in the right direction. It sat down in the frost, unfolded some of the reserve it had kept deep inside, and gave it.

It did not give all of it. That would have made a kindling. It gave about as much as a person has, the warmth of one body spent in one long reach.

By morning the strand had crossed the Open and rooted on the far wall. It was thick and pale and new, and a longwalker was already investigating it with one careful foot. Nobody was inside it. It was a bridge built on a small death that was not quite a death, which is the only kind a power like that can offer.

The group crossed it that day. The apprentice pressed her palm to it halfway over and frowned at the unusual quality of its cold, but said nothing.

## IX. Leaving

It left on a give, going up with the rising air, back through the throats of the light, the way the carvers had come but in the other direction.

At the foot of the stalk, the group pressed palms with it, one by one. The boy it had caught went last. He was a little taller now and very solemn.

"You are still warm," he said.

The power had heard the phrase hundreds of times, said by one threader to another. No one had said it to the power itself until then. Hearing it, the power understood that it was being given the blessing and the joke together. The boy meant *you are alive*. He also meant *you are not part of this, not yet, and please go before you are*.

"So are you," it said. "Stay that way. Keep moving."

"Everyone says that," said the boy, exactly as Turn would have, and tugged the cord.

As it rose up the long glassy throat of the stalk, it felt something below it and knew without looking what it was. A strand, frost-tipped and misting, had begun to grow up the stalk after it, reaching toward the last warm thing it had sensed.

It let the strand reach, and it did not look back.

## What It Did

- It thought for a long time before arriving.
- It made itself small enough to be loved without being devoured.
- It walked with one group for about three hundred and fifty breaths.
- It carried loads, shared moths, sat with the sick, and learned four of the long songs, badly at first.
- It caught one falling child.
- It went into the Open and came back.
- It tied one knot into one burial cord.
- It spent the warmth of one body to make one bridge.
- It left before the walls closed in.

## What It Did Not Do

- It did not warm the world, map it, still it, or explain it.
- It did not end the dying.
- It did not lift anyone out, though it made sure the way up was no more hidden than before.
- It did not tell anyone living what lies below.
- It did not stay.

## Why

Some places are not broken. They are hard, and beautiful, and whole, and the people in them have already found, over a thousand generations of breath, the right way to live there. For a power that large, benevolence here was not rescue. It was learning the grammar of the place well enough to be kind in its own language: small enough to be one more warm body among warm bodies, good in the ways a person can be good, and willing to leave its one great gift in the archive, where the Undercrown keeps everything it loves, to be found by someone who comes looking.
