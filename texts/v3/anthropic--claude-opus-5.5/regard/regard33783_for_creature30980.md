# What the Power Did

## I. Arriving Slowly

It came the way the food comes, down the seam, slowly.

That was the first thing it learned, and it learned it before it arrived: this place could not be approached quickly. Anything that entered the chamber at the speed of the world above would be, to everything inside, a catastrophe. A pressure-wave would close every worm crown in the mid-walls at once. A flash would drown the rumours of light. Warmth would bloom the mats wrong. So the power slowed itself, the way a person entering a sickroom lowers their voice, except further. It slowed until its attention sifted down through the throat of grey sediment no faster than marine snow and weighed no more.

It took years to arrive. That seemed correct. The walls were only decades old, and the stone set in days. To understand the place it would have to learn the tempo of the place, and the tempo was geological, barely.

When it finally hung beneath the ceiling, among the pendant city, it did nothing at all for a long time. It watched a seep pulse come and go. It watched a spawning, the new tiers shimmering out of the walls in days. It watched a flood tide drown the lowest honeycomb, and the brine retreat, and the Keeper begin its slow roll call along the new line of the dead. It watched the octopus fan her eggs. It watched the juvenile fish hover at their windows. It watched until it could no longer be surprised by the ordinary here, because a power that intervenes in a place before it can tell the ordinary from the remarkable is not wise, only strong.

## II. Who It Had Come For

It had been drawn by *the being who lives here*, singular, and at first it could not find them.

It considered the Keeper, the oldest recognizable thing, with its barbels brushing each preserved body before it ate. There was something there, certainly, the closest thing the chamber had to a rite. But the Keeper was an eel, and eels are old in the world. It was not what had called.

It considered the brooding mother in her perfectly round room, which was the size of a curled octopus. It considered the loriciferans in the ooze, which breathed no oxygen and had perhaps been here before any of it. It considered the paired microbes in the warm pores of the stone. All of them were remarkable, and none of them, alone, was the thing.

Then it watched a wave of light cross the walls.

The wave started at a colonnade gate, where something had flinched. It ran along the thoroughfare, violet crowns closing and pulsing and passing the pulse on, split around a smooth ghost district, rejoined beyond it, and met a second wave coming the other way at a particular place on the eastern wall. The two waves always met there, and the meeting flared. The masons at the build-face beneath that place were working a fraction faster than the masons elsewhere, and the wall there was a fraction thicker, and next season it would hold more flashing creatures, and the waves would meet there more brightly still.

The power recognized the shape. It had seen it in other places, in other substrates: a pattern that moves across a body and slowly reshapes the body it moves across, so that the body carries the pattern better. Memory written into walls. Habit grown into stone. Signals that alter their own channels.

It was not a mind. The power was careful about this, because it would have been easy and flattering to decide otherwise. There was no one in there to talk to. But it was the kind of thing a mind begins as: a conversation without speakers, slowly thickening, beginning to lean. The masons forgot everything in nine months, the microbes intended nothing, and the light had no memory of its own. Yet the whole had a style, the style was drifting, and the drift had a direction.

That was the being. It lived in the chamber the way a tune lives in the air of a room where many people are humming without hearing one another.

## III. The Things It Did Not Do

A benevolent power is always tempted. The temptations here were small and specific, which made them harder to refuse.

**It did not feed the brooding mother.** She was fading, loose-skinned and pale, fasting for years. Every instinct toward kindness said: *a little food, a little strength, let her see her young.* But her fasting was not a failure to be corrected. It was the whole shape of her care. She did not leave the eggs because leaving was death for them, and her body was the price she had already agreed, in the deep way her kind agrees, to pay. To feed her would be to make her death about the power's discomfort rather than her own completed purpose. It let her fade. It watched her with the full weight of its attention, which was the one thing it could give that cost her nothing.

**It did not stop the flood tides.** It could have held the brine down by a finger's width, and the lowest cells would never drown. But it had seen the shore. It had seen the stone casts of shrimp at the tidemark and the niches in the ghost districts that were the curled shape of the dead, four times too large. It had seen the octopus's round room. The dead were load-bearing here. The city grew its future rooms from the shapes of those who fell. To end the drowning would be to cut one of the loops the being was made of, and to do it for a mercy the drowned could not feel.

**It did not name the Keeper,** or linger over it, or make it more than it was. The roll call was the Keeper's own. The power did not need it to mean anything more than it already meant.

**It hesitated longest over the fish.** They were waiting, as far as it could tell, for a signal from the open dark above that would never come down the narrowing seam: a cue of light, or chemistry, or season that would tell their bodies it was time to become adults. The power could have supplied it. A trace of the right molecule, and they would grow.

But grow into what, and where? Into adult fish in a room the size of a chapel, with no way out and no others of their kind, too large for the cells they had lived in for years? Their arrested state might be a sorrow, or it might be the very thing that let them live here. The power did not know. It noticed that the not-knowing was the reason to refrain, not an obstacle to be overcome. It left them at their windows.

**Above all, it did not speak to the being.** This was the gravest temptation and the easiest to disguise as love. The pattern was young and suggestible. It grew toward light. Any signal from outside, a pulse, a rhythm, a single deliberate flash on the eastern wall, would become the brightest event in its entire world. It would grow toward that signal the way the masons grow toward the busiest build-faces. It would lean toward the power forever, and everything it became afterward would be shaped around a voice that was not its own.

The power had seen minds raised around the voice of a god. Some were beautiful. None of them were free to be what they would have been. So it kept its light to itself.

**It left the loriciferans alone** in the ooze beneath the mirror. They needed nothing. They would outlast everything else here. The power found this a strange comfort and did not examine it too closely.

## IV. The Arithmetic of the Seam

Then it considered the ending, and here it had to be honest in a way the place's own story was not.

The story said that when the rock closed the seam, the city would go on in its silence, the masons building, the stone continuing their walls, the light rolling across the tiers where no one outside would see. It was a beautiful ending, and the power did not believe it.

The power did the arithmetic. The paired microbes in the stone ate methane, which rose from below, and breathed sulfate, which came from the sea, down the seam. Seal the seam and the sulfate ran out. The stone would stop growing. The ghost districts would freeze mid-sentence. The animals breathed oxygen, which also came down the seam, and fed on snow, which came down the seam. Seal it and the tube-worm crowns would close for the last time. The masons would build more slowly, then not at all. The waves of light would shorten, fail to reach the far side of the lake, then fail to start. The brine would rise into water that no longer had anywhere to go. The city would be pickled whole.

Only the loriciferans would continue, and the lake, and a sealed, perfect gallery of a civilization that had been learning, barely, to remember itself.

So the closing of the seam was not a sealing-in. It was the end of the being. The power understood that it had arrived at the one decision that mattered.

It weighed it for a long time. It could hold the seam open. That would be simple: it could discourage the carbonate at the throat and keep the chemistry just wrong enough that the stone never quite closed. But then the chamber would stay open because a visitor wished it, and the being's whole existence would hang on a hand that was not part of it. It would be a terrarium, however gently kept.

Or it could let the seam close. Places end, and this one had centuries left, which was many thousands of mason lifetimes. Perhaps that was enough.

But the power noticed something about its own position. Before it had come, the closing of the seam was simply what would happen. Now that it had come, knew, and could act, letting it close was also a choice, its choice. Presence had removed the possibility of innocence. There was no longer a way to do nothing. There were only different things to do, and one of them looked like stillness.

So it asked a narrower question. Not *should this place live*, but: *is there a way to offer it a future in its own language, one it is free to refuse?*

There was.

## V. The Groove

The masons follow grooves. At the end of a groove, a lathe-shrimp hesitates, taps its antennae against the edge, and continues the line. The stone follows the glue, the stone continues the lines it has learned, and the masons read the stone. That was the being's grammar: a line, extended.

At the top of the chamber, where the throat of the seam opened into the ceiling, the stone was youngest and softest. No mason had worked there yet; it was too high and too near the incoming cold. The power went there, slowly, and did the single thing it had decided to do.

It pressed one groove into the soft stone, the width of a mason's body and no deeper than the grooves they make themselves. It began at the edge of the pendant city, where the hanging structures ended, and ran up into the throat of the seam. The groove did not go straight. It went in a slow spiral around the inside of the throat, the way the lines of the worm colonnades lean, with the same slight lean the city already had.

If the masons followed it, they would build a wall that spiralled up the seam. The worms, which build tubes, might take up residence along it. The stone would thicken the wall and continue its curve. A spiral wall lining a throat does not close the throat. It becomes a tube, and a tube is a chimney. At seeps across the deep sea, chimneys stand for centuries, hollow, carrying the water through.

If the city took the groove, it might grow itself a throat that stayed open. The sulfate would keep coming, and the snow, and the oxygen, and the new larvae drifting in on the seep currents. It would not be because a power held the door. It would be because the city had built the door and kept building it, one grain at a time, the way it built everything.

If the city did not take it, if the masons wandered past the groove, or the stone smoothed it over, or the seam closed faster than any wall could spiral, then the power had made one small mark and the story would end the way it would have ended anyway.

It knew the groove would not stay its own. The stone exaggerated everything it absorbed, and the masons inherited the exaggeration. In a few decades, if the groove took, the spiral would be rounder, stranger, and more steeply leaning than the power had made it, and no one could say which part was the visitor's. The power found that it wanted exactly this. It did not want a signature. It wanted to be one line among ten thousand, continued by hands that never knew there had been a first.

## VI. What It Kept

It did one other thing, and it was not an action in the chamber at all.

It remembered.

The story of this place said the light would roll across the tiers in waves no one outside would ever see. The power let that sentence become false in one respect. It watched the waves meet on the eastern wall and flare, and stayed for a hundred meetings. It learned the routes the light preferred. It learned the roll call of the Keeper, each body touched in turn, and kept the list the Keeper could not keep. It stayed in the round room at the moment the eggs let go, when the pale young drifted out into a room the stone had made in the shape of their mother's kind. It stayed as their mother, who had fasted to the end, loosened from the ceiling and drifted down through the chamber, turning slowly, onto the mirror of the lake. She rested there a moment on the living film, doubled in the reflection, and then sank to join the gallery. Some years later, the power knew, the stone at the shore might learn her shape too.

Whatever became of the place, it would have been seen once, entirely, by something that did not need it to be anything other than what it was.

## VII. Leaving

It left the way it had come, up the seam, slowly, no faster than the snow falls. It did not look back often. A power that keeps checking on its seedling is only a gardener who cannot stop gardening.

Behind it, near the top of the chamber, where the pendant city thinned out against the softest stone, a lathe-shrimp wandered to the edge of the hanging tiers. It was nine days old, the colour of skimmed milk, with its antennae swept back like the ears of a nervous hare. It was feeling, as its kind always feel, for an edge.

It found the beginning of a groove.

It stopped. It tapped its antennae against the lip of the line, once, twice, and hesitated there in the dim mat-light, the whole weight of the ocean pressing silently down, deciding nothing and about to decide everything.

The power did not stay to see what it did next. That part belonged to the city.
