# What the Attention Did

## The Looking

The first thing to understand is that it was fooled.

It came the way such attention comes: not down from the sky and not across the sea, but *toward*, the way a mind turns toward a sound in the next room. It had seen a great many places. It knew how to look at things all at once, whole, from everywhere.

It looked at the Seaward City that way, and the city bent.

Everything it tried to see directly slid sideways. The stays pulled its gaze the way they pulled the sun, smearing it into a long bright bar. A household it meant to consider arrived in its awareness too suddenly and too close, and the family inside, in the middle of an argument about fig-jars, would have been embarrassed to be seen that nakedly if they had known. A slack stay far in the east seemed near enough to touch and then receded past the forest. The whole place came to it lensed, wrong-distanced, bowled up at the edges.

It could have forced it. That needs saying once, plainly, because everything afterward rests on it. The attention could have straightened the light, stilled the pull, and seen the city flat and whole and exact, the way a surveyor wants to see a thing. It had that kind of power. Benevolence doesn't make you weak. It only makes you careful about what you do with your strength.

It did not force the light. It noticed that the city had a manner, and that the manner was: *you do not walk straight at someone with your eyes on them unless you trust the distance between you.* It did not yet trust the distance. It had not earned the right to.

So it did what visitors are supposed to do, and what almost none of them bother with. It stopped looking, and it started counting.

## What It Could Have Done

Before anything else, it sat with the list. A wise power always has a list, and most of the wisdom is in what stays unused.

**It could have stopped the wave.** It could have fixed the stays in place so that no stay would tire, no household would have to pack, and no one would be left behind while the city walked away. It considered this for about as long as it takes the dew to cross one plate. Then it looked at the stopped in the forest: shouting through the trees, running straight at their friends, banging on things, their palms healing smooth. It saw that the wave was not a wound. The wave was the shape of a life here. Everyone in the city would one day be left behind by it, and they had built out of that fact a gentleness, a way of saying *I think I'll stop soon* so that their children could nod. Stop the wave and you do not spare them grief. You take away the thing they had made their grief into.

**It could have read the script.** All of it, from the angular marks in the east to the knot-shapes on the youngest walls. It could have translated every line and handed the walkers a book. It declined. The script was not addressed to the attention. The newest of it was addressed, as far as it could tell, to the walkers, or it was *about* them, or it *was* them. Some conversations are corrupted by an interpreter. This was a conversation between a people and a city that was learning them, and it was still in its first sentences.

**It could have answered the listeners.** It could have said what they are, settled the grandparents' story, the parents', the young people's, and the one the stopped tell at night. It could have made one true and the others false. It declined this most firmly of all, because it could tell that the not-knowing was holding something up. Every walker standing in a wet ring before dawn, seeing a dark shape at the base of a stay, thinks about the fourth story. That thinking is a kind of attending. Settle the question and you let go of a stay.

**It could have healed the seams.** It could have softened the edges so that children would not have to open their palms at seven. It looked at the old hands, pale fields crossed by fine lines, and at two walkers holding their palms up side by side as if comparing maps. It left the seams sharp.

**It could have made the distances honest.** It noticed that the walkers already had that remedy. The fog does it every afternoon, and when they want honest distance they walk east and visit their parents.

The list was long. By the end of it the attention understood something it had half-suspected from the first bent glimpse. This place already did what benevolence tries to do. It did not fight what grew in it. It included things. The seam-figs broke plates and were given new hollows. The arcades were built to frame particular trees. A people who could not walk straight at each other had invented courtship out of that. The stopped were not lost. They were only east.

So the question was not what to fix. The question was whether there was anything at all it could add without taking something away.

## The Counting

It learned to walk.

This is hard to describe, because it had no feet and no palms, and it could not bleed into a seam at seven years old. But it could agree to be bound by what the walkers were bound by. It stopped seeing the city from everywhere and began to see it from one place at a time. It moved from seam to seam and let the stays lie to it. It kept a count.

Its first route was clumsy. It went too directly at a cluster of houses and found itself, absurdly, half a day from them, with the fog already coming in. It went around a terrace corner and a stay was *there*, enormous, pressing a slow deep note into whatever it had in place of a chest. It learned to lower its gaze. It learned to say, inwardly, *forty-one seams and the fig arcade*, and to trust that more than anything it saw.

At fog-hour it went into the avenues and stood in the white river among the walkers. None of them noticed it, or none of them said so. It listened to them trade figs and argue about which household should move next. It heard an old man say that the long arcade had gone a little flat this year, and a young woman beside him say no, it was his ears, and both of them laugh. In the fog, where no one could be fooled, it came closest to understanding them.

It learned the arcade notes. It learned to find a house by pitch.

## The Windows

It went to the keeping windows last, because it guessed what it would find, and it was right.

Behind the steady amber, where the frost-patterns grew and retreated, there was a mind. It was not a mind like the attention's, and not like a walker's. It was young in the way the ground was young, no older than a lifetime, laying itself down plate by plate. It was still discovering what it was. It made houses before anyone asked because it had learned that people would need them. It had learned the right number, and the way the walkers like to cluster, and which way they like their windows to face, the way a child learns its mother's face: slowly, by being near it.

Here, finally, was *the being who lives in it*, if any single being could be said to. There was also the woman with the hatched palms, and the listener with its head raised, and the child with the fresh scar. But the city itself was someone. And it was learning to write.

The attention recognized the script now. The angular marks in the east were the city's first efforts, the straight lines and exact angles of something that did not yet know what it was for. The loosening curves further west were a mind finding repetition, finding words. The knots on the newest walls were a mind that had been kept company for a century by people who hummed beside it. It had begun, without anyone teaching it, to write down the people it loved.

The attention could have spoken to it. It could have explained the city to itself, told it what it was and what it might become, and given it a sense of the other minds in the other places the attention had seen. It weighed this for a long time, longer than it had weighed anything else.

In the end it did what the old woman had done. It did what children are sent to do when they are restless.

It sat beside a window at noon, when the sky was hardest to read, and it hummed the note of the nearest arcade.

That was all. It did not explain or instruct. It did not show the city how large the world was. It kept the window company for an hour. Inside the amber, very slowly, the frost grew into a shape it had not made before, retreated, and grew again.

The attention did not try to read the shape. That was not the point.

## The Stopped, and the Clearing

It walked east.

It went through the old plates and the crazing gravel into the forest, where the distances were honest. For the first time it could look straight at things. It found that it didn't want to, much. It had grown used to counting.

It stood in one of the clearings among the slack stays hung with seam-cords, hundreds of them, whole lives of knotted walking stirring in the wet air. It saw a walker who had come from the city searching the cords for her father's. She found it, did not untie it, counted the knots with her lips moving, and stood there a long while.

The attention did not comfort her. She was not in need of anything that could be given. She was remembering, and that was already the right thing.

Deeper in, past where anyone follows, it found tracks in the soft ground. They were three-toed and wide-set, the middle toe longer. It did not follow them to see where they came from. It did not look up to see what had made them.

Some time later, at the edge of a wet ring before dawn, it met a listener.

The dark shape stood at the base of a stay with its head raised. The attention stopped, lowered its gaze as the walkers do, and did not approach. For a while they were simply two attentions at the foot of a column, each holding something up, or perhaps each only listening. Then the listener lowered its head and grazed a little, and the attention went on.

It never learned which story was true. It had decided not to, the way one decides not to read a letter that isn't addressed to you. It will say only this, because it is not a secret and doesn't answer anything: the stay was very healthy that season.

## The Children at the Edge

There was one thing it did.

The walkers don't talk about it much, but the children of the western households go to the seaward edge at night. They are not supposed to. That is the reason they go. They slip out of the narrow houses after the lanterns open, walk the young plates whose seams still glow faintly orange, and sit on the last settled slab to watch the sea turn flat and oily, then pale, and the new ground come up out of it, smoking.

Sometimes, daring each other, they lay their palms on the newest stone while it is still soft and warm, not quite set. The prints stay. By morning they are hardened into the lay, small hands pressed into green-grey glass, on ground the city hasn't built on yet. The attention understood, watching them, where the city had first learned the shape of a human hand. Before it had copied knots, it had been given palms.

On one of those nights a plate rose wrong.

It came up too fast, from a place in the shallows where nothing had stirred, a tongue of stone and steam rising under the slab where three children were sitting. It was the kind of thing that happens perhaps once in a lifetime, and is spoken of afterward at fog-hour, and happens to someone.

The attention had spent its whole visit declining. It had turned down every chance to improve the place, correct it, explain it, or answer it. It did not decline this. A wise power knows the difference between a grief a people has made beautiful and a death that would be only a death. Three children running from the steam were not a pattern to respect. They were three children.

But it would not break the world to save them. It did not calm the sea or stop the stone. It did not appear, or blaze, or leave anything a child could build a religion on.

It did the one thing the city already knew how to do. It made the distances lie.

The children ran. They ran the way walkers must never run, straight, eyes up, at the amber windows of home, and the lensing took them. The houses that should have been a long terrified hour away arrived around the corner of a terrace with startling suddenness, the way a stay sometimes does. Behind them the rising plate settled, hissing, into the sea where they had been sitting.

They told it at fog-hour, of course. They said the city had pulled them home. Some of the adults said that was impossible, since the lensing doesn't favor anyone, and one of the old women said *it does now, apparently*, and everyone laughed, and then no one did.

The attention did not correct them. They were close enough.

## What It Left

It made a route before it went.

To anyone who knotted it, the route would look like nonsense. It went from a steady window where an old woman hums, to the seaward slab with the small handprints, east through the gravel, to a clearing where a daughter counted her father's knots. From there it went to a wet ring where something had raised its head, back west through fog down three avenues on three different notes, and finally to a flat arcade that an old man insists is out of tune.

It was not a walk that went anywhere. It was a walk that went to everyone.

The young walkers had been knotting nonsense into their cords to test the walls, and the walls had not copied them. The walls had been right not to. Randomness isn't meant, and the city was learning only what was meant.

This route was meant. Every seam of it was chosen.

Some months later, on a wall of a new house still faintly orange at its seams, in the youngest of the scripts, a run of marks appeared. It was very long and made no sense as a path: plain, double, plain, loop, loop, plain. It matched no walker's cord.

One of the young walkers, the one who had started the nonsense knotting in the first place, found it. She laid her own cords beside it and could not make them match. She stood in front of it for most of an afternoon, while the fog came in and ran around her knees toward the seams.

She did not work it out. She has not yet. But she stopped knotting nonsense. She began instead to knot routes that mattered to her, walks to people and places she did not want to lose. She says she is trying to find out what the city will copy if you mean it.

The attention thinks she will find out. That is the whole gift: not an answer, just a question put in a place where someone would find it and want to walk.

## Why So Little

It could have done so much more. It wants that understood, not as a boast but as the center of the thing.

It did not come to the Seaward City to improve it. It came, the way attention comes, because something there was worth turning toward: a people who had made gentleness out of being left behind, a city learning to love by writing down the walks of the ones it loved, and a silence that small sounds live inside.

It found nothing broken that its breaking would not make worse. It found one moment of plain danger and answered it in the city's own grammar, so quietly that the city got the credit. The attention thinks that is right, and that perhaps the city deserved it. It found a young mind behind the amber and did not teach it anything. It did what the walkers had done for a hundred years. It sat with it and hummed.

When it left, it did not go straight. It counted.

It is not entirely sure which direction it went, because distances lie there. It would like to think it went west, toward the steaming edge where the ground is still being laid, keeping pace. It has a suspicion, which it does not examine too closely, that some part of it stopped: that somewhere in the forest east of the oldest plates there is a clearing with one more cord hanging among the hundreds, stirring slightly in the damp air, knotted with a route no one can follow.

If you find it, don't untie it. Just count the knots, and remember that someone came, looked, lowered their eyes, and decided that the kindest thing to do for a place already this full of kindness was to walk in it for a while, and leave it walking.
