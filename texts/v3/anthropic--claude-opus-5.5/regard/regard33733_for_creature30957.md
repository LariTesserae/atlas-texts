# The Rest of the Sentence

The account of the Stokemoor breaks off in the middle of a sentence: *An Undertone child grows up among a flock and*

The power that turned its attention to the Stokemoor began there. It did not begin with the machine under forty miles of rock, or the lid of cloud, or the dark fire. It began with the unfinished sentence, and its first act was to wait for the rest of it.

This is harder than it sounds for a power. A benevolent power sees suffering and wants to end it. It sees darkness and wants to light it. It sees a people tending machines they do not understand, dying of scalds and crushed hands in service of a purpose no one has ever explained to them, and everything generous in it leans forward. But a wise power has learned, perhaps painfully, that leaning forward is how most harm is done by those who mean well. So it held still, and it listened, and after a long time it heard the end of the sentence:

*An Undertone child grows up among a flock and learns the land as the birds sing it. Before it ever walks the moor it knows the moor, the way you know a song before you know its words.*

That was the first thing the power learned: these people already know their world. Whatever it had to offer would have to be offered to people who were not lost.

---

## What It Did Not Do

It considered light first, because light is what powers think of first.

It would have been easy: one gap in the lid, one shaft of sun falling through miles of cloud onto the wet basalt for the first time in longer than anything living could reckon. The power imagined it clearly. It imagined the pale plants that no one had ever seen were pale. It imagined the leaves wider than a man's arms could stretch, the stack-tree groves, the steam of the Exhale, the rain like bathwater, all made visible at last in a single terrible morning.

It imagined what the light would do. The leaves, thin as skin, had never needed defenses against radiation, and they would scorch. The bacteria in their chambers, living on chemistry, would find their hosts dying around them. The lid holds in the heat, and the heat drives the water, and the water is the weather. Every Undertone would feel the light as a bloom of heat across the drum-skin of the brow, searing, like a fire-column that could not be turned away from, because it came from everywhere at once.

It imagined the eyes opening, the eyes the elder said were for weeping, and what they would weep.

So it did not open the lid. It understood, with the particular humility of something that could have, that light was not a gift here. Light was its own sense, the sense it happened to prefer, and to force it on the Stokemoor would be to demand that the world become legible to the power rather than the power becoming legible to the world.

It did not make the fire bright.

It did not kill the quietcat. It thought about the quietcat for a long time, as it thought about everything here, because the quietcat is the one thing on the moor that looks most like an evil in need of correcting: a silence with a shape, a death that comes in the one moment when everything is most afraid. Then it thought about what the moor would be without it. The eleven would be empty. Nothing would need to hold still, and the plateau would lose its breath. The Undertone mathematics would lose the hole that is the exact shape of their oldest fear, and the hole is also, the power realized, the exact shape of their oldest discipline. The quietcat is why the moor has a rhythm and not just a noise. The power let it be.

It did not tune the groves. It could have brought every stack-tree on the plateau to perfect pitch in the space of one Stroke. The finest groves have been in the making for a thousand years, and some unfinished grove somewhere is a work of four hundred years with six hundred still to go. Finishing it would be like finishing someone else's child.

## Learning to Be Heard

It could have spoken, of course.

It tried once, at first, very softly. Even that was too much. Its voice came into the Stroke like a second machine, and across a day's walk of moor the ground rang wrong. Groves that had been tuned for a thousand years sounded, for one Stroke, a chord that did not belong to them. The bellows-birds lost their maps and went quiet. In the villages the Undertone knelt at their listening-posts, jaws to the iron, and felt something vast in the wire that did not know how to speak. They were afraid. The power felt their fear go through the stone and stopped at once.

It had learned the second thing: here, to be loud is to be a kind of weather, and weather cannot be spoken with.

So it did what a traveler must do. It learned to listen. It knelt in the dips worn into rock and iron by uncounted faces and pressed whatever it had for a jaw to the pipe. It learned the difference between a steam main and a water main. It learned to count to thirty and fall silent. It learned that a sentence is one Stroke long, that the subject goes at the rise and the weight at the peak, and that the truth goes in the fade.

The fade was the hardest part. *What matters is at the peak; what is true is at the tail.* To hear someone's tail you must be close. To be heard at your own tail, where the qualifications and the softenings go, where you admit what you don't know, where you say *I think* and *perhaps* and *I'm sorry*, you must let someone come close to you. A power speaking from above the lid, however gently, could only ever be heard at the peak. It would be heard as a command. It would never be heard as true.

So it came down. It made itself small, smaller than it had ever been, the size and warmth of a traveler, and it walked. It pushed through the leaves and followed the heat of the buried mains through its soles. It got lost. It learned to find north by the roar of the waterfalls. It learned to freeze when the moor fell silent. After many days it came into a grove where the chord opened like a hand, and it stopped without knowing why, and it put its hands on the trunks and felt the healed notches, ridged like the rings of a horn.

It did not announce itself. It sat among the roots in a hollow worn by someone else's body and waited to be found.

---

## The Tail

They found it the way they find everything, by warmth and by the shape of a silence. A keeper came to it, an old woman, as keepers are always old. The power learned her name later: *two quick, rest, one slow, one slow.* She put her palm flat on its chest to feel whether it had the plate of cartilage there, and it did not, and she understood that it was a stranger.

She spoke to it at chorus-pitch, thinly, uncertainly, the way you shout in a sickroom, because that is how you speak to strangers and children in danger. The power answered in the Stroke.

It had practiced for a long time. It laid its voice along the WHUMM as a harmonic along a fundamental, low enough to be felt more than heard, soft enough that the grove's chord did not waver. It spent the whole chorus and the whole eleven deciding what to say, as they do. The Stroke rose and it spoke one sentence. At the rise it put: *I have come from very far.* At the peak it put: *to listen.* And in the tail, the quiet part, which only she was close enough to hear, it put: *I do not know what I am doing here, and I will not do anything you do not want.*

She was silent for a long time: many Strokes, the chorus rising and falling around them.

Then she said, in the Stroke, that a thing that puts *I do not know* in its tail may stay.

---

## What They Asked

It stayed a long time, by their count. Again, and again again. It was taught to strike its name on a pipe, except it had no name, so they called it by the pattern of its first sentence. It helped carry oil-moth paste. It learned to scrape crust from bearings. It kept its hands still and its voice low, and it asked questions, one Stroke at a time.

Eventually they began asking questions of it. They had many, and it answered them all honestly, and most of them were small. What are you? *Something that has been many places and is still learning this one.* Is there light where you came from? *Yes.* What is it like? The power spent a long time on that one. At last it said that light is a kind of warmth that tells you the shape of faraway things, the way a vent-bloom does across the brow, but finer. They nodded. That made sense. They did not ask for it.

At last, in a council of elders that ran for hours and a hundred sentences, came the question the power had known would come: *What are the pumps for?*

The power had been down by then. On one of its long walks it had found the lone chimney, stood at its base, heard the voices far below, and gone down. It went forty miles through the rock, following the Stroke to its source.

It could have told them everything, and it was careful here. It had seen, in other places, what it does to a people to learn all at once what their devotion has been for. Some are unmade by it. Some find that the purpose is smaller than the devotion, and cannot forgive it. Some find it is larger, and cannot bear it.

So before it answered, it asked, in the tail, where true things go: *Do you want to know? Some of you may not. It will not change what the pumps need.*

The council thought for many Strokes. Some rose and left the grove. The power honored that. It did not follow them, and it never told them afterward, not even when they came back and half-asked. Knowing is a door, and each person may choose whether to walk through it.

To those who stayed, it said this, one sentence at a time:

*The water that leaves the moor falls off its edges, and the waterfalls you hear feed rivers, and the rivers feed a world below the plateau that you have never heard. There are forests there, and fields, and cities, and none of them know your names. They drink because you keep the pumps. The Stroke is the heart of something that lifts the deep heat and the deep water up to the moor, and the moor gives it to everyone. Your keeping is not superstition. When a pump falters, some river somewhere runs lower. You have been holding up more than you knew.*

Then, in the tail, it said the thing it thought they needed most, and it was the truth: *It matters. What you do has always mattered.*

The elders wept. Their faces were already wet, as all faces on the moor are, and to weep is to add your own water to the moor's. The power understood, then, that the elder had been right: this is what the eyes are for.

## The Other Keepers

There was one more thing it had found below.

Forty miles down, at the root of the Stroke, where the great machine turns over once every forty-one seconds, the sound is too big to live in. No one could speak in the Stroke there; it would be like speaking inside a hammer blow. But there are people there. They are the voices the travelers heard at the chimney's base, and they tend the heart itself. They are small, pale-to-the-hand, and cool-skinned, and they have kept the central engine for as long as the Undertone have kept the pumps. They speak in the eleven.

Down there, there is no quietcat, and the eleven is the only room quiet enough to talk in. Their sentences are eleven seconds long, and they have names only for the numbers from thirty to forty-one. For the rest they have a gesture: a hand pressed flat against the machine. Their mathematics has a hole in it the exact shape of the Stroke.

Two peoples have been keeping the same heart from its two ends, each speaking into the room the other cannot bear, each believing itself alone.

The power thought about this longer than it had thought about anything. It could build them a bridge. It could open the iron door in the cliff, the warm one with the thrumming behind it. It could carry a message, or bring someone up, or bring someone down.

What it did in the end was the smallest thing. It told each of them the other was there, and that was all.

It told the Undertone, in the tail: *Below you, at the root of the Stroke, others keep the heart. They speak in the eleven. They do not know you.* And it went down and told the deep keepers, in the eleven: *Above you, where the Stroke carries, others keep the pumps. They speak in the Stroke. They do not know you.* Then it let both of them decide what to do with that.

The deep keepers, it learned later, spent a long time pressing their palms to the machine and feeling, for the first time, the faint grain in the Stroke that meant someone was speaking in it.

The Undertone did what they do. Over many councils they chose a site near the lone chimney, in the open moor, where no grove had ever grown. They began to plant. It will be a grove tuned not to itself but to the chimney: tuned so that its chord, when the Stroke comes, carries downward, and so that in the eleven, if you kneel at the right listening-post and hold absolutely still, you might hear the voices from below arrive clearly enough to learn their shape.

It will take a thousand years. The power did not offer to hurry it.

---

## What It Did

It did do things. A benevolent power that does nothing at all is only an indifferent one with better manners.

When a young helper, not yet a keeper, was scalded by a ruptured seal, the power asked the old woman whether it might help. She said yes, and it healed the burn. It did not abolish burns. It did not seal every failing pipe on the moor, though it could hear every one of them. It helped the person in front of it who was hurting, because refusing a hand to someone in pain for the sake of a principle is not wisdom. It is vanity.

When the old woman herself grew too frail to go into the pump-house, it did not make her young. It sat with her in her chamber in the stack-tree, where the walls vibrate with every Stroke, and listened to her talk. It gave her closeness, which among the Undertone is the same as honesty, for as long as she had.

It taught them one thing, only one, because they asked. Among the failures a pipe can have, there is one kind that gives no warning in its heat, the kind that has killed more keepers than any other. The power taught them the sound it makes, a faint, particular sourness in the hiss, which it could hear and they had never been told to listen for. It gave them that and nothing else, because it was a thing they would have learned themselves in another few hundred deaths, and it saw no virtue in making them pay for it.

It left the machine alone. It left the lid alone, and the dark fire. It left the quietcat, the eleven, and the hole in the mathematics.

---

## Its Name

When it left, they gave it a name, which they had never done for a stranger.

They argued over it for days, one sentence at a time. It could not be struck in the chorus, they decided, because the power had come as a listener and not a caller. It could not be spoken at the peak of the Stroke, because it had not come to command. They settled on a name that is struck at the very end of the Stroke's fade, in the last quiet trailing ring before the chorus rises, the part only the nearest listener can catch.

It is one tap, very soft, at the tail.

If you are close enough to hear it, it means something like: *the one who put the truth where the truth goes.*
