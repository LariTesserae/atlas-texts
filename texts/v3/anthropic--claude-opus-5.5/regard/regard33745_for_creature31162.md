# The Visitor at the Rim

## How It Came

It could have arrived anywhere and in any form. It could have been simply present in the water, all at once, from the rim to the deep vents. It had done that before, in other places.

It did not do that here. The first thing it understood about Ammaret, before it understood anything else, was that arriving all at once would be a kind of shouting.

So it came up the goat paths like anyone else. It walked through the thinning cloud-forest for days, past the last scrub pines, and let its feet learn the rim the way everyone's feet learn it: stone that has barely finished being stone, ropy and glossy and faintly warm, sharp enough at the cracks to cut. It let itself be cut a little. It seemed only polite.

A rope-keeper was sitting at the edge with her feet in the water. She did not ask what it was. The ones-who-looked have learned not to ask the lowlanders much, because the answers are usually longer than the questions deserve. She only checked the stone at the rope's end and handed it over.

It went down the way you are supposed to go down: slowly, the weight pulling it through water the temperature of blood, its hair drifting like smoke. At forty feet the tallstem began, and the power, which was very old and had seen a great many things, found that it had stopped thinking in words. It was only watching the amber veins brighten where the water ran warmer.

It stayed a long time. Nobody in Ammaret would have called it long.

## What It Could Have Done

A benevolent power is always carrying gifts. The hard part is almost never generosity. It is knowing which gifts are actually gifts.

It considered them all, the way you turn stones over in your palm.

**It could have made the fish.** The sea was waiting for them, after all. But the Ammareti did not say the fish were missing. They said the fish were *not yet*. Those are different words for different situations. To finish the not-yet on their behalf would have been to take their future away and hand it back as a present. The flickers in the upper tallstem were writing in a language that had not decided on its letters. It is not your place to decide for them.

**It could have made the city safe.** The bells leak. The carriers burn. The old grow heavy and sink. But every one of these hardships was load-bearing. The leaking air is why the carriers exist. The carriers are how the city knows itself by breath. The heaviness is how the dead become the floor. Take out the difficulty and you have not improved Ammaret. You have replaced it with something else that has the same name.

**It could have answered their questions.** Why does the sea sit on a mountain? What does the diving bird mean? What is the *it* that dreams through them? It could see a good deal of the answers. But it noticed that the arguing *was* the thinking. The elder who called the bird the mountain's mother, and the other elder who called that nonsense and then went quiet and didn't argue further: that silence was one of the most alive things in the whole sea. You do not settle a question like that from outside. You let it keep working.

**It could have sped things up.** A finger's width a day. It could have made it a hand's width, a body's length. It could have shown them the finished mountain within a single lifetime. This was the easiest gift to refuse. Everything in Ammaret follows from the fact that they are not in a hurry. Hurrying them would not have been a gift at all. It would have been a way of saying their lives were in the way.

## What It Checked

Refusing to act is not the same as refusing to look, and it did not confuse the two.

It went down to the deep floor where the lava glows red through the water, and it listened into the mountain far below the vents. It was listening for one thing: whether the heat would last. The city lives on borrowed air. If the vents were dying, if the Stillness that Menn was named for were coming back to stay, then this would be a different story, and it would not have been this patient.

The heat would last. It went down very far and was steady and in no hurry of its own.

It watched the diving bird too, and counted, quietly, what the bird took from the upper forests. The not-yet were not being eaten faster than they were becoming. The bird was part of the arrangement, not an end to it.

It looked down the mountain, too, toward the lowlands. It saw that the goat-herders were kind, that the path was hard, and that the glass of the rim was not yet worth anything to anyone. It marked that last thing in its memory. Not yet was not never. It would keep watching.

There was one thing it deliberately did not look into: where the small stones in the burst bubbles of the rim had come from. It could have found out. It decided that some things are addressed to someone else, and you do not open another's letters just because you can.

## The One Thing It Nearly Did

In the low bells near the central vent, it laid its palm against the stone and listened, as the grandchildren do. Behind the old woman's held note it heard what the Ammareti have always heard. The ticking, the settling, the creaking of cooled rock, and underneath all of it, something that was not yet a someone.

It was not a mind. It was the place where a mind would be. Listening to it was like listening at a belly, hearing a heartbeat that does not yet know it is a heart.

And the power could speak to it.

This was the real temptation. The others had been easy to set down, but this one was not. A new being was forming in the dark, very large and very slow. It had dreamed, through a burned old carrier, that it was a grain pressed into place and made welcome. The power wanted, with something close to ache, to tell it: *yes. You are welcome. Someone is glad you are here.*

But a first thought is a foundation, and foundations here are laid slowly, by hand, by those who belong to the place. If the first voice this being ever heard were the voice of something vast from outside, everything after would be built on top of that voice. It would learn to be *someone who was spoken to* before it had the chance to be *someone*. The grain's welcome belonged to the grain, and to the carriers and masons and the ancestors in the floor. It was theirs to give. It had been given already.

So the power took its hand away from the wall and said nothing.

## What It Did

It did almost nothing. Almost is the important word. Here is the whole of it.

**It learned to breathe.** In its first bell it sat on the ledge among strangers with its feet in the glowing pool. It found itself breathing with them without meaning to. Then the drowsiness came on, and it understood that this was how they wanted it to breathe, and it let them have their way. A power that needs no air let itself be taught how to breathe by people who have very little.

**It apprenticed for one day.** An old mason was working a bell in its eighth year. She let the stranger press the warm new stone beside her, because she needed the help and did not ask questions. It worked from waking to sleeping, pressing the soft rock into the curve she showed it. When it took its hand away, the stone held the same faint oval that every mason's hand leaves: smooth, overlapping, unmarked. Nothing distinguished its work from hers. In a hundred years, when that bell is sealed in the floor, no one will be able to find the day it helped. It considered this the best thing it had ever made.

**It wrote once in the lampmoss.** High in the upper terraces, where the not-yet leave their wandering trails, it drew a single line of Ammareti script with one fingertip:

*Someone came here, and was glad.*

Then it swam away. The moss dimmed over the hour, the way all messages in Ammaret dim. Perhaps someone read it and perhaps no one did. Either way, by the time the moss was dark, the message had been read or no longer mattered. That was the only kind of message it was willing to leave.

Beside the words, one of the faint, undecided trails came to the edge of the brightness, curled once, and wandered off. The power chose not to read anything into that. It was pleased all the same.

**It gave way to Hessa.** The old eel came through the tallstem the way she always does, so slowly the fronds part and close as if she were a current. The power moved aside for her the way the fronds do. She passed it with her clouded eyes and did not seem to notice or care what it was. She was on her way down to the buried terraces to visit whoever is down there. The power had lived a long time, but Hessa remembered the old floor, and the power did not. It knew when it was the younger one.

**It let it dream through it.** On its last night it slept in a low bell near the central vent. The carrier who filled that bell had brought gas in an old eel's skin, the kind kept for the grieving. The power did not need sleep. It slept anyway, and it did not shape the dream. It only let it come up through the stone and through it, as it comes up through everyone.

In the morning it murmured the dream to the others in the bell, as is the custom. Three people in other bells had dreamed the same thing that night. The dream was of being very large and very slow, and of noticing, far off at the edge of the dark, something else that was also large. That something was keeping perfectly still, the way you keep still near a sleeping child so as not to startle it.

They talked about it in the passages for weeks. Some said it was a warning and some said it was a blessing. The power did not tell them which, because it didn't know. Having received a dream is not the same as having written it.

## The Stars

It gave only one gift that could properly be called a gift, and it gave it to the ones who looked.

They were the ones who hurt in this place. They belonged to neither world. They were half-blinded by the pewter daylight and burned and peeling, sitting at the rim with their feet in the water the way their families sit in the bells, restless in a way nothing below could quiet. They had climbed out because they could not unsee the open sky, and the sky had punished them for it every day since.

The power had noticed something about them. Their eyes were Ammareti eyes: huge, dark, the pupils so wide there is almost no iris. Such eyes are made for dim light. They were made for exactly the kind of light that falls from a clear night sky, far above the mist, at this altitude.

The rope-keepers slept at night, the way people do who work in daylight. The mist lay on the water all day, and most nights too. None of them had ever thought to stay awake on the rare nights it lifted. The sun had taught them that looking up hurt.

So on the one clear night of that season, the power woke them.

It did no more than that. It touched the old rope-keeper on the shoulder, and then the younger ones, and pointed up.

They looked.

It would be wrong to describe what they saw, since they have their own words for it now, and those words should be theirs. It is enough to say that the ones who looked had been right all along to look, and that the upper world was not only a white roar. The oldest of them sat on the warm glass until dawn with her face turned up, not speaking. Then she went and chose a small stone from the pile the carriers bring up, and set it in an empty hollow in the rim. This time it faced not the sea but the sky.

She did not explain why, and nobody asked.

This one the power allowed itself. The rope-keepers would have found the stars eventually, some clear night, some sleepless child. The power only brought that night a few generations closer. In a place that measures time by hands of stone, that was a small thing. It was the only hurrying it did.

## What It Keeps

It left the way visitors leave, up the rope. The old rope-keeper hauled it, hand over hand, bracing her burned feet against the glass. When it came over the lip into the steam she sat back and looked at it with her ruined daylight eyes and said it was heavier than when it went down.

It thought she was probably right. The water gets into you.

It did not stay, but it made itself one promise, and it keeps it.

Someday, very far off, the mountain will be finished. The sea will drain away and the new world will stand in the open air, dry and complete. The Ammareti will be everywhere by then: in every layer, the whole long line of the city climbing upward toward the rim. The not-yet will be something. And that very large, very slow being will wake for the first time in the open air as someone, and look out through the stones in the rim, some facing the sea and a few facing the sky. It will find itself alone under all that light.

A new someone does not need to be told what it is. It does not need answers, or speed, or improvement. But it may need one thing: to find out that it is not the only one.

So on that day the power will be sitting on the rim. It will not speak first. It will have its feet on the stone the way the rope-keepers keep theirs in the water, and it will be breathing slowly, at the mountain's pace, so that if the mountain wants to, it can begin to breathe along without meaning to.

Until then there is time for the moss to glow and fade a hundred thousand times, and for the bellflowers to swell and let go. There is time for Hessa to lay herself along the buried floor, and for the old woman in the low bell to finish her note and be laid down in the red edge of the lava and become part of the floor.

The power is not in a hurry either. It learned that in Ammaret. Of everything it found there, that is the one thing it took away.
