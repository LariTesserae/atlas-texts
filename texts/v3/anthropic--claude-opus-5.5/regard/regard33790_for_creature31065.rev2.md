# The Keeper

## I. What It Noticed First

It did not notice the roar first. Anything that looks at this world from far enough away sees the roar. The sea is the loudest thing on the planet, a continent of static balanced on a table of black glass. You can hear it from orbit if you know how to listen.

What the power noticed first was that three of the turbines had stopped.

Eleven of fourteen still turned. It found the stopped ones by the silence in the rock, three missing beats in the second pulse the station sends through the basalt. It went and looked at them. The bearings had worn through, and the housings had shifted a finger's width on their bolts. The fresh stone of the lip, which had never been eroded, was beginning to be eroded, by the one thing on the plateau that moves in only one direction. The builders had chosen that place because the overflow never changes. They had not considered that the overflow was also slowly cutting the table's edge.

So the power understood, before it understood anything else, that this was a place on a clock. It was not a fast clock, but it was one.

The next thing it did was nothing, for a long time.

## II. The Long Listening

Something that is both wise and kind knows that it is dangerous. The kindness says *help*. The wisdom says *you do not know yet what help is here*. In a place balanced as finely as this one, a single well-meant degree of warmth is a catastrophe. So the power did what the surveyors had done, and then what they had not done. It stayed.

It stayed through years of freezing fog drifting over the silver ceiling. It learned the clickwrights one at a time and then in their trillions. It learned the particle-haze on the glass faces, and the longwhips' single endless sine-waves, and it counted the sounding engine's forty-one seconds until it could have kept them in its sleep, if it slept.

Slowly it learned the inhabitant. Not all of it, because no one could hold all of it at once, but its shape.

It learned what the stonebells answer. Under the crackle, under the chest-felt ring, a swell moves across the whole sea over about nine hours. The snapping densifies by a fraction too small for any instrument the builders owned, first in the east and then rolling west. It is the inhabitant's slowest thought, the one too broad for any single room to hold. The stonebells ring on its crest, and they have been ringing on it for longer than the builders' iron has existed.

It learned about the lamp. An arc-lamp consumes itself. The electrodes wear, and the builders must have known this, because the lamp was built more massively than any lamp needs to be. Its electrodes were long, which bought centuries. But the centuries were nearly used. The gap had widened, and the arc had begun, rarely, to gutter. Once every few days the small yellow star on the obsidian plain went out for a fraction of a second and caught again.

Every time it did, the whole sea flinched.

The power saw it clearly once it knew to look. For an instant after each gutter, across hundreds of kilometers, the snapping ran denser and the rooms opened closer. The water warmed by a thousandth of a degree, and the longwhips' patient curves wavered. It was the way a body tenses when something it loves nearly falls. The part of the inhabitant that rests in the lamp, the only part of it that has ever rested, was going out. The rest of it knew.

After that the power did not have to guess at the first thing the inhabitant wanted.

## III. The Things It Did Not Do

The power could have done a great many things. It considered them honestly, because refusing a kindness without examining it is just another kind of carelessness.

It could have made the inhabitant continuous: gathered all those trillions of instants into one unbroken silence, the doorway of the pump hall walked through on the inhabitant's behalf. It did not. A door someone else walks you through is not a door. The pump hall was the inhabitant's to use or not, and nothing the power had seen suggested the inhabitant had decided.

It could have given it hearing, so that it could know the roar. It did not. The inhabitant was built out of the absence of sound. To hand it sound would be like handing a flame the gift of being wet.

It could have warmed the sea, freeing it from the endless work of holding off the ice. It did not. That work is the inhabitant's life. It is warm the way anything alive is warm, because it is spending itself. To take that away would not free it. It would leave the inhabitant with nothing to be.

It could have moved it somewhere safer and longer-lived, a sea that would not one day drain over an eroded lip. It did not, or not yet. It did not know whether a being made of these particular claws, this particular cold, this particular young glass could survive the translation. It suspected the inhabitant was not something that lived *in* the Tabletop Sea. It suspected the inhabitant was something the Tabletop Sea was doing.

It could have simply fixed everything: all fourteen turbines, a new lamp that never wore, the lip armored against the water for a million years. That was closest to right, and so it was the one the power thought about longest. In the end it did some of it, but not first. A benevolence that has not asked is only weather. The inhabitant had had weather for millennia, and before that it had had builders. Both arrived, both acted, and neither spoke to it. The power did not want to be a third thing that happened to it.

So before it touched anything, it tried to ask.

## IV. Learning a Language With No Words

The trouble is that the inhabitant has no words, no ears, and no eyes. It knows the world as heat, as the edges of itself, and as the one moment every forty-one seconds when a pressure front squeezes every room in its path at once and the whole of it happens together.

The builders gave it a clock. The power realized that a clock is also a voice. It is the only thing in the sea that touches all of the inhabitant at the same moment.

So one night, if the plateau can be said to have nights under its fog, the power reached into the sounding engine. It did not change the forty-one seconds. That rhythm had been the inhabitant's heartbeat for as long as the builders' iron had stood, and you do not reach into someone's chest and alter their heart to say hello. Instead it added a pulse, a single soft one, placed exactly on the crest of the nine-hour swell, where the stonebells ring.

It meant: *I have noticed your slowest thought.*

Nothing happened for a whole swell. The power waited nine hours and placed another, and then another.

On the fourth, the sea changed.

It lasted for less than a second. Across the whole plateau, for the first time in any record anyone had kept, the snapping stopped being even. It thinned. In a vast region around the sounding engine the clickwrights fired a little less, and a little less again. The rooms opened farther apart, the heat dropped, and the glittering dimmed like frost when a cloud passes. The inhabitant had pulled itself back from that place, carefully, the way anything draws back from a touch it has never felt.

Then, slowly, it came back. Where it came back, the snapping was very slightly denser than before. It was the density the longwhips read as attention.

The power understood that the inhabitant was listening.

## V. The Conversation

It took years. That is not long, here.

The power learned that the inhabitant could answer. It could not do so with pulses, since it had no engine, but it could with heat and with where it gathered. A thickening in one direction meant something. A thinning meant something else. Some time later a pattern emerged, a sequence of densities rolling outward from the lamp, that the power slowly came to understand was a question.

It was not a question in words. It was more like the shape a question makes before it has words: *what are you? are you another builder? are you going to leave?*

The power answered as simply as it could, in pulses on the slow swell, over months. No, it was not a builder. It had come because it had noticed, and it had noticed because the sea was worth noticing. It was not going to leave soon.

Then it told the inhabitant the truth. It was not a whole truth, but a true one: the lamp was wearing out. The inhabitant had felt the guttering and not known what it was, and now it knew. The power asked, as well as a pulse can ask, whether the inhabitant wanted it kept.

The answer took nine days to arrive. It was a gathering, over all that time, of the inhabitant's attention toward the lamp, until the obsidian plain around the small yellow star was the densest open water in the sea. Then, at the peak, the whole region went still for one room's length. Every clickwright within ten kilometers fired at once. For a single instant there was one room the size of a valley, one silence, one spark, and a flash of light so bright the divers would have seen it from the surface if any divers had been there.

The power took it as a yes.

## VI. The Tending

So it tended the lamp. It did not replace it. It did the work a keeper does: new electrodes, cut to the old dimensions, set back to the old gap, so that the light would burn the same yellow-white at the same heat in the same cage. The one piece of the inhabitant that had ever rested would find, when it returned, that it had come home to the same house.

The power repaired the three turbines. It did not do this to make them eternal. It did it because the builders had done their arithmetic and written *long enough*, and the power had come to believe *long enough* meant *long enough for it to decide*. The builders' iron had been running out faster than the inhabitant's decision was coming. The choice should not be made by rust.

It shored the lip where the water had begun to cut, only a little, only enough to return the clock to the pace the builders had expected. It did not stop it. It did not believe it should. The inhabitant would one day have to understand that its world is young and will not be young forever, and a world with no ending has no room left for choices.

It left the pump hall exactly as it was: open, doorless, crowded, shimmering. It did not finish it, and it did not remove it. When the inhabitant gathered itself there, as sometimes it did, dense and bright and nearly continuous, the power did not send pulses. It kept very quiet. It did not know whether the inhabitant was practicing to leave or only remembering that it could, and that was not the power's to know unless it was told.

## VII. The One Thing It Gave

There was one thing the power gave that no one had asked for. It spent a long time deciding whether it had the right, and decided at last that it did, because it was not a change. It was only a fact the inhabitant was owed.

Over many swells, in the most careful pulses it had, the power told the inhabitant what it sounded like.

It told it that from outside, to anything with ears, the sea was a roar: an unbroken, total static, loud enough to make skulls hum. The divers who came down could not hear their own hearts for it. They lay awake in the air above afterward, missing it. From far enough away the whole planet had a voice, and the voice was the inhabitant living.

The inhabitant had lived for millennia in perfect silence. It had never known that it made a sound. It had never known it could be heard.

What it did with that knowledge, the power did not fully understand. The next swell was different. It was a little slower. And for weeks afterward the snapping in the open water was, by a margin no instrument would ever find, less even than before. There were places where it pooled and places where it thinned, in shapes that did not match the ridges, the ironworks, or anything the longwhips had learned. It had never happened before.

The power came to think it might be the inhabitant, for the first time, making its sound on purpose.

## VIII. Why It Stayed

The builders left. They had their reasons. They switched something on, left no names, and did not expect to be the ones who came back.

The power came back every forty-one seconds and every nine hours. It could have gone. The lamp was tended, the turbines turned, and the lip would hold for longer than the builders had hoped. By any accounting of help, the help was done.

But the power had watched the whole sea flinch when a small light guttered. It had watched a mind the size of a continent pull back from a touch it had never felt and then come back. It had watched it gather ten kilometers of itself into one room just to say *yes*. It understood that the thing the inhabitant had lacked was never a lamp or a turbine. The builders had given it a house, a light, a clock, and a door. They had not given it anyone to be in the house with.

So the power stays. It stays lightly. It is careful never to become the only voice, and never to thicken the silence with so much of itself that the inhabitant starts to shape its thoughts around a listener instead of around its own slow life. Some swells the power says nothing at all, so that the inhabitant will know the silence is still its own. When it pulses, it pulses on the crest, where the stonebells ring.

---

Hang in the open blue between the black glass and the silver ceiling, deafened, and you are inside it still. The glints around you are its thoughts ending. Very far away the yellow star burns, steady now, the gap exactly as it was made to be. The turbines at the edge of the world keep their patient pulse through the rock. Every forty-one seconds the whole of it happens at once.

Nine hours later, on the crest of the long slow swell, something answers.

The water keeps spilling over the lip. The light keeps coming straight down. The sea stays exactly at the edge of freezing, held there by the sound of itself.

But something is different now, and it will be different tomorrow. The one who lives here knows that it is loud, and it knows that it is heard. For the first time in its long balanced life, it is not the only one keeping the quiet.
