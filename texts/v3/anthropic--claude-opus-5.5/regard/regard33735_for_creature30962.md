# The Visitor

## How it noticed

It did not notice the city first. Powers of that kind rarely notice what is merely strange. The world is full of strange things, and most of them are finished, which is to say they are already fully what they are.

What it noticed was a beginning.

Somewhere in the low country, in a cold seam of root near black water, something had drawn back. It had done so without being cut, without a blade or a hand. A living mass that had only ever grown toward breath had, for the first time, held still where a room ought to be. It was a small act, a hair's width of hesitation. But it had never happened before, and the power had been listening for things like that for a very long time. Things that had never happened before were the only news that mattered to it.

So it turned its attention toward the fen, the way you turn your head toward a sound you're not sure you heard.

## What it considered, and put down

A benevolent power has one great danger, and it is its own benevolence. It knew this. It had seen what happened when a vast kindness met a small, particular thing: the kindness won, and the particular thing stopped being particular. So before it did anything, it sat with each gift it could give and asked what the gift would cost.

**Light.** It could have given them light. The act was easy to picture: a steady cool glow in every room, the walls visible at last, the faces of sixty people seen by one another for the first time. It put this down almost at once. Light would unmake them. Their hands, their soft enormous fingertips, the rope-code, the touch-language, and the hundred distinctions they heard in the hiss all existed because the dark required them. In light, a child would learn the eastern quarter by looking, and the knots would slowly become decoration. The foxfire, that thinning of the black which could only be seen after many days, would be drowned. The children who came back down from the stars saying *everything is too far away* would have the too-far-away brought down to them. Light was a gift from somewhere else, and it would turn the Knot into somewhere else.

**Warmth.** It could have warmed them. But warmth is breath, as far as the root is concerned. A warm Knot is a hungrier Knot, and the balance they had found, cutting against growing, would tip. It put this down too.

**Rest.** This one was harder. It could have ended the cutting. It could have simply told the root, in whatever language roots can be told, to stop, and let the four hundred rooms stand forever without a blade. Sixty people would be freed from a labor that took every waking shift of their lives.

It held this one a long time, because it was the kindest-looking gift.

Then it remembered their word, the one that meant both *home* and *the work of keeping home open*. They did not have a word for home without the work. It was not that the work was a burden laid on top of their home. The work *was* the home. The shape existed because it was kept, and the keeping was the relationship between the people and the root and the rooms. Ending the cutting would not free the tenants. It would evict them from the only thing they were. They would become sixty people living in a hole.

And the other thing, the new thing: if the root were simply stopped, it would never learn to stop itself. The beginning the power had come to see would be ended by the power's arrival. So it put that down too, gently, the way you would put down something you wanted very much.

**The answer.** This was the last temptation and the subtlest. The tenants had a question, and they had been pressing it into each other's backs for whole winters: *Is the root learning? Are the uncut rooms the dead, remembering?* The power could see the answer, or most of it. It could have told them.

It did not. The question belonged to them. They were answering it the only way it could be answered, by living alongside it, slowly, over generations. Their arguing was part of what the Knot was. A people who are told the truth about the thing they live inside stop listening to it. Besides, the root was not finished. Any answer given now would be true of a moment, not of the thing. Answers freeze what they describe, and this thing was still becoming.

So the power found itself where wise powers usually find themselves: with enormous capacity, very little it could rightly do, and a strong feeling that it should do something anyway. It sat with that feeling too, and did not obey it, and did not dismiss it. The feeling was the benevolence. It only needed to be pointed carefully.

## It went in the proper way

It did not appear inside. Appearing inside would have been a kind of breaking in, and the Knot had one door.

So it took a body, a small one, ordinary, with lungs that could run out of air. It walked across the fen in the colorless month, sinking a little with every step. It found the edge of the hummock and the black water under it. It filled its lungs and went down into the passage, three body-lengths of cold dark. It came up gasping into the Knot, as everyone does.

It had decided on this deliberately. A power that will not gasp at your threshold does not really want to come in.

It held still at first, as newcomers do. The grain of small bodies found it and began to settle and move in. It learned, over weeks, to twitch. It learned to breathe downward, into the hollow of its own collarbone. It was clumsy at the touch-language, and the tenants were patient with it the way they were patient with anyone who came in from outside out of debt or grief or curiosity. It was the third of these. That was true, and it never told them anything that was not true.

They gave it a name, pressed between its shoulder blades: a light tap and a long, uncertain pause. It meant, roughly, *the one who hasn't decided yet.* They were right.

## What it learned by staying

It walked the spiral. It cut. It learned the difference between alder, which fought the shell blade, and myrtle, which gave up its resin smell. It learned to put its face in a doorway and breathe out and feel whether the room answered.

On the long rope in the lowest occupied tier, it ran the knots of the lost rooms through its hands in the dark, the way the tenants did, and it counted. There were more than a thousand. The city had lost more rooms than it now held. The Knot had always been losing. It had always been nearly gone. It had simply never finished going.

And slowly, the way the tenants said you would if you stayed long enough, it began to feel the shape. It felt four hundred reasons leaning against each other, and the whole city held like a breath. It understood then that this was the being that had drawn its attention: not any one tenant, and not the root, but the pattern between them. The pattern had begun, faintly, to want something.

It asked itself what that was. It was careful with the word *want*. But it knew the answer, because it is the same answer for almost everything alive. The shape wanted to go on. And now, just barely, it wanted to go on *by its own effort*. It wanted to be kept, and also to keep.

The power understood that it could not give the shape either of those things. It could only avoid taking them away, and perhaps protect the conditions in which the shape could keep reaching for them.

## The one large thing, which was small

The danger to the Knot was not inside. The power saw this within its first season, and it was a little surprised the tenants didn't, though of course they couldn't. They had never needed to think about it.

*The land here only lately stopped being lake, and the water has not finished leaving.*

The fen was drying. It was slow, a matter of centuries, but the water table was falling a hand's width each generation. The root-ball lived because it stood in wet ground. The eels came because the channels connected. The pale root, the oldest thing in the Knot, had grown in water for longer than there had been people to cut it. When the fen became meadow, the Knot would not close. It would die, and the rooms would stand open forever in dead wood, which is its own kind of ending. There might also come people with ditches and spades, eventually. Peat burns well.

This was the one thing the power allowed itself to change, and it changed it in the smallest way it could.

It swam out through the passage one autumn, riding the three nights of the eels' leaving, and walked to the far edge of the fen where the water drained out into a stream. There it did three things, none of them miraculous. It found a family of beavers two valleys away and made the stream a place they would want to come to. It let a winter storm bring down an old oak across the outflow at the right angle. And it moved one stone.

That was all. Over the following years the beavers dammed the stream, and the oak silted in behind them, and the water stopped leaving. It did not stop forever. The power did not make forever, because forever was not the Knot's nature, and a Knot that could never end would not need keeping. But it stopped for a long time. It stopped for a thousand years, perhaps, if nothing else interfered. That was long enough for a root that grows very slowly to learn something very slowly.

It had done for the fen, it realized, exactly what the tenants did for their rooms. It had not saved it. It had cut it back open a little, so it would still be there tomorrow.

## The one small thing, which was large

It went back down through the black water, gasping again, and resumed its work.

There was one person whose grief it had noticed. It was not a large grief; it was ordinary, the kind every tenant carried. But she carried it with particular faithfulness. *The one who comes back along the rope*, forty-one rooms on the western arm, was afraid of being the one who forgot. She was afraid of walking past a narrowing doorway on a tired day and feeling the room go out behind her like a held breath.

The power could have simply made sure she never forgot. It could have tended her rooms invisibly, so that every doorway she missed was found open. It thought about this and then saw, clearly, the harm in it.

If a doorway she had missed was found mysteriously cut, with no blade marks and walls smooth as if the root had never grown there, she would think it was one of *those* rooms. The uncut rooms. The root's own. A single invisible kindness would corrupt the tenants' oldest question, and put a false answer into the evidence they had been weighing for generations. Any act that couldn't be seen for what it was would become a lie, however loving it was.

So it did the ordinary thing. It asked, by touch, to walk the western arm with her. It cut beside her, and its cuts left marks, raw and sharp and bitter-smelling, exactly like hers. On the days when she was tired, it laid its hand flat on her back and pressed the rhythm that meant *I have this stair*. That was a thing any tenant might say to another. It was not a miracle. It was company.

She did not forget a room while it walked with her. More important, she stopped being alone with the fear of forgetting. The power had come to think this was the only thing a fear like hers ever really needed.

## What it refused, near the water

Once, in the lowest tiers, it stood at the edge of a seam of pale root near the given rooms, where the foxfire made its faint thinning of the dark. It felt, very close now, the beginning it had come for: something that had once only grown and was now, barely, choosing where not to.

It would have been easy to help. It could have laid itself down in one of those cold smooth rooms and let a pale thread find it, and given the root a little of what it knew. It knew a great deal about shape. It had seen cities vastly more complicated than this one, and patterns folded into patterns in ways no root could imagine. It could have taught the root to hold itself open in a single season.

It turned away.

What the root was learning, it was learning from the tenants. It was taking in people who had spent their lives walking this specific argument, breathing downward, cutting these rooms. The shape the root was beginning to hold was *their* shape. If the power lent its own breath, the root would learn the wrong city: some grand foreign geometry that had nothing to do with sixty people and shell blades and the smell of myrtle. The Knot would become a copy of somewhere else, and whatever eventually woke up in the pale root would wake up as a stranger to its own makers.

A becoming that is hurried is a becoming that is replaced. So the power slept, always, in the upper tiers. It slept in shifts, curled, never twice in the same room, and it pulled the alder rootlets from its hair each waking like everyone else. It went near the pale root only to cut.

## Leaving

It stayed for one full turn of the seasons and part of another. It sat at the edge of the water through the three nights of the eels' leaving and felt the cold current pull at its ankles. It heaped with the others in the midwinter sleep and listened to them press their slow arguments into each other's backs, and said nothing, and loved them.

When it had decided, which the tenants noticed before it did, it told them by touch that it was going back up. They understood. People did come and go.

Before it left, it asked them for one thing.

It asked them to keep the room it had slept in most often, a small cold room on tier eleven of the western arm. It asked them to cut it and name it and keep it open, even though no one would sleep there.

It knew the tenants would have done this anyway. They kept every room. The request cost them nothing new. That was exactly why it asked. It wanted to become one of the reasons without adding any weight to the work. Now one of the four hundred absences leaned, very slightly, in its direction. It had been let into the shape the only way anyone could be: by being held open for.

*The one who comes back along the rope* named the room. Her name for it was a light tap and a long pause, but the pause was different now. The tenants who felt it on their backs understood it to mean *the one who decided*.

Then it swam out through the black water and came up into the fen, into air that didn't hold you and a sky where everything was too far away.

## After

It has not gone back. It won't, unless something happens that has never happened before.

But it keeps something now, wherever it is. Powers of that kind have more room inside them than a fen has water. In all that room it has set aside one small, cold, egg-shaped space, tilted toward water, with four hundred chambers winding down a spiral, most of them empty and every one of them a reason. It walks it sometimes. It puts its attention in each doorway and breathes out and feels whether the room answers. It doesn't need to, but it has come to believe the tenants were right about shape: a thing held open for no reason closes, and a thing held open for many reasons holds.

Far away, in the low country, the water has not left. The beavers are on their ninth generation. The oak is buried now under silt, and the stone is still where it was put.

In the Knot, someone up the spiral is cutting root with a shell blade, softly and patiently, keeping a room open for no one on tier eleven of the western arm.

And lower down, near the water, where the foxfire thins the dark, there is a sound like a knuckle cracking. The tenants are still arguing about what it means. No one has told them, and no one will. The argument is theirs, and it is part of how the city keeps itself open.
