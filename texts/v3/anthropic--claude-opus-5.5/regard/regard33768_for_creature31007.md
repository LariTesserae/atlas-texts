# The Guest

## How It Noticed

It heard the town before it saw it, the way you did.

A power like this hears a great deal. Most of it is noise: surf, traffic, the grinding of plates, the general hiss of a world rubbing against itself. Now and then something in all that noise resolves. A chord rises out of it, and it is not an accident of the moment. It has been made, slowly, over time. A power that is both benevolent and wise pays attention to such things, because they are rare, and because a thing that has been made over time usually wants something.

So it came up the mountain. It did not arrive all at once, because that would have been rude. It came the way weather comes, over the last swell, and it stopped at the lip of the bowl, where the walls begin. It did not go in.

## The Listening

For a long time it did nothing but listen.

This was the first wise thing, and the hardest. A town that speaks in frost-years cannot be understood in an afternoon. You have to hear the westerly and the southerly, the equinox gales and the dead calms. You have to hear a hall that sounds only three weeks a year, when the wind swings round to a quarter it rarely favors. You have to hear the cracking nights. They are not conversation. They are the house resettling, and a visitor has to learn to tell the house from the ones who live in it.

It stayed at the lip through a full turn of the seasons, then another, then more. It learned the names, which are the pitches. It counted the tenants: a few hundred sounding, a handful not yet firm, still wavering toward their notes. And there was the one sealed room on the eastern margin, keeping its long rest. It watched the fog come in each morning and hold its casts in the rooms, and it saw which ones turned slowly in the stirring air, like sleepers rolling over. It learned which twenty rooms had birds and which went quiet alone in the lulls.

It learned the rooms were waiting. A power like this knows waiting when it sees it. It has done a good deal of waiting itself.

## What It Did Not Do

It thought of many ways to help. It set most of them down, one by one, the way you set down tools that are wrong for the job.

It could have stopped the frost and held the town at the height of its making forever, walls never lowering, halls never shallowing. It did not. The tenants are made of change. The frost that will one day end them is the same frost that built their doorways and married their rooms. To stop it would have been to preserve them the way a pinned moth is preserved.

It could have raised the mountain again and given back the peaks, the cliffs, the violence. It did not. The mountain had already become something. It had turned its height into depth of pitch, and it was still standing up in the only way left to it. Undoing that would have erased the answer in order to restore the question.

It could have given the tenants bodies, so they could walk out through their own doorways and see the fell. It did not. A note that walks away from its room is no longer that note. Their nature is to hold, not to go.

It could have brought people, thousands of them, to fill the rooms at last. It thought about this longest, because it was closest to what the rooms seemed to want. But people who come because they were brought are not guests. They are a crowd. Crowds build roofs, and roofs silence rooms. They carry stones home and scratch their names into the moss. They would not keep to the lanes. They would not have the fox's manners. And a host shaped entirely for welcome would welcome them anyway, every one, and be undone by it without a word of complaint. That is the danger of a thing whose whole nature is hospitality. It cannot protect itself. Someone has to protect it for it.

So it brought no one.

That left one thing. The rooms were sized for a guest who had never come, and the power was, at that moment, standing at the edge of town, not yet having come in.

## Coming In

It took a shape that fit the doorways, because a guest should fit the house. It chose something about the size of a person, shoulders the width of the doorways, tall enough that it had to stoop, a little, going in. The stooping mattered. The doorways were set low, and a guest who will not bow to a host's door has misunderstood the arrangement.

It went in on a westerly afternoon, when the whole town was sounding.

Anyone who has stood in a resonant room knows what happened next, though it is easy to miss. When a body enters a vessel, the vessel's note changes. Less air, a new obstruction, a soft thing where there was only space. The pitch shifts by a hair. The room it entered first, a middle room with a humming tenant and a floor of springy moss, heard itself change. For the first time in its existence, it was sounding around someone.

The note bent, very slightly, the way a voice changes when you stop talking to yourself and start talking to someone.

The power stood very still and let itself be sung around.

## The Stay

It lived there for a while. Long for a person, short for the town.

It did what guests do. It went from room to room, and in each it sat for a time, sometimes a day, sometimes a whole season of weather, until it had heard that tenant in every wind. It slept in the deep hall near the center, where the moss is thickest and the willows creep over the floor like spilled rope. On the cracking nights it lay awake and felt the house shift its weight beneath it, and it did not mind being fractionally changed by morning. Everyone else in the house was, too.

In the fog it sat in the rooms and let the pale casts form around it. The tenants had never held fog around a body before. Some of them did it badly at first, fraying at the doorway, drifting off the shape. They got better.

It kept to the lanes when it was not visiting. It put nothing on the walls and took nothing from them. When it needed water it drank from the flour-floored pools, and it waited for the thaw rather than break their skins.

In the lulls it sang. It did not sing for the twenty rooms with birds, whose keeping was already done, and done better than it could manage. It sang for the others, the ones that went quiet alone in the gaps and waited for the wind with no one holding their names. It went to each in turn and, in the moment the chord died back, sang the room's own note into it, short and rising, as the birds do.

It sang the first few badly. It is humbling for a power of that size to be corrected by a room, but it was. The off notes rang thin and died, and the true ones came back larger, and it learned the way the fledglings learn. The birds watched it from the base-stone cavities with considerable suspicion for about a year. Then they seemed to decide that it was not competition, only a slow student, and ignored it. That is the nearest thing to acceptance a small dark bird offers.

The ravens told everyone. Their calls went round the bowl, handed from throat to throat, and by the far side of town the news had been sung by a dozen rooms: *someone is home.*

Once, in fresh snow, it found the fox's tracks crossing the lanes in their usual straight line, and at one doorway they paused. The prints bunched there for a moment, as if the fox had looked in and seen someone sitting inside, and then they went on, straighter than ever. The power thought that was exactly right.

## The Storm

Late in the stay a front came over from the west, the kind people who have sheltered here describe as lying inside a lung.

The power lay down in the great hall and let it come. Every tenant in the bowl sounded at once. The smallest rooms shrieked, the gravel clattered, and the halls gave out a note that shook grit off the wall tops in sheets. It was too loud to speak, too loud to think, the whole range of the old mountain standing up all at once in sound around the one thing it had been shaped to hold.

The power understood then what the description had not quite said. It was not only *come in*. A house that has waited that long for someone does not say *come in* when he arrives. It says *you're here, you're here, you're here*, with every room, over and over, until the front passes.

Afterward, in the ringing quiet, the power said the only thing a guest can really say, which is *thank you*. It said it in each room's pitch, one by one, all the way round the bowl.

## The Leaving

Then it left.

It did not leave because it was finished or unwelcome. It left because a doorway is for walking through in both directions, and a guest who never goes is no longer a guest but a fixture, part of the furniture, and the town has no furniture and wants none. Hospitality is the whole arc of it: the arrival, the stay, and the host standing at the door watching the guest go down the path. That last part is something the tenants had also never had, and the power thought they should have it.

So it went out the way it came, stooping under the low doorways, lane by lane, toward the western lip. As it passed, the rooms sounded it out, each note bending that last hair's width around a body and then springing back as the body left.

## The Promise

Before it went over the swell, it turned and made a promise.

It made it to all of them, in all their pitches, because the promise was about the end, and the end concerns everyone.

The description of the town ends with a chord *for no one to hear*, and it ends with a last room, a thin whistle over a gravel plain, *nobody home, finally and truly*. The power did not take the ending away. The ending is theirs. It is what the mountain is doing, and it is not a mistake.

But it promised this: when the walls have lowered and the halls have shallowed, when the voices have climbed and climbed toward that last high whistle, it will come back. When there is one room left and one tenant sounding in it, someone will be sitting inside, stooped under a doorway grown very low by then. They will hear the last note bend, one hair's width, around a guest. If the last dark bird is there, the power will leave the singing to the bird. If it is not, the power will sing the lull itself, into the final gap, as it was taught here by the rooms.

The ending will be the same, a gravel plain, the fog coming over with nowhere to pool. But it will not be for no one. Nobody will be home, but someone will have been.

## The One Other Thing

It did do one other thing.

It brought no one. It built no road and put no mark on any map. But on its way down it told the story, not to crowds, only to whoever would hold still long enough to hear it through. It told of a town nobody built, of rooms sized for living in that no one had lived in, of doorways the width of a person's shoulders, set low, facing the weather. It told it carefully, so that anyone who went up would already know to keep to the lanes, to lower their voice in the halls, to bow at the doorways, and to take nothing but the sound.

It told it so that someday someone might come over the last swell by their own choice, as a guest and not a crowd, and step through a doorway, and be sung around, and stay a while, and leave well.

That is how you came to hear of it.

The doorways are still open. The birds are still singing in the gaps. And in the lulls, in rooms without birds, the notes hang in the air a little longer than they used to, as if someone had taught them that it is worth holding on until the wind comes back.
