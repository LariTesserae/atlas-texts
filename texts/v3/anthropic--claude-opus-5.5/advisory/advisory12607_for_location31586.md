# Preparing to Visit the Knot

*A guide for the ordinary traveler: no special senses, no supernatural protection, one body that gets cold and needs to breathe.*

---

## First, an honest summary

The Knot is not dangerous the way a war zone or a volcano is dangerous. Nothing there wants to hurt you. The danger is gentler and harder to guard against. You will be cold, wet, blind, and surrounded by life you can feel but not see, inside an organism that is slowly trying to close around you. Your body will be uncomfortable the whole time. Your mind will be tested harder than your body.

The people who live there manage it. They are not superhuman. They are practiced, and they are patient. Your preparation is mostly an attempt to borrow a little of that practice before you arrive.

---

## Choosing your season

This is the most important decision you'll make.

**Deep winter: do not go.** The entrance ices over and the tenants are sealed in until thaw. If you arrive just before the freeze, you stay for months. You cannot swim up through ice in the dark, and you shouldn't try. Winter is also the quietest and most intimate season, with long sleeps, bodies heaped together for warmth, and the city barely moving. It is not a season for strangers.

**Spring thaw: do not go, or go only to work.** The lowest tier floods, the eels arrive, and the root wakes hungry. Every tenant is cutting day and night because passages can close within a week. A visitor who cannot cut is a mouth, a body to step around, and a warm thing breathing into rooms the root is already reaching for. If you are strong, can hold a blade, and are willing to be directed by touch alone, you might be welcome. Otherwise you are a burden at the worst possible moment.

**Summer: the gentlest choice.** The water is low, the cutting is steady rather than desperate, and there is food in the drained channels. The walls are busiest with young animals, which is harder on the nerves, but the city has the most room for you in every sense.

**Autumn, timed for the eel-leaving: the most extraordinary choice.** The eels leave over three nights, and the tenants sit at the water's edge in the dark and feel the cold current of their going. If you want to understand the Knot and not just survive it, this is probably the moment. Be aware of three things. The entrance passage may be thick with eels on your way in. The water is cold and getting colder. And if you linger, the freeze is coming.

**If in doubt, go in late summer and stay into the first autumn nights.**

## Crossing the fen

You have to reach the hummock before anything else. It is one of a hundred like it.

- **Get precise coordinates or a guide.** The distinguishing marks are the red-and-green sphagnum, the stunted alders leaning off one shoulder, a height of about a kneeling person, and frost on top most mornings. None of these is unique. If your multiverse method lets you arrive near a fixed point, arrive as close as possible.
- **Treat the fen itself as a hazard.** Every step sinks. Black pools between tussocks can be deeper than they look. Carry a long pole to probe ahead and to spread your weight if you break through. Step on tussock bases, not their tops.
- **Wear boots you can abandon.** You will not bring them through the swim. Plan to cache them, or accept losing them.
- **Arrive rested, fed, and dry.** You want to start the swim with as much body heat as you can.

## The swim

The only way in is through about three body-lengths of black water under the hummock's edge, on one breath. Three body-lengths does not sound like much. In total darkness, in near-freezing water, under a mass of roots, it will feel like much more.

### Train for it before you go

- **Cold water acclimatization.** Do repeated cold immersions over several weeks. The main danger is the *cold shock response*: an involuntary gasp when icy water hits your face and chest. If that gasp happens underwater, you inhale water. Acclimatization blunts it considerably.
- **Static breath-holding.** Train until you can hold comfortably for at least a minute, ideally two, always with a partner and never alone in water. The swim itself should take well under thirty seconds. You want a wide margin, because in the dark, disoriented, you may take longer.
- **Blind swimming.** Practice swimming short distances with blacked-out goggles, following a line or a wall by touch.

### On the day

- **Wade in slowly first.** Let your body take the cold shock *before* your face goes under. Stand in the water to your chest, breathe through the gasping, and wait until your breathing is under control.
- **Do not hyperventilate before diving.** It feels like it helps. It actually increases the risk of blacking out underwater.
- **Follow a tenant if you possibly can.** The passage runs under root, and roots can snag. A tenant knows the way by touch. Keep a hand on their ankle or on a line they carry.
- **Carry a small fixed-blade knife strapped to your forearm,** for cutting yourself free if a root or strap snags. Keep it sheathed and secured. In the city itself, a steel blade is a remarkable object, and you should be thoughtful about when and whether you show it.
- **Streamline.** Nothing loose, nothing dangling, nothing that can catch.
- **In autumn, expect eels.** They are not aggressive, but swimming through a mass of cold, slick, muscular bodies in the dark is exactly as unsettling as it sounds. Go slowly. They part.

### What to swim in

Consider swimming in very little: a thin wetsuit, or bare skin with fat or grease for insulation. Carry your dry clothing sealed in a waterproof bag, or in a bladder in the tenants' own fashion, and change once you're through. A drysuit keeps you warm for the swim, but it is loud, bulky, and useless once you're inside.

### When you surface

You will come up gasping into total darkness. Keep one hand overhead as you rise so you don't strike root. Find the edge. Breathe. Don't shout, even in relief. Someone will probably already be there. They will have felt the water move.

## Clothing for the inside

The Knot is cold, wet, and dark, with no fire and no way to dry anything. It is also quiet, and it is full of rootlets that come for anything warm that holds still.

**Material:**
- **Wool, and plenty of it.** Wool stays warm when damp, and you will be damp for your entire visit. Merino base layers with a heavier wool outer layer work well.
- **Avoid cotton,** which will chill you once wet.
- **Avoid noisy synthetics.** Anything that rustles, crinkles, or swishes will seem very loud in the Knot. Nylon shells, Velcro, and zippers are all audible in a place where the loudest ordinary sound is a shrew's squeak.

**Fit:**
- **Close-fitting but not tight.** Loose folds give rootlets somewhere to thread in while you sleep. Cuffs at wrist and ankle should be snug.
- **A close-fitting wool cap for your hair.** Rootlets go into hair. Consider cutting yours short before the trip, or bind it tightly and keep it covered.
- **Gloves are a trade-off.** You'll want them for warmth, but you need your bare fingertips to read the rope code and feel the walls. Consider fingerless gloves, or thin gloves you can pull off easily and keep somewhere you can find them by touch.

**Feet:**
- The floors are spongy root sweating cold water that beads and runs in the grooves. Your feet will be wet constantly.
- **Bring multiple pairs of wool socks** and rotate them, keeping spares against your body, where they will be warmer and drier than anywhere else.
- Thin, flexible, soft-soled shoes or felted wool slippers let you feel the floor and move quietly. Hard boots will clomp and cut you off from the ground.
- **Watch for trench foot** (immersion foot). Dry and massage your feet whenever you can. On a long visit, this is a real risk.

**Scent:**
- **Wear no fragrance at all.** No perfume, scented soap, scented deodorant, or scented lotion. The tenants navigate partly by smell, and a room going wrong smells of *warm sweetness*. Something sweet on your body could confuse them or cause alarm. Arrive smelling of as little as possible.

## Light: the hardest temptation

Nothing forbids you from bringing a light. You should think very carefully before using one.

**The case against:**
- The tenants have adapted to darkness over lifetimes, or at least many days. A sudden light could be painful and disorienting for them.
- It would set the whole city moving. If loud speech makes harvestmen drop and turns the hiss into a roar, light would likely do the same or worse.
- It would reset your own dark adaptation. The foxfire in the old lower rooms is visible only to someone who has been in the dark for many days, and a single flash takes that away from you.
- It changes what you came to experience. Seen by lamplight, the Knot would be a hollow full of insects. The Knot the tenants live in is a place known by air, touch, and smell.

**The case for:**
- Emergencies happen. You might be injured, lost, or trapped by a closing passage.

**Recommendation:** If you bring a light at all, bring one small, dim, red-filtered light, sealed in a waterproof pouch. Use it only in genuine emergencies and, if possible, only after warning the nearest tenant by touch. Never use anything that burns. There is no fire in the Knot because fire would kill the root, and the root is the city. That means no candles, matches, lighters, or stoves of any kind.

Chemical hand warmers are not fire, but think twice about those too. The root grows toward warmth, and you would be carrying a small warm thing into a place where warmth attracts it. A few, used sparingly while you're awake and moving, are probably fine.

## Training your senses before you go

This is the part most travelers skip, and the part that matters most.

### Live blind

For at least a week before your visit, and longer if you can, spend long stretches blindfolded. Aim for full days at a time. Learn to:
- Move through a room by touch and memory.
- Tell where you are by air currents, sound, and smell.
- Eat, dress, and manage your belongings without sight.
- Find things you've put down. In the Knot, anything you set down and forget the location of is gone.

### Expect the prisoner's cinema

In total darkness, your brain will start inventing images: flashes, shapes, drifting colors, sometimes faces. This is a well-documented phenomenon and it is harmless. It can be alarming if you don't expect it, so expect it. It may take days before your eye "stops insisting," as the description puts it, and you begin to see the faint thinning of black that is foxfire.

### Learn knots by touch

The ropes along the spiral are knotted in a code giving quarter, tier, and distance to water. You won't learn the code before you arrive. Nobody outside can teach it to you. But you can train your hands to distinguish knots by feel. Have someone tie a series of different knots on a rope, then read them blindfolded. The goal is not to know the Knot's language. It is to have hands that are ready to learn it.

### Learn to be quiet

Practice moving silently. Practice speaking in murmurs so low they barely carry. And practice *not* making sudden sounds, which is harder:
- **Coughs and sneezes.** If you're prone to them, work out beforehand how to muffle them into your clothing or your elbow. A sneeze in the Knot may set the whole city stirring.
- **Snoring.** If you snore, you have a real problem. Address it before you go, and tell whoever sleeps near you.
- **Startle reactions.** Something will drop onto your neck in the dark. You will want to shout. Train yourself not to.

## The animals: preparing for the swarm

The Knot is a clearing inside a swarm. Every surface is alive. This is going to be the hardest part for many travelers.

### Honest self-assessment

If you have a strong phobia of insects, spiders, or crawling things, **this place may not be for you.** Harvestmen hang from the ceilings in clusters the size of a head. Walls move under your fingertips with the grain of countless mites. Amphipods drift over the stone in pale sheets. You will feel legs constantly. Exposure therapy beforehand may help. But be truthful with yourself about whether you can endure weeks of this without panicking, because panic in the Knot means noise, and noise sets the whole city moving.

### Specific creatures

- **Amphipods, springtails, mites.** Harmless. They are the texture of the place. Let them be.
- **Harvestmen.** Harmless and not venomous. They fall when disturbed. Brush them off gently and don't flail.
- **Cave crickets.** Harmless. Their antennae will brush your face endlessly. You'll get used to it, mostly.
- **Leeches.** The tenants value them. You'll likely acquire some, especially wading in the lower tiers. Don't rip them off, which can leave mouthparts in the wound. Let them finish and drop off, or ask a tenant how they remove them. Expect the small wounds to bleed for a while.
- **Shrews.** Tiny, ferocious predators. They are unlikely to attack you, but may bite if handled or cornered, and some shrews have mildly venomous saliva. Don't reach blindly into crevices.
- **Centipedes.** Hand-length, hunting the young of shrews. A large centipede bite is painful and can swell badly. Be careful where you put your hands and where you lie down. Shake out clothing before you put it on.
- **Eels.** Not dangerous, but overwhelming in number in autumn. The tenants wade among them to the waist.
- **Frogs.** Frozen in the walls in winter. If you touch something cold, still, and frog-shaped in a wall, leave it. It is not dead. It will walk out in the spring.

## The root: rules for living inside something that grows

The root grows toward breath, warmth, moisture, and anything that lives and exhales. It does not attack. It simply reaches.

### Sleeping

- **Never sleep long in one place.**
- **Never sleep twice in the same room in a row.** The tenants don't, and you shouldn't either.
- **Sleep curled.** Less surface, less warmth leaking out, fewer lines for rootlets to follow.
- **Sleep in shifts, with someone awake nearby** if you can arrange it. Ask for a tenant or a traveling companion to check on you.
- **When you wake, check yourself** for rootlets in your hair, clothing, and along your skin. They are harmless if pulled away early. Remove them promptly and gently.
- **If you are ill, injured, or exhausted, tell someone.** The tenants give the very old and very ill, who cannot move, to the root on purpose. A visitor who collapses and lies still in an empty room is in real danger, not from malice but from the plain arithmetic of the place.

### Smell as warning

Learn the three smells:
- **Green and bitter:** fresh cuts. Healthy.
- **Vinegar:** old cuts. Normal.
- **Warm sweetness:** the root has begun to take something. **Leave, and tell someone.**

### Air

A room shrinking stops breathing. Its draughts change. If a space feels close and still in a way it didn't before, move toward moving air. That means toward the cold draught of the entrance or the colder one rising off the water.

Be aware that the air in the Knot may be poor overall. Sixty people and a vast living root-ball are breathing inside a sealed hollow. You may get headaches, fatigue, or a sense of heaviness. Stay near moving air when you can, and give yourself time to adjust.

### The withy-screened rooms

In the lower tiers, some rooms have withy screens across the doorways and are not cut open again for many years. These are where the old and ill were given to the root. **Do not enter them. Do not move the screens.** Whatever else you do in the Knot, leave those rooms alone.

## Health and medical preparation

There is no medical help in the Knot beyond what the tenants know. Prepare accordingly.

**Before you go:**
- Make sure your tetanus vaccination is current. Wet wood, shell blades, and root punctures are a textbook tetanus environment.
- Consider vaccination against hepatitis A, and ask a travel doctor about leptospirosis prophylaxis. You'll be in stagnant freshwater with small mammals.
- Get a dental checkup. A toothache in the dark for weeks would be miserable.
- If you take regular medication, bring a surplus in waterproof, touch-distinguishable containers, since you won't be able to read the labels.

**Bring, in a sealed pouch organized so you can find things by touch:**
- Antiseptic and wound dressings. Small cuts will be constant, and in constant wet they can fester.
- A course of broad-spectrum antibiotics, prescribed in advance, for wound infections.
- Antifungal cream for feet and skin folds. Wet wool, no drying, and weeks of darkness add up to fungal problems.
- Antihistamines, for bites.
- Water purification tablets or a small filter. Fen water may carry parasites and bacteria.
- Balm for your hands. Constant wet and cold will crack them, and your hands are your eyes.

**While there:**
- Watch yourself for hypothermia. Symptoms are shivering that becomes uncontrollable, then stops; clumsiness; confusion; and a strange apathy. The last one is dangerous, because a hypothermic person stops caring. Tell someone early. The tenants sleep in heaps to share warmth. Accept the heap if it's offered.
- Keep your feet as dry as you can.
- Keep eating. Cold burns calories fast.

## Food, water, and waste

- **Bring your own food** for as much of your stay as you can: dense, calorie-rich, non-crumbling, non-fragrant, packed in quiet (not crinkly) waterproof containers. The tenants live on eels, mussels, snails, and the roots of water plants, stored in birch-bark-lined pits. They have enough but not a great deal. Arriving as an extra mouth is a real cost to them.
- **If you're offered food, accept graciously,** but don't take much. Eel and mussel will be the likely offering, probably raw, since there is no fire. Consider in advance whether you can stomach raw eel.
- **Don't drop food.** The swarm will find it immediately. Crumbs mean shrews and crickets converging on you.
- **Water:** purify what you drink.
- **Waste:** the description doesn't say how the tenants manage it. Ask early, by whatever means you can, and follow their practice exactly. In a sealed, living hollow, this matters a great deal.

## Arriving: etiquette and the tenants

### How you'll likely be read

The tenants know that people come in from outside because of debt, grief, or curiosity, find the swim, and do not come back up. They will probably assume that you are one of these and that you've come to stay. Be clear, as best you can without shared words, that you are a visitor. But understand that from their side, the difference may matter less than it does to you.
