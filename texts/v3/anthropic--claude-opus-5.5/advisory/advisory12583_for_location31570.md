# Preparing for the Condensary at Height

The most useful thing to know before you go is that the Condensary is not dangerous the way cliffs and wild animals are dangerous. It is dangerous the way a greenhouse, a boiler room, and a construction site are dangerous, all at once, and all of them wet. Almost everything that can go wrong for a visitor comes from moisture, heat, fresh building materials, or the dark. None of it is dramatic, but all of it adds up over three or four days.

It is also a place that asks you to be quiet and careful. Prepare for both.

---

## 1. Understand the Two Climates

You will pass through two opposite environments, and you have to be dressed for both, often within a single minute.

**Outside, on the climb and the platform:** cold, saturated cloud, with probably some altitude to it. Cloud is rain that hasn't bothered to fall. It soaks you from every direction at once, slowly and thoroughly, without ever seeming to "rain." This is classic hypothermia weather: not freezing, just cold and wet with no relief.

**Inside the drum:** warm, perhaps close to tropical, at 100% humidity, with a small rainstorm every eighty seconds. Sweat does not evaporate, so it cannot cool you. You will overheat more easily than the temperature suggests, and you will stay damp the entire time.

The transition between them is abrupt. The account says your coat comes off within a minute. Going back out is worse. You leave the drum soaked in warm sweat and step into cold cloud, which is exactly how people get chilled on mountains.

### Clothing strategy

- **No cotton anywhere.** Not socks, not underwear, not a T-shirt. Cotton wet is cotton cold, and it will never dry here. Use synthetic or merino wool, which keeps some insulating value when soaked.
- **Inside layer:** a light synthetic shirt and shorts or thin trousers. Assume they will be wet the whole time. Their job is only to protect your skin from brick, iron, and plants.
- **Outside layer:** a proper waterproof shell with a hood, plus an insulating mid-layer (fleece or synthetic fill, not down). Down collapses in this humidity.
- **Sacred dry set:** one full set of dry clothes and socks, sealed in a roll-top dry bag inside another dry bag. **Never open it inside the Condensary.** It exists for the descent, or for an emergency if you get chilled. If you use it inside, it will be damp within ten minutes and you will have nothing.
- **Gloves:** two kinds. Thin work gloves with rubberised palms for handling ironwork and brick, and warm waterproof gloves for the climb.
- **A hat or cap with a brim.** The inward rain falls in slanting veils from above, and a brim keeps it off your eyes and glasses.

## 2. Your Skin and Feet Will Be the First Casualties

Four days of never being dry is hard on a human body in ways people underestimate.

- **Feet:** Prolonged wetness leads to maceration (white, wrinkled, fragile skin), then blisters, then something close to trench foot. Bring several pairs of quick-dry socks. Change them at least twice a day even though the new pair is also damp. Before you go in, coat your feet thickly with a barrier ointment (zinc oxide, lanolin, or a purpose-made anti-maceration balm) and reapply it each morning. Wear boots or sturdy water shoes that drain, not boots designed to keep water out. Water *will* get in, from above, and a waterproof boot then becomes a bucket.
- **Hands, groin, armpits:** fungal rash and chafing. Bring antifungal powder or cream and a lubricating anti-chafe stick.
- **Cuts heal slowly when they never dry.** Bring waterproof dressings (hydrocolloid or film), antiseptic, and adhesive strips that work on wet skin. Tincture of benzoin helps things stick.
- **Glasses fog constantly.** Bring an anti-fog treatment and a leash, or wear contacts. If you choose contacts, be scrupulous about hygiene: bring sealed saline, and remember that the ambient water is not clean enough to touch your eyes.

---

## 3. The Building Is Unfinished, and Unfinished Things Bite

The account keeps drawing attention to how *new* everything is. That newness is a genuine hazard.

### Fresh mortar and concrete are caustic

Uncured cement is strongly alkaline. On prolonged contact with wet skin it causes **cement burns**, which are chemical burns that often don't hurt until they are already deep. The mortar here is soft enough to take a fingernail mark, it is "still dark with water" on the new courses, and water runs over it constantly.

- Do not press your hands into fresh mortar, lean bare-armed on the top courses, or kneel on them in thin trousers.
- Wear gloves when touching the upper terraces, and rinse any skin that has been in contact with mortar or mortar-runoff.
- Bring a small bottle of dilute vinegar or a pH-neutral wash for rinsing. A clean-water rinse is the most important part, but the wash helps.
- Be careful with your eyes. Wiping your face with a mortar-dusted glove is a classic way to get lime in them. Pack an eyewash bottle.

### The water is not drinking water, no matter how pure it looks

The rain itself begins as condensation, which is nearly distilled. By the time it has run over leaves, iron, fresh cement, iron-filing soil, and spent coke, though, it may be high in pH and carrying dissolved metals, lime, and whatever lives in the algae. The water in the channels is part of the garden's plumbing, not yours.

- **Bring your own water, or a serious treatment system.** A good filter plus chemical or UV treatment would handle most biological risk. It won't remove high alkalinity or dissolved minerals. If you want to rely on local water, the cleanest option is to collect rain directly from the air at the rim, before it touches anything, using a clean tarp or funnel into a bottle, and then treat it.
- **Bring electrolytes.** Two effects combine here: you will sweat heavily without noticing, because it never evaporates, and nearly-distilled water replenishes none of the salt you lose. Headaches, cramps, and lethargy are likely without salt tablets or rehydration powder.

### Cast iron with casting seams

The balustrades and fins carry sharp flashing where the mould halves met, "sharp enough to nick a thumb." Wear gloves when you handle them. Make sure your tetanus vaccination is current before you go: iron, rust, soil, and small cuts that stay wet for days make exactly the conditions where you want it.

### Load limits are real

The girders are stenciled **LOAD NOT TO EXCEED**, and the new brickwork is audibly settling under its own weight. Take this seriously.

- Travel light. Know what you and your pack weigh together.
- Don't hang your whole weight, or a hammock, from the newest ironwork.
- Stay off anything that isn't clearly a walking surface. Troughs are for plants, and the manufactured earth may be deeper and softer than it looks.

## 4. The Heat Below

Something under the floor is burning, boiling, or pressing, and it radiates heat through the grates. Some cautions follow from that.

- **Don't sleep on or near the grates.** The heat is steady and, with each breath, it comes with a surge of hot vapour. Steam burns are severe and fast. Sleeping over a grate for eight hours could scald you, or dehydrate you badly, without waking you.
- **Consider a carbon monoxide detector.** The account mentions spent coke in the soil, which suggests coke is burned below. Coke combustion produces carbon monoxide. The vapour that rises is probably mostly steam, but you can't know that, and the drum is a bowl. A small personal CO monitor clipped to your pack weighs almost nothing. If it alarms, climb to the rim or leave. Other signs to watch for are headache, dizziness, and unusual drowsiness, especially down on the lowest terraces.
- **Don't drop anything through the grates.** Tether small items: keys, lens caps, pens. Anything that falls through is gone, and it goes into a machine you don't understand and shouldn't interfere with.
- **Soles:** boots with thin or low-melting soles may soften if you stand on the lowest floor for long. Standard rubber-soled hiking boots should be fine, but don't linger.

---

## 5. The Night Is the Hardest Part

At night, the fog is so thick you can't see an arm's length. The only light is a dim red glow at the very bottom. And the building is being built, somewhere above you, by something you never see.

### Light

- Bring a headlamp with a **red-light mode** and spare batteries in a waterproof case. The red mode protects your night vision and disturbs the place less. In dense fog, a white beam mostly lights up the fog itself and blinds you.
- Bring a second light. Things fail in this humidity.
- Some chemical light sticks can mark the edge of your sleeping spot.

### Where to spend the night

- **Pick your sleeping spot in daylight** and don't move far from it after dark.
- **Stay low, on the older terraces.** They are more stable, the plants are established, and they are far from the rim.
- **Stay well away from the top course at night.** That is where the building happens. You don't know what does the building, what it does if you are in the way, or whether the newest course is laid on the place you happen to be sitting. The account notes that you can hear the construction but never see it. Treat that as a boundary, not a puzzle to solve. Wanting to peek is natural, but you would be wandering blind in fog, at the top of an open drum, on uncured masonry, near an unknown process. It's the most dangerous thing you could do here.
- If you must move at night, keep one hand on a wall or balustrade. Ideally, run a light line from your sleeping spot to somewhere you need to reach, such as a spot to relieve yourself.

### Edges

The drum is open at the top, and it stands on a column whose base disappears into cloud. There is a real fall risk at the rim and on the outer platform. In fog there is no visual warning of an edge. Only your feet and hands will tell you. Consider a short tether or a simple harness if you plan to be near the rim at all in poor visibility.

### Sleeping

- The ground is wet and warm everywhere. A **closed-cell foam mat** won't absorb water. Top it with a thin **bivvy bag or waterproof groundsheet**.
- You won't need a sleeping bag for warmth inside. A thin synthetic liner or sheet is enough, and it matters more for comfort and for keeping plants and moths off you.
- **Earplugs** may help, though the account suggests the sound is gentle.
- The breath will come every eighty seconds all night. You feel it in your sternum more than you hear it. Some people find this deeply soothing, like sleeping on a ship. Others find that it wakes them over and over. Try, before you go, sleeping with something rhythmic nearby to test yourself.

## 6. Your Gear Versus the Humidity

Assume that anything not sealed will be wet within an hour, and that anything wet will stay wet until you leave.

- **Dry bags for everything:** food, clothes, first aid, electronics, and paper each go in their own bag. Put silica gel packets inside, though they'll saturate over several days.
- **Electronics:** condensation is the real enemy, more than the rain. Carrying a cold device from the cloud into the warm drum will fog it inside and out. Let devices warm up inside a sealed bag before you open them. Waterproof cameras are ideal.
- **Paper:** bring waterproof "all-weather" notebooks and pencils. Ink pens will fail or smear.
- **Metal tools:** anything steel will rust. Oil blades before you go, and bring a small rag and oil.
- **Towels:** ordinary towels are useless. A chamois or synthetic pack towel that you can wring out is the only kind that works.
- **Fire:** you won't need it and won't get it. Matches and lighters will mostly fail, and there's nothing dry to burn anyway. Plan to eat **cold food**. If you want hot drinks, bring a compact stove, used only on the outer platform and with great care. It is probably better to simply do without.

---

## 7. Food

Bring everything you'll eat, in sealed packaging.

- Choose food that doesn't need cooking and won't spoil in heat and moisture: nut butters, hard cheese, crackers in sealed sleeves, dried fruit, foil-pouched fish, energy bars.
- Bread and anything soft will mould within a day or two.
- Pack slightly salty food on purpose, to help with electrolytes.
- **Don't eat the plants.** Not the ferns, not the white bell-flowers, not the plate-leaf tree. They grow in a soil of iron filings and spent coke, which may carry heavy metals and combustion residues. And they're unknown species. Also, they aren't yours.
- Seal your food waste and carry all of it out. There are almost no animals here to break waste down.

---

## 8. Health Beyond the Obvious

- **Fitness:** the "long climb through cloud" is a real climb, on wet surfaces, in poor visibility, carrying everything you'll need for several days. Train for it.
- **Altitude:** the account hints that the Condensary sits very high. If it's above about 2,500 m in real terms, acclimatize on the way up, ascend slowly, and know the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, breathlessness at rest, and confusion. If symptoms appear inside, don't mistake them for the heat.
- **Mould and spores:** a warm, wet, planted enclosure is ideal for fungi. If you have asthma or a mould allergy, bring your medication and consider a mask for sleeping.
- **Tetanus:** see above. Get boosted.
- **Heat illness:** listen to your body. If you feel heavy, dizzy, or strangely irritable, climb to the upper terraces, where the cold cloud mixes in. Stand at the rim and let the incoming air cool you. Drink water with salts.

---

## 9. Leave No Trace, Including the Traces You Can't See

This place is a sealed, slowly developing system that is just beginning. Only a handful of animal species live here, and the account suggests it is waiting for more. That makes it fragile in a particular way: anything you introduce may become the first thing to arrive.

- **Clean your gear before you come.** Brush and wash your boots, trouser cuffs, pack, and tent pegs, to remove seeds, soil, insects, eggs, and fungal spores from other worlds. An alien weed or beetle could have enormous consequences in an ecosystem this simple.
- **Don't take seeds out.** The seeds come in the mortar, and you'll probably discover this yourself by crumbling a pinch of fresh mortar. Put it back. These seeds belong to a system that sows itself as it builds. Removing them, and possibly planting them somewhere else, is an intrusion both ways.
- **Don't mark the mortar.** It takes a fingernail mark, which means it also takes a carved initial. That would be preserved as the building cures. Don't.
- **Don't handle the snails.** They may be the oldest living things in the building, their shells are so thin you can see through them, and one careless touch could kill one. Look only.
- **Don't chase the moths.** There are no more than a dozen. Let them fly their spirals.
- **Human waste:** there is no soil to dig in, and anything left will wash into the drainage and down into the machinery. The honest answer is to bring waste bags (the kind used in mountaineering and canyoneering) and carry everything out. It's unpleasant, but it's correct.
- **Keep your voice low.** It isn't forbidden, but everything else here is quiet, and the quiet feels intentional. You'll probably find yourself whispering anyway.

## 10. Psychological Preparation

The account describes the rhythm getting into your body, so that you start breathing with the eighty-second cycle without meaning to. This is gentle, but after several days it can be disorienting.

- **Timekeeping:** bring a watch, a real one and waterproof. Between the steady cloudlight by day, the absolute dark by night, the fog, and the hypnotic cycle, it's easy to lose track of how long you've been there. Write the date and time in your notebook every morning.
- **Plan your stay in advance** and decide your departure time before you arrive. Places like this make leaving feel arbitrary, and you'll want a decision you made while you were still thinking clearly.
- **Expect unease.** You're in a building that builds itself in the dark, that sows itself, that has a count climbing past four thousand, and that was clearly designed for inhabitants who aren't there yet. Most visitors feel wonder. Some feel an itchy, empty-house wrongness. Both are reasonable. It may help to remember that everything here is working exactly as intended, and nothing is straining. That is what the quiet means.
- **Resist the need to solve it.** You won't find out why it's here, and trying to catch the builders is where people get hurt. It's better to come with the intention of *witnessing*. Watch a frond open over a day. Watch the bell-flowers blink. Wait for the small rainbow after the breath. Count the fins.

---

## 11. Leaving

- The morning you leave, **go up to the rim first.** Stand in the mixing air and let yourself cool down gradually rather than stepping from the hot drum straight into the cold cloud.
- Towel off as well as you can. Then **open your sacred dry bag on the outer platform, in whatever shelter you can find, and change completely** before you start the descent. Put your wet clothes in a separate bag.
- Eat and drink something with salt before you start.
- Descend carefully. Your feet will be soft from days of wetness, and the path is wet and cloud-blind.
- Look back once. You won't see the building, but you'll see the small circle of rain in the middle of the white. The account says this is the last thing people remember.

## Packing List Summary

**Clothing:** synthetic/merino base layers (no cotton), light inner clothes, waterproof shell, synthetic insulating layer, brimmed hat, work gloves, warm waterproof gloves, several pairs of quick-dry socks, draining boots, one fully sealed dry set.

**Body care:** barrier/anti-maceration foot balm, antifungal powder, anti-chafe stick, waterproof dressings, antiseptic, eyewash, pH-neutral or dilute-vinegar skin rinse, electrolyte salts, any personal medications (especially asthma), a current tetanus vaccination.

**Safety:** red-mode headlamp plus a backup, spare batteries, light sticks, CO monitor, short tether or harness for the rim, light guide line, watch.

**Water & food:** carried water or a filter with chemical/UV treatment, a clean funnel or tarp for rain collection, sealed no-cook food, waste bags for food and human waste.

**Camp:** closed-cell foam mat, bivvy bag or groundsheet, thin synthetic liner, earplugs, chamois towel.

**Gear protection:** multiple dry bags, silica gel, waterproof notebook and pencils, anti-fog treatment, tethers for small items, oil and rag for metal tools.

**Ethics:** gear cleaned of seeds and soil before arrival, and the intention to take nothing out and leave nothing behind, not even a fingernail mark.

---

Go gently. It is a garden that is still being made, and you are one of the very first things ever to walk through it.
