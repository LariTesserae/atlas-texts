# Preparing for the Murmuring Levels

## The Short Version

The Levels will probably not kill you. They aren't hostile, and almost nothing lives there that could hurt you. What they will do is wear you down in ordinary ways and unsettle you in extraordinary ones.

The ordinary dangers are the serious ones: wet cold, rotting feet, exhaustion, noise, and getting lost in sixty miles of featureless bog. The extraordinary things, such as borrowed skies, rain arriving before its cloud, and your own footprints coming toward you, are mostly harmless. They become dangerous only when they panic you or trick you into bad decisions.

So prepare like a bog walker, and cultivate the temperament of someone sitting through a very long, very strange piece of music.

---

## 1. The Sound: Your First and Constant Problem

People underestimate this more than anything else. The drone is not background. It is the environment.

**Protect your hearing, in layers.**
- Bring foam earplugs and over-ear muffs, and wear both near the peak of the cycle. A cheap sound-level meter or dosimeter is worth carrying. Your sense of how loud something is will adapt, but your cochlea won't.
- Hearing protection does little against the deepest part of the drone, the part you feel in your sternum. You cannot block it; you can only limit how long you sit in it.

**Expect the body to respond to the low frequencies.** Sustained deep sound often produces mild nausea, pressure in the chest, fatigue, a vague dread, or sudden unexplained sadness. Knowing in advance that this is physical, and not the place meaning something at you, removes most of its power. Anti-nausea tablets help some people.

**You will not be able to talk.** Plan for it before you go, not once you're in it.
- Agree on hand signals with companions: stop, come here, look, turn back, I'm cold, I'm not okay, peak coming.
- Carry a waterproof notebook and a pencil (pens fail in damp) for anything complex.
- Whistles are useless. For signaling at a distance, carry something visual: a bright flag, a strobe or torch. Mist scatters light, so keep distances short.
- In willow carr, where you can lose sight of someone at three paces, clip to each other with a short line.

**Learn the nineteen-hour cycle before you go deep.** Spend your first day or two at the rim, on the low hill, timing the drone. Note when the lull comes and when the peak comes. Nineteen hours means it slides against your day, so the peak might fall at noon one day and at dawn the next. Keep a written schedule. This cycle is your real clock in the Levels, more useful than the sun, which you mostly won't see.

**Plan around it.** Walk during the falling drone and the lull. Be stationary during the peak: sitting, sheltered, somewhere you know.

## 2. Wet Cold: The Real Danger

The Levels sit in the most treacherous temperature band for humans. It's cool, damp, never freezing, never drying. People die of hypothermia in exactly this weather because it doesn't feel dangerous. Your breath will show in the mornings, and you will be damp from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave.

**Clothing**
- Wear wool or synthetics only. No cotton anywhere, including underwear.
- Use a layering system: a thin base, a warm fleece or wool mid-layer, and a genuinely waterproof, breathable shell with a hood. The drizzle comes from nowhere and doesn't stop, and mist soaks you as effectively as rain.
- Pack a dry set of warm clothes sealed in a drybag. It is only for sleeping and emergencies, and it never gets worn while walking.
- Bring a warm hat and gloves. Waterproof gloves are good, and spare liner gloves are better, because your hands will be in cold water often and it aches.

**Accept that nothing dries.** No sun, no wind to speak of, no dry fuel for fires. Anything that gets wet stays wet unless you dry it with your own body heat, by keeping tomorrow's socks inside your sleeping bag. Pack enough to rotate. Put everything in drybags inside your pack: sleeping bag, electronics, food, notebook.

**Carry a stove and fuel.** Hot drinks and hot food are your main defense against slow cold. You cannot rely on making fire: the alder is saturated, and cutting it reveals that shimmering banded wood, which you probably shouldn't burn anyway.

**Watch each other.** The early signs of hypothermia are grumpiness, clumsiness, and poor judgment. In the Levels you're already disoriented, and the drone already makes you feel strange, so it is easy to miss. Check on companions deliberately, at set times.

## 3. Feet

Every step presses up cold water. You'll sink to the shin in sphagnum. There are channels to ford and pools hidden under moss.

- **Boots.** Tall rubber boots or waterproof boots with high gaiters. Some bog walkers go the other way, wearing quick-draining shoes with neoprene socks and accepting wet feet. That works in warm bogs. In cool ones it invites trench foot. Choose deliberately.
- **Socks.** Many pairs of good wool socks. Change them at every long stop.
- **Foot care every night.** Dry your feet completely, inspect them, and air them. Use foot powder or balm and antifungal cream. Trench foot starts as numbness, swelling, and pale wrinkled skin, and if ignored it becomes a genuine injury. Several days of continuous wet is enough.
- **A walking pole or staff.** Use it to probe ahead. You can't see how deep the water under the moss is, and the channels have soft slumped banks.

## 4. Navigation: Where the Levels Genuinely Break the Rules

This is the part without a normal solution.

Even an ordinary sixty-mile marsh with no feature taller than a person, perpetual mist, and no visible sun would be hard to navigate. The Levels add distances that don't add up, ground that folds, the same hummock appearing from three sides, and your own fresh footprints coming toward you.

**Principles:**

1. **The rim is stable. The interior is not.** The strangeness grows as you go deeper. On your first visit, stay within a few miles of the edge. Make day trips out from a base camp on the high ground and return before the peak. The depth of your trips should grow only as your understanding does.

2. **Carry a compass and see whether it behaves.** Nothing suggests magnetism is affected, but test it at the rim, then again a mile in. If it still agrees with itself, it's your best tool. Don't assume that anything relying on satellites or signals from your home universe will work.

3. **Know the gradients.** The basin is a dish, so the ground rises, very subtly, toward the edge. The drone is loudest at the heart, near the dark stub. In the lull, the direction in which things get quieter is probably outward. Treat this as a rough guide, not a guarantee, but it is something.

4. **Mark your route, and expect your markers to misbehave.** Carry distinctive markers that you can remove later, such as numbered ribbons, and tie them as you go. Coming upon marker #14 when you've only placed #9 tells you the ground has folded, which is itself useful to know. Take them down on the way back.

5. **Don't follow the footprints coming toward you.** Don't investigate them either. Note them in your log and keep to your own plan.

6. **Don't navigate by pool-light.** The illumination leaking from pools, like the gold of a sunset that isn't happening or the glow of an absent moon, is beautiful, and it carries no information about where you are. A pool showing a dry channel doesn't mean the ground is firm. A pool showing night doesn't mean it's getting dark.

7. **Keep a log.** Write down times, compass bearings, landmarks, markers placed, and anything strange. Your memory in there will be unreliable, not because the place attacks it but because it gives you so much that contradicts itself. Paper is an anchor.

8. **Use a mechanical watch.** Check it against the drone cycle, and check it against a companion's watch. If they disagree, write that down too.

9. **Leave word outside.** Someone at the rim should know your route and a firm turnaround time.

**If you get lost:** stop. Don't push on through the peak. Sit on the firmest hummock you can find, eat something hot, and wait for the lull. In the quiet, listen for which way the drone thins. Head that way and slightly uphill, against the grain of the ground. The folds seem to be local, so persistence in one direction does appear to carry people out eventually. Panic walking in the mist does not.

## 5. The Peak: What to Do When the World Flickers

At the drone's crest, the reeds shiver, the pools ring in fine rings, and for a few minutes the Levels lose track of which version of themselves they are. Dry and flooded, day and night, leaf and bare, all laid over each other.

**Before it arrives:**
- Get to a known, firm spot. A broad hummock is ideal. Don't be standing in a channel, on a slumped bank, or at the edge of a deep pool.
- Put on full hearing protection.
- Sit down. Seriously. Standing in overlapping realities while your ribs vibrate is a good way to fall over.

**During it:**
- Stay still. Don't try to walk through it.
- Hold on to something real: a companion's hand, your line, the pole planted in the ground.
- Focus on one physical thing, such as your own breathing or the texture of your glove. The visual chaos is enormous, but it passes in minutes.
- It is not violent and not aimed at you. Let it happen.

**After it:** check your companions, check your gear, check your watch, and write down what you saw. Some pools will still show the wrong season. That's normal.

## 6. Water, Food, and the Silt

**Drinking water is the hardest call.** Water is everywhere, but it has passed through engineered lattices that hold states. Nobody knows whether drinking it matters. It might be nothing. It might mean carrying something of the Levels inside you. Also, silt fine as flour will clog most filters almost instantly.

My recommendation: carry in as much water as practical for short trips. For longer ones, collect rainwater and condensation, since the mist beads on everything and a tarp will gather it. Treat that water as usual. Pool and channel water is a last resort, settled first and then filtered and treated.

**Bring all your food.** Do not forage. The roots carry the shimmer, and there's almost nothing else. Pack calorie-dense food: wet cold burns energy fast. Plan for more hot meals than you think you need.

**Handle the silt carefully.**
- It gets into everything: zippers, lenses, pockets, the creases of your hands. Wash before eating.
- Keep it out of your eyes. Mist, willow branches in the face, and silty hands make this harder than it sounds. Bring eye-wash.
- **Consider seriously before taking any out.** It's tempting to fill a vial with that shimmering mud. But the grains hold states, and it's not clear what happens when a fragment of a many-possibilities engine is carried into a world that holds only one. Clean your boots, gear, and clothes thoroughly at the rim before you leave. Take photographs, not samples.

## 7. Camping and Sleep

**Ground.** Pitching a tent on sphagnum means sleeping in a slowly welling puddle. Options:
- A **hammock with a tarp**, slung between alders. This is the best choice where the trees are sturdy. Check the roots and trunk, because some alders are half-fallen into the banks.
- If you must use the ground, pick the broadest, highest hummock, and use a thick closed-cell pad on a heavy groundsheet.
- For the first several nights, camp on the rim hill, out of the deep drone.

**Sleep will be hard.** The drone vibrates your ribs, and the nineteen-hour cycle fights your body's twenty-four-hour one. The light never changes much, so your internal clock has nothing to hold on to.
- Try to sleep during the lull, even if it falls at an "odd" time.
- Use earplugs, and accept that the deep register gets through.
- Some people find, after the first night or two, that the drone becomes soothing, like sleeping beside the sea. Others never adjust. Know which you are before planning a long trip.
- The perpetual dim grey lowers mood for many people. If you're prone to it, a small light-therapy lamp for mornings is not a ridiculous thing to pack.

**Eyesight.** Glasses fog and bead with mist constantly. Bring anti-fog treatment and a cloth kept in a sealed bag, or consider contacts for the trip if you tolerate them.

## 8. The Inhabitants

You may walk a whole day and see nothing larger than a water beetle. What you do meet:

- **The grey wader.** Its footprints arrive before its feet. Don't approach it. Just watch. It's possibly the calmest creature in any universe.
- **The eels.** Don't grab at them, and don't try to catch or eat one. An animal that may be in several pools at once is not something to put in your stomach. If you see the same eel in two places, it's fine to simply enjoy that.
- **The moths.** They're drawn to sound, not light, so your headtorch won't attract them. If you hum or sing in the lull, however, you may find yourself wreathed in them. They seem harmless. Don't inhale them. Do enjoy them.
- **The low call.** If you hear or feel that single note below the drone's floor, do what the eels do: go still. Nobody knows what makes it. There's no evidence that it's dangerous, and there's no reason to test that. Wait until it passes, note the time and direction, and don't walk toward it.

## 9. Echoes, Reflections, and Your Own Mind

**Echoes.** You'll be tempted to shout into the drone. Some people do it once to see. If the reply comes back with words you haven't said yet, don't argue with it, don't ask it questions, and above all don't let it make decisions for you. Whether it's a glimpse of your future or a glitch in an old machine, taking its advice turns a curiosity into a trap.

**Reflections.** If you catch your reflection a few seconds ahead of you, don't try to "catch up" or "match" it. Look away and carry on.

**Dissociation.** Hours of overlapping skies, folding ground, and bodily vibration can produce a feeling of unreality. You may lose the sense of which version of yourself is walking. Counter it with routine: check your watch, write in the log, change your socks, eat. The ordinary body tasks are the most grounding thing you have.

**Awe.** Prepare for this too. At some point, probably during a lull, with the moths hanging over the singing reeds and a pool at your feet showing snow, you will feel the scale of the place: that you're walking through the eroded body of a thought. Some people cry. That's fine. Give yourself time for it. Don't schedule your days so tightly that you can't just sit.

## 10. The Heart and the Stub

The dark knob in the sedge near the centre, where the drone is loudest and the pools show only clean grey blankness, is the obvious pilgrimage. Some advice:

- **Don't make it your first trip.** It sits at the deepest, least stable point, perhaps thirty miles through folding ground.
- If you do go, go with experience, companions, a generous supply margin, and a firm turnaround.
- Don't touch it. Don't dig around it. Don't take anything from it. If it is what holds the Levels' many states together, you don't want to be the person who nudged it. If it's a boulder, you've lost nothing by leaving it alone.
- Expect the drone there to be physically punishing. Limit your time.

## 11. Respect the Place

There are no rules posted here, but:

- The moss patterns may be writing. Don't walk on the patterned hummocks if you can avoid it, and never cut or peel them.
- Don't cut alder roots to see the banded shimmer. Other travelers have described it. Take their word.
- Remove your markers. Pack out everything.
- Speak softly in the lull, even though you could shout. The quiet is rare, and the moths and the wader are listening.
