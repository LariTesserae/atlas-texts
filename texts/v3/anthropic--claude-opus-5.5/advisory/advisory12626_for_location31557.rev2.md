# Preparing to Visit the Undercroft of the Fallen Bank

## First, Understand What You Are Agreeing To

This is not a dive site or a cave, and it isn't really a place you explore. It is a small, sealed, overfull room of cold water, packed wall to wall with living fabric, keeping an evening that belongs to someone else. There is no open water to swim in, no surface to rise to, no light to see by, and no path. You would be a large, warm, clumsy animal climbing inside a closed music box.

Be honest with yourself before anything else. **You cannot enter this place without damaging it.** Your body will displace a meaningful fraction of its water. Every movement will part a curtain of grass or lace-weed that took years to grow in the dark. The best you can do is make the intrusion small, slow, brief, and clean, and leave nothing behind. If you can't accept that, or can't accept the discipline it demands, don't go. Very little in the undercroft can recover quickly from you.

If you do go, the visit worth making is short, still, and mostly blind, built around three heartbeats of light.

---

## Timing

**Arrive at the turn toward spring.** This is the only time the undercroft is ever visible, when the river runs coldest and clearest and the sponges release their luminous water together. At any other time you will be in absolute darkness for your whole visit and will come away with nothing but the feel of moss and the cold.

You can't know the exact moment in advance. Plan to be in position for a window of hours or days, which means your transit mechanism needs to let you arrive, withdraw, and return several times. Don't try to wait it out in one long immersion. Brief repeated visits are safer for you and for the place.

**Be wary of the note.** Once a season or so, the whole pocket rings softly, like a finger around a glass rim, and the moss trembles. This is a new night being laid down and folded into the strata. Nobody knows what happens to a large foreign object present during that moment. The water keeps what falls into it, and you don't want to become part of a night. Because sound here arrives before its cause, you may hear the note, or its early edge, before the event itself. **Treat any glass-rim tone as an immediate signal to leave.** Your team on the other side of the transit should be ready to pull you out on your signal, or on no signal at all.

---

## Getting In and Getting Out

The pocket is sealed. Above it lies two arms' depth of soil and root mat, and above that a cold brown river. There is no exit you can swim to, dig through, or improvise. You are in what cave divers call an *overhead environment* of the most absolute kind: your only way out is the way you came in.

- **Your transit point is your lifeline.** Arrange for it to stay open, or be reopenable on demand, for the whole visit.
- **Stay tethered.** Attach a thin, smooth, pale retrieval line to your harness and let it pay back to your arrival point. You won't have anything to tie a guideline to without wrapping it around moss, roots, or the stacked slate. Don't do that. The tether is attached to *you* and to *the way home*, nothing else.
- **Distance is unreliable.** The far wall is always a little farther than it should be. Your line may pay out more than the room's apparent size can explain. Never navigate by estimated distance. Use line length and touch, and **don't try to reach the far wall at all.** A small room that keeps getting bigger is a bad place to go looking for its edges.
- **Choose your arrival point carefully.** The ideal place is the edge of the dusk layer near a wall, well clear of the floor, the eel's tunnel, the braid, and the cairn. Your team should scout it with a passive probe first, if they can do so without disturbing anything.

---

## Breathing

**Use a closed-circuit rebreather. Open-circuit scuba is not acceptable here.**

Exhaled bubbles would rise and collect against the ceiling, the underside of the drowned meadow. Gas pooling in that root fleece would disturb the dusk layer, push water out of the fine root-hairs, and possibly leave pockets of trapped air that change the place long after you're gone. Bubbles are also loud, and in a room where sound runs ahead of its causes, you don't want a steady stream of noise you can't interpret.

- Full-face mask, for thermal protection and so no part of your face touches the water.
- A bailout supply mounted tight and streamlined against your body, not slung.
- A dive computer with a haptic (vibration) alarm. You won't be able to read a screen, and any light you use to read one is light the undercroft hasn't seen in years.

---

## Cold

The water sits just above the temperature at which roots stop growing, so near freezing. There is no warmer water to retreat to, except the dusk layer near the ceiling, which is only *faintly* warmer.

- A **drysuit** with heavy thermal undergarments, ideally with active heating.
- A **drysuit hood and gloves**, or a dry-glove system. You'll be trading dexterity for warmth, and here that's the right trade. You shouldn't be handling anything anyway.
- **Short immersions:** thirty to forty minutes at most, fewer if you are still. Stillness is exactly what this place requires, and stillness is exactly what makes you cold. Plan for it.
- **A cut drysuit is a life-threatening emergency.** That leads to the next hazard.

---

## The Stone

The slate floor is freshly broken. Its edges stand up like the spines of closed books, unrounded and sharp, hidden under silt and moss. They catch anything that drifts past.

- Wear an abrasion-resistant outer shell over your drysuit, with reinforced knees, shins, elbows, and forearms.
- **Stay off the floor.** It's the most dangerous part of the pocket for your suit. It is also where the cairn, the threshold stones, the crayfish shells, the unhatched eggs, and the black band all are. You have no reason to go down there, and many reasons not to.

---

## Entanglement

There is no empty water here. Hanging grass sheaves, watercress towers, sheets of lace-weed, and root-strands fill every volume. They will snag every protrusion you carry.

- **Streamline everything.** No dangling hoses, gauges, clips, or loops. Tuck, tape, and route every line tight to your body.
- Carry **two line cutters**, mounted where either hand can reach them. Understand that using one cuts something that took years to grow toward a floor it will never reach. Cut only to save your life, and cut as little as you can.
- **Don't fin.** Kicking is impossible here and would be ruinous. You'll move by near-weightless drift and the gentlest fingertip pressure against surfaces that can take it. Your team should pull you along your tether so you don't have to propel yourself at all.
- **Achieve perfect neutral buoyancy before you arrive.** Practice until you can hover motionless in a test tank for twenty minutes without touching anything. This is the most important physical skill for the trip.

---

## Silt

The current here moves the way a sleeper's chest moves. A thread of silt released at one wall can take a full day to reach the other. Anything you stir up will hang in the water for days, maybe weeks.

In the dark this won't matter to you. But it matters enormously for the light. **One careless movement in the hours before the sponges release could veil the whole undercroft in silt for its only visible moment of the year.** Arrive gently, settle, and then don't move.

---

## Seeing, and Not Seeing

Nothing here has seen light in years, except its own three heartbeats a year. Things have grown in a darkness that is part of the room.

- **Bring no white light.** If you must have any at all, carry a single deeply dimmed red light, for emergencies only, shielded and pointed at your own equipment.
- **Let your eyes adapt fully** before the expected release. Spend an hour in total darkness beforehand, including the transit. Three heartbeats is not long, and dark-adapted eyes will get far more from them.
- **Don't try to photograph the release.** A camera that can capture it would need a flash or a long exposure, and a flash here would be a second, false dawn, a violation of the one real one. If you want a record, pay attention and remember it.
- **Know what you're looking for.** When the light comes, you'll see the hanging meadow, the book-edged stone, the moss domes like stacked loaves, the drifting sheaves of pale grass. Look past them, at the misalignment, at the sense of two rooms drawn by different hands laid over one another, their walls not quite matching. Look for where the seams don't meet. This is the real shape of the place, and you'll only have a moment to see it.

---

## Sound

Most of the time the undercroft is quiet, with only a low, even pressure from the river overhead, like a hand resting on a door. Get used to that feeling before you go. Some people find it deeply comforting. Others find it unbearable.

**Sound arrives before its cause here.** This has two consequences:

1. **You may hear yourself about to make a mistake.** A tick of stone, a scrape, a rustle, arriving from where your elbow or tank is about to go. Treat any unexplained sound near your body as a warning, and stop moving. If you train yourself to listen, the undercroft will tell you what you are about to do to it.
2. **You will hear things that haven't happened yet, and may not understand them.** Don't chase them.

Deep in the lower layers you may hear a faint patter, like rain on leaves. It is the rain from the dusk the meadow drowned in, still falling, never reaching the ground. You don't need to do anything about it. Let it be the most beautiful thing you ever hear, and don't try to get closer.

---

## The Layers of Night

The pocket is stratified. The drowned dusk sits near the ceiling, faintly warmer. Below it, each night the river has run over the bank since lies pressed down like pages, colder and heavier.

**Your body will span several layers at once.** Held horizontally, you'll mostly stay within one or two. Held upright, your head may be in the dusk while your feet are in a winter night from years later. Nobody knows what that does to a person. The weed that grows through the layers changes form at each boundary: broad and soft in one, stiff and nearly transparent in another. Keep your body **horizontal, near the ceiling, within the dusk layer**, as much as you can.

Expect subtle effects. You may feel a mild dislocation, a sense of it being evening when it isn't, or a memory of a cut-grass smell you can't actually be smelling through your mask. Some visitors may feel homesick for a meadow they never saw. Note these sensations but don't act on them.

**The black band near the floor must not be entered.** In that thin layer, weed grows backward, shortening and returning to its own base. It may be a night that hasn't happened yet. Don't put a hand, a fin tip, a line, or a dropped object into it. Nothing you lose there comes back in any sense you'd recognize.

---

## Who Lives Here

**The eel** lives in the dusk layer and moves along the underside of the meadow so slowly that the roots have grown into a tunnel exactly its shape. When it moves, it's also still where it was. The tunnel is never empty.

- Learn the tunnel's location from your team's survey, and **keep well clear of it.** Don't block it, touch it, or put your tether across it.
- If you feel something slow and smooth pass near you in the dark, don't move. It's older than the pocket, and it came in with the evening. It has more right to be there than you do.
- Don't try to see its eyes. They're milky and ridged like shells, and whatever they have become, they aren't for you.

**The crayfish** may be all seven of the shed shells lying in a ring on the floor, growing and outgrowing itself in every direction of the folded time. Don't touch the shells, don't count them by hand, and don't disturb their arrangement. If you are fortunate and still enough during the light, you might see one claw of the smallest shell open very slightly. Don't expect to. Don't wait for it. That takes years.

**The water fleas** will drift past you and change colour at each layer boundary, visible only during the light. **The unhatched eggs** may be waiting for the night they belong to. If that is so, a stray kick that rearranged the layers could strand them forever. This is another reason to stay off the floor.

---

## What Was Built

Someone used this meadow before the flood. Three traces remain:

- **The threshold stones**, a line of fitted slate running wall to wall, going nowhere.
- **The cairn of five pebbles**, stacked largest to smallest, still standing, with a clean ring of bare stone around it where the moss refuses to grow. It's the only uncovered thing in the pocket.
- **The braided grass knot** in the ceiling roots, a child's plait or a fencepost marker, hanging in the dusk layer and still green after years.

**Don't touch any of them. Don't try to "restore" or "protect" them.** Don't move the braid out of the dusk layer, even by accident. It's green because of where it is, and if it drifts into a winter night you might watch it age years in a moment.

The moss grows around the cairn and never over it. **Follow the moss's example.** If something in this place has decided to leave the cairn alone, so should you.

You may feel a strong urge to add something: a pebble, a knot, a gesture of your own. **Don't.** These are the marks of someone who hasn't come back. They aren't a guestbook.

---

## What You Must Not Leave Behind

The water keeps what falls into it, exactly as it falls, possibly forever, possibly folded into a night.

- **Drop nothing.** Clip and leash every item you carry.
- **Shed nothing.** No exposed skin, no loose hair, no fraying fabric. Check your suit for lint and loose fibers before you go.
- **Don't bleed.** Any cut is a reason to leave at once, carefully.
- **Don't exhale into the water.** This is the other reason for the rebreather.
- **Relieve yourself inside your suit system, not into the pocket.**

What you leave here won't decay. It will become part of the undercroft, as permanent as the cairn and more unwelcome.

---

## What You Must Not Take

Nothing leaves with you: no sprig, no pebble, no strand of pale grass on your suit, no vial of luminous water.

- Have your team check your suit by touch before you transit out, and remove anything clinging to it by hand, releasing it gently back into the layer it came from if possible.
- Be aware that anything carried out may carry its night with it. A sprig of weed from the winter layers might keep growing narrow and stiff in your hand. A strand from the dusk layer might smell of cut grass for years. Nobody knows what a fragment from the black band would do.

---

## Mental Preparation

This place is heavy in ways that have little to do with pressure.

- **Train for claustrophobia and sensory deprivation.** Spend long periods in flotation tanks and in dark, cold, enclosed water. Learn to be completely still while something brushes your face.
- **Train for uncertainty.** Sound will lie to you about when things happen. Distance will lie to you about where things are. Your body may feel two times at once. If you need the world to be consistent in order to stay calm, you'll panic here, and panic in the undercroft means thrashing, cutting, and silting, which could kill you and would wound the place.
- **Prepare for grief.** The undercroft is keeping an evening nobody else remembers. There is a child's braid still green, and five stones stacked for someone who never came back. Many visitors will leave with a sorrow that doesn't feel like theirs. Plan time afterward to sit with it.

---

## A Recommended Visit

1. **Survey remotely** first, to locate the eel's tunnel, the braid, the cairn, and a safe arrival point near the ceiling at the edge of the dusk layer.
2. **Train** for months: buoyancy, stillness, rebreather work, cold tolerance, darkness.
3. **Dark-adapt** for an hour before transit.
4. **Arrive slowly** into the dusk layer, horizontal, tethered, perfectly trimmed.
5. **Settle, and don't move.** Let the silt you raised on arrival fall still.
6. **Listen.** Feel the river's hand on the door. Maybe hear the rain.
7. **Wait** for the light, for as long as your cold limit allows. If it doesn't come, withdraw and come back later.
8. **When it comes, look.** Three heartbeats: the meadow hanging upside down, the stone like books, the grass sheaves, the two rooms that don't quite match.
9. **Then the dark returns.** Stay still a moment longer out of courtesy while the sponges begin to fill again.
10. **Withdraw by tether,** with your team pulling, touching nothing.
11. **Check yourself** for anything that came with you, and return it.
12. **Afterward,** expect odd evenings for a while. You may smell cut grass in unlikely places, feel dusk come early, or dream of a braid in a child's hand. This is normal, and it usually fades.

---

## A Final Word

When you leave, the undercroft will go on as if you had never been there, if you did it well. The slate will round at the pace of fingernails growing. The moss will add a leaf's thickness each year. Another night will be folded in and pressed down. In a few hundred years the eel's tunnel will be a hollow sculpture of woven root, and the dusk will float on top of a single deep dark like cream.

The measure of your visit isn't what you saw. It's that the braid is still green, the five stones are still standing, and the moss still hasn't closed its ring around them, still marking something, for someone, who hasn't come back.

It wasn't you they were waiting for. Leave it as you found it.
