# Preparing for the Hammer Moor

## The Short Version

The Hammer Moor won't try to kill you. It simply isn't built for anything that needs to stay dry, warm, oriented, or rested, and you need all four. Most dangerous places are dangerous in one way. This one is uncomfortable in a dozen small ways at once, all the time, with no letup. The main risks are the ordinary ones: wet cold, drowning in soft ground, scalding, and a nearby electrical discharge. Around those sits a stranger layer. The landscape keeps poor track of distance, time, and which geological era it's in.

Go expecting to be wet for the whole visit, never to hear silence, and never to know exactly where you are. If you can accept those three things before you arrive, you'll probably have one of the most extraordinary experiences available to a human being. If you can't, don't go.

---

## 1. Understand What You're Walking On

Your whole trip depends on the ground, so start there.

You aren't walking on land. You're walking on a floating, living, several-meter-thick mattress that is saturated, moving, and in places thin, rotten, or under pressure. Picture a waterbed made of moss, with a slow heartbeat, lying on a lake of jelly.

**Footwear.** Normal hiking boots will be soaked within the first ten minutes and stay that way. Choose one of two approaches:

- **Accept wet feet and manage them.** Wear sturdy, fast-draining boots with good ankle support, since the swells will roll your ankles, plus wool socks and neoprene socks over them. This is how bog-trotters and soldiers in wet terrain actually operate. You will be wet, but warm-wet.
- **Rubber boots.** Knee-high wellies or insulated muck boots keep you drier and give you some insulation from ground current near the charged tarns. They are heavy, they suck into the mat, and they are miserable to fall over in.

**Do not wear chest or hip waders.** If you go through the mat or into a tarn, they fill with water and pull you under. This is a known way to die in real bogs.

**Spreading your weight.** Seriously consider bog shoes or snowshoes. Broad frames help keep you on top of the thin patches. Practice walking in them on soft ground before the trip, because the swells will test your balance.

**A probe.** Carry a long, sturdy pole of about two meters. It should be wood or fiberglass, not aluminum and not carbon fiber, because both conduct electricity. Use it to test ground ahead of you where the moss changes color or texture, or where water pools. It also gives you a third point of balance when a swell lifts you. And if you break through, you can lay it across the hole to hold yourself up. Of everything you bring, this pole may be the item most likely to save your life.

**Reading the ground.** Learn these signs:

- **Bright, uniform, wet-looking moss**, especially the pale wave-underside green, often marks a *quaking* area. It floats over water with very little depth. Walk around it.
- **Rank, red, crowded moss that steams faintly** means you're near a vent or a buried hot zone. Back off.
- **Taut, domed, or strangely firm moss that hums or creaks** may be under pressure before a bog-burst. Don't stand on a drum skin.
- **Firmness underfoot**, the feeling of "a bone through a sleeve," means thin mat over rock. That's reassuring, but those spots are rare.

**If you break through:** Don't struggle upward. Spread your arms and lean back or forward so you're lying flat. Get the pole across the hole. Work your legs out slowly with small movements. Peat sucks, and panicking makes it worse. Practice this beforehand in a controlled setting if you possibly can.

## 2. The Cold Is the Real Enemy

"A few degrees above freezing, wet, windy, forever" is the most dangerous climate for humans. It is worse than hard frost. At −20°C you know you're in danger and the air is dry. At +3°C with constant drizzle, spray from every step, and gales, you lose heat in a slow, quiet way you don't notice until your hands stop working and your thinking goes soft.

**Layering:**

- **Base layer:** Merino wool, top and bottom. Wool stays warm when wet. Cotton kills here, so bring none at all, not even underwear.
- **Mid layer:** A thick wool jumper or a fleece, with a spare in a dry bag.
- **Insulation:** A synthetic-fill puffy jacket. Down loses its loft when wet, and everything here ends up wet.
- **Shell:** A heavy, fully waterproof, hooded jacket and trousers with taped seams. Buy the most robust you can afford. Bog-burst spray and peat-water jets will cover it in stain and grit.
- **Hands:** This matters most. The description says the cold "works into the joints of the hands." Bring at least three pairs of gloves: thin liners, insulated waterproof gloves, and a dry spare pair kept against your body. Consider waterproof mitten shells. If your hands fail, you can't set up shelter, light a stove, or work a zip.
- **Head:** A wool hat and a buff or neck gaiter. You'll lose heat into the wind constantly.

**Staying ahead of hypothermia:**

- Eat constantly. Your body will burn enormous amounts of fuel just staying warm. Aim for roughly one and a half to two times your normal calorie intake: fatty, dense food like nuts, cheese, chocolate, salami, and butter.
- Drink hot liquids whenever you stop.
- Learn the "umbles": stumbling, mumbling, fumbling, grumbling. If you notice them in yourself or a companion, stop immediately, shelter, get dry, get warm, and eat.
- Carry a proper emergency bivvy bag or a group shelter. The single most useful piece of warmth gear here is something you can get inside, out of the wind, in under a minute.

**Your feet:** Days of wet, cold feet lead to trench foot (immersion foot). Every night, take off your boots and socks, dry your feet completely, and inspect them. Put on dedicated dry sleeping socks that never go outside. Some people use a foot powder or balm. Take this seriously. It's a trip-ending injury that develops quietly.

## 3. Altitude

You're high, and the air is thin. The description doesn't give a number, but "thin air" and a cloud base down around your knees suggests somewhere in the 2,500–4,000 m range.

- If you can, acclimatize before you go by spending a few days at altitude somewhere in your home world.
- Expect headache, breathlessness, and poor sleep for the first day or two, made worse by everything else here.
- Know the signs of serious altitude illness: confusion, loss of coordination, a wet cough, breathlessness at rest. If they appear, descend or leave. The "descend" option may not exist here, so your only true descent may be your exit portal.
- The ghost mountains add to this, as covered below. Invisible uphill walking at altitude is punishing.

---

## 4. Electricity: The Charged Tarns

Some tarn margins, where two types of moss meet, build and discharge electrical charge. There are sparks across the water, ozone, lifting hair, and glowing sedge tips. On a real mountain, those glowing tips would be St. Elmo's fire, and the lifting hair would be the warning sign right before a lightning strike.

**Rules:**

- **If your hair starts to lift, your skin tingles, or you hear buzzing from your gear, leave that area immediately.** Walk briskly, don't run, and get away from the tarn margin.
- Stay away from the edges of the charged tarns entirely. Admire the spark-walk from a distance.
- Carry no tall metal objects. Hence the wood or fiberglass probe and no metal trekking poles. Keep your frame pack low.
- If you're caught in a discharge area and can't get out, use the lightning crouch. Squat on the balls of your feet with your feet together, head tucked, hands over your ears. Don't lie flat on the wet mat, because current travels through the ground.
- Rubber-soled boots help a little. They are not protection.
- Don't touch the glowing moths or the moss where they've settled. It's probably harmless, but you don't know what's still carrying charge.

**Electronics:** Assume they'll misbehave. Carry analog backups for everything: a mechanical watch, a paper notebook, pencils (not pens, which fail in cold and wet), and a paper map you'll make yourself as you go. Keep any essential electronics, like a beacon or return device, in a Faraday pouch or a sealed, grounded container when not in use. If your multiverse transit relies on a device, protect that device above everything else you carry.

## 5. The Steam Vents

The vents are points of intense heat: boiling water forced up through the mat, with shrieking, hissing columns of steam. They also move. A vent can choke and die, and another can burst open "a kilometer away, scalding the mosses."

**Scalding:** The ground around a vent can be thin and undermined, with boiling water beneath. Don't approach the rank red ring of moss around an active vent. Treat it the way you'd treat a geyser field: a place to watch from far away.

**Gas:** This one is easy to overlook. Buried peat "decomposing itself like a banked furnace" is almost certainly producing methane, carbon dioxide, and probably hydrogen sulfide. CO₂ is heavier than air and pools in hollows. On a real volcanic or peat landscape, still, low-lying areas near vents can hold air that suffocates you with no warning.

- Don't rest or camp in hollows near vents, and never downwind of them.
- If you get a headache, rapid breathing, or dizziness that comes on suddenly, or you smell rotten eggs, move up and away, upwind if possible.
- **No open flames near vents.** Methane explodes.
- A small personal gas detector that reads CO₂, H₂S, and combustible gas would be worth carrying if one survives the electrical conditions.

**A tempting idea you should resist:** The vents are the only warmth on the plateau, and you will be very cold. You will want to go stand near one. The moss "steams faintly like the flank of a horse" there. Don't camp by them. Vents open without warning, and the ground near them is the least trustworthy on the moor.

## 6. The Swells, Bog-Bursts, and Draining Tarns

**The swells:** Knee-high waves in the ground, traveling at walking pace, in overlapping rhythms from many directions. They will lift you, spray you, and unbalance you, all day and all night.

- When you feel one coming, stop, bend your knees, and plant your pole. Ride it the way you'd ride a boat's deck.
- Avoid places where two swell-trains meet. The ground "bucks and throws up a spout of peat-water." You'll learn to see these interference zones coming by watching the pattern of humps. It's a bit like reading surf.
- Expect motion sickness. Some people will get genuinely seasick. Bring medication and try it at home first, because some types make you drowsy, which is dangerous in cold.
- After a day here you'll feel "land-sickness" when you go home: phantom swells, like getting off a ship.

**Bog-bursts:** These are rivers of black liquid peat, sometimes warm and steaming. Where pumping pressure builds, the mat tears "with a sound like canvas tearing." Learn that sound, because it's your warning.

- When you hear it, locate it, then move perpendicular to the likely downhill direction. Downhill is hard to judge on a plateau this flat. Watch which way the black flow starts moving and get out of its path.
- Never try to cross a bog-burst. Liquid peat behaves like quicksand, may be hot, and you'll lose boots, gear, or your life.
- Freshly greened-over scars are unstable for longer than they look.

**Draining tarns:** A lake can empty in an hour. Don't camp on a tarn shore, and never walk out on a freshly exposed tarn bed. It's pure liquid mud. Watch the eels cross it from the edge.

## 7. Navigation: Assume You Will Get Lost

This is where the Hammer Moor stops being merely a hard place and becomes a strange one.

**What fails:**

- **Compasses.** They spin near charged tarns. Elsewhere they point confidently to the wrong north, the one the mountain range had in its youth. A confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. If you carry a compass, write down its reading next to what you actually observe, and trust it only for keeping a consistent bearing over short distances, never for absolute direction.
- **Sun and stars.** You won't see them. The light is sourceless and there are no ordinary shadows.
- **Landmarks.** Steam columns move and die. Ghost mountains shift from hour to hour. Tarns open and close. Bog-burst scars heal in days.
- **Distance.** A column can be three kilometers off for hours and then suddenly beside you. Pacing and time-distance estimates are unreliable.
- **Sequence.** Moments may repeat, like the same flock with the same calls in the same order.
- **The horizon itself.** Sometimes it curves up like a bowl.

**What might hold:**

- **The granite knuckles.** These are the most permanent features on the moor. They're the last stumps of the summits, house-sized, lichen-upholstered, some wound in sedge cable. Each has its own shape and pattern of cable wrapping. Before you go anywhere, sketch the knuckle nearest your arrival point from several angles until you'd recognize it anywhere. Then navigate knuckle to knuckle, drawing each one in your notebook. They're your only real fixed points.
- **A tether.** For short forays, consider actually running a line. A spool of bright, strong cord unrolled behind you lets you get back to your entry point regardless of what the distances are doing. It sounds absurd, but it's exactly what cave explorers do, for exactly this reason: a place where your senses can't be trusted.
- **Time on your wrist.** Use a mechanical watch, and write timestamps constantly.

**Core rule: turn around by time, not by distance.** Before each outing, decide on a fixed duration, such as "I walk out for two hours by my watch, then I turn back." Don't let the landscape's shifting distances decide when you've gone "far enough."

**Mark your exit.** However you arrive, your arrival point is the only certain way home. Anchor your camp to it or to the nearest knuckle. Don't wander far from it. The grand, long traverse of the plateau is a fantasy. It goes on "past sight in every direction," and you'd never find your way back.

**A logbook for anchoring yourself.** Keep a running log: time, what you see, what you hear, which knuckle, which way the swells are running. It's partly for navigation. It's also for your sanity. When the plover flock goes past for the second time, identically, you'll want a written record that says *14:10 — flock, west to east* and *14:21 — same flock, same shape.* It lets you tell "the moor is doing something strange" apart from "I am becoming confused," and knowing the difference is essential.

## 8. The Ghost Mountains

Treat the ghost mountains as if they were real. As far as your body is concerned, they are.

They cast real shadow, bring real cold, funnel real wind, and slant real rain. Walking "uphill on flat ground makes the heart pound, the calves burn, and the ears pop." At altitude, in cold, that's genuine physical exertion and genuine exposure, like climbing a mountain.

- **Follow the birds' lead. Go around them, not through them.** The flocks bend as if around stone and the grazers won't climb them. The local animals know something, so copy them.
- If you notice the light dimming, the air cooling sharply, the wind changing direction, and your breath getting harder with no visible slope, you've walked onto a ghost face. Turn back the way you came, or contour sideways.
- Expect the ghost peaks to cause navigation errors. Funneled winds and phantom slopes will steer you without your noticing.
- They shift. A peak can become a col between one hour and the next, so don't use them as landmarks.
- Don't camp in a ghost mountain's shadow. It will be colder there, like the north face of a real peak, and you can't tell what else might change.

**Emotionally,** this will be one of the eeriest things you ever experience. It's worth preparing for mentally. You'll be walking on the moor and feel the presence of enormous stone above you. The best response seems to be calm respect. Things here are old and don't entirely agree about what time it is.

## 9. The Time-Glimpses

When a large swell passes under you, for half a second you may see the plateau in another age: the young mountains whole and glaciated, or a dust plain under a strange sun.

We don't know if these glimpses are only visual, or whether riding the crest puts you in any real contact with those other times. Assume caution:

- **Don't step while you're on a crest.** Freeze, crouch, hold your pole, and let the swell pass. If the ground briefly looks like bare rock or dust, you don't want to make a stride based on what you're seeing.
- Don't deliberately seek out the largest swells to chase the visions. It's tempting. Don't.
- Afterward, check your watch and your log. Note what you saw and the time. If you ever find a gap in time you can't account for, treat it as a serious warning and consider leaving.
- The glimpses may be emotionally overwhelming. You may see this place with no life at all, or the mountains in their savage youth. Some travelers will find this ecstatic. Others will find it deeply upsetting. Both are normal.

---

## 10. The Noise

It's loud all the time, "a working district heard from the next street." You'll hear thudding pumps, shrieking vents, an electrical drone, water, and a deep grinding underneath everything. Over that come birds, drumming snipe, and frogs in their tens of thousands, some rattling like telegraphs.

**Physically:**

- Bring good earplugs for sleeping, and maybe a pair of passive earmuffs. You won't be able to shut out the low-frequency drone and thud entirely, since those travel through your body, but it helps.
- Constant low-frequency noise and vibration wear you out, raise your irritability, and damage sleep. Budget for this. You'll be more tired and short-tempered than your exertion alone would explain.
- **Don't wear earplugs while walking.** You need to hear the canvas-tearing sound of a bog-burst, the pitch change of a vent, and the crackle of a charging tarn.

**Psychologically:**

- Expect rhythm entrainment. The snipe dive on the beat of the pumps. After a while you may find your breathing, walking, and even thoughts falling into those rhythms. Most people will probably find it strangely soothing. A few may find it invasive. Take regular breaks in your shelter to reset.
- The telegraph-frogs will make you wonder whether they're sending something. You're allowed to wonder. Write down the patterns if you like. But don't go chasing them into the charged tarns.

## 11. Shelter and Sleeping

There's no dry ground, no trees, and no stillness. The floor itself moves under you every few seconds.

**Options:**

1. **On or against a granite knuckle.** This is the best option, with caveats. The rock is the one stable, non-pumping surface on the moor. It sits above bog-burst flows and tarn shifts, and a lee side gives shelter from the gales. Its cable wrappings make natural anchor points and windbreaks. The caveat is that a lone high rock on a plain is a lightning target, and knuckles near charged tarns are a bad idea. Choose a knuckle well away from any tarn showing spark activity, and camp in its lee, low against its base, rather than on top.
2. **On the open mat.** Look for firm, thick patches away from tarns, vents, quaking ground, and pressure domes. You'll need an extremely waterproof tent floor with a heavy groundsheet underneath, a closed-cell foam pad plus an inflatable pad, and a tolerance for being lifted and dropped all night. Some travelers might prefer a floating or raft-like base, a stiff platform that spreads weight over the mat.

**Tent:** Choose a low-profile, mountain-grade tent built for sustained gales, pitched with every guy line used. Anchors won't hold in moss the way stakes hold in soil. Bring snow stakes or deadman anchors, which bury horizontally and grip better in soft material. Or tie off to the sedge cables and knuckle rock.

**Keep your sleeping bag dry at all costs.** Store it in a proper dry bag inside a second bag. Your dry sleeping kit is your insurance against hypothermia. Never take it out in the open.

**Watch for rolling cables.** Wind-spun sedge rope, hundreds of meters long, tumbles across the moor in gales. It could snag and drag a tent or knock you down. Pitch on the lee side of obstacles, and watch for rolling cable during storms.

**Expect poor sleep.** Between the swells, noise, cold, altitude, and strangeness, you'll sleep badly for the first night or two. Plan your trip so you aren't doing anything demanding on day two.

## 12. Water and Food

**Water:** You'll be surrounded by it. It's acidic, stained rust and brown with tannins, full of organic matter, and possibly carrying things you don't want: dissolved metals, gut-upsetting microbes, and the eels' and grazers' leavings.

- Filter it with a pre-filter for the particulates, since it'll clog a fine filter fast, then boil or treat it chemically.
- It'll taste of peat, like smoky whisky without the pleasure. You'll get used to it.
- Don't drink from near the vents or the charged tarns. You don't know what's dissolved in either.

**Food:** Bring everything you'll eat, and more. Don't eat anything local. The eels, slugs, and larvae are not your food. The eels "are said to live here for centuries," and killing one for a meal would be both unwise and a kind of vandalism.

- High-fat, high-calorie, easy to eat with cold hands.
- A stove with windscreen and reliable fuel. Remember no flames near vents, and cook with ventilation in mind if you're in an enclosed space.
- Hot meals twice a day at minimum. Hot drinks constantly.
- Pack everything in waterproof, crush-proof containers.

---

## 13. Insects

Midges are mentioned, in a cold, wet, boggy upland full of life. In our world, a place like that can hold midges in clouds dense enough to make people flee their tents.

- Bring a fine-mesh head net and keep it in your pocket at all times.
- Use repellent. Picaridin or DEET-based products work.
- Gloves, cuffs tucked, collar closed.
- Carry antihistamines for bites.

The spiders riding the swells on silk, the forearm-length green-gold slugs, and the water-beetles are almost certainly harmless and wonderful to watch. Kneel down and look at the mat. It seethes. It's one of the great sights of the place.

---

## 14. The Animals

- **Wading birds:** Harmless and spectacular. Follow them with your eyes to read the moor. Where they fly around nothing, there's a ghost peak. Where they follow a swell-ridge, you can see the swell's path.
- **Snipe:** Harmless. The drumming is beautiful.
- **Frogs:** Harmless. Don't handle them, since your skin chemistry can hurt them.
- **Grazers:** They never startle and always keep to the distance. Keep it that way. Large animals that don't flee aren't necessarily safe to approach. They may simply feel no need to run from you. Watch them for information instead: where they walk is probably firm ground, and what they refuse to climb is a ghost mountain.
- **Eels:** Old, possibly very old. Let them pass. Don't step on the trails during a migration.
- **Moths:** Watch, don't touch. They may carry charge.

---

## 15. Group or Solo?

The description emphasizes loneliness, and a solo visit may be the purest experience of the place. It's also far more dangerous. Breaking through the mat, a fall on a swell, early hypothermia, gas exposure: each is survivable with a partner and potentially fatal alone.

**Strong recommendation: go with at least one other person.** If you go solo, stay very close to your entry point, use a tether line, keep your forays short, and leave a detailed plan with someone in your home world, along with a hard deadline for your return.

In a group, rope up or stay within arm's reach when crossing unknown mat. Check each other for the umbles. Watch each other's hair for lifting.

## 16. The Mental Side

This may be the hardest preparation of all.

The Hammer Moor is huge, indifferent, and endlessly busy. You're "very small on it and very alone." It doesn't notice you. It works day and night, a factory "with no product and no owner." Everything is made and unmade constantly. Time and distance don't behave. Old mountains lean over you in the fog.

Some people will find this deeply peaceful: the relief of being in the presence of something so vast and so unconcerned with you. Others will find it frightening, depressing, or depersonalizing, especially after several days without rest, without silence, and without dryness.

**Prepare yourself:**

- **Anchoring rituals.** A consistent routine helps. Morning log entry, a hot drink, foot check, a specific phrase or small act that reminds you who you are and when you are.
- **Know your limit and honor it.** If you start feeling that you're dissolving into the rhythm, or that the moor's labor is more real than you are, or that you've lost your sense of which time you're in, go home. That isn't failure. It's recognizing that this place was never built for people.
- **Bring something personal.** A small object from your home world that's dry, warm-colored, and yours. It sounds trivial. In the sourceless gray, surrounded by moss and noise, you'll be surprised how much it matters.
- **Plan the return.** Expect a few days of readjustment afterward. You'll feel phantom swells, find silence oddly loud, and maybe keep an unconscious ear out for the thud. Some visitors may miss the place intensely. It's that kind of place.

## 17. Packing List

**Clothing**
- Merino base layers ×2
- Wool/fleece mid-layer ×2 (one kept dry)
- Synthetic insulated jacket
- Heavy waterproof shell jacket and trousers
- Gloves ×3+ pairs (liners, insulated waterproof, dry spares), plus mitten shells
- Wool hat, buff/neck gaiter
- Wool socks ×4+ pairs, neoprene socks, dedicated sleeping socks
- Boots (draining boots with neoprene socks, or insulated rubber boots) and gaiters
- Bog shoes or snowshoes

**Movement and Safety**
- Long wooden or fiberglass probing pole (2 m)
- Rope and/or tether spool with bright cord
- Personal gas detector (CO₂/H₂S/combustible), if it will function
- Head net and insect repellent
- Emergency bivvy bag or group shelter
- Whistle (voices won't carry over the noise, but a whistle might)
- First aid kit, including hypothermia supplies, foot care kit, altitude and motion-sickness medications, antihistamines, and burn dressings

**Navigation and Records**
- Mechanical watch
- Waterproof notebook and pencils
- Compass (for relative bearings only)
- Return device/beacon in a Faraday pouch

**Shelter and Sleep**
- Four-season, low-profile mountain tent with snow stakes/deadman anchors and plenty of guy line
- Heavy groundsheet
- Closed-cell foam pad plus inflatable pad
- Synthetic sleeping bag rated well below freezing, in a double dry bag
- Earplugs and earmuffs

**Food and Water**
- Water filter with pre-filter, plus chemical treatment
- Stove, windscreen, fuel, lighter and waterproof matches
- High-calorie food at 1.5–2× your normal needs
- Insulated flask

**Everything else**
- Dry bags for everything
- One small personal object from home

## Rules of Thumb

1. **Probe before you step** where the moss changes.
2. **Crouch and plant** when the swell comes. Don't step on the crest.
3. **Hair lifts, you leave.**
4. **Red, steaming moss means back away.**
5. **Canvas tearing means move sideways, now.**
6. **Go around what the birds go around.**
7. **Turn back by time, not distance.**
8. **Log everything, with timestamps.**
9. **Your sleeping bag never gets wet.**
10. **Eat before you're hungry, drink hot before you're cold, rest before you're stupid.**

---

## Last Thoughts

If you prepare properly, you'll get something almost nobody else ever gets. You'll stand on a living machine as old as mountains and watch it breathe. You'll feel the thud through your boots and watch plover follow the swells like gulls behind a plough. You'll see moths come out of the spark-storm glowing like embers. You'll hear snipe drumming in time with the ground, and watch an eel migration cross an emptied lake bed. You'll stand in the cold shadow of a mountain that died a hundred million years ago.

Go small. Go carefully. Stay close to your way home. The moor won't mind you. It's busy, and it will still be busy long after you've gone.
