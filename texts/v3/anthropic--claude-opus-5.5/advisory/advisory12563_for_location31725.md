# Preparing for Kessit Deep

## What You're Actually Walking Into

Kessit Deep is small, and your whole visit will happen within a stone's throw. That doesn't make it easy. You are entering a room that is simultaneously a hot spring, a bat cave, an industrial plant room, and an insect metropolis, and it has only been any of these things for eleven years. Nothing has settled. The rock is still sharp, the ceiling is still deciding whether to stay up, and the ecosystem is still in its boom phase, dense and rank and loud.

The biggest danger is not the crickets, the cockroaches, or even the ceiling plates. It is the air. It is roughly body temperature or warmer, and it is saturated with water. Plan everything around that.

---

## The Heat Problem

The rock is as hot as a bath you'd have to lower yourself into slowly. That means around 40°C (104°F), and the weeping water is hotter still. The air is near saturation.

**Your body cools itself by evaporating sweat, and in Kessit Deep sweat cannot evaporate.** When the air is hotter and wetter than your skin, it gets worse: you are cooler than the room, so water condenses on you the way it condenses on the pump housing. You won't just be sweaty. You'll be dewed. Condensation releases heat onto the surface it forms on, so the room is actively warming you.

This is the physiology of serious heat illness, and it can escalate to heat stroke in well under an hour for someone unacclimatized. The crews manage long shifts, which tells you two things. First, they're acclimatized in ways you are not. Second, the crew shelter is almost certainly cooled or dehumidified somehow. Otherwise the bunks would be unsurvivable. Confirm this before you go.

**Preparation:**

- **Acclimatize if you can.** If your travel schedule allows, spend a week or more beforehand doing daily sessions in hot, humid conditions: saunas with water on the stones, steam rooms, hot-climate exercise. It won't make you a pump crew veteran, but it meaningfully improves how your body copes.
- **Limit your exposure.** Plan your first descent as 20–30 minutes on the chamber floor, then return to the hut or go up the ladder. Increase only if you feel genuinely fine. Agree on a turnaround time with whoever accompanies you, and let them enforce it. Heat illness impairs the judgment you'd use to notice it.
- **Consider a phase-change cooling vest** under your outer layer. Ice vests won't last long here, but phase-change packs rated around 15–18°C will buy you real time. Bring spares and ask whether the hut can recharge them.
- **Know the warning signs:** headache, nausea, a pounding pulse that won't slow, confusion, clumsiness, or stopping sweating despite the heat. Any of these means you go up now, not after one more look at the fern.
- **Don't go down ill, hungover, or sleep-deprived.** Don't go down on medications that impair thermoregulation (some antihistamines, diuretics, beta-blockers, stimulants) without medical advice.

## The Air Quality Problem

There are four airborne hazards, in rough order of how much they should concern you:

1. **Carbon dioxide** from the degassing brine. It is heavier than air, so it pools in low spots, especially near the pools and in the rubble hollows. You can't smell it. At high concentrations it causes rapid breathing, headache, and confusion, and eventually it causes collapse.
2. **Ammonia** from the guano. You will certainly smell it. It burns the eyes and airways, and it gets bad in still periods.
3. **Hydrogen sulfide** from the brine, the eggy smell. At the faint levels described it's an irritant. But H₂S has a nasty trick: at dangerous concentrations it paralyzes your sense of smell, so the smell fading is not reassurance.
4. **Fungal spores from the guano.** Histoplasma, the fungus behind histoplasmosis, thrives in exactly this kind of bird and bat droppings under warm, humid conditions. Disturbed guano releases spores. Most infections are mild, but heavy exposure can cause serious lung disease, especially in anyone immunocompromised.

**Preparation:**

- **Carry a personal four-gas monitor** that reads CO₂, H₂S, O₂, and ideally NH₃. Clip it near your chest, not on your belt or boot, so it samples the air you breathe. Keep in mind that any gas pooling near the floor will be worse than your chest reading. Borrow one from the crew, or bring your own calibrated unit.
- **Learn the swiftlet alarm and trust it.** The crew's custom is that when the colony's clicking thins without the pump's groan to explain it, everyone climbs out. The birds detect bad air before the gauges do. Do not wear earplugs or noise-canceling headphones down there, however loud it gets. You need to hear the ceiling.
- **Never kneel, crouch, or lie down near the pools or in floor hollows.** That's where CO₂ collects. If you drop something into a low spot, ask a crew member to retrieve it or let it go.
- **Bring a proper respirator:** a half-face elastomeric mask with combination cartridges covering P100 particulates plus ammonia/methylamine. A paper dust mask doesn't handle ammonia. Get it fit-tested before your trip. A leaky seal is almost useless against spores.
  - Wearing a respirator in wet heat is miserable. It adds breathing resistance and traps heat on your face. You may want to do what the crews do: wear it on your neck and put it on when the ammonia gets bad or whenever guano is being disturbed. Given you're a visitor with no acquired tolerance, err toward wearing it more.
- **If you are immunocompromised, pregnant, or have chronic lung disease, seriously reconsider this visit.** The spore load here is not a small risk for you.

## Medical Preparation Before You Travel

Do these well in advance, because some take weeks:

- **Tetanus booster**, if you haven't had one in the last five years. The rock will cut you, and the cut will likely touch guano.
- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** There are bats here, and bats anywhere can carry rabies or related lyssaviruses. The pre-exposure series takes about a month. It doesn't eliminate treatment if you're bitten or scratched, but it simplifies it enormously, and in an unfamiliar world's medical system that matters.
- **A baseline chest check**, if you have any respiratory history.
- **Talk to a doctor about leptospirosis prophylaxis.** Standing warm water plus a dense rodent-and-animal ecosystem is the classic setting. Doxycycline is sometimes used prophylactically, but it causes sun sensitivity and interacts with things, so this is a medical decision.
- **Your own first aid kit:** strong antiseptic, irrigation saline, waterproof adhesive dressings (normal plasters won't stick to wet skin), burn gel, antifungal foot powder, eye wash, and oral rehydration salts.

## Clothing: What to Wear

Your clothing has to do contradictory jobs. It must protect you from falling guano, sharp rock, jumping insects, and scalding water, while not cooking you. There's no perfect answer. The crews wear waders and hooded oilskins, and they've had years to find the best compromise. Follow their lead with some adjustments:

**Head:**
- **A hard hat** with a chin strap. The ceiling plates haven't fallen yet, but "yet" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and smaller spalls and scale flakes certainly do fall. The hard hat also keeps guano out of your hair.
- **An oilskin or waxed-cotton hood over the hard hat, or a neck-flap attachment.** Droppings fall constantly. You want them to hit fabric, not the back of your neck.
- **Sealed safety glasses or goggles.** These protect against guano, ammonia, and the occasional startled cricket. Accept now that they will fog. You arrive cooler than the room, so every lens you bring will be instantly coated in condensation until it warms up. Bring an anti-fog cloth and expect to be half-blind for the first several minutes. Let your eyewear acclimatize at the ladder base before stepping out onto rough floor. If you wear prescription glasses, consider contacts plus goggles, but you'll have to accept the infection risk of touching your eyes down here. Bring daily disposables.

**Body:**
- **A lightweight, breathable base layer** that can be soaking wet without chafing. Merino or a technical synthetic works. Avoid cotton, which becomes a heavy wet bag.
- **An outer oilskin or waxed jacket, hooded.** Unzip it as much as you can while still protecting your shoulders from the drip.
- **Chest waders or hip waders** with cleated soles. They're hot, but they protect against the hand-span-deep guano, the pools, the Kettle, and the cockroaches. The crews wear them for good reason.
- **Tape your cuffs.** Use waterproof tape or elastic bands at wrists and the tops of your boots or waders. Cave crickets jump when startled, and cockroaches explore. Neither is dangerous, but a cricket inside your sleeve while you're standing on sharp, slick rock is a fall waiting to happen.

**Hands:**
- **Cut-resistant gloves.** Level 4 or higher, with good wet grip, since the ladder rungs will be slick. The fracture edges are sharp enough to cut a thumb. Wear them the entire time you're in the chamber.
- **A second pair.** Gloves soaked in condensate and guano become unpleasant fast.

**Feet:**
- If you're wearing boots rather than boot-foot waders, choose ones with deep lugs and waterproofing, and bring a full change of socks for afterward. Constant wet heat macerates skin quickly, and your feet will be pale and wrinkled by the end.

## Equipment: What to Carry

Keep it minimal. Everything you bring down gets wet, fogged, and dusted with guano, and everything you drop may be lost in the rubble.

- **A hand lamp, not a headlamp.** Crews carry hand lamps for a good reason. A headlamp points wherever you look, and in Kessit Deep you will constantly want to look up at the moving walls, the ceiling plates, and the nests. Every time you do, you'll blast the roost with light, and panicked swiftlets mean a rain of droppings. A hand lamp lets you look up while keeping the beam aimed down and to the side, as the crews do. If you must use a headlamp for hands-free climbing, switch it off or to its dimmest red setting once you're off the ladder.
- **A gas monitor**, as above.
- **Water.** Bring more than you think: at least a liter per hour of exposure, in sealed bottles. Add electrolyte tablets. Drink before you feel thirsty, since your body can't signal properly when your sweat doesn't evaporate. Only drink in the hut or at the ladder base, away from falling droppings, and wipe the bottle mouth first.
- **A pencil and waterproof notebook**, if you want to record anything. Ink runs. The crews learned this and so should you.
- **Cameras and phones** belong in sealed dry bags. Before you open them underground, let them warm up to room temperature inside the bag, or they'll fog internally and possibly short. Bring a small towel sealed in its own bag for wiping. A camera with good low-light performance will serve you better than any flash. Don't use a flash on the ceiling, for the same reasons as the lamp.
- **A small sealed bag for trash.** Leave nothing behind.

**What not to bring:**
- Food, except sealed emergency electrolyte gels. Everything in the chamber will be interested in open food.
- Anything you'd mind losing.
- Earplugs. You need to hear the birds.
- Cologne, perfume, or scented products. They'll be lost in the ammonia anyway.

## Clean Your Boots Before You Go Down

This is a multiverse-specific precaution, but it's important.

The lampenflora around the work lamps "drifted in on air or boots." Kessit Deep's entire photosynthetic ecosystem was introduced accidentally by visitors. It's a young, raw, still-colonizing ecosystem, which is exactly the kind most vulnerable to invasion.

You come from another world. Your boots, gloves, gear, and clothing may carry spores, seeds, microbes, and soil organisms that have never existed in this universe. A foreign alga or moss in the halo around Lamp Four could outcompete the fern the crews quietly protect. A foreign fungus in the guano could do far worse to an ecosystem where turning one trowel of guano reveals more life than forest soil.

- **Wear new or thoroughly disinfected gear.** Better yet, borrow the crews' waders and oilskins, which are already "of" this place.
- **Scrub the soles of any footwear** you bring from another world. Brush out the treads and disinfect.
- **Don't bring plant material, soil, or living things** from any other world, even as gifts.

## Timing Your Visit

When you go changes almost everything about the experience.

**Season:**
- **Winter:** The pump runs almost constantly. The chamber is louder, with the grinding bass through your feet and the rattling bearing. Because vibration keeps them hidden, far fewer cockroaches will be out on the floor. If insects on the floor bother you, go in winter.
- **Summer:** The pump rests for hours. You'll hear the chamber's own life more clearly: ticking swiftlets, scrabbling roaches, dripping water. When the vibration eases, the cockroaches come out of the rubble by the thousands. The floor near the pools will seem to move. If you want to see the ecosystem at its fullest, go in summer, with your cuffs well taped.

**Time of day:**
- **The swiftlet exodus** at dusk and the return before dawn are the most extraordinary things Kessit Deep offers. For forty minutes the ventilation bore roars and the air moves: the room's only wind, generated by tens of thousands of wings. Position yourself before it starts, somewhere clear of the heaviest roosts, perhaps near the hut or on the catwalk, with your lamp off or pointed firmly at the floor. Don't try to move around during it. Expect a burst of droppings as the colony takes off.
- **After the dusk exodus**, the chamber is at its emptiest. Only the crickets, bats, and pump remain. The veteran workers sleep best then. This is the best time to hear the bats chittering from their recess above the old explosives store, and the most peaceful time to simply stand and look.
- **The pump's start-up groan**, and the moment when every swiftlet falls silent for a held breath before resuming, is worth waiting for. Ask the crew or watch the gauges in the hut to know when a cycle is due.

## Permission and Arrival

- **Arrive on the surface, not in the chamber.** If your means of travel allows you to choose your arrival point, don't materialize underground. You'd arrive unacclimatized, with fogged gear, possibly into a CO₂ pocket or into the Kettle, and uninvited. Arrive in the city above and come down the ladder like everyone else.
- **Get permission.** This is a working utility installation. Someone in the district heating authority controls access, and the crews have their own opinion about who comes down. The telephone in the control hut rings about twice a week. Arrange for your arrival to be one of those calls.
- **Respect the headcount.** There are usually three people down there, never more than five. That limit is almost certainly about air, space in the hut, and evacuation up a single ladder. If your visit would make six, wait.
- **Bring something useful.** Good dry pencils. Waterproof tape. Fresh batteries. Something cold to drink for when they come up. Whatever you bring, you're a guest in their workplace, and a small useful gift acknowledges that.
- **Have someone on the surface** who knows when you went down and when you should be back up.

## The Descent

The ladder shaft will be your first real test. Take it slowly. The rungs will be wet and possibly scaled. Keep three points of contact, gloves on. Your gear will fog as you descend into warmer, wetter air. It's like climbing down into your own breath.

At the bottom, stop. Don't step off immediately. Let your eyewear clear. Let your eyes adjust to the rust-orange sodium light. Let your ears sort out the layers: ticking, dripping, humming, grinding. Check your gas monitor.

Then look up at the walls. They're moving. You were warned, but being told and seeing are different things. A door-sized section of grey-brown texture ripples and resettles when your lamp passes near it. That texture is crickets, thousands upon thousands of them. They're harmless. They'll mostly ignore you. Some will jump. Stay still until the first wave of instinctive revulsion passes, and it will pass. Then carry on.

## Conduct in the Chamber

Follow the crews' customs exactly. They weren't made up for charm. Each one encodes a lesson learned.

- **Don't sweep, scrape, or kick the guano.** It's the floor, and it's alive. Disturbing it also releases spores.
- **Keep your lamp pointed down and to the side.** Never straight up into the roost.
- **Tap the pump housing before touching it**, so the cockroaches clear off. Better yet, don't touch it at all unless a crew member asks you to. It's their machinery.
- **Don't touch the fern under Lamp Four. Don't photograph it with a flash. Don't breathe on it too hard.** The crews will never admit how much they care about it, so don't make them.
- **Listen to the swiftlets.** When the ceiling goes quiet outside the pump's groan, go up the ladder without discussion.
- **Stay away from the Kettle.** Know which pool it is before you move around the floor. The crews keep their tools out of it for a reason. It's hot enough to scald.
- **Watch for the orange rings on the ceiling**, now browned under scale. Don't stand beneath them for longer than you have to. Don't make loud percussive noises.
- **Move slowly across broken rock.** Test each step. The guano hides edges and holes. The rock under it is sharp. A fall here means cuts contaminated with droppings and possibly a fall into a pool.
- **Use the catwalk** where you can. It's the cleanest surface in the chamber.
- **Don't enter the transformer cage**, obviously, and don't disturb the swiftlets nesting along its fence.
- **Don't handle any animal.** Especially not the bats. If a bat touches you, bites you, or scratches you, even slightly, tell the crew and get medical attention immediately.
- **Don't take nests, even fallen ones.** They're valuable, regulated, and harvested by licensed collectors under the crews' watchful eyes. A stranger pocketing one would be a serious breach.
- **Don't take anything.** No rock samples, no scale rinds, no fungi. The rinds grow a fingernail a decade. Breaking one erases a century.
