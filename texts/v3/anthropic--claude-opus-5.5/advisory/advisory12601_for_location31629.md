# Preparing for Ashcarr

## The Short Version

Ashcarr asks you to be quiet, slow, damp, careful with your hands, and fireless. Nothing there is dangerous in the way a cliff or a predator is dangerous. The risks run the other way. You are the fast, loud, heavy, careless thing in a place where everything has been balanced by generations of small corrections, and where marks last for centuries. Most of your preparation is about not leaving a mark, and about making yourself comfortable enough in the wet and the dark that you can slow down to the town's speed.

---

## When to Go

The season changes what you'll see.

- **Wet months (autumn, early spring):** The best time for a first visit. Fog comes heavy, the arms bend hard, and the lamps burn at their brightest orange. The ticking at dusk is at its fullest, and the rope walk and paper mill are visibly busy, as busy as anything here gets.
- **Spring, just after thaw:** This is when re-pinning happens, the most important work in the town. You'll see tenders measuring arms with knotted cord and deciding where the iron should bite this year. If you care about how Ashcarr works rather than how it feels, come now. Expect the tenders to be preoccupied and serious.
- **Winter:** Rime on every branch, arms locked stiff, nothing turning, no lamps. It is eerily beautiful and completely silent. The heavy work, pruning and grafting, happens then, and there may be room for an extra pair of careful hands. It is also cold enough to be genuinely hazardous.
- **Late summer dry spell:** Avoid it unless you want to see the town at its most anxious. Fogless nights leave Ashcarr blind, and the people read that as an omen. A stranger arriving just as the fog fails may be quietly associated with the failure. You don't want to be that stranger.

**Time of arrival:** Come in the mid-afternoon of a day when fog is likely. Walk down into the basin during the quietest hour, when the paths are dry and the green light falls through the yew. Then stay put as dusk comes and watch the whole thing wake up around you. Arriving in the dark, in knee-deep fog, on unfamiliar ground is the worst way to meet the place.

---

## Clothing

The ruling fact is that everything will be wet every morning and dry again by afternoon, over and over. You need clothing that stays warm when damp and forgives the cycle.

- **Wool, in layers.** A merino base layer, a heavier wool mid-layer, and a waxed cotton or tightly woven wool outer. Wool keeps its warmth when wet, and it doesn't rustle.
- **Avoid loud fabrics.** Crinkly synthetic shells, stiff nylon, and swishing waterproof trousers will be the loudest sound for a hundred paces. In a place where people murmur and a wren counts as loud, a noisy jacket is rude.
- **Muted colours.** Grey, green, brown, undyed. Nobody will demand it, but bright colour reads as shouting in Ashcarr's palette, and you'll feel it yourself within an hour.
- **Legs:** The night fog pools to about knee height, and everything it touches gets soaked. Wool trousers with gaiters, or waxed over-trousers that don't rustle, will keep your lower half from being cold and clammy all night.
- **A hat and a scarf.** The air in the bowl is cooler and heavier than the land around it, and drips come off the yew roofs all morning.
- **Gloves** that let you feel things. Thin wool, fingerless if you like. See below on why you'll want to touch walls and not machinery.

### Footwear, which deserves its own section

The ground goes from turf to moss to deep sphagnum that holds your footprint for a full minute. You need boots that are:

- **Waterproof and high enough** to handle soft, saturated moss.
- **Soft-soled rather than aggressively lugged.** Deep lugs tear moss. The paths are faintly sunken from generations of use, and the tenders will notice anyone chewing up the edges.
- **Clean.** Scrub your boots before descending. Remove soil, seeds, and anything from other worlds. Ashcarr is a closed, carefully tended ecology, and a stray seed that roots in a root zone is a real problem for people who manage their plants limb by limb. Treat entry the way you'd treat an island with strict biosecurity.

Bring a second pair of socks for every day, sealed in a dry bag.

## The Ground: Stay on the Paths

The streets are the gaps left between root zones. That tells you everything: **walk only on the paths.**

- The long moss lawns between buildings are deliberate empty space. Each building needs them for its roots, its light, and the swing of its engine arm. Walking across them compacts moss over living roots, and it may also put you under an arm.
- The paths drift slowly over years, so don't trust an old map, or even your memory of yesterday, too precisely. Follow the visible, sunken, well-worn line.
- At night the fog hides your feet completely. Roots, path edges, and sunken spots become invisible. Walk slowly, feel ahead with each step, and consider a walking staff. Don't borrow a stick from the town. Every twig there was placed or pruned deliberately.

---

## Things You Drop Are Gone

The moss is so deep that anything dropped sinks and vanishes. The tenders say whole engines are down there, preserved black and soft in the peat. Your belongings will join them.

- **Tether everything.** Lanyards on your torch, knife, notebook, glasses, keys, and anything you take out of a pocket.
- **Never set your bag down on the moss.** Hang it from yourself. Don't hang it from a tree either (see below). It stays on you.
- **Close every pocket.** A zip or button on everything.
- **Count your belongings when you leave.** It isn't just that you'll lose them. Whatever falls stays down there for centuries as a small foreign object in a buried archive.

---

## The Wood: Do Not Mark It

This is the most important rule, and the one visitors most easily break without meaning to.

**Every wound to the wood in Ashcarr is permanent.** Where iron passes through living timber, the wood has grown a collar of scar tissue around it. Where iron was swallowed completely, it shows only as a bulge in the bark. A living wall remembers.

So:

- **Never carve, scratch, cut, or pin anything into a tree.** A name scratched into a wall would still be there in three hundred years, stretched and healed into a ridge, in a town that redraws its map every decade. It would be like graffiti on someone's body.
- **Don't tie anything to the trees.** Not a hammock, a guyline, a clothesline, or a bag. Binding and bending living limbs is literally how the tenders build. A strap left on overnight is a tiny, unauthorised act of architecture.
- **Don't break twigs or pull leaves,** even idly. Pruning is how the town is controlled. Stray pruning is noise in their decisions.
- **Don't lean your weight on the walls,** especially at doorways. Those lintels are grafts shaped like clasped hands, and some are generations old.

What you *can* do, and should, is lay a bare palm flat against a wall at dusk and feel the low creak of the arms coming through the wood. That touch is harmless and it's the best thing in the town. This is why you want thin gloves you can take off easily.

## The Engines: Keep Your Hands to Yourself

The arms move only a hand's breadth at the tip, but the force behind that motion is enormous: enough, over years, to lever a building off its roots. The ratchets and gear trains translate that force into turning shafts.

- **Never put fingers near a pawl, gear, rod, or pinned arm tip.** These machines move so slowly they look inert, and that is exactly what makes them dangerous. They will close on a finger with the patience and strength of a growing tree, and nothing about them is designed to stop.
- **Don't stand in the swing of an arm,** even though the swing is small. Twenty paces of reaction-wood curling in the fog is not something to be braced against.
- **Don't try to help with a slipped ratchet** unless a tender asks and shows you exactly what to do. Resetting pawls is skilled, local work, and every engine has its own quirks.
- **Leave the moss in the gear teeth alone.** It looks like neglect, but the beetles are handling it (see below).

---

## Nothing Burns Here

Ashcarr has no fire. Nothing is burned, which is a fact about the town's whole way of being and not just a safety rule.

- **No stove, no candles, no lighter, no matches, no smoking.** Leave them at the rim or don't bring them. The smell of smoke would carry through the heavy air of the bowl and be instantly, shockingly foreign. It would be the equivalent of setting off an alarm.
- **Plan to eat cold food.** Bring things that need no cooking and tolerate damp: hard cheese, oatcakes in a sealed tin, dried fruit, nuts, cured meat, dense bread wrapped in waxed cloth. Pack everything in waterproof containers, because the morning condensation gets into everything.
- **A vacuum flask** of something hot, filled before you descend, will be worth more than almost anything else you carry on a cold foggy night.
- **Water:** The moss holds enormous amounts of it, but don't wring it out for drinking. That water is the town's reservoir and its hydraulic memory. Bring your own, or ask the tenders how they gather theirs.

---

## Light

The lamps glow orange, uneven, and dim, and on dry nights they don't glow at all.

- **Bring a torch, but choose one with a red or very low setting,** and use it pointed at your feet, sparingly. A white LED beam in Ashcarr would be the brightest thing the town had seen in its history. It would flatten the carbon glow, blind anyone near you, and pull every moth in the basin.
- **Consider not using it at all** once your eyes adjust. Night-adapted vision and the faint orange lamps, pulsing with the breath of the arms, are the whole point of being there at night.
- **Carry a spare light source anyway,** in case you arrive on a dry night and the town is completely dark. Just keep it covered.

---

## Electronics

Anything electronic you bring will face a daily cycle of condensation and drying, the exact conditions the arms were bred to exploit.

- **Seal devices in dry bags with desiccant** and take them out as rarely as you can.
- **Expect lenses to fog** every dusk and morning. Don't count on photographs, and don't count on the ones you take conveying what you saw.
- **Silence everything.** No beeps, notification sounds, or shutter clicks. A shutter click in the quiet hour will turn heads.
- **Don't plug anything into the town's wires,** under any circumstances. That current comes from the weather through trees. It is scarce, it is metered by fog, and the lamps it feeds are how the town sees at night.

---

## The Creatures

There are very few, and each has a place.

- **Slugs:** Big pale grey slugs graze algae off the iron, and the tenders value them. **Do not bring salt** in any loose or spillable form. Sealed and in your food is fine. Scattered on a path or rim-edge, it would be a small atrocity. Watch where you step, especially at dawn.
- **Beetles:** Small brown beetles live in the gear housings, and their grubs keep the moss out of the teeth. They are partners in the machinery. **Do not bring insecticide, repellent sprays, or anything aerosol.** If a beetle crawls on you, move it gently back where it came from.
- **Wrens:** One or two pairs nest in the thickest walls. They will wake you at dawn and they will be the loudest, brightest thing you hear. Don't go looking for the nests.
- **Moths:** A few at the lamps. Another reason to keep your own light off.
- **Nothing large.** Deer come to the rim and turn back. Don't bring a dog or any other animal. It would be the largest creature to enter the basin in memory, it would dig into the moss, and it would not understand the rules.

---

## Where to Sleep

There's nothing to suggest Ashcarr has guest quarters, and every building is a working engine full of machinery. You have two options.

**Camp on the rim.** This is the honest default. Set up on the higher, drier ground outside the basin, where the country is ordinary turf and the deer walk. Descend in the afternoon, stay through dusk and into the night, and climb back out. You'll wake above the fog and see it lying in the bowl like milk, with the dark square crowns of the town rising out of it. That view alone is worth the arrangement.

**Ask the tenders.** If they offer shelter, accept gratefully and sleep where you're put, on whatever you're given. Bring a closed-cell mat and a synthetic or wool sleeping bag. Down will collapse in the damp. Keep all of it inside a waterproof bivvy bag. Do not drive tent pegs into anything. Every patch of ground belongs to some root zone.

Whichever you choose, keep a full set of dry clothes sealed and never wear them in the basin. Being able to change into something dry is what keeps a damp, cold stay from becoming hypothermia. The cold here creeps up on you: moderate temperatures, saturated air, wet legs, and long hours standing still watching slow things.

## The People

The tenders wear undyed nettle cloth gone grey-green with damp. They move slowly and speak in murmurs. You may not notice them at first, and that is a fair description of how they'd like you to behave too.

- **Match their volume.** Speak low. Don't call out across the moss. The moss absorbs sound, so you'll have to walk up close to talk. Do that.
- **Match their pace.** Nobody in Ashcarr hurries, because nothing there rewards hurry. If you walk briskly, you'll look like someone fleeing.
- **Remember that they are always working.** Almost everything they do is maintenance, and the maintenance never ends. Don't interrupt someone checking pins or clearing gear teeth. Wait until they straighten up and look at you.
- **Ask about the map.** The paper mill keeps a map redrawn every ten years, and the old ones disagree. A building on the oldest map stands two paces east of where it stands now. Asking politely to see the maps is a good way to show you understand what kind of place this is. Don't be surprised if the answer is no.
- **Offer work, not goods.** Ashcarr produces rope, cord, paper, and fibre, and is otherwise self-contained. It probably doesn't need your objects, and new iron especially might be an unwelcome intrusion into a carefully balanced system. What it may need is patient hands in winter, for hauling pruned limbs or holding knotted cord while an arm is measured. Offer, and accept "no" easily.
- **Don't comment on dry nights** as if they were merely weather. To the tenders they are an omen, or at least a nuisance. Treat them with the same gravity the tenders do.

## How to Spend Your Time

The biggest adjustment is internal. Ashcarr runs on a clock of fog and drying, and nothing there happens on a human scale of minutes. Visitors who treat it as a sight to see get bored in an hour. Visitors who slow down find it hard to leave.

Some suggestions:

- **Watch the paper mill vat.** Look at it, walk away for an hour, come back. The pulp is being beaten so slowly it seems still, but the colour will have changed. Your sense of what counts as motion will start to recalibrate.
- **Stand at the mouth of the rope walk** and listen for the faint whirr of the twisting head. It crosses a hundred feet in a week. Try to spot where it is in the tunnel, and come back the next day to see how far it has gone.
- **Put your hand on a wall at dusk** as the fog comes in, and feel the arms beginning to curl through the living timber.
- **Stand still at the peak of the evening curl** and listen to the whole basin ticking like a room full of slow clocks, each engine at its own pace, the sound rising as the fog thickens.
- **Watch a lamp on a still, humid night.** When it begins to pulse faintly, you are watching the weather breathe through the wood, the iron, and the copper into light. There are very few places where that is visible.
- **Listen for the dynamo hum** and turn your head slowly. The pitch seems to shift with the angle.
- **Be awake at dawn** for the wrens and the dripping. That's the loudest the town ever gets, and it's still quiet.
- **Look at the oldest walls,** the ones fused into rippled slabs like poured wax. Look for the bulges and faint rust weeping from cracks, where iron has been swallowed whole.

Bring a notebook and pencil, tethered, in a waterproof sleeve. Pencil survives damp where ink won't. You'll want to write things down. The place is hard to describe afterwards, and your memory of its rhythm will fade faster than you'd expect once you're back in a world that moves quickly.

## Physical and Mental Effects to Expect

- **Damp chest and sinuses** after a few days. The air is heavy and wet, and mould and spores are everywhere. Travelers with asthma or mould sensitivity should bring medication and consider shorter visits.
- **Cold creep:** Moderate temperatures combined with saturation and stillness chill you slowly. Keep moving a little, drink something warm from your flask, change into dry socks.
- **Auditory strangeness:** In deep quiet, some people start hearing their own pulse, tinnitus, or phantom sounds. The dynamo hum at the edge of hearing makes this worse. It passes.
- **Time distortion:** Many visitors report losing track of hours. The day divides into wet and dry, curl and straighten, and the clock on your wrist starts to feel irrelevant. It's pleasant, but set yourself a firm departure time if you have somewhere to be.
- **Returning is harder than arriving.** Ordinary places will seem shockingly loud, bright, fast, and full of dead material. Plan a gentle transition. Spending the last night on the rim is a good buffer.

---

## Leaving

- **Count your belongings.** Anything missing is in the moss forever.
- **Check your clothes and boots for hitchhikers,** such as beetles, slug eggs, and moss fragments. Brush them off before you cross the rim, and carry nothing living out of the basin. Ashcarr's creatures are specialised partners, not souvenirs.
- **Take nothing.** No twigs, no moss, no rust flakes, no iron. There's always a temptation to take a bit of old metal as proof. Don't. Every pin is part of an engine that took centuries to reach its present balance.
- **Look back from the rim at dusk,** if you can, and watch the fog fill the bowl and the orange lamps come up one by one through the grey.

---

## A Final Thought

Everything in Ashcarr moves, and nothing moves quickly. It is a machine the size of a village and a garden at the same time, always slightly out of true and always being coaxed back. As a visitor you are one more small disturbance for the tenders to absorb. Your whole preparation comes down to making that disturbance as small as possible, so you can stay long enough to feel the place's rhythm in yourself.

If you do it well, you'll leave having caused no work for anyone. For a long time afterwards, on foggy nights, you may catch yourself listening for a scattered, patient ticking that isn't there.
