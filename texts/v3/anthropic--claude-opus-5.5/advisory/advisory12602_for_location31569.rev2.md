# Preparing for the Tabletop Sea

## A Field Guide for the Ordinary Traveler

The Tabletop Sea is not violent. Nothing in it wants to eat you. There are no storms below the surface, no currents except at the lip, no predators, and no disease that anyone has recorded. It is still one of the most punishing places an unaugmented human can choose to visit, because every one of its defining features works against the body. The air is thin. The water is at freezing. The noise never stops. The void has no reference points. The stone is as sharp as a scalpel. The sea is not trying to hurt you. It is simply built out of conditions your body was never meant to meet all at once.

The good news is that every hazard here is steady. Nothing changes, so if you prepare correctly for one hour, you have prepared correctly for all of them. This place punishes improvisation and rewards planning more than almost anywhere else.

---

## Part One: Before You Leave

### Get certified properly, and not just in diving

At minimum you need all of the following. They are not optional.

- **Drysuit certification with real cold-water experience**, meaning water under 4°C and ideally ice diving. You should have practiced drysuit failure drills until they bore you.
- **Altitude diving training.** Your decompression planning has to account for low surface pressure. That is covered below.
- **Blue-water or open-ocean diving experience** with no bottom reference. This is the skill most visitors underestimate.
- **Proficiency in hand signals, slate communication, and line signals** with your dive partner. You will not be able to speak underwater, and you will not be able to hear anything, including alarms.
- **If you plan to go below about 40 meters**, technical training on trimix or a closed-circuit rebreather. Most visitors should not go that deep, and there is little reason to. The water is so clear that the whole character of the place is available from 20 to 30 meters.

### Test your hearing now

Get a baseline audiogram before you go and another when you return. This place will stress your hearing more than anything else you are likely to do. You need to know what you lost, if anything, and whether it came back.

### Consider your medical fitness honestly

Do not visit if you have any of the following:
- A history of migraines triggered by sensory overload
- Vestibular disorders, Ménière's disease, or chronic tinnitus
- Heart conditions. Cold, altitude, and the effort of diving together put heavy strain on the heart.
- Uncontrolled anxiety, panic disorder, or a history of dissociative episodes. The sensory environment is extreme and hard to escape.
- A patent foramen ovale, or any history of decompression sickness

## Part Two: The Air Above the Water

People think of the Tabletop Sea as an underwater destination and forget that they have to live on the surface between dives. The surface is a high, cold, oxygen-poor plateau.

### Altitude

The plateau was pushed up into thin air, and the description strongly suggests conditions like the high Andes or Tibet. Plan for somewhere between 3,500 and 5,000 meters equivalent.

- **Acclimatize before you dive.** Spend at least three to five days at altitude, ideally somewhere with a hospital, before you arrive. Arriving through a portal directly at the rim and diving on day one is how people die here.
- **Bring altitude sickness medication** (acetazolamide, if your physician approves) and learn the signs of high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema: breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, confusion, loss of coordination.
- **Bring supplemental oxygen** for the surface camp. You will want it after dives in particular.
- **Expect poor sleep, headaches, and reduced appetite** for the first several days.

### Cold and fog

Slow fronts of freezing fog drift across the plateau for weeks at a time. You may arrive in clear weather and then spend a fortnight unable to see ten meters.

- Your surface shelter needs to be **heated, insulated, and wind-sealed**. A tent is not enough. Think of an expedition hut or a heated pod.
- Frost will form on everything. **Wet gear left outside will freeze solid within minutes.** Your drysuit zipper, your regulator, and your mask strap can all freeze after you surface. Have a warm, dry place to strip and store equipment right next to your entry point.
- **Mark your route between shelter and water physically**, with a fixed guide rope. Fog will erase every landmark, and there are few landmarks to begin with.

### UV and glare

Thin air and clean, clear water mean fierce ultraviolet exposure, both at the surface and at depth. Light reaches down unbroken, and at 40 meters the water is as bright as an afternoon.

- High-altitude glacier glasses on the surface
- **A tinted or UV-filtering mask lens** for diving. Eye fatigue from glare in a featureless, evenly lit blue will build over a long dive.
- Sunscreen on any exposed skin, though with correct thermal protection very little skin should be exposed.

## Part Three: Thermal Protection

The water sits at about one degree above freezing, sometimes half a degree. It never varies, and there is no warmer layer to retreat to.

### Your suit

- **A drysuit, without exception.** A wetsuit will not work here for more than a few minutes.
- **Heavy thermal undergarments**, and ideally **an electrically heated vest or undersuit** with its own battery. It is expensive, and worth every penny.
- **Dry gloves** with a ring system, not wet gloves. Your hands will lose function fast in this water, and without your hands you cannot manage your gear. You also cannot talk, so your hands are how you communicate.
- **A thick, full hood**. It matters for warmth, and it also matters for sound, as covered in Part Five.

### Protect the suit from the rock

This section connects directly to Part Six. **A drysuit tear in one-degree water, on a dive with a decompression obligation, is an emergency that can kill you.** The basalt and obsidian here were never weathered. The edges of fresh volcanic glass are sharper than surgical steel. Obsidian blades are used in surgery on some worlds for exactly that reason.

- Wear an **abrasion-resistant outer shell** over your drysuit: Kevlar, Cordura, or a similar armored over-suit.
- Wear **reinforced knee and forearm pads**.
- Wear **cut-resistant overgloves** on top of your dry gloves if you intend to touch anything at all, and ideally do not touch anything.
- Carry a **drysuit patch kit** and know how to manage a partial flood: go head-up, vent carefully, and abort.

### Dive time limits

Even with perfect gear, plan short. Thirty to forty-five minutes is realistic for most people in water this cold. Your heated vest will buy you more. Your hands, face, and judgment will run out before your gas does.

**Cold degrades thinking before it degrades anything else**, and in this place your thinking is also being attacked by the noise. You will not notice it happening. Set your turn times on the surface and obey them without negotiation.

## Part Four: Breathing

### Regulators and freeze-flow

In water at or near zero degrees, standard regulators can **freeze open**. Moisture ices in the first or second stage, and the regulator dumps gas uncontrollably. It is a well-known cold-water failure.

- Use only **environmentally sealed, cold-water-rated regulators**.
- Run **two fully independent first stages**, on a manifolded twinset or with a pony bottle, so that a freeze-flow on one does not end your gas supply.
- Avoid heavy breathing and big inflator bursts, which make freeze-flow more likely. Breathe slowly, which is good advice here anyway.
- Keep your regulators dry and warm until the moment you enter the water.

### Rebreathers

A closed-circuit rebreather has real advantages here. It gives you longer dives for your gas, less gas cycling through regulators that might freeze, and warmer, moist breathing gas. There are drawbacks. **CO₂ scrubbers perform worse in extreme cold**, so you will need an insulated canister and conservative scrubber duration. Rebreathers also depend on the diver hearing and responding to alarms, which you will not be able to hear. Use one only if it has **vibrating or visual alarms**, such as a heads-up display, and only if you are already a highly experienced rebreather diver.

### Fresh water changes your numbers

The sea is fresh, "clean, with nothing dissolved in it." Account for three things:

- **Buoyancy.** Fresh water is less dense than salt water, so you will need **noticeably less lead**. Do a proper weight check in the shallows on your first entry.
- **Depth gauges.** Some older gauges are calibrated for salt water and will misread slightly. Make sure your computer is set to fresh water.
- **Altitude.** This is the serious one.

### Decompression at altitude

At the plateau's surface, air pressure is significantly lower than at sea level. Relative to where you started, every meter of descent is a larger pressure change than it would be at the coast, and your ascent returns you to a lower-pressure surface. **Standard sea-level dive tables will under-predict your decompression risk.**

- Use a **dive computer with altitude correction**, and confirm it has detected the altitude correctly before your first dive.
- Dive **more conservatively than the computer says**. Use high gradient factors and add safety stops.
- **Never ascend to higher altitude after diving.** If your portal exit sits higher than the water, or if you plan to cross any ridge, wait at least 24 hours.
- Descending off the plateau is safe in pressure terms. Going *down* off the edge, if you somehow could, would not add decompression risk. Going *up* would.
- Ideally, your expedition should have **a portable recompression chamber** at the surface camp. The nearest real medical care may be on another world.

## Part Five: The Sound

This is the defining hazard of the Tabletop Sea, and the least familiar one. Nothing in ordinary dive training prepares you for it.

### What it is

Trillions of thumbnail-sized animals are each producing a gunshot-loud cavitation snap, spread evenly through the whole volume of the sea. Real snapping shrimp on Earth produce some of the loudest biological sounds in the ocean. A dense colony sounds like frying bacon when you dive over a reef. Here the entire sea is that colony. The sound comes from everywhere, at a level that "makes the skull hum and the teeth ache."

### What it will do to you

- **Hearing damage.** It is real, and it builds with time. Underwater, much of what you perceive reaches your inner ear through **bone conduction**, meaning through your skull, which bypasses the eardrum. Ear plugs alone will not protect you, and **you must never wear ordinary earplugs while diving**. They trap air, cannot be equalized, and can cause barotrauma.
- **Headaches, jaw ache, and dental pain** from constant vibration.
- **Nausea and vestibular disturbance.** Your balance organs sit in your inner ear, alongside your hearing, and this much stimulation can confuse them.
- **Cognitive interference.** The description says you "cannot hear yourself think in words." Take this seriously. Your inner monologue, the voice you use to reason, plan, and count, will feel muffled or absent. Visitors describe it as thinking through wool. **Complex decisions made underwater here will be bad decisions.**
- **Post-dive disorientation.** When you surface, the sudden return of true quiet "feels like falling." Expect vertigo, a disorienting sense of absence, and possibly tinnitus that persists for hours. **Do not climb out onto rocks, and do not handle heavy tanks, in the first few minutes after surfacing.** Hold onto something, float, breathe, and let your head settle.

### How to protect yourself

- **A thick, well-fitted neoprene hood** gives modest attenuation and is your first layer.
- **A full-face mask** covers more of your head and gives more protection than a half mask, though no full-face mask will block the sound completely.
- **The best protection is a hard helmet**, a commercial-style diving helmet with a padded, acoustically damped liner. It is heavy and unusual for leisure diving, but it gives meaningfully more protection than anything else. If your trip involves long or repeated dives, this is the right choice.
- **Limit total exposure.** Treat time in the water like a noise dose at a factory. Keep a log. Space your dives. Take full rest days.
- **Track your hearing daily.** A simple hearing-test app at the surface camp, each evening, will show you whether you are recovering between dives. If your thresholds are dropping day after day, stop diving.

### Communication is gone

You cannot speak. You cannot hear your partner. **Acoustic underwater communication systems, and there are several, will be completely useless.** The noise floor is too high. You will not hear a tank-banger, a whistle, a buzzer, or an alarm on your own computer.

Plan around this completely:

- **Hand signals.** Agree on an extended set before the trip, including signals for "I'm cold," "I'm disoriented," "my head hurts," "the sound is too much," "I need to go up now," and "something's wrong but I can't explain."
- **Wrist slates and pencils** for anything more complex.
- **Light signals.** A torch pulsed in a pattern. Light carries very well here, since the water is extraordinarily clear. Circle the beam for "OK" and wave it side to side for "problem."
- **A buddy line**, a short tether between you and your partner, lets you signal by pulls: one pull for "OK?", two for "going up," and so on. It also stops you from losing each other in the void.
- **Hardwired communication.** If your group has the budget, a cable-connected helmet comms system (a surface-supplied or tethered arrangement) is the only reliable way to talk to the surface or each other. It works by wire, not by sound through water, so the crackle cannot stop it.
- **All alarms must be tactile or visual.** Choose a dive computer and rebreather controller that vibrate, flash, or show a heads-up display. Test this before you go.

### Sonar and acoustic navigation won't work either

Echo sounders, acoustic positioning, and sonar are all drowned out. The expedition's own sounding engine pulses every forty-one seconds and is inaudible. Even the longwhips have to read the noise itself to navigate. You will navigate by compass, line, and sight.

## Part Six: The Void

Most of the sea is "simply space," with hundreds of meters of transparent water between floor and surface and forty kilometers between ridges.

### Spatial disorientation

In featureless blue water with no current, no particles, and no visible floor or surface, many divers lose any sense of up, down, rising, or sinking. The even, directionless light here makes this worse. You will not even get a gradient of brightness to tell you which way is up. The noise will be tearing at your balance at the same time.

- **Watch your depth gauge constantly.** Uncontrolled sinking or rising is the main way you will get hurt in open water here, and your body will not warn you.
- **Carry a descent or reference line.** For open-water hanging, the classic blue-water technique is a weighted line dropped from a surface float, marked at intervals, which divers stay close to. It gives your eyes and hands something to anchor to.
- **Watch your exhaled bubbles** for which way is up. They always rise.
- **Do not free-hang without a tether** until you have done several dives here and know how your body reacts.

### Depth illusion

The water is so clear that the black floor can look perhaps ten meters away when it is actually a hundred and fifty. Divers in exceptionally clear water have followed the floor down, thinking it close, and ended up far deeper than planned. At depth, **nitrogen narcosis combined with noise-muddled thinking** is a dangerous pairing.

Set a **hard maximum depth** on your computer's alarm, and make sure that alarm is one you can feel or see.

### Distance and getting lost

Features are tens of kilometers apart. Without sonar or landmarks, it is very easy to swim out into the blue and have no idea which way leads back to your entry point.

- **Use a distance line** tied off at your entry and paid out from a reel, like cave diving in reverse, for any horizontal excursion.
- For long transits, such as following cables to the ironworks, use **diver propulsion vehicles (scooters)** with extra battery margin, and **follow the cables themselves as your line**. They are reliable, continuous, and visible from far away against the black floor. Do not leave them.
- Carry a **compass**, and a **delayed surface marker buoy** you can send up so the surface team can spot you.
- Your surface support should have **a boat or a stable floating platform** that can reach you. Surfacing forty kilometers from anything, in freezing fog, at altitude, is a situation to prevent at all costs.

## Part Seven: The Stone

Covered partly above, but worth its own section.

- The basalt columns have hexagonal corners "that could cut a hand." Believe it.
- The obsidian ridges are "broken plates of black glass." They will slice neoprene, latex seals, hoses, and skin without resistance.
- **There is no sand and no mud.** There is nowhere soft to kneel, rest, or settle.
- **Don't land on the floor.** Keep perfect buoyancy and stay off it.
- If you must touch or brace against rock, do it with cut-resistant gloves only, and never with a hose, inflator, or drysuit seal against the stone.
- Watch your **low-pressure inflator hose and regulator hoses** in particular. A cut hose in freezing water is a very bad day.
- The glass faces are beautiful, black and bottle-green and smoky bronze, and shine without flickering. **Do not take samples.** See Part Nine.

## Part Eight: The Fauna

### Clickwrights

They are invisible until you learn to look for "a faint lens, a small kink in the light." Each defends about a meter of water around itself by snapping.

What this means for you:
- **You will constantly be intruding on their space.** You are several meters of foreign object moving through a sea of animals that each insist on a meter-wide sphere.
- A snap at arm's length is gunshot-loud. At contact range, a cavitation strike can bruise, sting, or produce a tiny burn, since the collapse is briefly very hot. Earth's snapping shrimp can stun small fish. A clickwright is unlikely to seriously injure a suited diver, but **exposed skin around the mouth, and your eardrums, are vulnerable**. This is another reason to prefer full-face or helmet systems.
- Cavitation erodes metal. Ship propellers wear from it. Short visits won't cause meaningful damage, but check your mask lens, regulator faceplate, and any exposed metal for tiny pits after multiple dives.
- **Move slowly and steadily.** Sudden movement probably provokes more snapping.
- **Never grab at them, net them, or try to collect one.**

### Longwhips

They are train-length, glass-clear, and eyeless, moving on a constant sine wave.

- They are almost certainly not dangerous in intent. They are filter feeders.
- They are large, invisible, and blind, and they navigate by reading the noise. **A diver may be a hole in the noise-texture they don't recognize.**
- **Keep well clear of their path.** They move at a constant rate and do not seem to stop or turn quickly. Watch for the long ripple of distorted light and move perpendicular to it.
- Their size and silence can trigger a strong startle or panic response when one passes close. Know this in advance.

### Stonebells

They are gray and domed, and they cling in small groups to the obsidian ridges.

- Their "ring" is felt in the chest, not heard. This is almost certainly infrasound or very low-frequency vibration.
- Infrasound can cause **unease, anxiety, a sense of dread or presence, nausea, and chest pressure** in humans. These feelings are physical effects, not intuition. If you feel sudden fear near a stonebell colony, including the one on the drowned railcar, recognize the cause and move away rather than panicking.
- Don't touch them.

### Scatterflies

They are bright, brief, and fast, and they flock near the ironworks and the lamp.

- Nothing is known about their danger, which is to say nothing is known. They are the only animal here that moves suddenly, and **sudden movement will startle you in a place where nothing else moves suddenly.** Be ready for it.
- Don't pursue them.

### The mineral haze

This is the "not quite alive" particulate that drifts off fresh glass faces in threads.

- It's the base of the entire ecosystem. Nobody knows what it is.
- **Do not let it into your breathing loop, your mouth, or your eyes.** A full-face mask helps here too.
- Rinse your gear thoroughly with filtered water after every dive, and keep an eye on any skin irritation.

## Part Nine: The Ironworks

These are the only structures in the sea, and they are probably what you came to see. They are also where most of the specific, avoidable dangers are.

### The Lip Station: the most dangerous place in the sea

Fourteen church-sized turbine housings sit at the edge where the whole sea pours off the plateau into cloud. Eleven are still turning.

- **Every hazard of a hydroelectric intake applies here.** The overflow is "the one place in the whole sea with a steady gradient," which means **the one place with a current**. It flows in one direction, continuously, toward the edge.
- **Turbine intakes are lethal.** Water rushing into an intake creates suction you cannot swim against. Divers have died at real dams this way. **Do not approach the intakes, the housings' upstream faces, or the lip itself.**
- **Beyond the lip is an eleven-hundred-kilometer waterfall** into cloud. Nobody comes back from that.
- If you want to see the Lip Station, view it **from a safe upstream distance, tethered to a fixed anchor, with surface support that knows exactly where you are**, or better, from the surface on a secured boat. You can feel its thrum through the rock for a kilometer, and that is close enough to appreciate it.
- Treat the current zone as starting much farther back than you think. With no silt or particles in the water, **you will not see the current**. You will only discover it when you are already moving.

### The cables

They are armored and furred with clickwright moults, running hundreds of kilometers.

- They power the lamp, the sounding engine, and possibly more. **Assume they are live.** Do not cut, pry at, or strip them, and do not disturb the crust.
- They are your best navigation aid on any long excursion, as covered in Part Six.

### The sounding engine

It is a bronze diaphragm that fires every forty-one seconds.

- You won't hear it. At close range, though, a device designed to send pulses across a continent-sized sea may produce **a strong pressure wave**. It could hurt your ears, lungs, or sinuses.
- The longwhips avoid it in a wide arc. **Take the hint and do the same.** Keep a generous distance, and never be directly in front of the diaphragm.

### The lamp

It is an electric arc in a glass-and-iron cage on a pole.

- Arc lamps are hot, and this is a live electrical installation underwater. **Don't touch the cage.**
- Arc light emits strong UV. Don't stare into it from close range. At a distance it is a small yellow-white star, and that is the best way to see it.
- The scatterflies gather here, so expect sudden movement.

### The drowned railcar

It lies on its side, with the cable running in and stopping, cold iron racks inside, and stonebells on the roof.

- **Do not enter it.** It is an overhead environment, meaning no direct ascent is possible from inside. It contains old iron equipment with sharp, corroded edges, and a live cable ends inside. Penetrating wrecks requires specific training, and in water this cold, with this much noise and no communication, even trained divers should think hard.
- The stonebell colony on the roof means the infrasound effects will be strongest here.

### The pump hall: do not enter

This is the one place visitors should treat as off-limits entirely.

- The clickwrights are packed closer than a meter apart. The noise is "louder than anywhere else in the world, loud enough to shimmer the water visibly." The light inside "flickers with sparks."
- **Water that shimmers visibly from sound pressure is carrying enough acoustic energy to injure you.** Serious hearing damage is very likely in seconds to minutes. Sinus, lung, or other gas-space trauma is plausible. Disorientation severe enough to lose your way out of an enclosed iron shell is possible.
- Dense cavitation of this intensity may damage equipment and seals.
- **View it from outside, at a distance, if at all.** Even outside, limit your time. You will feel it.

### Respect what the builders left

"There are no bodies, no names, no signs, no rubbish." The builders left engines and nothing else. Whatever they came for, they finished it and went. Leave it exactly as you found it. **Do not switch anything on or off.** Nobody knows what depends on these machines. The sea itself depends on a balance.

## Part Ten: Don't Break the Balance

The Warmth Rule states it plainly: *if the clickwrights stopped, the sea would freeze in a season.* This is a closed, sterile, perfectly balanced system that has held for millennia.

It is fragile to exactly the kind of thing visitors bring.

- **Biological contamination.** The sea is sterile, and nothing has ever grown there. You are carrying billions of microbes on your skin, gear, and breath. Algae, bacteria, or fungal spores that take hold on the bare rock, feeding on the mineral haze or on moults, could compete with or disrupt the base of the food web. **Decontaminate every piece of gear before entry**: disinfect, rinse with sterile water, and dry fully. Rebreather loops, BCD interiors, and drysuit undergarments are common carriers.
- **No food, no waste, nothing organic in the water.** "Mud needs death, and decay, and time." Don't introduce the first two.
- **No chemical contamination.** Avoid sunscreen that could wash off, avoid lubricants beyond minimal seal grease, and never release anything into the water.
- **No collecting.** No clickwrights, no glass, no moults, no haze samples.
- **No noise-making or interference devices.** It isn't clear what would disrupt the snapping, and it isn't worth finding out.

Visitors who treat the Tabletop Sea like any other dive site put something irreplaceable at risk. Treat it like a sterile room that happens to be the size of a continent.

## Part Eleven: Your Mind

The physical preparation is a checklist. The psychological preparation is harder to plan for, and the place is genuinely strange.

### What people report

- **The noise becomes silence.** Within an hour or two, the brain gives up treating the crackle as sound and files it as the background of existence. This is disorienting in itself.
- **Loss of verbal thought.** The noise seems to crowd out the inner voice. Some find this frightening. Some find it peaceful in a way they struggle to explain later.
- **A sense of permanence and insignificance.** Nothing here changes, not in a day and not in a thousand years. Hanging in an endless, balanced, bright blue void, with no sense of up or down and a machine at the edge of the world still turning, can produce a profound, almost vertiginous feeling of timelessness. Some divers find it transcendent. Others find it deeply unsettling.
- **The fall into quiet.** Surfacing into real silence is the moment many describe most vividly. It can feel like the ground dropping away, sometimes emotionally as well as physically.
- **Lingering effects.** Tinnitus, an odd sensitivity to silence, trouble concentrating, or strange dreams can last for days after you leave.

### How to prepare

- **Make every decision on the surface.** Your dive plan, turn times, depth limits, and contingencies should be written on your wrist slate before you enter. Underwater, you should only be following instructions, not composing them.
- **Use simple, rehearsed routines.** Checklists, repeated drills, and a fixed sequence for every entry and exit. Make it automatic, because you will not be thinking clearly.
- **Dive with someone you trust completely**, who knows your non-verbal cues. You will be communicating through posture, hand signals, and your eyes.
- **Build in rest days** so that both your hearing and your mind recover between dives.
- **Plan for the surface.** Have your warm shelter ready, have your partner or support crew waiting, and give yourself ten quiet minutes after each dive before doing anything complicated.
- **Talk about it afterward.** Journal, debrief, whatever works for you. Visitors who process the experience seem to integrate it better than those who don't.

## Part Twelve: A Suggested First Visit

**Days 1–5:** Acclimatize to altitude somewhere with medical care. Hike and rest. Watch for altitude sickness.

**Day 6:** Arrive at the plateau. Set up the heated shelter and fixed guide rope to the water. Prepare and decontaminate all gear. No diving.

**Day 7:** A short **shallow orientation dive** of about fifteen minutes, from 5 to 10 meters, near the entry point, over rock you can see. Check your weighting in fresh water, get used to the cold, and meet the sound for the first time. Come up, sit quietly, and check your hearing.

**Day 8:** Rest. Evaluate. Were your hands working? How are your ears? How did your head feel after surfacing?

**Day 9:** A **blue-water hang** of 30 to 40 minutes on a weighted reference line from a surface float, at 15 to 25 meters. Look up, look down, and get used to the void. Look for clickwrights, the lens-kinks in the light. Watch for the tiny sparks.

**Day 10:** Rest.

**Day 11 onward:** If everything is going well, plan a **scooter excursion along a cable**, with a strict distance limit and surface support following, toward one of the ironworks. The lamp is a good first target: visible from a distance, and low-risk if you keep clear of the cage. View only. Do not enter anything.

**Near the end:** If you want to see the Lip Station, do it **from the surface or from a fixed, tethered upstream position**, with the most experienced person in your group running the plan.

**Final day:** Pack out every single thing you brought. Wait at least 24 hours after your last dive before gaining any altitude.

**After you return home:** Take a follow-up audiogram, and let yourself have some quiet.

## Packing List

**Thermal and suit**
- Drysuit (plus backup seals)
- Heavy undergarments; heated vest or undersuit with batteries
- Dry glove system; cut-resistant overgloves
- Thick full hood
- Abrasion-resistant outer shell, knee and forearm pads
- Drysuit patch and repair kit

**Breathing and dive systems**
- Two fully independent cold-water-rated sealed regulators, or a rebreather with insulated scrubber
- Altitude-correcting dive computer with vibration or visual alarms
- Backup computer or depth gauge and timer
- Full-face mask or damped helmet, plus a backup
- UV-filtering or tinted lens

**Navigation and signaling**
- Compass
- Distance-line reel; weighted reference line and surface float
- Delayed surface marker buoy
- Buddy line
- Wrist slates and pencils
- Primary and backup lights for signaling
- Diver propulsion vehicles and spare batteries, for cable excursions
- Hardwired comms system (optional, strongly recommended)

**Surface camp**
- Heated, sealed shelter
- Supplemental oxygen
- Portable recompression chamber (strongly recommended)
- Fixed guide ropes
- Glacier glasses, high-SPF sunscreen
- Warm, dry gear-storage area near entry
- Boat or floating platform

**Medical and personal**
- Altitude sickness medication
- A first aid kit with trauma supplies for deep cuts
- A hearing-test app or portable audiometer
- Headache and anti-nausea medication
- Journal

**Decontamination**
- Disinfectant and sterile rinse water for all gear
- Sealed containers to carry every piece of waste back out

## A Final Note

The Tabletop Sea asks very little of you and forgives very little. It will not change to accommodate you, and nothing in it will come to your rescue. What it offers is something almost nowhere else can: a world held perfectly still by its own roar, bright, clean, unfinished, and indifferent. For a short while you can hang inside that balance, where a lamp nobody tends burns on a pole in the blue and turbines hum at the edge of the world.

Prepare for it fully. Dive it gently. Leave it exactly as it was.
