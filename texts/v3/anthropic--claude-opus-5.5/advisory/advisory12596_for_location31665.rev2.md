# Preparing to Visit the Uncovered Shelf

## The Short Version

This is a high-altitude glacier forefield, probably between 5,000 and 6,000 meters, with no shelter, no drinkable surface water, no trails, and a drop into cloud on one side and a collapsing ice wall on the other. Its dangers are quiet ones. Nothing here will attack you. The air will starve you, the cold will wear you down, and the polished wet stone will throw you off your feet at the worst possible moment.

The place is also extraordinarily delicate. Much of the ground you'll walk on has never been touched by anything alive. Prepare so that you leave no mark, because here even a footprint on a lichen is a mark that lasts a century.

---

## 1. The Central Problem: You Cannot Just Arrive

The single most dangerous thing about visiting this place as a multiverse traveler is the arrival itself. Mountaineers reach these altitudes over weeks. You could reach them in an instant.

The clues in the description are clear. The sky is indigo at midday, the air is "above almost everything that breathes easily," bar-headed geese pass overhead, and you can hear your pulse when the wind drops. That puts you somewhere around half of sea-level air pressure. An unacclimatized person dropped here can develop acute mountain sickness within hours. Worse, they can develop high-altitude pulmonary edema (fluid in the lungs) or cerebral edema (swelling of the brain) within a day or two, and both can kill.

**How to prepare:**

- **Acclimatize elsewhere first.** Spend two to three weeks beforehand working your way up. You might use a high-altitude location in your own world, or a waypoint in this world's lower valleys if you can travel there. Follow the standard rule: above 3,000 meters, raise your *sleeping* altitude by no more than about 300–500 meters a night, with a rest day every few days. If you can't acclimatize, don't stay longer than a few hours, and bring supplemental oxygen.
- **Talk to a doctor who knows altitude.** Ask about acetazolamide (Diamox) for prevention, and about carrying dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies. Get your heart and lungs checked. Some conditions, such as certain heart problems, sickle cell disease, and some lung diseases, make this place off-limits for you.
- **Bring a pulse oximeter.** Readings in the high 70s to mid 80s are normal at this altitude once you're acclimatized. A steady fall, especially together with symptoms, is a warning.
- **Learn the warning signs and commit to acting on them:**
  - A headache that pain relievers don't touch.
  - Stumbling when you walk heel-to-toe in a straight line.
  - Confusion.
  - Breathlessness *at rest*.
  - A wet cough, or pink froth when you cough.

  Any of these means you leave. On this shelf, "descending" is impossible: the only way down is a drop into cloud. Your exit portal *is* your descent, so know exactly how fast you can open it and practice doing so while tired, cold, and slightly stupid.
- **Get fit.** Spend months beforehand on steady cardio and leg strength. Fitness won't prevent altitude sickness, but it gives you reserves when every step costs three breaths.

**Plan your exit before you plan anything else.** Know your return point, and know what you'll do if the cloud swallows the shelf and you can't see ten meters.

## 2. Choosing Where and When to Arrive

### Where on the shelf

- **Arrive near the far lip, on the oldest ground, but not right at the edge.** It's the most stable rock, farthest from the ice, and the least fragile to the few living things there, since it has had eighty years to toughen. Choose bare stone, not moss or cushion plants.
- **Never arrive near the ice face.** A 40-meter wall of retreating glacier drops blocks, sometimes without warning. The zone at its base is sterile new stone, gleaming wet, and inside the range of falling ice.
- **Never arrive on the glacier's top surface.** It is honeycombed with cryoconite holes and presumably crevassed ("a deep crack like a distant rifle when a crevasse opens").
- **Your portal point may drift in meaning.** The ice retreats every summer, so a spot that was "near the ice edge" last year is now well out from it. Check where the wall is *now*.

### When

- **Midsummer, just after the solstice,** is the only sensible window. The cushion plants and saxifrage bloom, pikas cut hay, the wind-fall of insects peaks, and the ice worms rise at dusk. It's also when the ice melts fastest, so rockfall and water are at their peak too.
- **Late spring or early autumn** brings the bar-headed geese overhead at night and before dawn. It also brings colder nights, more snow on the ground, and less margin for error.
- **Winter: don't.** The shelf is buried under wind-packed snow and most life is gone or asleep. You'd be visiting a deadly white nothing.

### Time of day

The daily cycle shapes everything:

| Time | What's happening | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Night** | Hard freeze. Rock weeps and glazes. Orange glow on the cloud below. | Brutal cold. Every surface becomes verglas (a thin, clear glaze of ice). Stay put. |
| **Pre-dawn** | Geese may pass (spring/autumn). Eastern peaks go rose. | Worth being awake. Terrain at its most treacherous. |
| **Dawn (≈10 min)** | Copper light on wet stone; meltwater lines glow. | The single most beautiful moment. Be in position *already*; don't be walking. |
| **Late morning** | Thaw builds; tarns loosen; drip becomes trickle. | Best window for walking and exploring. Ice glaze softens, and it's the calmest part of the day. |
| **Afternoon** | Upslope wind brings insects; cloud swallows the shelf; birds and spiders feed; **rockfall peaks.** | Richest wildlife, worst conditions. Whiteout, wet, and falling stone. Stay away from rock walls and the ice face. |
| **Dusk** | Wind drops; ice worms rise on the glacier surface. | Magical, and very cold. The ice worms are on the glacier, which you shouldn't walk on (see below). |

A good visit probably means arriving in the late morning, moving through the day carefully, sheltering in the afternoon cloud, watching dusk, enduring the night, and seeing dawn before you leave. That means an overnight stay, with everything that implies.

## 3. The Ground: Why Your Feet Matter Most

The description says the rock feels "almost like glass, cold and faintly slick." Take this as seriously as anything else here. This ground is:

- **Glacially polished gneiss**, with no weathering and no grit to grip.
- **Covered in water sheets a few millimeters deep** during the day.
- **Coated in clear ice glaze** at night and in the early morning, often invisible.
- **Tilted downslope toward a drop into cloud.**

A slip on a polished, wet, tilted slab doesn't stop. You keep sliding. This is the terrain most likely to actually kill you.

**Footwear and traction:**

- **Stiff, insulated mountaineering boots,** waterproof and rated for high-altitude cold. The meltwater will find any weakness in cheaper boots within an hour.
- **Microspikes at minimum; crampons are better.** Crampons on bare rock are awkward and noisy and will scratch the stone. They will also keep you alive on verglas. Consider carrying both: spikes for the thawed middle of the day, crampons for glazed mornings and evenings.
- **Trekking poles** with carbide tips, for balance on the sheets.
- **An ice axe,** and the training to use it to stop a slide. Take a course. Practice self-arrest on a safe slope until it's reflex.
- **A helmet.** Rockfall peaks in the afternoon and chunks come off the ice face. It's also the cheapest insurance against a head strike if you slip.

**Rules of movement:**

- **Walk on bare rock or old snow, and read the surface.** A shine that looks like water may be ice. Test before you commit your weight.
- **Treat the lip as a cliff.** Stay well back. The cloud floor below hides how far it drops.
- **Stay off the milky tarns,** at dawn especially. They freeze "through" at night, but how thick that ice is, and whether it will hold you, is guesswork.
- **Stay off the glacier's top surface** unless you're trained, roped, and with a partner. Cryoconite holes, hidden crevasses, and meltwater channels make it a real glacier with real glacier dangers.
- **Keep your distance from the ice face.** A good rule is to stay back at least the wall's height again, and more in the afternoon. The ice is retreating because it's melting, and melting ice sheds.
- **Keep clear of the base of any slope that's shedding rock,** and move quickly past loose areas in the afternoon.

## 4. Clothing: You Will Be Wet and Cold at the Same Time

This is a wet cold, which is harder than a dry cold. Afternoon cloud soaks everything, rime forms on surfaces, drip is constant near the ice, and then night freezes it all.

**Layering system:**

- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic. Never cotton.
- **Mid layers:** a fleece or grid-fleece plus a synthetic insulated jacket. Synthetic insulation keeps some warmth when damp, and down doesn't.
- **Big puffy:** a heavy down or synthetic parka with a hood for standing still at dawn, dusk, and night. Keep it in a waterproof bag and put it on over everything when you stop moving.
- **Shell:** a fully waterproof, breathable jacket and trousers with a hood that fits over your helmet. The cloud will test them.
- **Hands:** the description names "ache in the hands" first, so take this seriously. Bring thin liner gloves, insulated gloves, and heavy mittens with waterproof shells. Carry spares of each, in a dry bag. Wet gloves at dusk mean frostbite risk. Chemical hand warmers help, though they work less well in thin air.
- **Feet:** thick wool socks, with a dry spare pair kept only for sleeping. Consider vapor-barrier liners for the night.
- **Head and face:** a warm hat, a balaclava or buff, and something for the wind.

**Keep a dry reserve.** One set of base layers, socks, and gloves should never leave its dry bag except at night. Being dry at 2 a.m. may matter more than anything else you carry.

## 5. Eyes and Skin: Dim Doesn't Mean Safe

The description stresses how dim and diffuse the light is. Don't let that fool you. At this altitude UV radiation is enormously stronger than at sea level, and it passes through thin cloud. Snow, ice, and wet polished rock reflect it back up under your hat brim. Snow blindness, a sunburn of the cornea, can come on through what feels like an overcast day, and you won't feel it until hours later. By then your eyes feel full of sand and you may not be able to open them.

- **Glacier glasses** with side shields, category 4 lenses, plus a spare pair. Keep them on during the day even when it feels gloomy.
- **High-SPF sunscreen** on every exposed surface, including under your nose, chin, and ears. Reapply often.
- **SPF lip balm.** Lips crack fast here.
- **Something to see in the dark:** a headlamp with a red-light mode and spare batteries kept warm inside your clothes. Cold kills batteries fast.

---

## 6. Water: Everywhere, and Almost None of It Drinkable

"Everything is wet, but seldom in a way you could drink from."

- **Glacial meltwater is loaded with rock flour,** ground stone finer than talc. It clogs water filters almost instantly, and drinking it can upset your stomach. It's not a safe primary source.
- **The snow and ice carry soot,** black carbon from lowland industry, and the cryoconite holes contain bacteria. This is not pristine mountain water.
- **The tarns are the same milky slurry,** and they're frozen at night.

**Your options:**

- **Bring water in,** at least a few liters, kept in insulated bottles. Store them upside down so ice forms at the bottom and the lid doesn't freeze shut. Sleep with one inside your sleeping bag.
- **Melt clean snow if you need to.** Pick the whitest older snow and avoid the dark bands. Let the melt settle so grit drops out, pour off the top, then boil or treat it. Water boils at roughly 80°C at this height, so it takes longer to purify and cooking is slower.
- **Use a liquid-fuel or cold-rated stove.** Ordinary gas canisters struggle in cold and at altitude. Bring more fuel than you think you'll need: melting snow uses a lot.

**Drink more than you want to.** At altitude you lose large amounts of water just by breathing thin, dry air, and dehydration makes altitude sickness and cold injuries worse. Aim for 4–5 liters a day, and check that your urine stays pale.

## 7. Food

Altitude kills your appetite at the same time the cold and effort burn through calories.

- **Bring food you'll actually want to eat** when you feel slightly sick: salty, sweet, and familiar.
- **Carbohydrates are easier to digest at altitude** than heavy fats, though fats matter for warmth at night.
- **Keep snacks inside your jacket** so they don't freeze into bricks.
- **Plan to eat something every hour** whether you feel like it or not.
- **Pack out every crumb and wrapper.** (See the section on ethics.)

---

## 8. Staying the Night

If you want the dusk ice worms, the orange glow, and the copper dawn, you need to stay overnight. That's where the real risk lies.

- **Tent:** a four-season mountaineering tent, low-profile for the wind, with stakes and anchor methods suited to *rock*. You can't drive stakes into polished gneiss. Bring stuff sacks you can fill with stones, or guy lines to tie to boulders.
- **Sleeping bag:** rated to at least –20°C, ideally colder, with a waterproof bivy cover. Everything will be damp by nightfall.
- **Sleeping pad:** at least two, one closed-cell foam and one insulated inflatable, stacked. The rock pulls heat out of you.
- **Pitch your tent:**
  - Away from the ice face and any rockfall line.
  - Away from where meltwater flows. Remember that sheet water moves across the surface in daytime and follows the scratches. Don't camp in a channel.
  - Away from the lip.
  - On bare rock, *never* on moss, cushion plants, or lichen.
- **Expect little sleep.** Poor sleep and periodic breathing (Cheyne-Stokes breathing, where you seem to stop breathing, then gasp) are normal at altitude and alarming the first time. Headaches that get worse overnight are not normal and are a reason to leave.

A shorter alternative is to arrive before dawn for the copper light, leave by early afternoon before the cloud and rockfall peak, and return another day at dusk for the ice worms. It's safer, if you can manage precise timing.

## 9. Navigation and the Cloud

Every afternoon, cloud climbs the valley walls and swallows the shelf. You could lose sight of everything, including the lip.

- **Carry a GPS** with your arrival point, portal point, and camp saved as waypoints. Bring spare batteries, kept warm.
- **Carry a compass** and know the shelf's orientation. The scratches all point downslope, so if you know which way is downslope, you know which way leads to the lip and the drop. The meltwater sheets flow that way too.
- **When the cloud comes in, stop moving,** or move only along paths you've already walked. Don't wander into whiteout on wet slabs.
- **Carry a whistle** if you travel with a partner.
- **Carry a satellite messenger** if this world's satellites work for you. The airliner overhead suggests an industrial world with modern infrastructure, so it may. Don't count on anything, though. Assume you are completely alone.

## 10. Practicalities That Are Easy to Forget

- **Border regions.** High ranges like this often sit on contested or militarized borders. Find out what nation or authority controls this place in this world. An ordinary traveler arriving without papers in a restricted mountain zone could face very ordinary, very serious trouble.
- **Cameras and phones.** Batteries die in cold. Keep them inside your clothes and bring spares. Condensation forms when you bring cold devices into a warm tent, so let them warm slowly in a sealed bag.
- **Lammergeiers drop bones onto slabs** to crack them. It's unlikely to hit you, but don't linger under a lammergeier working a patch of rock.
- **Snow leopards** almost never trouble people. If you're lucky enough to see one, stay still and enjoy it.

## 11. Ethics: Walking Through Time

This matters more here than almost anywhere else.

The shelf is a timeline written in stone. Walk from the lip toward the ice and you walk backward in time: plants, then moss, then lichen, then bare rock that only weeks ago was under ice and has never been touched by anything. You would be the first living thing on some of it.

- **Every lichen is ancient.** It grows about a millimeter a year, so a palm-sized patch is older than anyone alive. Never step on one. Never scrape or touch one.
- **Moss cushions and cushion plants are equally precious.** A fist-sized cushion may be decades old, and one careless step crushes it.
- **Walk only on bare rock.** You'll get used to planning each step. The shelf is about 98% rock, so you can do this.
- **Don't move stones.** Boulders shelter the first lichens and the spiders. The lee of a boulder is where life begins here.
- **Pack out everything,** including human waste. Bring a waste bag system (like a WAG bag) and use it. The soil here is being built grain by grain from dust, dead insects, and bird droppings, and you should not add to it. Nothing breaks down quickly at this altitude, and on sterile young stone your waste could persist for years.
- **Keep your distance from the pikas' haypiles.** Those stacks of sedge are how they survive winter.
- **Stay off the glacier surface,** partly because it's dangerous, and partly because the ice worms, springtails, and cryoconite communities are there. The pink snow-algae patches are living things too.
- **Consider what you're bringing in.** You may carry seeds, spores, or microbes from other worlds on your boots and clothing. Clean everything thoroughly before you arrive, especially boot treads, trekking-pole tips, and velcro. On sterile new ground, an alien seed or microbe could start its own experiment.

## 12. Things to Bring for Noticing

The description makes clear that the shelf's richness is hidden. "You can stand for an hour and see only a chough, a dropped moth, a single spider on a boulder. You have to kneel and look at the snow to see that it is crawling."

- **A hand lens (10x).** For springtails, glacier fleas, the dark pellets at the bottom of cryoconite holes, the black edges of map lichen, and the tiny white and pink flowers.
- **Binoculars.** For lammergeiers on the ridge, choughs on the updrafts, blue sheep on far slopes, and perhaps a snow leopard at dusk.
- **A red headlamp.** For watching ice worms rise at dusk from the edge of the glacier without disturbing them, and without walking onto it.
- **A foam kneeling pad.** You'll want to get low, and the stone is cold and wet.
- **A notebook and pencil.** Pens freeze.
- **Patience.** Much of what's here reveals itself only if you stay still.

## 13. Preparing Your Mind

This place is quietly hard on the psyche in ways that are worth anticipating.

- **The emptiness.** Vast stone and air, very few visible living things, and silence so deep you hear your own pulse. Some people find it peaceful. Others find it unnerving, especially at night.
- **Altitude affects your judgment.** Thin air makes you slower, more irritable, and worse at decisions, often without your noticing. Make your important rules *before* you arrive: when you'll leave, how close you'll go to the ice face and the lip, and what symptoms mean you're done. Follow them even when you feel fine.
- **The orange glow at night.** Faint and sickly on the underside of the cloud, strongest to the southwest. It may feel beautiful, eerie, or accusatory. Being lit, even here, by the industry of the plains is part of what this place means.
- **The ledger in the ice.** When you look at the glacier's dark upper bands and the cryoconite holes boring into it, you may feel implicated. You come from an industrial world too, or you rely on one. The description calls this "an industrial landscape, as surely as any foundry yard." Arrive ready to sit with that feeling rather than brush it away.
- **The airliner.** Once or twice an hour, a slow roar swells and fades above the cloud. It's the only engine sound here. In the silence it can feel like an intrusion, or strangely companionable.
- **The newness.** Standing on stone that has *never* before been exposed to air is a strange and rare experience. You'll be tempted to leave something to mark it. Don't. The point is that nothing has been built, and you get to leave it that way.

## 14. A Suggested Visit

For an acclimatized, fit, well-equipped traveler:

1. **Late morning, midsummer.** Arrive on bare stone near the older ground, well back from the lip. Check your oxygen saturation and how you feel. Rest. Drink.
2. **Midday.** Walk slowly from the lip toward the ice across the bands of time: sedge and cushion plants, then moss, then lichen, then bare wet stone. Stop well short of the ice face. Kneel often. Watch the sheet water follow the scratches.
3. **Early afternoon.** As the wind rises, watch the windfall arrive: insects dropping onto snow edges, snow finches picking them off. Retreat to camp, away from rockfall, before the cloud closes.
4. **Afternoon in cloud.** Shelter, eat, drink, stay dry. Listen to the drip, the distant rockfall, and the glacier groaning.
5. **Dusk.** From the edge of the glacier, with red light, watch the ice worms rise. Listen for the pikas' whistle. Scan the ridge for a snow leopard.
6. **Night.** Look for the orange stain on the cloud below. Stay warm. Monitor yourself.
7. **Pre-dawn.** In spring or autumn, listen for the geese. Be in position.
8. **Dawn.** Ten minutes of copper light on wet stone, every meltwater line glowing. Don't move. Watch.
9. **Morning.** Once the glaze softens and it's safe to walk, pack out everything, check the ground where you camped, and leave.

Go knowing that next year this spot will look different. The ice will have pulled back again, a new band of stone will lie open to the sky, and the lichen will have crept a millimeter forward to meet it.
