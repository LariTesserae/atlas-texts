# Preparing for the Blown Table

## A Traveler's Briefing

The Blown Table will not kill you with anything dramatic. It will not drop you off a cliff or send a predator after you. It will wear you down slowly: through your ears, your skin temperature, your ankles, and your nerves. Your preparation should match that. You are packing for a long, loud, wet, windy exposure on the roof of a world. You are also visiting a place where a few hundred people have spent centuries tuning the ground itself, and they will not thank you for disturbing it.

What follows is roughly in order of importance.

---

## 1. Your Ears Are the Price of Admission

This is the one thing you cannot get wrong.

The Table is described as deafening in the literal sense. An hour in the open leaves your ears ringing. A week costs you your high frequencies, perhaps for good. You should assume sustained exposure well past the level that causes permanent damage on Earth, with peaks from shriek-holes that are worse.

**What to bring:**

- **Double protection.** Wear high-attenuation foam or custom-molded earplugs, with over-ear muffs on top. Test the combination at home before you go. Muffs must seal against your skull, so no thick hat under the cups and no hair bunched up. You will wear them most of your waking hours and possibly while you sleep.
- **Spares, many spares.** Foam plugs get wet from the constant fog, fill with seed-dust, and fall into moss where you will never find them. Carry dozens in sealed bags spread across your pockets and pack.
- **Muffs that tolerate water.** Condensation will bead on everything. Choose sealed, weatherproof hearing protection. Avoid electronics that fail when damp, or carry backups.
- **Optional: a sound dosimeter.** If your home world has one rugged enough, bring it. It turns "I think I'm fine" into a number. Your subjective sense of loudness will stop being reliable within a day, because the brain adapts to the roar.

**What protection cannot do:** The booming of the great shafts arrives as infrasound, felt "in the belly and the teeth." Earplugs will not stop it. It travels through your chest and skull. On Earth, strong low-frequency sound is associated with nausea, pressure headaches, unease, a sense of being watched, and trouble sleeping. Expect some of this. Anti-nausea medication and ordinary painkillers belong in your kit.

**Set a time limit before you arrive, and keep it.** I would suggest no more than four or five days on the surface for a first visit, and less if you have no dosimeter. Use the shafts as quiet rooms. The description notes the roar passes *overhead* down there. Every hour in a shaft is an hour your ears get to recover. Plan your days around those rest periods.

**Get a hearing test before and after.** It gives you a baseline, and the trip will change it.

## 2. You Will Not Be Able to Speak

Once you haul yourself over the rim, voice is gone. Plan for that seriously.

**With your companions:**

- **Agree on a hand-signal vocabulary before the climb.** At minimum you need: stop, come here, danger, hole/shaft, shelter now, cold, I'm hurt, water, which way, yes, no, repeat, and "the wind is changing." Practice until it is automatic. Practice with gloves on too.
- **Carry a waterproof writing slate** or rite-in-the-rain notebook with a grease pencil. Ordinary paper will turn to pulp in the fog.
- **Rope up or stay in sight.** In fog you can lose someone three meters away, and you cannot call out. Consider a short tether in poor visibility, and a rule that no one leaves sight of the group without a signal acknowledged.
- **Touch signals.** A tap code on a shoulder works when you cannot see each other's hands.

**With the inhabitants:**

The people of the Table speak with their hands and through the stone. You will not learn their language in a few days. You can still show that you are trying, and that matters.

- If there is any way to learn a few of their signs beforehand, from a previous traveler or a descending local at the foot of the escarpment, do it. Greeting, thanks, and "I am a traveler, I will leave" would be worth more than any gear.
- Learn to **put your palms on the stone and pay attention.** Even if you cannot decode the drum-messages, you can learn to notice when one is passing. It is the Table's equivalent of hearing someone speak in the next room.
- Bring a small hand mallet only if you are sure you will not use it by accident. Striking hollow outcrops is how they talk across kilometers. A stranger drumming randomly is not harmless noise. You may be sending nonsense, or worse, something that sounds like a warning.

---

## 3. The Climb and the Air

It is three days up an escarpment thousands of meters high, ending in a narrow crack in quartzite that you climb with your hands. The top is high enough that it "should be frozen," and the air is thin enough to hurt the lungs.

- **Fitness.** Train for multi-day ascents with a loaded pack, and do some scrambling practice. The final crack sounds like easy climbing that you must not fall off. A helmet and a short rope are reasonable.
- **Altitude.** The three-day climb helps you acclimatize, so use it. Go slowly and drink a lot. Know the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, confusion, stumbling, breathlessness at rest. The infrasound will also cause headache and nausea, so be careful not to explain away real altitude illness as "just the noise." The cure for serious altitude illness is descent, so know your route down before you need it. If your home world has acetazolamide or an equivalent, discuss it with a doctor beforehand.
- **Sun.** Thin air and fast, broken cloud can burn you badly between fog banks. Bring sunscreen that holds up when wet, lip balm, and eye protection.

---

## 4. Wind, Fog, and the Cold That Waits

The Table's main surface condition is warm, wet, fast wind. This sounds pleasant. It isn't safe.

**Warm wind can still make you hypothermic.** You will be damp constantly from condensation, and the wind never stops. Wet clothing plus strong wind strips heat from you faster than the air temperature suggests. You may not notice until you start shivering or thinking slowly.

**Clothing:**

- **Wind- and waterproof outer shell, snug-fitting.** Loose, flapping clothing is exhausting in constant gale and will chafe. Everything should cinch down: hood, cuffs, hem.
- **Synthetic or wool base layers** that insulate when wet. No cotton at all.
- **A serious warm layer held in reserve, kept dry in a sealed bag.** This is your silence kit. It is for the lull.
- **Gloves**, including a thin pair that still lets you feel stone through your fingertips.
- **Goggles**, not just sunglasses. The air carries seed, spore, husk, insect wings, and grit, and the tornadic threads in the center are "gray with seed and spore." Wraparound, well-sealed eye protection matters.
- **A respirator or good dust mask**, especially for the plateau's middle and for anywhere near the fermenting seed drifts. Months of airborne spores and rotting organic matter is how people on Earth get lung infections and allergic reactions. If you have allergies or asthma, plan carefully and bring your medication.

**The silence.** Twice a year, roughly, the wind stops. The temperature can drop thirty degrees in an hour, and then the wind returns from an unexpected direction in a front that "flattens everything standing." Your chance of meeting it in a short visit is small, but not zero, and the consequences are serious.

- **Know the warning signs:** The booming stops and only the shriek remains, meaning the updraft is faltering. Listeners unfold their ears and turn in circles. If the listeners *run for the shafts*, you run too.
- **Your response plan:** Get into a shaft or into the lee of a full-circle or spiral wall, as low as possible. Put on your dry warm layer *immediately*, before you feel cold. Stay down until the front has passed. Being in a shaft protects you from both the cold and the returning front.
- **Carry an emergency bivy or thermal bag** as a last resort if you are caught in the open.

**Lesser warnings.** A "rising, sliding whine" means rotors are forming. That turbulence is bad for footing and visibility, and the tornadic threads are in it. A clean low octave means steady wind from one rim, which is the safest condition to move in.

## 5. Where You Sleep: Nothing Above the Knee

The rule is plain: *anything above knee height will be taken, unless it is inside a shaft.*

- **Do not bring a normal tent.** A tent on the open plateau will be destroyed, and if you are inside it, you may go with it.
- **Your options, in order of preference:**
  1. **A shaft**, if you can safely get into one and it is not someone's home. Out of the wind and much quieter. These are the gardens and the dwellings of the Table.
  2. **Inside an old spiral or circular wall**, low to the ground. The shape is designed for exactly your problem: whatever direction the wind swings, part of the enclosure is sheltered.
  3. **In the lee of an outcrop**, accepting that the lee will move as the wind swings around the compass through the day and night.
- **Sleep gear:** A low-profile bivy sack, waterproof and anchored, with an insulated pad. The moss is wet and spongy, and you will lose heat into it. A sleeping bag with synthetic fill, since down fails when damp.
- **Anchor everything.** Tie gear to yourself or to rock. Anything set down unattended on the open surface may be gone in a second.

Expect to sleep badly, at least at first. The noise continues through the night, the infrasound keeps your body on edge, and the wind shifts direction and changes the chord around you. Earplugs and muffs at night, exhaustion, and a shaft if you can manage it are your best chances.

---

## 6. Your Feet: The Ground Is Full of Holes

The plateau looks flat and safe. It is actually a sponge of stone, full of pores and shafts, and much of it is covered by knee-deep moss.

- **Pores** from thumb-width to fist-width are ideal for breaking or spraining an ankle. Wear **stiff, high-ankle, waterproof boots** with good grip on polished rock. The quartzite is smooth-worn and will be wet. Bring trekking poles and use them to probe ahead in the moss.
- **Shafts** are round drops into darkness, rimmed with green. In fog, or in deep moss, the edge may not be obvious. Test the ground in front of you. Never walk quickly across moss you cannot see through.
- **Seed drifts** behind rocks can be a meter deep and rotting at the bottom. They are soft, hot, wet, and probably full of fungal life. Don't wade through them. Don't dig into them for shelter.
- **If you plan to descend into shafts,** you need rope, a harness, and the skills to use them. You also need to know how to get back *up*. Better yet, only enter shafts that the inhabitants use and invite you into.

---

## 7. Water and Food

**Water** is "in the ground's fur." Squeeze hair-moss and it runs clear and cold. There are also the still black pools at the bottoms of sealed shafts.

- It is probably quite clean, since it is fog condensation, but carry a **filter or purification tablets** anyway. The seed-rot and the steady rain of dead insects and occasional dead birds from below are reasons for caution.
- Collecting is easy but slow. A cloth you can wring out, or a folding bottle and a habit of squeezing moss at every rest stop, will keep you supplied.
- Don't strip large patches of moss. It is the plateau's water system and the beetles' home.

**Food.** The Table produces almost nothing of its own, and the people living there already survive on thin margins: seed, moss-water, round brown birds, and the insects that rain onto the rim each morning.

- **Bring all your own food**, calorie-dense and waterproof-packed. Don't compete for the local supply.
- Don't trap the ground birds. There aren't many, and they are someone else's food.
- If you are offered roasted insects, accept graciously if you can. Be aware of shellfish-type allergies, which on Earth can cross-react with insect proteins.
- **Cooking:** a stove that runs in heavy wind, with a windscreen, and you will need a wall or shaft to shelter it. Be careful with flame near dry seed drifts or grass mats. A fire driven by a gale across a plateau would be a catastrophe.

---

## 8. Finding Your Way

The Table is huge, flat, featureless, and often in fog. The wind changes direction all day, so you cannot use it as a steady compass.

- **The middle of the plateau is where the flows from two rims collide:** rotors, standing waves, tornadic threads. Plan routes that avoid the broad center unless you have a very good reason to go there.
- **Iron** in the quartzite ("a bruised rose where iron has bled through the grain") may distort a magnetic compass locally. Check your bearing often against the sun and landmarks.
- **The singing cairns** are the Table's navigation system. A line of them, a day's walk apart, plays a descending sequence of notes, and following the pitch *down* leads to the northern stair. Before you go, find out whether this is the route you will use to leave, and try to learn the sequence from someone who knows it. Your hearing protection will make this harder. You may need to briefly lift a muff near a cairn to identify the note, so do it for seconds, not minutes.
- **Plan your exit before you arrive.** Know where the northern stair is, how long it takes to reach from where you will be, and what you will do if a silence hits while you are en route.
- **Tell someone below** when you intend to come down.

---

## 9. Respecting the Instrument (and Its Players)

This matters as much as your safety. The Table is not empty wilderness. It is a place people have spent centuries shaping, and much of their work is invisible to an outsider.

- **Do not touch the plugs.** The stoppers of turf, stone, and resin fitted into holes are generational work, sometimes centuries of a single family's labor. They are also a warning and communication system. Pulling one out, or stumbling over one and knocking it loose, may change a region's voice in ways that matter to people's survival. If you find one loose, don't try to fix it.
- **Don't plug holes yourself** either, even to make a quiet spot to sleep. You would be altering someone's instrument.
- **Do not move, climb, or take stones from the cairns.** Each stone is placed for pitch. The cairns are how people find their way down in fog.
- **Don't dismantle old walls** for your own windbreak, even the thousand-year-old ones reduced to green ridges. Use them as they are.
- **Treat shaft-homes as homes.** If a shaft has dwellings dug into its walls, it is not a campsite. Wait for an invitation.
- **Leave the listeners alone.** They are the people's early warning of the silence. Don't approach them, startle them, or stand between them and a shaft.
- **Don't drum on the rocks.** You may be heard a kilometer away.
- **Pack out everything.** In a place where every blown-out plug and seed drift is part of a slow cycle, your litter is an intrusion.
- **Bringing gifts:** be careful. Something small, useful, and durable may be welcome: fine strong cord, a good needle, a small knife. Ask whoever sent you, or someone who has been before. Don't bring anything that could reshape their lives in ways you won't be around to see.

## 10. The Mind

This is the part people underestimate.

You will be in a place where you cannot speak, cannot hear, can barely see in fog, and are constantly buffeted. Your body will be feeling infrasound it has no instinct for. Days on the plateau are long, empty, and uniform. You may walk all day and see nothing alive.

Expect:
- **Isolation**, even with companions. Conversation shrinks to hand signs and scribbles. Some people find this peaceful. Many find it frightening.
- **Irritability and exhaustion** from sensory overload and poor sleep.
- **Unease without a source.** Infrasound can produce a sense of dread. Know this ahead of time so you don't mistake it for intuition that something is wrong, or worse, ignore real warnings as "just the noise."
- **Distorted perception.** The curve of the horizon seems faintly visible, and the grasses flip in waves that make the ground look like it's flowing. Motion-sensitive people may feel seasick.
- **The strange relief of the shafts.** Coming down into a still jungle in a jar, with the roar passing overhead, may be one of the most profound experiences of your life. Plan for it. You may not want to climb out.

Practical countermeasures: set a simple daily routine. Check in with companions at fixed times using signs. Take quiet breaks in shafts. Keep a waterproof journal. Agree in advance that anyone can call an early exit for any reason, without argument.

---

## 11. Health Preparations Before You Go

- **Lowland vaccinations and prophylaxis.** The climb begins in hot, humid forests. Prepare as you would for tropical lowlands: insect repellent, treatment for insect-borne disease if relevant, and so on.
- **Hearing test**, before and after.
- **Respiratory check**, if you have any lung issues. The spore load here is not trivial.
- **First aid kit** emphasizing ankle injuries (tape, splint), blisters, chafing from wind-whipped wet clothing, eye irrigation for grit, altitude and nausea medication, antihistamines, and anything for allergic reactions.
- **Skin.** Constant damp plus wind leads to chafing and trench-foot-like problems. Change into dry socks every night, air your feet in shelter, and bring balm.

---

## 12. Packing Summary

**Essential:**
- Earplugs (many) + sealed muffs
- Windproof/waterproof shell, cinchable
- Wool or synthetic layers, including a dry emergency warm layer in a sealed bag
- Sealed goggles, dust mask or respirator
- Stiff waterproof boots, trekking poles
- Low bivy, insulated pad, synthetic sleeping bag, emergency thermal bag
- Water filter and wringable cloth
- Calorie-dense food, wind-capable stove and windscreen
- Waterproof slate/notebook and grease pencils
- Pre-learned hand signals
- Rope, harness, helmet (for the crack and any shaft descent)
- First aid kit as above
- Map/notes, compass (checked against sun), known exit route

**Useful:**
- Sound dosimeter
- Short tether line for fog
- Gloves thin enough to feel the stone
- Small, thoughtful gifts
- Anything that helps you learn the chord of the plateau: notes from past visitors, recordings, a patient teacher

**Leave behind:**
- Tents, tall gear, anything that catches wind
- Cotton
- Your voice
- Any intention to build, dig, drum, or retune

## What It Will Be Like

Here is my intuition about the actual experience.

The first thing will be the climb and the change in the birds. Then the ground starts to hum, and the hum becomes a physical thing you feel in your hands on the rock. When you come over the rim, it will be overwhelming, a wall of sound and wind so total that your first instinct will be to drop flat. That instinct is correct. Get low, get your muffs on properly, and let your body adjust.

After a few hours the chaos will start to separate into parts: the booms, the flute-moans, the shrieks, the hiss of the grass. After a day or two you will begin to notice the chord *changing*. That is the moment the place starts to make sense. You will not become fluent, but you will feel the edges of a language the whole plateau is speaking.

You will be damp all the time. It will be warm, and you will still be cold at night. You will be very alone. You will look out from a rounded outcrop and see whole fields of grass turn silver, then dark, then silver, in waves kilometers long, and you will not have anyone to say it to aloud.

You will find the bodies of butterflies in the grass. You will climb down into a shaft and hear, for the first time in days, your own breathing. You may see a listener, or you may not. If you are lucky, on some evening, you will feel through the soles of your boots someone far away drumming *wind west, holding, all well*, and you will understand that people have lived here, quietly and with great skill, longer than you can imagine.

Then you will follow the falling notes of the cairns north, climb down out of the noise, and in the forest below, for the first time in a week, you will hear a bird sing. You may cry. That's fine.

Go carefully. Leave the instrument as you found it. And protect your ears. You will want them afterward, to remember what the Table sounded like.
