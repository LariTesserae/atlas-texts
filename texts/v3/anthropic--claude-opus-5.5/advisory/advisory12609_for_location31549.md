# Preparing for the Deep Fan

## The one fact that shapes everything else

You cannot stand on this floor. No ordinary human body can.

At four thousand meters the water presses with roughly four hundred atmospheres, close to six thousand pounds on every square inch. Your lungs would collapse, the air spaces in your sinuses and middle ears would implode, and the gases in your blood would behave in ways that end conversations. Even the best armored diving suits are rated for a few hundred meters. The description mentions a hand pressing between nodules, and the hand you send will almost certainly be made of titanium and hydraulics.

So preparation starts with accepting that you will visit this place *through* something: a pressure hull, a manipulator arm, a sonar screen, a hydrophone. The first skill to build is being present through instruments. People who expect raw sensory contact come back disappointed. People who have trained themselves to feel through a machine often come back changed.

That said, you can get close. A thumb's width of titanium between you and the broth is close. You will hear the hiss through the hull. You will feel the thud in your seat. That is worth preparing for properly.

## Part One: Getting to the water

### Do not open a crossing at depth

This matters more than anything else in this guide. If your method of travel opens any kind of aperture, threshold, or transfer point, it must open at the surface or into a sealed vehicle already at pressure equilibrium. A gateway to the seafloor that connects to a room at one atmosphere becomes a four-hundred-atmosphere water jet. It will cut through everything on your side, including you. Arrive at the sea surface, or arrive already inside your pressure sphere and already at depth, and nothing in between.

### Assume you are alone up top

The Deep Fan sits on an abyssal plain, which means hundreds of kilometers from any coast. The net-sinkers suggest humans once fished the shelf in this world, but that tells you nothing about whether anyone is there now, or whether they have ships, radios, or rescue services. Plan as if no one will come.

That means one of two setups:

- **A support vessel you bring or charter.** It needs a launch-and-recovery system, a crew who has done this before, an acoustic communications suite, and enough fuel and supplies to stay on station for the full dive plus several days of contingency.
- **A fully autonomous vehicle.** This would be a sub that can launch itself, dive, surface, and keep you alive while floating for days until your extraction window opens. It is harder and lonelier, but possible.

Check the surface weather before you commit. The benthic storms are partly born from eddies at the surface, and a rough sea state makes recovery the most dangerous part of the whole trip. Many deep-sea incidents happen at the top, not the bottom.

### Check the physics

You are a multiverse traveler, so verify that this world's water is water, that its gravity matches your expectations, and that pressure increases with depth at the usual rate. The description reads as ordinary Earth-like deep ocean. Confirm it with instruments anyway before you trust your life to your hull's rating.

## Part Two: The vessel

Treat this as a list of requirements, not suggestions.

**1. A full-ocean-depth or 6,000-meter-rated crewed submersible.** A 4,000-meter rating is not enough margin. Your hull should be a proven design, typically a titanium sphere, certified by people who were not trying to sell it to you. Never trust an experimental hull at this depth.

**2. Days of life support, not hours.** Plan for a dive of six to twelve hours and carry life support for at least ninety-six. The reason is simple: *the floor buries things*. The description says a patch of bare stone can lie under a hand's depth of ooze by morning, and a turbidity current can deposit much more than that in minutes. If you are pinned, stuck, or entangled, you will be waiting.

**3. Redundant, independent ascent systems.** Drop weights, releasable skids, jettisonable manipulator arms, and ideally a way to shed the entire lower frame. Each system should work electrically, mechanically, and on a timer that triggers even if you are unconscious. Test that each can release under a load of mud. A drop weight buried in clay may not drop.

**4. A shape that sheds sediment.** Choose a smooth hull, minimal external clutter, shielded thrusters, and nothing that can scoop silt. Every cavity will fill with fine clay. Anything that can snag, whether a cable, a sample basket, or a poorly stowed arm, is a liability in a current that moves nodules.

**5. Thrusters that can move you against a current coming from anywhere.** The basin has no lee. Currents collide and double back. Your vehicle needs thrust in all directions, and enough power to hold position without running its batteries down in an hour.

**6. No tether, or a tether you can cut instantly.** A cable to the surface sounds like a lifeline. In a benthic storm it becomes a sail and an anchor at the same time. If you use one, fit a guillotine you can fire from inside.

## Part Three: Sensing a place with no sight

### Lights will blind you

Your instinct will be to turn on floodlights. Resist it. In water this thick, a bright light shows you the silt itself: a glowing white wall centimeters from your viewport, like driving in a blizzard with high beams on. Worse, the light is visible to every animal nearby and may trigger the alarm flashes of whatever drifts past.

Bring instead:

- **Low-intensity red light**, placed well away from your camera lenses to cut down on backscatter. Use it only for close-up work at the viewport or the manipulator tip.
- **Nothing at all, most of the time.** Train yourself to sit in true darkness. The rare blue smears of bioluminescence are one of the few things here you can see with your own eyes. You will only see them if you are not drowning them out.

### Sound is your eyesight

High-frequency sonar, the kind that draws crisp pictures, will be badly scattered and absorbed by the suspended clay. Carry a range of frequencies:

- **Low-frequency sonar** to find the gentle swells and see the shape of the ground over longer distances.
- **Short-range imaging sonar** for the nodule field directly around you, accepting that it will be grainy.
- **Sub-bottom profiling** if you want to understand what lies beneath the surface skin. The foundations here are the old, unchanging part of the place.
- **Doppler current profilers**, which will tell you more about the basin's mood than anything else. You can watch a storm build in the numbers before you feel it.

### Touch through the arm

If you want to press a manipulator between two nodules and find a third beneath, use a force-feedback system. Some arms let the operator feel resistance in a hand controller. It is not the same as your skin, but you will feel the botryoidal texture as vibration and the soft give of the grey clay as a sudden lack of resistance. Practice with the controller on land beforehand, blindfolded, on sand, gravel, and clay, until you can tell them apart by feel.

### Hydrophones, always on

Carry a sensitive hydrophone array and listen. The hiss is the resting state. The low grinding means a storm has reached the nodules. The deep distant thud, carried through the rock, matters most of all.

## Part Four: The storms, and the thud

### Understand the two kinds

**Benthic storms from eddies** are the frantic surface skin described in the entry. Currents stream sideways, visibility collapses to nothing, ripples form and re-form, and nodules rock in their sockets. They are disorienting and exhausting. They are rarely lethal to a sound vehicle, but they will push you around, cover you in silt, and make station-keeping a battle.

**Turbidity currents** are the killers. A slope fails up the rise and a slurry comes down many meters thick, kilometers wide, and faster than a river. Turbidity currents in our own world have snapped undersea cables one after another across hundreds of kilometers. By the time a flow reaches a distant fan like this one it will have slowed, but it can still carry you, roll you, and bury you.

### The thud is your clock

This is the most important operating rule. The thud comes through the rock, and sound travels through rock at kilometers per second. The mud travels at meters per second. **When you hear the thud, you have time, but not much, and you do not know exactly how much.** It could be tens of minutes or it could be hours, depending on how far up-slope the failure happened.

Set a protocol before you dive and follow it without discussion:

1. **Thud detected:** stop whatever you are doing. Retract the manipulators. Secure all samples and loose gear.
2. **Rise.** Turbidity currents hug the bottom. Climbing well above the floor, a hundred meters or more if you can, generally lets the flow pass beneath you. Do not try to outrun it horizontally, since you don't know where it is coming from or how wide it is.
3. **Wait above it.** Watch your current profiler and downward sonar. You may see the flow's top surface pass below you as a sudden haze on the returns.
4. **Descend again only when the profiler says the bottom has calmed.** The floor you return to will not look like the floor you left. You will need to re-map.

The safest way to spend time here is hovering a few meters above the floor rather than resting on it, so that rising is a matter of seconds. Landing on the nodules is tempting because it is quieter, cheaper on the batteries, and lets you feel the grinding. Do it only in a confirmed lull, with a pre-set timer that brings you back up if you lose track.

### The burial protocol

If the worst happens and you are buried or pinned:

- Do not panic-thrust. Thrusters in clay dig you deeper and overheat.
- Shed weight systematically: drop weights first, then external equipment, then the skid frame.
- Once free of the load, positive buoyancy works slowly. Give it time. Clay grips, then lets go all at once.
- If nothing works, you are in a sealed sphere with days of air. Lower your metabolism: stay warm, stay still, breathe slowly, and ration your lights and heat. Wait for the next storm. The same currents that buried you may scour you out. The plain rearranges everything, and eventually it rearranges you.

It is grim to plan for, but have an honest conversation with yourself about it before you go.

## Part Five: Your body in the sphere

**Cold.** The water outside is two degrees above freezing, all the time, everywhere. Your titanium hull will conduct that cold inward. Over hours the cabin will drop toward single digits Celsius. Condensation will bead on every surface and drip on you. Wear layers of wool or synthetic thermal fabric, never cotton, which stays wet and cold. Bring a hat, thick socks, and insulated gloves thin enough to work controls in, with a spare dry set sealed in a bag. If the vehicle has a heater, don't rely on it.

**Air.** Your oxygen supply and CO₂ scrubbers are your real clock. Know how to read your CO₂ monitor and recognize your own early symptoms of CO₂ buildup: headache, flushed face, shortness of breath, irritability. Practice swapping scrubber cartridges in the dark.

**Descent and ascent time.** Expect two to four hours each way, spent in darkness and cold, sitting nearly still in a space about the size of a large wardrobe. The ascent is often worse. You are tired, it is colder, and you have nothing left to look at.

**Food and water.** Bring calorie-dense, low-residue food that doesn't crumble. Crumbs float into electronics and into your mouth when you least want them. Carry plenty of water. Cold suppresses thirst, and dehydration dulls judgment.

**Waste.** Bring a sealed urine bottle and be at peace with it. Test it on land.

**Medical.** Get cleared first. People with untreated heart conditions, uncontrolled claustrophobia, or panic disorders should not make this trip. Carry motion-sickness medication for the surface recovery, since bobbing in a sealed sphere in open ocean is miserable.

## Part Six: Your mind

This may be harder than the physical preparation.

**Sensory deprivation.** Hours of true darkness, a hiss that never stops, and a cold that never lifts. Some people find it meditative. Others find their brains start inventing things: shapes in the dark, voices in the hiss. Practice beforehand in a sensory-deprivation tank, or just sit in a sealed dark room for several hours listening to recorded water noise. Learn what your mind does when it is starved.

**Pressure, psychologically.** The entry says it plainly: *to be here is to be pressed.* You will know, constantly, that the world outside your sphere is pushing on it with the weight of four kilometers of ocean. Some travelers feel this as dread. Others feel it as an embrace. Decide ahead of time which frame you will reach for.

**Scale of time.** The nodules are older than the present shape of the continents. The burrows in the stone were made by animals from before flowers. Contact with deep time can be disorienting. Some visitors feel very small, others strangely comforted. Expect a long silence in yourself afterward and don't rush to fill it.

**Patience.** Animals here are rare, and each one is an event. You may spend six hours and see one sea cucumber. That is not failure. That is the place. If you arrive needing spectacle, you will spend the whole dive frustrated. Arrive expecting to wait.

## Part Seven: How to behave

The rules of this place are strict and it enforces them: *everything that falls in, stays.* That should shape your conduct.

**Drop nothing you don't have to.** Every tool, sample bag, zip-tie, or fragment of hull coating you lose here will stay essentially forever. It will be churned by storms, and over millions of years it may become the nucleus of a nodule. Tether every tool. Inventory the vehicle before and after. Your drop weights are an unavoidable exception, since most submersibles must shed ballast to rise. If you have a choice, use plain iron or stone ballast instead of anything toxic. The plain will simply absorb it. Accept that, but keep the list short.

**Minimize your plume.** Your thrusters will raise silt. In a place where filter-feeders survive on what the current carries, a thick plume of your making can smother glass sponges and clog crinoid arms during the lulls that are their only chance to feed. Move slowly, stay high when transiting, and point your thrust away from anything living. Land only in bare patches, never on the pale frost-like mats in the hollows. They are always beginning, and don't be the thing that ends them.

**Do not touch the standing sponges or crinoids.** They are anchored by threads of silica to single nodules and are far more fragile than they look. Most of their kind already lie on the floor as dropped lace. Don't add to them.

**Leave the octopus alone.** It lives somewhere on the western swell and keeps rebuilding its ring of stones after every storm. It is the only thing in the basin doing anything like building. Do not move its stones. Do not shine lights into its hollow. Do not park near it. If you are lucky enough to find it, watch from a distance on sonar, and leave.

**Do not take the nodules.** People in many worlds want these for their metals. You will be holding something that took ten million years to reach the size of your fist. Take pictures. Take measurements. Leave it in its socket.

**Think hard before you take a made stone.** The net-sinkers, the flints, and the dished grinding stone are real human objects. They were carried down from the shelf thousands of years ago and are now slowly being claimed by the plain, with the first millimeter of manganese already forming on the oldest. Removing one would pull it out of the most patient process in its world. You might decide it belongs with the people who descend from its makers, if any exist. You might decide it belongs here. Make that decision on land, not in the moment with the arm already reaching. Most travelers who have thought about it leave them.

## Part Eight: The honest alternative

For many travelers, the best preparation is deciding not to go in person. Send a robotic proxy: a well-built, tethered or autonomous vehicle, while you sit in a warm control room at the surface with force-feedback controls, a big sonar display, and live hydrophone audio in good headphones.

You lose the physical sense of pressure around you, the cold, and the knowledge that the hull holding back the ocean is also holding *you*. You gain unlimited time, no risk of burial, and the ability to wait out a dozen storms and catch the rare lulls when the crinoids open and the mats spread.

There is no shame in this. Nearly everyone who has touched the deep sea has done it this way.

## Part Nine: What it will actually be like

You leave the surface in grey daylight. Within minutes the blue deepens to black. After about an hour you notice the temperature dropping and pull on your gloves. At two hours you are in water that has never seen the sun, and the only light is the glow of your instruments, which you dim.

Near the bottom the sonar begins to return a surface. It is flat, flatter than you expected. The profiler shows the current leaning one way, then another. You slow, and at some point you realize you are hearing something: a soft, continuous hiss through the hull, like sand poured slowly through fingers, never stopping. After twenty minutes you stop hearing it as sound. It is just what the world is.

You settle a few meters up and wait. The water around your viewport, in the faint red of your one working light, looks like weak tea with dust in it, and it is moving. Grains drift past at an angle, change direction, and drift back. Nothing is still.

Maybe you extend the arm. Through the controller you feel it slow as it pushes into the broth, then touch something hard and knobbly, then a gap, then another hard lump, then yielding softness below. It is cobbles in an old street, with clay underneath.
