# Preparing for the Hammer Moor

## The Short Version

You are going to a cold, wet, high-altitude bog that moves under your feet, vents scalding steam and probably toxic gas, throws electrical discharges, and cannot be trusted about distance, direction, or which century it is. None of it is aimed at you. That is the good news and the bad news. The moor will not try to hurt you, and it will not make any allowance for you either.

The people who come back from places like this are rarely the strongest or the best equipped. They are the most patient. They move slowly, they read the ground, they pay attention to animals, and they treat their own senses as one source of information among several rather than as the truth.

Plan for the cold and the wet first, since those are what will most likely kill you. Plan for the ground second, the gases third, the electricity fourth. Plan for the strangeness last. The strangeness is the part you will remember, but it is the least likely to be what gets you.

## Part One: The Body You Bring

### Fitness

Walking on a floating moss mat is nothing like walking on a trail. Every step sinks, sucks, and rebounds. Your stabilizer muscles (ankles, knees, hips, core) work constantly to correct for ground that shifts and occasionally rises under you at walking speed. Expect to cover one or two kilometers an hour on a good day, and much less once you add fog, swells, and detours.

Before you go:

- **Train on soft, uneven ground.** Sand dunes, deep snow, real bogs if you can find them. Train with a loaded pack.
- **Build leg and core endurance** rather than peak strength. Step-ups, lunges, long days on the feet.
- **Train balance.** Wobble boards, standing on one foot with your eyes closed, walking on a slackline if you can bear it. The swells are knee-high and come often. Being lifted and set down many times an hour is tiring in a way you won't expect.
- **Train for climbing.** This sounds absurd on a flat plateau, but the ghost mountains impose real uphill effort on flat ground: pounding heart, burning calves, popping ears. You may end up doing the physiological work of a hill ascent without warning and without any visual cue.

### Altitude

The plateau is high enough that the air feels thin. Nothing in the description suggests extreme altitude, but assume at least the range where altitude sickness becomes possible.

- If you can, acclimatize somewhere comparably high before you cross over.
- Learn the symptoms of acute mountain sickness: headache, nausea, poor sleep, unusual breathlessness at rest. Learn the dangerous forms too, HAPE (fluid in the lungs) and HACE (swelling of the brain). The remedy for those is descent. Here, "descent" may not be available in any simple sense. Your exit point may be your only real way down.
- Talk to a physician about acetazolamide.
- Be aware that entering a ghost mountain's shadow may add effective altitude on top of the real altitude. Ears popping on flat ground means the air around you has changed. Treat it as a real change.

### Who Should Not Go

- **Anyone with a pacemaker, implanted defibrillator, insulin pump, or other implanted electronics.** The charged tarns produce spark discharges, ambient fields strong enough to lift your hair, and a mains-frequency hum you can feel in your teeth. Do not bet your heart rhythm on any of that.
- **Anyone with significant heart or lung disease.** The ghost-mountain exertion effect arrives unannounced.
- **Anyone with Raynaud's or poor circulation in the extremities.** The cold here is the worst kind for hands and feet: wet, persistent, a few degrees above freezing.
- **Anyone who cannot swim or who panics in water.** You will probably not have to swim. You may have to haul yourself out of a hole in the mat, calmly, with cold water up to your chest.

## Part Two: The Cold and the Wet

This is the core of your preparation. Everything else is secondary.

"A few degrees above freezing, constantly wet, constantly windy" is the textbook recipe for hypothermia. It is more dangerous than hard frozen cold. Water conducts heat away from the body about twenty-five times faster than air, and nothing on the moor is dry. Frozen places at least give you dry snow. This place gives you sleet, spray from swells, jets of water from every footstep, rain curtains, and fog that soaks everything it touches.

### Accept That You Will Be Wet

You cannot stay dry here, so don't try. Aim to stay **warm while wet** and to have **something dry held back** for sleeping.

- **No cotton anywhere.** Not your underwear, not your socks, not a bandana. Wet cotton kills.
- **Base layers:** merino wool or synthetic. Wool stays warmer when wet and smells less over many days.
- **Mid layers:** fleece or synthetic insulation. Down clumps and loses its loft when wet. Leave down at home unless it is sealed in a waterproof bag and kept strictly for the sleeping bag.
- **Outer shell:** a proper waterproof, breathable hard shell jacket and trousers. Get a hood that fits over a warm hat and cinches down, and long sleeves with adjustable cuffs. You will be wearing it almost all the time.
- **Consider a fishing- or sailing-style layer system** instead of a mountaineering one. Wet, windy, cold, and relentless is the North Atlantic, and the people who work there have solved this problem better than hikers have.

### Feet

Your feet will be your greatest ongoing concern, because the ground is water.

**Immersion foot (trench foot)** is a real risk. It sets in after hours to days of cold, wet feet. The skin goes white and wrinkled, then numb, then painful and swollen. In bad cases tissue dies. It crippled soldiers in the First World War in conditions much like this moor.

- **Footwear:** there are two schools of thought, and you should pick one knowingly.
  - *Tall rubber boots* (knee-high or higher, insulated, with good tread). These keep out surface water well until you step in over the top, and then they hold the water in. Good for short trips.
  - *Non-waterproof, fast-draining boots with neoprene or waterproof socks and gaiters.* These admit water but let it out, and the neoprene keeps the water next to your skin warm. Better for long trips.
- **Bog shoes.** Seriously consider these. In Estonia and elsewhere, people have long crossed bogs on wide, flat frames strapped to their boots, much like snowshoes. They spread your weight and keep you from punching through thin mat. Modern snowshoes with a wide deck can work. Your pace will drop even further, but so will your risk of breaking through.
- **Many pairs of socks,** each kept in its own waterproof bag.
- **Daily foot routine, without exception:** once a day and every night, take your boots off, dry your feet, inspect them, massage them, apply foot powder or a barrier balm, and put on dry socks for sleeping. Keep one pair of sleep socks that never touches the ground.

### Hands

The cold "works into the joints of the hands," and your hands are what you probe with, grab with, and use to pull yourself out.

- Carry several pairs of gloves: thin liners, insulated waterproof gloves, and waterproof overmitts for the worst weather.
- Consider neoprene gloves, the kind fishermen or kayakers use. They are warm when wet.
- Rotate gloves and keep a dry pair against your body.
- Chemical hand warmers are worth their weight.

### Recognizing Hypothermia

Learn the stages. The dangerous part is that hypothermia impairs judgment early, and this place is already confusing your judgment through the fog, the distance distortions, and the ghost terrain.

- **Watch for the "umbles":** stumbling, mumbling, fumbling, grumbling.
- **Shivering is good.** It means your body is fighting. If a cold, wet person stops shivering without warming up, that is an emergency.
- **Travel with at least one partner and watch each other.** Agree beforehand that either of you can call a halt for warming without argument.

### Warmth Management

- **Eat constantly.** Calories are heat. Fatty, dense foods are best. Graze through the day instead of sitting down to meals, since stopping chills you fast.
- **Hot drinks** are worth the fuel.
- **Stop before you get cold, not after.** Put on insulation when you stop. Take a layer off before you start moving hard, so you don't soak your clothes in sweat from the inside.
- **Carry an emergency shelter.** A group bothy bag, which is a windproof fabric shell that several people sit inside together, is ideal for this landscape. There is often nothing to hide behind except a granite knuckle miles away.

## Part Three: The Ground

### Understanding the Mat

The mat is several meters thick and floats over dark jelly that sits over rock. Most of the time it will hold you, the way thick lake ice holds a person. But it varies. It is thinner in some places, torn and healed in others. It is soft over channels where water or hot fluid runs through it, and liquefied where bog-bursts have recently poured out.

The deep layer, "dark jelly that was once plant and is now closer to oil," is the thing you do not want to be in. If you break through the living mat into that, getting out will be very hard.

### The Bog Pole

Carry a long pole, two to three meters, made of **wood or fiberglass. Not aluminum or carbon fiber.** The electrical reasons are covered below.

The pole does three jobs:

1. **Probing.** Push it into the moss ahead of you. If it sinks too easily, go around.
2. **Spanning.** Carry it horizontally in dubious ground. If you break through, the pole catches across the edges of the hole and keeps you from going under.
3. **Rescue.** It gives a partner something to hold out to you.

### Roping Up

In poor visibility and uncertain ground, walk roped to your partner at a distance of ten to fifteen meters, much as climbers rope up on a glacier with hidden crevasses. If one of you goes through, the other lies flat to spread their weight and holds.

This also solves part of the fog problem. With the cloud down around your knees, a rope is how you know where your partner is.

### Reading the Ground

Learn these signs and treat them as warnings:

- **Bright red, rank, crowded moss that steams faintly:** a thermal zone. Scalding water runs close underneath. Do not walk on it. The ground in thermal zones elsewhere collapses into boiling pools, and there is no reason to think it is kinder here.
- **Brilliant green, very flat, very smooth patches:** possibly the thin new moss over a recent bog-burst, or the skin over open water. On real bogs these are the most dangerous spots. On English moors they are called "featherbeds."
- **Fresh black scars:** recent bursts. Their surfaces may be setting while the inside is still liquid.
- **Grazer paths:** see below. This is good news.

### Riding the Swells

A knee-high hump of ground will rise ahead of you, travel toward you, lift you, and set you down, while water jets out of the moss all around your feet.

- **Knees soft, never locked.** Ride it the way you ride a boat's deck or a moving train.
- **Feet about shoulder-width apart,** ideally angled so the swell doesn't hit you square in the direction of travel.
- **Don't try to walk through a swell.** Stop, let it pass, then continue.
- **Use your pole as a third point of contact** when a swell comes.
- **Watch for interference zones.** Where two rhythms meet, "the ground bucks and throws up a spout of peat-water." These are the places where you're most likely to be thrown down, and where the mat is most stressed and most likely to split. Learn to hear the rhythms coming from different directions and work out where they cross. Stay out of those crossings.

Your intuition should tell you something here. The moor is a pump, and a pump has pressure points. The places where waves collide are where pressure spikes, and those are where bog-bursts are born. Keep your camp and your route away from them.

### Bog-Bursts

The sign is "a sound like canvas tearing." When you hear it:

- Find the sound. Then **move sideways,** perpendicular to the likely direction of flow, the way you'd get out of the path of an avalanche or a lava flow. Liquid peat will flow down whatever gradient exists. On a plateau this flat the gradient may be too slight to see, so watch for water movement on the surface as a clue.
- **Do not cross an active flow** under any circumstances. It is dense, it is warm or hot, it steams, and it will not let go of you.
- **Do not cross a fresh scar** until it has fully greened and your pole tells you it is firm. Given that "within days the scar is moss again," you'll often be looking at a thin living skin over something still wet underneath.

Real peat slides on Earth have buried roads and killed people. Take them seriously.

### Tarns

Tarns may drain in an hour, and new ones may fill where there was nothing.

- **Never camp beside a tarn.** Draining creates suction and collapse. Filling floods your tent.
- **Never cross a drained tarn bed.** The drain channel is somewhere below you and the refill may come back.
- **The shoreline is not stable.**

### The Granite Knuckles

These are your best friends. They are **the only solid ground on the moor**: house-sized domes of granite, the last stumps of the old summits.

- They are **landmarks,** perhaps the only reliable ones, although see the section on navigation below.
- They are **camp sites.** Here, and nowhere else, the ground won't split, flood, or swell under you in the night.
- Their **lee** gives shelter from the wind.
- They are **anchors** for tents, ropes, and yourself.

Some cautions. Lichen-upholstered granite is slippery when wet, which is always, so move carefully. The lee sides are exactly where eddies spin sedge into cable, so you may be sheltering in a rope works. And if the ground near a knuckle is thin because rock is close under the mat, the mat could behave differently near the edges.

Plan your route as a string of knuckles, like islands on a sea crossing, and do not count on reaching the next one on schedule.

## Part Four: The Air

### Gas

The description doesn't say this outright, but your intuition should raise it at once: **a vast mass of rotting organic matter, dense enough to run hot, is a gas factory.**

Anaerobic decomposition of peat produces **methane, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide.** Around the steam vents there may well be much more of all three.

- **Carbon dioxide** is heavier than air. In still conditions it collects in hollows, even shallow ones. You won't smell it. It makes you drowsy, then unconscious, then dead. On this moor the wind usually stirs things, but the lull between storms, or the pocket behind a knuckle, could be a trap.
- **Hydrogen sulfide** smells of rotten eggs at low concentrations. At higher concentrations it **deadens your sense of smell**, so the smell disappears just as the danger becomes lethal. Steam vents are classic sources.
- **Methane** is flammable. Now consider this: **there are sparks.** Blue-white discharges walk across the tarns. Somewhere on this moor, methane and spark are meeting. Be ready for flash fires, or at the very least brief flames. Real bogs produce will-o'-the-wisps. This one may produce something more dramatic.

What to bring:

- **A portable multi-gas detector,** the kind confined-space workers and miners wear, reading O₂, CO₂ or CO, H₂S, and flammable gas (LEL). Clip it at chest height and carry a second one to hold low. It is one of the most important pieces of equipment you will carry.
- **Never sleep in a hollow.**
- **Never approach steam vents closely,** for both the thermal and the gas hazard.
- **Keep flames well away from any charged tarn.** Don't light a stove near one.
- If you suddenly feel very sleepy, headachy, or confused while sitting in a sheltered spot, **get up and move into moving air first.** Wonder why afterward.

### Ozone

You will smell ozone near the charged tarns. That clean, sharp electric smell is harmless in passing, but ozone irritates the lungs with sustained exposure. Don't linger.

## Part Five: The Current

### What You Are Dealing With

The charged tarns produce spark discharges across the water, ambient fields that lift your hair and make the sedge heads stand stiff and glow, and on bad nights a plateau-wide hum.

**Hair standing on end** is the same sign mountaineers get just before a lightning strike. It means the field around you is strong enough that you may become part of the discharge path.

### Precautions

- **Stay away from charged-tarn margins.** Learn to spot them: sedge heads all turned toward one tarn, cotton standing stiff and glowing at the tips, a crackle in the air, ozone. The sedge is a warning sign. Read it.
