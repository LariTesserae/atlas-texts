# Preparing for the Hush at Chalkmouth

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Chalkmouth is not dangerous the way a volcano or a war is dangerous. Nothing here wants to hurt you. The people are gentle, the animals are unafraid, and the air is clean. Most visitors who get into trouble don't fall, freeze, or offend anyone. They simply don't come back up, and they don't mind.

So prepare on two levels. The first is ordinary: the cold, the damp, the dazzle, the silence, the climbing. The second is subtler. You are about to enter a place that dissolves the wish to leave, and you need to arrange your own retrieval *before you go in*, while you still care about it.

---

## The Body: Cold and Wet

**Expect deep, constant, stone cold.** It never bites and never lets go. Picture a cave that has stayed at the same few degrees above freezing for a thousand years. Breath fogs, and fingers ache after an hour. You will stop noticing it after a while, and that is exactly when it starts to cost you. Low-grade chilling that you have stopped registering is how people get slow, clumsy, and foolish.

**Dress in wool, all the way down.** Everything in the pit is damp. Condensation beads on every surface and will bead on you. Cotton will soak and stay soaked, and synthetics will feel clammy and wrong. Wool stays warm when wet, and it is also quiet, which matters more than you'd think. A good layering plan:

- Fine wool against the skin.
- A thick felted or knitted middle layer.
- An outer layer of waxed or tightly woven wool. Avoid crinkly technical fabrics. They are loud in your own ears and alien in a place built of chalk, flint, hazel, and fleece.
- A close wool cap, and fingerless mitts with a pair of full mitts over them. You will want your fingertips free, because you'll be talking with your hands.

**Your feet are the real concern.** In the deeper throughs you sink ankle-deep in cold, wet moss, over and over, for as long as you stay. Trench foot and chilblains are real risks. Bring:

- Several pairs of thick wool socks, and change them often. Dry the damp ones inside your clothes against your body, since there is little spare heat down there.
- Soft, supple boots with grippy soles. Felted boots over a thin rubber sole, or well-greased leather moccasin-style boots, would suit. Hard heels are wrong on three counts: they crush moss, they slip on the ladders, and they announce you.
- A small tin of foot balm or tallow, rubbed in every night.

Check your feet every time you rest. Numbness that you have stopped noticing is the warning sign.

**Food.** The town has granaries, and you may be fed, but don't count on it. Bring dense, quiet, warming food that can be eaten completely: oatcakes, hard cheese, dried fruit, nuts, fat. You'll burn a lot of energy just staying warm. Avoid wrappers. You'll see why below.

**Water.** The only obvious source is the round basin in the oldest room, the one that is always exactly as full as yesterday. The blind shrimp live there, and the people regard them as the eldest residents. **Do not drink from it, touch it, or dip anything into it unless someone clearly invites you to.** Carry your own water, a day or two's worth, in a skin or wooden flask. Metal is rare here, and a steel bottle will feel like bringing a trumpet to a library.

## The Eyes: Shadowless Light

This is the preparation people most often overlook.

**The dazzle.** Light comes from everywhere at once: white sky, white chalk, whitewash, and thousands of beads of water, each holding a point of brightness. Visitors describe a constant mild dazzlement. Expect eye strain and headaches in the first day.

- Bring **pale grey tinted glasses**, not dark ones. You want to soften the glare without losing detail. A hat brim won't help, because the light doesn't come from above. It comes from all sides.
- Rest your eyes often. Close them. The moss has a greenness, lit from within, that the brain finds tiring in a way it can't name.

**Shadows judge depth, and there are none here.** In ordinary life, much of your sense of how far away something is, and how deep a step or drop goes, comes from shadow. In Chalkmouth a ladder-shaft may look like a darker patch of floor. A step may look flat. The edge of a bridge may vanish into the bright air beneath it. This place is forty feet deep and built in stacked tiers, with ladders worn smooth as horn.

- Before you go, practise moving carefully in flat, diffuse light: fog, snow-glare, a white room. Train yourself to **feel for edges with your feet before shifting weight**.
- Consider a light walking staff, hazel or ash, for probing. The locals will understand it.
- On ladders, keep three points of contact, always. Test every rung. Smooth wood plus condensation makes for a slippery combination.

**Sleep.** It is always the same bright hour of the same late-winter morning. Night may not properly reach the bottom. Bring an eye mask, dark wool or linen. Without one you may not sleep well, and without sleep, everything below gets harder.

## The Ears and Mind: The Silence

**Rehearse silence before you arrive.** Chalkmouth behaves something like an anechoic chamber. Sound dies within a room's length, and a dropped pot gives one thud and is gone. People who have never experienced true quiet are often unsettled by it. You will hear your heartbeat, the blood in your ears, the creak of your own joints, and possibly a ringing you never knew you had. Some people feel pressure, disorientation, or rising anxiety.

- Spend time beforehand in the quietest places you can find: deep woods in snowfall, a cave, a sound-dampened room. Learn what your own body sounds like, so it doesn't frighten you.
- If you have tinnitus, know that it will be louder here, because nothing masks it. Decide in advance how you'll cope.

**You cannot call for help.** This is the practical danger of the silence. A shout carries perhaps a room's length. If you fall, get stuck, or get lost in the throughs, nobody will hear you.

- Go with a companion, if you can, and agree on a **touch-signal system** in advance: squeezes for yes, no, stop, help, and "I need to leave now."
- Stay within sight of each other. In this light, at least, sight is reliable.
- Carry a small object for signalling by sight or touch, such as a strip of bright cloth. A whistle is useless here.

**Learn to talk with your hands, humbly.** The inhabitants have a full gestural grammar, subtle enough for haggling and jokes. You won't learn it before arrival, but you can prepare:

- Practise expressive, *slow*, soft gesture. Watch how deaf communities and people who share no spoken language manage.
- Learn the first gesture you will receive: **palm down, fingers spreading. *Settle.*** It's good to be able to return it.
- Watch more than you gesture. Don't imitate signs you've seen until you're fairly sure what they mean. A gesture grammar fine enough for jokes is fine enough for insults.
- When unsure, stillness and an open, lowered hand are almost certainly polite.

**About singing.** Speech is kept for song, sung mouth to ear, one person to one listener. If someone ever does this for you, it is a profound intimacy. Receive it without comment, and don't record it or try to write it down afterward in their presence. The song is meant to be heard by exactly one person and then be gone. Let it be.

## Conduct: How to Move Through a Town That Is One Building

**The throughs pass through people's homes.** Walking through six kitchens and a sleeping loft to cross the town is normal and not an intrusion. But move like a good guest passing through a room where someone is reading: soft, steady, eyes lowered from private things, no lingering. If a family is eating, pass without stopping. Everyone keeps the throughs clean, so if you track anything in, clean it. Helping to tidy a through is probably one of the best courtesies you can offer.

**Nobody is afraid of anybody, and that includes the animals.** Wrens, stoats, voles, bats, and slugs all share the throughs. Nothing startles here, and you must not be the first thing that does.

- No sudden movements. No swatting, no shooing, no crushing. The snail trails that shine like writing are on every surface; step around snails where you can.
- **Check your pockets, hood, and any bag you set down before you pick it up again.** Wrens nest in coat pockets. Don't bring a wren up out of the pit by accident.
- Bring no animals. A dog or cat would break something that has been unbroken for a very long time.
- If you meet a roe deer standing blinking in a courtyard, let the locals handle it.

**The rooms of the dead.** Some throughs pass through old rooms where the dead were laid with the doors left open until the moss took them. You may walk past soft green shapes on soft green beds without knowing at first what they are. Watch what the locals do: a brief hand touched to the wall as they pass. Do the same. Never step on a raised shape of moss in an old room. Never dig into moss anywhere to see what's underneath.

**Nothing new, nothing thrown away, nothing carried out.** These three customs hold the whole place in balance, and they should shape everything you pack.

- **Nothing new.** Don't offer to build anything, improve anything, or introduce clever tools. If you want to give something, give *mending*: a hand at twisting nettle cord, repairing a basket, or re-daubing a wattle panel under instruction. Bring a small repair kit of natural materials, such as linen thread, a bone or wooden needle, and beeswax. Better still, just offer your hands.
- **Nothing thrown away.** Broken things are set on sills for the moss to take. So you cannot leave rubbish. No wrappers, no plastic, nothing that won't return to the moss. Pack food wrapped in cloth or leaves, or in nothing at all.
- **Nothing carried up.** This is the hard one. Take no souvenirs: no pebbles, no moss, no fern frond, no flint flake. Carry out only exactly what you carried in, and bring in as little as possible, so this is easy. I'd suggest making a written list of every item you bring and checking it against your pack before you climb out, and checking that pack for wrens. Honestly, consider leaving the camera behind. A photograph may not count as carrying something out. But taking it would change how you are *in* the place, and the place does not reward being watched through a lens.

**Metal and fire.** There is almost no metal here. A steel knife, zips, buckles, and coins will feel foreign, and a dropped metal tool is the kind of sound that does, briefly, carry. Minimise them. Use wooden toggles, horn buttons, and a small flint or bone blade if you need one. Bring no open flame, lighters, or matches unless absolutely necessary for survival. There are few hearths, and fleece-and-heather lofts are stacked on top of one another.

## The Real Danger: Settling

Everything above is manageable. This part is not, unless you prepare for it in advance.

The description is honest about what happens. After an hour you stop noticing the cold. After a day you stop noticing the light. After that, you find it hard to remember what the world above looked like, or why anyone would want a world where things keep moving. Time down here is muffled, perhaps literally. Children grow slowly and people age slowly. You may not be able to trust your own sense of how long you've been there.

None of this feels like a threat from inside. It feels like peace, and that is precisely why you must build your exit before you enter.

**1. Set a fixed departure, outside yourself.** Decide how long you'll stay, and don't rely on your own judgment to enforce it. Arrange for someone on the downs above, or back in your home world, who will come to the rim at a set time. That person should *not* be someone who will climb down to fetch you. They risk the same softening. Have them wait at the lip and signal.

**2. Write a letter to yourself, now.** Write it while you are loud and restless and full of weather. Say who you love. Say what is unfinished. Describe the downs: larks, wind, grass browning and greening. Say *why* you want a world where things keep moving. Read it on arrival, read it daily, and read it before the departure hour. Put it in an inner pocket. Paper you brought in is paper you carry out, so this breaks no custom.

**3. Carry something that marks time.** Bring a mechanical watch (quiet, and forgive the metal) or a simple mechanism like a knotted cord where you tie a knot after each sleep. The point is an outside witness to duration, because your internal sense will lie to you.

**4. Notice the signs.** If you find yourself unable to picture the sky above, reluctant to reread your letter, or thinking "just one more day" for the third time, those are signals to leave *now*, not at the scheduled hour.

**5. Know that the locals will not trap you.** Nobody will stop you from going. They simply won't need you to go, and the place won't either. The only thing holding you will be you.

## Leaving, and What Comes After

Climb out the way you came: through the first ivy-house, past the yews, up the outer ramp. Check your pockets one more time.

**Prepare for re-entry shock.** Those who have left say they missed the silence first, and that up top everything is so loud and so full of weather. You've been living in a sealed bell of stillness. Wind will feel violent, birdsong shrill, and your own footsteps absurd. Unfiltered sunlight, with its hard shadows, may briefly confuse your depth perception in the other direction.

- Plan a decompression day in somewhere quiet and simple. Don't step straight from Chalkmouth into a city.
- Expect grief, a homesickness for a place that was never your home. It fades, mostly.
- Expect your voice to feel strange for a while, and your hands to move when you talk. Some visitors report that they speak more softly for the rest of their lives. That is probably not a bad thing to bring back.

## A Short Packing List

- Wool layers from skin to shell, plus cap and two sets of mitts
- Many wool socks, foot balm, and soft grippy boots
- Pale grey glasses and a dark eye mask
- A light hazel or ash staff
- Cloth-wrapped food that can be eaten entirely, and water in a wooden flask or skin
- A small natural-material mending kit, to offer your hands and not objects
- A written inventory of everything you carry in
- A letter to yourself, kept in an inner pocket
- A timekeeper: a quiet watch or a knot-cord
- An agreed touch-signal system with a companion
- A person waiting at the rim, at a fixed hour

## And a Short List of Nothing

No noise-makers. No flame. As little metal as you can manage. No plastic. No pets. No souvenirs. No wrens in your pockets.

---

Go in softly. Accept the gesture, *settle*, and settle. Sit a while in the bright, shadowless green and listen to your own heart. Let one song be sung to you, if you're lucky. Then keep your promise to the loud, restless person who wrote your letter, and climb back up into the weather.
