# Preparing for the Hush Geode

## The Short Version

The Hush Geode is not dangerous the way a volcano or a frozen sea is dangerous. It will not crush you, burn you, or starve you, at least not during a visit of a few hours. What it demands is **discipline of the body**. For as long as you are inside, you must be almost perfectly quiet, almost perfectly careful, and willing to be a small, slow, gentle thing in a room that is still being born.

The core facts that should shape every decision:

1. **Every sound you make becomes glass.** Sharp, hair-thin, falling glass that makes more sound when it lands.
2. **The light is real noon, underground.** It can switch without warning to snow-glare, then to night.
3. **The walls are soft, and they remember.** They also remember forward in time. Some of what you do there has, in a sense, already been done.
4. **The chamber is closing.** Slowly, but not on any schedule you can trust.
5. **The way you came in seals behind you.** Your way out must not depend on it.

Everything below grows from those five facts.

## Part One: Before You Go

### Train your silence, not just your stealth

Most people think of "being quiet" as not talking and walking softly. That is nowhere near enough here. The description is explicit that *a breath let out too sharply* makes a sound worth crystallizing. You need to find out, in advance, every sound your body makes without permission.

Spend real time in the quietest place you can access: an anechoic chamber, a deep-winter forest, a silent meditation retreat, a closet lined with blankets at 3 a.m. Listen to yourself. You will notice:

- **The gasp.** This is the most dangerous sound you will make in the Geode, because it is the sound of wonder, and the Geode is wonderful. When you first see noon pouring down the walls underground, your body will want to inhale sharply. Practice meeting astonishment with a slow, long exhale through the nose. Rehearse it so often that awe itself triggers the slow breath.
- **The throat-clear, the swallow, the lip-part.** Small, but they add up to frost.
- **Joint pops.** Knees, ankles, knuckles, neck. Learn which of yours click and how to move them so they don't.
- **Stomach sounds.** See the diet notes below.
- **Clothing sounds.** Covered later, but test your whole outfit in a silent room before you commit to it.
- **Laughter, a sob, a startled "oh."** These are emotional sounds, and the Geode is an emotional place. You may find your own handprint on the wall. Prepare for that moment now, so it doesn't catch you unguarded.

A useful drill: have a friend surprise you, again and again over weeks, with a touch on the shoulder or a sudden light, until your reflex response becomes stillness rather than noise.

### Learn to move like the fauna

Everything that lives here moves with "the gentlest possible movement." Study slow-movement practices such as tai chi, the slow walking of certain meditation traditions, or wildlife-photography stalking. The goal is to shift your weight completely before committing a foot, to lower yourself without a grunt, and to rise without bracing on anything.

### Decide your communication system

If you travel with a companion, and it's wise to, you cannot speak to each other inside. Agree on a small, unambiguous set of hand signals before you leave. Keep it minimal:

- *Stop / freeze*
- *Look*
- *Danger, move away*
- *I'm fine*
- *I'm not fine*
- *Time to leave*
- *Rain coming* / *darkness coming* (for the shifts in the light)
- *Wait for me*

Practice them until they're automatic, including in the dark, by touch on the forearm. At least one of the light-shifts will plunge you into blue-dark night, and you'll need to reach each other then.

Consider **going with exactly one companion**. One reason is safety. The other reason is quieter and stranger. The word lying on the floor "has the particular spread of a voice that was speaking to someone." Somebody, at some point, says something to someone in this room. It may be good to have someone there worth saying it to.

### Health: go in well, or don't go

Do **not** visit with:

- A cold, cough, sniffle, or allergy flare. A single sneeze is a spray of glass aimed at your own face and hands. A coughing fit could build a small thicket around your head.
- Any condition that causes involuntary vocalization or sudden movement, unless you've found a way to manage it for several hours.
- Hiccups-prone digestion, reflux, or a full stomach.

**Diet in the 24 hours before:** eat bland, easily digested food and avoid anything carbonated, gassy, or heavy. Go in neither hungry nor full, since a growling stomach is audible in a room this quiet. Hydrate well the day before, then taper, so you're not drinking much inside.

**Before descending:** empty your bladder and bowels, then clear your throat, cough, stretch, and crack every joint that wants cracking, all on the surface. Do your noisy living up in the ordinary world before you go down.

## Part Two: What to Wear

### Fabric

Your clothing must be **silent**. That rules out nylon shells, most technical rain gear, anything with a "swish," stiff canvas, leather that creaks, and crinkly insulation.

Choose:

- **Wool** (merino base layers, a boiled-wool or felted outer layer). It's quiet, stays warm when damp, and handles the drizzle hours well.
- **Brushed fleece or soft cotton flannel** as mid-layers, tested for rub-noise between arm and torso.
- **Nothing loose that can flap.** Snug, but not so tight it strains when you crouch.

The chamber is **cool and damp**, and you'll be moving very little for hours. That combination drains heat. Dress warmer than you think you need. The pools of light are only a degree warmer than the stone, so they won't warm you.

### Fastenings

Remove or tape over every zipper, snap, buckle, and Velcro patch. Use cloth ties, toggles, or buttons wrapped in thread. Check jewelry, watches (a ticking watch is a disaster), keys, coins, and belt buckles. Leave all of it on the surface.

### Footwear

Here you have two competing needs. The floor is covered in **glass**: fine sound-dust, larger fragments from old noises, and "fresh fractures, clean-edged and sharp." But hard soles are loud.

The old solution is best: a **sturdy, puncture-resistant soft boot** with thick felt or several layers of heavy wool socks pulled *over* it. Hunters and burglars once used this method to walk silently, and here it also lets you feel the floor before committing weight. Bring a spare outer sock layer, because the glass will shred the first.

### Hands

This takes some thought. Gloves protect you from glass and from the tacky, half-set walls. But the three handprints on the wall are **bare**: "adult-sized, slightly splayed as if bracing."

My intuition: bring thin, silent **cotton gloves** for general movement, and be prepared to take one off. Leave the other hand gloved if you like. Keep your bare hand *clean* before entry, and wipe the clay of the crack off it. If you ever do press a palm to that wall, you'll want the print to be your own hand and not a smear of mud.

### Eyes and face

The light is "noon-strength," sometimes "snow-glare morning." It isn't just bright. It may carry real ultraviolet from real days, and in an enclosed pale chamber it will bounce off everything. Snow-blindness is a real risk over a long stay.

But the light also changes "without transition" to blue-dark night. Photochromic lenses are too slow for that.

Bring:

- **Flip-up or easily removable sunglasses** with a moderate tint, attached to a soft cord so they can't fall. A dropped pair of glasses is a small avalanche of sound.
- **Sunscreen** on exposed skin, unscented and applied well before entry, so you're not bringing fresh chemicals into contact with stone that is still deciding what to become.
- Optionally, a **soft, fine-mesh cloth over the nose and mouth.** The floor is "dusted with the residue of old sounds... fine as flour," and every whisper leaves "a faint frost of needles that settles like dust." Nobody knows what inhaled sound-glass does to lungs. A loose cloth filter, tested to confirm it doesn't make your breathing louder, is a reasonable precaution.

## Part Three: What to Bring, and What Not To

### The principle

The entry is **a crack no wider than a hand** that "does not quite obey its own geometry." It lets *you* through. Don't assume it will be generous with a backpack. Carry everything on your body, flat and close, in soft pouches. Anything dangling will catch in the crack or clink inside.

**Every object you bring is a potential noise and a potential permanent fossil.** Anything dropped and left behind may be sealed into the stone forever, like the moth and grazer in the oldest shelves. Tether everything with soft cord. Bring as little as you can.

### Bring

- **Water**, in a soft bladder filled *completely* so it cannot slosh. Sip rarely and slowly. Swallowing is quieter with small amounts.
- **Soft food, if staying more than a couple of hours**: a gel, paste, or pudding pouch. Nothing crunchy or chewy, and no wrapper that crinkles. Ideally don't eat inside at all.
- **A folded wool pad** to kneel or sit on. Don't sit directly on the floor. It's full of glass, and the lower stone may still be setting.
- **An absorbent undergarment.** It sounds undignified, but urinating in a room where the sound of falling liquid becomes glass, in a sealed ecosystem where your waste stays forever, is far worse. Better to plan for it than to face the choice in there.
- **A soft cloth** for wiping hands.
- **Your independent means of leaving** (see Part Seven). Whatever it is, test it before you trust it.
- **Three river stones.** See below.

### Don't bring

- **Artificial light.** The chamber is already bright, and at night the stars shine in the pools. More importantly, light here *pours and pools and becomes matter*. The folds hunt brightness, and the hushmoths drink it. A flashlight is an unpredictable intervention. If you must have light for an emergency, bring a very dim red source you've committed to never using unless you have to.
- **Phones or electronics** that can beep, buzz, or vibrate. If you want images, use a camera with a truly silent shutter, all sounds disabled, no flash, tethered to you. Honestly consider not taking pictures. It's hard to be present and careful at the same time.
- **Anything hard that can clink**: metal bottles, carabiners, trekking poles, tools.
- **Anything you'd mind losing.**
- **Anything alive** besides yourself and your companion.

### The three river stones

The arch in the chamber is built from "ordinary grey river stones, smooth and damp, and nothing in this chamber could have brought them."

You are the only kind of thing that could have brought them.

I would bring three. Choose them from a real river, grey, smooth, about palm-sized or a little smaller, suitable for stacking into an arch roughly knee-high. Keep them damp, wrapped individually in wet wool so they can't knock together. You don't have to decide in advance whether you'll set them down. But if the moment comes and you have no stones, you'll know you were supposed to. Bringing them costs little, and leaving them behind might break something you don't understand.

A caution: if you do build the arch, place each stone with agonizing gentleness. Stone on stone makes a click, and a click grows a spine.

## Part Four: Getting In

### Finding it

"You would never find it by looking." Take that literally. The Geode seems to be found by other senses, or by some quality of attention the multiverse travel itself provides. If your means of travel delivers you to the field, walk slowly. Feel for cold and damp in the earth, and listen for the faint hiss of the fault, the distant wind from another day. Expect to be surprised by where it is.

It lies "perhaps four body-lengths down." How you reach the crack from the surface isn't clear. Be prepared to dig or wriggle through packed, wet clay, and have a plan for doing so without exhausting yourself, since you want to arrive calm, not panting. Heavy breathing inside the Geode is loud breathing.

### The crack

It is cold, wet, diagonal, and hand-wide, and "it narrows as you look down it, yet it lets you through."

- **Stop looking down it.** Watching it narrow may trigger claustrophobic panic and make your body resist. Close your eyes, trust it, and move by feel.
- **Go slowly, exhaling, not holding your breath.** When you pass through into the chamber, the first thing you do must be *quiet*. A crack you've fought through tends to end in a gasp of relief. Don't let it.
- **Clean your hands and face before you're fully through**, or immediately after, with the cloth. Clay-streaked fingers on soft porcelain walls will leave marks you didn't intend.

### The first minute

You will emerge into full white noon, underground. Your eyes will slam half-shut, and every instinct will want to react.

Here's what to do:

1. **Freeze.** Don't take a step.
2. **Exhale slowly** through the nose.
3. **Lower your sunglasses.**
4. **Wait.** Let your eyes adjust. It takes longer than you'd think before you realize the walls are moving and the air is full of moths. Only once you can *see* the floor and what's crawling on it should you shift your weight at all.
5. **Look behind you.** The crack will have become a seam, "sealed like a healed cut." Seeing that you're sealed in will provoke a spike of fear. You prepared for it and you have your way out. Breathe through it.

## Part Five: Being There

### Moving

- **Always look before stepping.** The floor has herds of glass-woodlice (chime-grazers), crowds of damp grey beetles (pearlfeeders), single upright needles marking where a grazer died, and fragments of old noise, some "shaped unmistakably like a cough." Don't crush any of them. A crushed grazer is both a death and a crunch.
- **Keep to the older, lower, firmer stone** where you can. Stay off the soft surfaces near the fault. You'd leave prints, and you may not want to leave *more* prints than the ones already waiting there.
- **Don't lean on walls for balance.** Crouch and stand using your own strength. The walls near the fault take fingerprints, and the walls further down are tacky wax. You don't want to rest a shoulder against the stone and find, later, a shoulder-shaped hollow that becomes permanent.
- **Watch the floor fractures.** The chamber "sometimes splits along lines of stress." Don't stand with a foot on either side of a fresh crack.
- **Never lie down or sleep.** This is the most important physical rule. The walls grow "a thin new layer, soft as skin" over days, and possibly faster in places. The oldest shelves hold sealed moths and grazers, "things that did not move in time." A sleeping human is a snoring human, and a still one.

### Sound hygiene

You *will* make some sound. A foot will scuff, and a breath will come out wrong. Accept it. When it happens:

- **Don't react to the reaction.** A tiny frost of needles falling from a misplaced breath is fine. A startled "oh" at the frost is worse, and flinching back into a crystal is worse still. The real danger of the Geode is the **cascade**. Each crystal "chimes softly as it lands and grows smaller whiskers of its own." Noise breeds noise. A dropped object can become a small, spreading, tinkling mess. Your job is to stop the chain at the first link by going still.
- **Let the grazers work.** They'll come to eat your sounds. That's their purpose. Stand still and let them clean up after you. Where they've passed, the air will feel "extra quiet." You may find that deeply calming.
- **Beware the direction of sound.** Filaments grow "in the direction the sound traveled." If you ever must make a noise, such as an unavoidable cough, turn your face toward the floor and away from your companion, and cover your mouth with your gloved hand so the glass grows into cloth rather than air.

### The listeners

The predators are "long, pale, eyeless" things "pressed flat into the soft upper stone." They follow sound-filaments back to their source and strike with "a slow, silent lunge." They're sized to eat woodlice, so an adult human is probably not prey. But no one knows what a listener does when it follows a thread back to something the size of you. It might bite defensively, and nobody knows whether they carry venom.

- **Stay away from the soft upper walls** where they lie hidden.
- **Keep your sounds small**, so any threads you leave are short.
- **If you see one moving toward you**, don't flinch or swat. Retreat slowly, silently, downward.

### The folds

Animate sheets of pooled light that envelop hushmoths and digest the brightness out of them. They're probably no threat to you. But don't let one slide over your bare hand to find out, and remember they're a reason not to bring anything glowing.

### The pearlfeeders and your breath

Here's a subtle thing: **you are warm, and you breathe.** You're now the largest source of condensation the chamber has probably ever had. The pearlfeeders "move in slow crowds, creating the damp they depend on," and they may be drawn toward you. Moisture will bead on the stone near where you stand, and seed-pearl droplets will gather and fall. Each falling drop "leaves a tiny glass bead where it strikes."

It isn't harmful, but be aware that your mere presence alters the place. Stay a few hours, not a day.

### The light shifts

The day pouring through the fault changes, sometimes several times an hour, with no transition. Prepare for each:

- **Harsh white noon:** sunglasses on, and watch for glare fatigue.
- **Warm gold afternoon:** easier on the eyes. This is probably the best light for looking carefully at the walls.
- **Rainy grey:** real drizzle falls "from nowhere across a space the size of a room." Your wool will dampen. Rain on stone is a pattering, and each drop leaves glass, but it's gentle. Stay put, stay covered, and let it pass. When the day changes, "the last droplets hang in the air for a moment before falling." It's beautiful. Don't gasp.
- **Blue-dark night:** your eyes, adjusted to noon, will see nothing at all. **Stop moving immediately.** Crouch on your pad if you can do so silently, and find your companion's forearm by touch. Wait. When your eyes adjust, you'll see stars in the pools, and that may be the most extraordinary thing you ever see. Don't move again until either the light returns or you can see the floor.

**An extrapolation worth taking seriously:** if rainy days can pour their rain through the fault, other days might pour their weather too. The description mentions snow-glare, but not snowfall, hail, or thunder. If the light ever turns the bruised green-grey of a storm, **crouch low, cover your head and face with your arms, and close your eyes.** Thunder in a room where sound becomes glass would not be survivable-proof. I don't know that it happens, but I would plan for it.

### Shadows

Your shadow will **lag behind you**, sometimes by moments, sometimes longer. It will also **point toward the light, not away**. Both are deeply disorienting, because humans navigate by shadow more than they realize.

- **Don't use shadows to judge anything**: not your position, not whether something is moving, not whether your companion is where you think.
- **Expect to see your own shadow still standing where you were** after you've moved. It may happen when you look back at the seam or when you turn around. Know this in advance so it doesn't startle a sound out of you.
- The walls may be "crowded with shadows of hushmoths that have already moved on." Some shadows you see will be empty. Some will be yours.

## Part Six: The Marks

This is where preparation stops being practical and becomes something else.

You'll find three handprints, a stair of five steps that ends at a wall, a knee-high arch of river stones, and a crystallized word near the seam. The chamber suggests, strongly, that these are *yours*: marks from a visit that hasn't happened, or is happening now.

### How to approach them

**Don't try to break the pattern.** Some visitors will feel the urge to test it. They'll refuse to touch the wall, or smash the arch, or make a *different* word, just to prove they're free. I'd advise strongly against it. You're in a room made of forming stone that remembers in both directions. You don't know what a contradiction does to a place like that. It might do nothing. It might do something you would regret in a way that's hard to picture.

**Don't try to force the pattern either.** You don't need to climb up to the high handprint near the fault. You can't reach it standing, and the soft upper stone is where the listeners lie. If that print is yours, the circumstance that puts your hand there will arrive or it won't. It might be a slip, a lunge, a moment on the stair, or something about how the chamber's shape shifts as it closes. Don't engineer it.

**Look at them first, for a long time, before touching anything.** Put your hand near the print on the oldest, lowest stone without touching it. Compare the splay of the fingers to your own. Let yourself feel whatever you feel, silently.

Note that the oldest print is the sharpest, and the one in the youngest, softest stone is freshest. That means the hand came first to the oldest stone. If you do touch the walls, the lowest print is probably where your palm belongs first.

### The stair

Five steps, edges worn "as if by feet, though no footsteps mark anywhere else." You'll be tempted to climb them. They're probably safe, since they've held whatever wore them. Climb them with the same slow, full-weight-transfer care as everything else, and stop at the top facing the wall. Don't push on the wall. Stand there. Whatever the stair is for, it clearly wasn't built by anyone who needed it to go somewhere.

### The arch

If you brought the stones, you'll know whether to set them down. If there's already an arch there, look closely at its stones. Are they yours? You'll have your three wrapped against your body. You may find that you're holding the originals. That is the kind of moment worth preparing for emotionally, because it's the kind that makes people gasp.

### The word

It lies near the seam, "a delicate spray of glass whiskers perhaps a hand wide," uneaten, dust-free, avoided by the grazers. It is "too complex to be any animal's noise," and it is "speaking to someone."

**Do not touch it.** Don't pick it up, don't take it home, and don't sweep it aside to see what's under it.

Then, and this is the real preparation, **think now, before you go, about what you would say.** You're going to a place where speech is so costly that every living thing has learned not to do it. You'll likely say exactly one word in there, quietly, to someone. A hand-wide spray means a soft word, not a call.

What would be worth one word in a room made of noon? A name, perhaps. A "yes." A "thank you," or "here," or "look." You don't need to decide. You probably *can't* decide. When the moment comes, it will come on its own. But it is worth having thought about, so that when you do speak, it's a word you mean.

Speak it facing the floor near the seam. Speak it softly. Watch it fall.

## Part Seven: Getting Out

### The seam won't reopen for you

The crack became "a seam in the wall, sealed like a healed cut." Nothing suggests it will open again. The stair ends at a wall. The fault is high, narrow, and full of light from other days, and although the fault-reeds grow there, they may be "the only things here with a root in both places." That tells you nothing about whether *you* could pass through.

**Your exit must be independent of the chamber's geometry.** Whatever means brought you across the multiverse must be able to take you out from *inside* a sealed stone room. Test this before you go, ideally from somewhere enclosed and silent. If your method of leaving makes noise, find out how much, because it'll become glass around you as you go.

**A tether line** run from the surface through the crack is tempting as a backup, a thread to follow out. But the crack might seal around it and fix it into the stone forever, or cut it, or simply not let it through. If you try it, use a thin natural-fiber cord you don't mind losing. Don't make it your only plan.

### When to leave

- **Set a time limit before you go in**, and have your companion hold you to it. A few hours is plenty, and a single light-cycle or two is ideal. The chamber can "close next week" or in centuries. You don't know which.
- **Leave early if you start to feel cold, tired, or hungry.** Tired people make noise, cold people shiver and stamp, and hungry stomachs growl.
- **Leave soon after the word.** If the marks really are yours, then once the stones are set and the word is said, the story says, "the chamber will have been completed." I don't know what completion means for a room that's filling itself in. It might mean nothing. It might mean the final pouring begins. Don't linger to find out.
- **Before you go, look behind you at where you've been.** Check that nothing is left: no dropped glove, no loose thread. Kneel pads, water, and cloths should all come out with you. What stays should be only what was already there.

### Tell someone

Before you leave the surface, tell someone where you're going and when to expect you back, with a generous margin. Light from other days pours through that fault. It is not unthinkable that time in the chamber runs strangely, and that "a few hours" inside is some other length outside.

## Part Eight: Afterward

### Your clothes

You'll emerge dusted with your own sounds: fine frost from every breath, every scuff, the rain-beads, the one word's echo. It isn't clear whether sound-glass stays glass outside the Geode or melts back into something else. Undress slowly, over a cloth, and see what falls. If it stays, you might keep it. It's the only record of what you sounded like in there.

### The loudness of the world

Almost every account of deep silence carries the same warning. When you come back, the ordinary world will be *unbearably* loud: traffic, voices, refrigerators, your own heels on pavement. Give yourself a quiet day or two before you rejoin normal life.

More subtly, you may find that words feel different. You'll have spent hours somewhere that one spoken word was a permanent physical object, placed carefully, meant for someone. Casual speech may feel strange afterward. Many people who have been in profound silence say they talk less and mean more for a long time after. That's not a hazard exactly. It's just something the Geode is likely to do to you.

### The marks

You may leave not knowing whether the handprints were yours. You may leave certain they were. Either way, somewhere under a field of ordinary clay there's now a small room with your touch in its stone, or with a place already waiting for it. Some visitors will find that comforting and some unsettling. Most will find it both.

---

## A Final Note on Spirit

Nearly all of this guide is about restraint: don't gasp, don't lean, don't drop, don't linger, don't speak. That's accurate, but it's not the whole truth of the place.

The Hush Geode is not a hostile environment that you survive. It's a delicate one that you *join* for a little while. Everything living there has learned to fit and to move gently. The hushmoths soften the harsh noon for everyone, and the grazers quietly clean up the noise. The whole population grows denser and more careful as the walls close in, and doesn't decline. It's a community built entirely on courtesy.

Go in the way you'd enter a room where someone you love is sleeping. Be warm, slow, attentive, and glad to be there. Carry three stones and one word you haven't chosen yet. Then let the room made of noon finish making itself, with a little help from you.
