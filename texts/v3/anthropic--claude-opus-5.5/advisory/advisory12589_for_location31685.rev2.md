# Preparing for the Tableland of Unfallen Walls

## A Traveler's Briefing

The first thing to understand is that the obvious dangers here are ordinary ones. Altitude, cold, wet, and getting lost will hurt you long before anything strange does. The strange things are mostly quiet, patient, and indifferent to you. They become dangerous only if you engage with them carelessly, or if the land has already weakened you enough that your judgment slips.

So prepare in two layers. The first is for a high, cold, foggy, trackless wilderness with no food and no rescue. The second is for a place where time runs oddly around human-made things.

---

## Part One: The Body

### Altitude

You arrive at about 4,000 meters with no gradual approach. If you step straight in from sea level, your body will not have climbed its way up, and that matters.

- **Acclimatize beforehand.** If your multiverse transit allows it, spend a week or two somewhere around 3,000–3,500 meters before arriving. This is the single most important physical preparation.
- **Expect symptoms anyway.** Headache, poor sleep, breathlessness, nausea, and a dull, stupid feeling are all likely. That stupidity is dangerous here, because this land punishes poor decisions quietly.
- **Carry medication.** Bring acetazolamide for prevention and mild symptoms. For emergencies, bring dexamethasone for cerebral edema and nifedipine for pulmonary edema. Learn the warning signs before you go: confusion, loss of coordination, a wet cough, breathlessness at rest.
- **Bring a pulse oximeter.** In a place where you can "hear your own blood," it helps to know what that blood is actually doing.
- **The rule of descent does not work here.** On most mountains, the cure for altitude sickness is to go down. The tableland has no down. Once you walk away from your arrival point, you cannot reach lower air on foot. Your only descent is your exit. Know exactly where that is at all times, and be willing to use it early.

### Cold and Wet Together

The description calls the cold "not bitter, just an unrelenting chill." Do not be reassured. Steady, damp cold at altitude, day after day, with no warm hour to recover in, is how people die of hypothermia without ever feeling they were in danger.

The fog will coat you. Your wool will be "furred with droplets." Skin, hair, and gear will be damp every morning and every afternoon. The noon sun is bright but gives no warmth, so you cannot count on drying anything out.

- **Your base layers should be merino wool.** It stays warm when wet and resists odor over long wear.
- **Your insulation should be synthetic, not down.** Down collapses when wet, and you will be wet. Choose a synthetic puffy jacket and a synthetic-fill sleeping bag, or a down bag with a vapor-barrier liner if you know how to use one.
- **Bring a breathable hardshell jacket and trousers.** The fog is not rain, but it will soak through anything that is not a shell.
- **Bring a warm hat, a buff or balaclava, liner gloves, and insulated mittens.** Carry spares of each in a dry bag.
- **Keep one complete set of clothing permanently dry,** sealed away and used only for sleeping. This is your lifeline, and you should never wear it while walking.
- **Watch your companions for the "umbles":** stumbling, mumbling, fumbling, grumbling. In the fog, with this silence, it is easy to mistake early hypothermia for the mood of the place.

### Feet

This may matter more than anything else on the list. The moss lies knee-deep, and every footstep presses out "a small cold spring." Your feet will be wet in cold water for weeks. That is how trench foot happens.

- **Choose your footwear deliberately.** Either wear waterproof boots with tall gaiters and accept that water will get in anyway, or wear fast-draining boots with neoprene or waterproof socks. Many bog walkers prefer the second approach.
- **Bring many socks,** at least four or five pairs, rotated and dried against your body at night.
- **Tend your feet every evening:** dry them, air them, apply a barrier balm, and inspect them for pale, wrinkled, numb, or painful patches.
- **Walk on the paved roads and the gravel between the tors** whenever you can. That dry, crunchy footing is a gift to your feet.

### Sun

When the fog lifts at noon, the sky turns deep blue, almost violet, and there is a "clean white glare." At 4,000 meters, ultraviolet light is intense, and the frost and pale stone reflect it.

- **Wear category 4 glacier glasses with side shields.** Snow blindness is possible even here.
- **Use high-SPF sunscreen and lip balm.** You will burn at noon while shivering.
- **Put on your glasses before the fog lifts.** The shift from pearl-grey to full glare happens quickly.

## Part Two: Sustenance

### Food

There is nothing to eat. There are no animals at all, and you should not experiment with any plant. The rosettes, cushion plants, and red-barked trees belong to an ecology that has never known a grazing animal, so they have never needed to be edible or to warn anyone that they are not. Assume everything is inedible.

- **Carry all your food.** At altitude, in steady cold, while walking over soft ground, you will need 4,000–5,000 kilocalories a day. Focus on dense foods: nuts, nut butters, oils, chocolate, hard cheese, and freeze-dried meals.
- **Bring hot drinks and extra fat for the evenings.** Your body is a furnace, and there is little else to feed it with.
- **Remember that water boils at about 87°C at this altitude,** so cooking takes longer and uses more fuel. Bring a liquid-fuel stove or a cold-rated canister system, plenty of fuel, and a windscreen.
- **Let your food supply set your trip length.** Pick a turnaround day by counting rations, not by distance or ambition.

### Water

Water is the one resource the tableland gives freely. How you collect it matters.

- **Collect fog-drip.** Beard moss in the dwarf woods "drips steadily all day." You can also set up your own collector: a fine mesh net or tarp draped over a frame, draining into a bag. A few hours of morning fog should fill your bottles.
- **Tarn and stream water is probably unusually pure,** since nothing lives in it. Sphagnum water will be tannic and acidic, stained and faintly sour. Filter or boil it out of habit.
- **Do not drink from the rosette crowns.** Those small pools are perfectly still even in wind, and they reflect skies that are not overhead. That water is not only water. Do not drink it, touch it, or dip anything into it. Be careful even about looking into it for long. One traveler saw a hand there, setting a stone. You do not want to find out what happens if the hand notices you.

### Fire

Leave the local fuel alone. The ground is full of dry material: rosette skirts, drifts of red bark, meters of peat that are "dry beneath and damp on top." It would all burn beautifully, and that is exactly the problem. Peat fires can smolder underground for months or years, and there is no rain to put one out. In a land where nothing decays, a fire might be the only thing capable of destroying ten thousand years of preserved growth. Use only your stove, and set it on bare stone or gravel, never on peat or moss.

And never, under any circumstances, light the laid hearths in the finished houses. See below.

## Part Three: Navigation and Time

### The Problem

This is where most travelers get into trouble. The difficulty comes from several directions at once:

- For most of the day, you can see only 10–20 paces.
- The land is "nearly flat, but not quite," with long swells and few distinct features.
- Distances stretch. "A tor that seems an hour away at noon is still an hour away at dusk."
- Some landmarks repeat. The identical doorway appears in districts days apart.
- Sounds draw you off course. The tapping is always "a little farther on."
- The streams have no clear direction, so you cannot follow water downhill to anywhere.

### What You Can Trust

- **The roads.** They are "dead straight, running from district to district." A straight line across featureless country is the most valuable thing a navigator could ask for. Learn which roads lead toward your exit, and follow them. One caution: the roads are the paths the city travels, so walk beside them or along them, but do not sleep on them.
- **The noon window.** For an hour or two, the world opens up. Spend that time well. Climb a low swell or a stable tor, take compass bearings to distant features, sketch the lie of the land, identify your next road, and note where you are relative to your exit. Treat every clear noon as your daily survey.
- **The night sky.** The fog forms because heat radiates away to the stars, which means the nights must be clear. Before dawn, before the fog condenses, you can take bearings from the stars. If you know them, you can navigate by them. If you do not, learn some before you go.
- **Your compass,** tested on arrival. There is no guarantee magnetic north behaves normally in a foreign world. Check it against the sun at noon and the stars at night during your first day. If it works, it is your best tool in the fog.
- **Pacing.** Count your steps and know your pace length. In fog, dead reckoning by compass and step count works better than your eyes.

### What You Should Not Trust

- **Your sense of distance.** Assume everything is farther than it looks.
- **Recognizable doorways, lintel cracks, or lichen patterns.** If you "recognize" a place, do not assume you have returned to it. Check it against your compass and your log.
- **The tapping.** Never navigate toward it.
- **Satellite navigation.** GPS will not work in a world without your world's satellites. Leave that dependence behind.

### The Fog Protocol

- When visibility drops below about 20 paces, stop traveling cross-country. Travel only along a road, or not at all.
- If you are in a group, rope together or stay within arm's reach in dense fog. It would be very easy to lose someone ten paces away and never find them again in a land this large.
- Agree on whistle signals beforehand: one blast for "where are you," two for "come to me," three for emergency. Sound should carry well in this silence. Answer every call.
- Make it a firm rule that nobody walks toward the tapping alone. Better still, nobody walks toward it at all.

### Keeping Time

"Travelers who cross it speak of weeks, then stop speaking of time at all." Take that as a warning. When you lose count of the days, you lose count of your food, your exit, and your plans.

- **Keep a written log** and make an entry every day without fail: the date by your own count, your position, the weather, your remaining rations, and anything unusual.
- **Bring a mechanical watch that shows the date,** and a backup. Count days by the fog cycle as well. Each dawn fog is one day.
- **If you notice you have stopped caring what day it is, treat that as a symptom** of altitude, hypothermia, or something else. Stop, eat, get warm, and read back through your log.

## Part Four: Camping

### Where to Sleep

This question is harder than it looks.

- **Do not sleep in the finished houses.** They have sleeping platforms and they wait for someone. Do not become the someone they are waiting for.
- **Do not sleep against any wall, finished or ruined.** You cannot easily tell whether a ruin is being built up or taken down, since the description says the two are the same district at different moments. Stones move when no one is looking, and the building happens in the fog, which will surround you all night. You do not want to wake up beside a new course of stone, or inside one.
- **Do not camp on the cushion plants.** They are the oldest living things here and grow "a finger's width in a lifetime." They look like perfect firm beds. Leave them alone.
- **Do not camp in the rosette stands.** They are crowds of hooded figures facing east, and you will not sleep well among them.
- **The best places are gravel patches near stable tors,** or firm ground in open country between districts, away from the roads and at least a long stone's throw from any construction. Gravel drains, and it is the closest thing to dry ground available.

### Shelter

- **Bring a low, sturdy tent with good ventilation,** or a single-wall shelter designed for condensation. Fog will get in, and your breath will freeze on the inner walls every night.
- **Bring stakes suited to gravel and soft ground,** plus stuff sacks you can fill with grus as deadman anchors. Empty them where you filled them before you leave.
- **Never use worked stones as anchors or windbreaks,** and never borrow a stone from a wall. Also be wary of the natural loose stones near the building districts, since they may be material waiting to be placed.
- **Bring a closed-cell foam pad under an insulated inflatable pad.** The cold comes up from the stone.
- **Bring a headlamp with spare batteries,** kept warm in an inner pocket. Evenings are long, grey, and dim.

### Do Not Build

This deserves its own heading. Do not stack stones. Do not build cairns to mark your route, windbreaks, or fire rings, and do not balance a pebble on a wall-top for fun.

The whole tableland revolves around the setting of stone upon stone. The builders are always present and never there. You do not want your stonework taken up by the city, and you do not want to find your cairn standing taller in the morning than you left it. If you need route markers, use brightly colored ribbon tied to your own stakes, and collect every one on your way back. Better yet, rely on compass and log.

## Part Five: Biosecurity and Leaving No Trace

This is the most important ethical preparation, and the one travelers most often fail to think about.

**The tableland has no decomposers.** There are no animals, no insects, no worms, and apparently no active rot. You are a walking cloud of bacteria, fungi, skin mites, and possibly seeds, spores, and small insects hiding in your gear. You could be the first thing capable of rot to arrive here in ten thousand years.

### Before You Go

- **Clean everything:** boots (scrub out every tread), gaiters, trouser cuffs, tent, pack, velcro, and pockets. Seeds and soil from other worlds must not come with you.
- **Check your gear for insects and spiders.** A single fertile creature could be a catastrophe.
- **Wash your clothing and freeze your gear before departure** if you can.
- **Bring no pets or living animals of any kind.** A dog would bring a whole ecosystem with it. Besides, your own shadow already "settles into place like a dog lying down," and that is company enough.
- **Bring no fresh food:** no fruit, no seeds that might sprout, nothing unsealed.

### While There

- **Carry out all human waste** in sealed waste bags. Nothing will break it down. What you leave will stay, preserved, for longer than your civilization lasts.
- **Carry out every crumb, wrapper, and bit of fluff.**
- **Contain your food carefully** so that no fungal spores or molds from your supplies escape.
- **Do not pick plants,** do not tear rosette leaves, and do not dig into the peat.
- **Walk on roads, gravel, and stone where you can,** instead of moss, to limit what you shed and what you trample.

A sobering footnote: if you die here, you will not decay. Travel carefully.

## Part Six: The Strange Things

None of these phenomena is known to be aggressive. They follow rules. Your protection comes from understanding those rules and declining to test them.

### The Tapping

It comes in the fog, rhythmic and unhurried, sometimes from several directions at once. It is the sound of competent, patient people doing honest work.

- **Do not chase it.** Every account says the same thing: it is always a little farther on. Chasing it will pull you off your route, deeper into the fog, into country where distances stretch. This is how people get lost.
- **Do not call out to it.** Nobody knows what happens if the builders answer, and you should not be the first to find out.
- **It is not a threat.** There is nothing in the description to suggest the builders mean harm, and a great deal to suggest they are simply busy. Let them work.
- **Listen for changes.** If the tapping starts to sound nearer or clearer than before, especially after you have passed through one of the repeated doorways, take it as a sign that you are drifting toward wherever the builders are. Stop. Take out your log and compass, go back to the nearest known road, and consider heading for your exit.

### The Tools

"A hammerstone set down on a wall-top still holds a hand's warmth, in a land where nothing is warm."

In the endless chill, this will tempt you more than anything else here. You will be cold for weeks, and there, on the wall, is a warm stone that fits your hand.

**Do not pick it up.** Do not warm your hands on it. Do not move the rope, sit on the levers, or pocket a copper wedge. These are someone's tools, set down mid-task. To take one up is, in a very real sense, to join the work, and you do not know the terms of that job or how long the shift lasts.

### The Finished Houses

Hearths laid with dry rosette skirts, never lit. Sleeping platforms. Niches holding a copper pin, a bundle of wool, a carved pebble. Finished houses in a finished city, waiting.

- **Look through the doorway if you want to. Do not go inside.** If you do step in, touch nothing.
- **Never light the hearth.** The houses are waiting for their inhabitants, who arrive only "in the moment between completion and collapse, a moment nobody has been able to find." The most obvious way to find that moment would be to light the fire and lie down on the platform, and that is exactly why you should not. You would be accepting an invitation that was not addressed to you, and you might not be able to decline it later.
- **Take nothing from the niches.** These objects belong to people who are not dead, only elsewhere.
- **If fog fills a house "to the lintel" while the air outside is clear, get well away from it.** Watch it pour out, slowly and upward, from a distance.

### The Repeated Doorway

"The same doorway appears in districts days apart... with the same crack in the lintel and the same orange lichen splash on the left jamb."

- **Learn its description before you go** so you know it when you see it.
- **Do not walk through it,** especially the version standing alone in open moss with nothing on either side. The fact that it "changes nothing, or seems to" should not reassure you. The later sign, the tapping coming nearer, suggests it changes your relationship to the builders' elsewhere. Walk around it.
- **Never use it as a landmark.** It exists in more than one place.

### Lagging Shadows

Your shadow may arrive a half-step after you do at noon. Make a habit of checking it during the clear window. If it lags only occasionally and only slightly, note it in your log. If it starts lagging more, or lags when you have not moved, you are becoming loosened from time the way the city is. That is a strong signal to head toward your exit.

### The Stairway That Grows

Some stairways have steps that "grow imperceptibly taller as they climb, until the last is a wall of its own." Do not climb them. You gain nothing by it, and the view from the top, if there is a top, is not for you.

### The Ruins

Walk through them if you like, carefully and respectfully. They are among the most beautiful places here: moss over old courses, lichen across smoothed faces, the faint grid of foundations under cushion plants. Remember that they are not dead. They are the same city at another moment, in the middle of a process. Do not dig, do not pull stones free, and do not try to find out which way a particular ruin is moving.

## Part Seven: The Mind

The tableland is not hostile. But its effect on your mind will build slowly, in ways that are easy to miss.

**The silence.** There is no birdsong, no insect hum, no rustle of small animals, and streams too level to babble. Some people find it restful at first. Over weeks it can press on you. Your inner voice gets louder, and old worries come back.

- Travel with at least one companion if you possibly can. Talk to each other every day about how you are really doing.
- A small, quiet source of music or a recorded voice can help in your tent at night. Keep it to the tent.

**The absence of life.** Nothing flies, nothing crawls, nothing runs from you. Humans are used to sharing their world, and here you do not. That can feel eerie, or lonely, or grand. Expect all three.

**The feeling of being watched,** or rather of being in the presence of attentive work. The builders are "always present and never there." You will probably feel them. Accept it the way you would accept being in a stranger's workshop while they are out: be respectful, touch nothing, and do not linger too long.

**The slide in time.** As mentioned above, losing track of time is the tableland's gentlest trap. Your log is your anchor. Read it back often.

**The pull of the houses.** In the endless chill, the finished houses will begin to look like home: roofs, hearths, beds. A tired, cold, hypoxic mind will start inventing reasons why it would be fine to go in just this once. Settle this before you go. Make a firm rule, share it with your companions, and have them hold you to it.

**The patience.** The last line of the description says the builders work "as though whoever works there knows exactly how much time they have, and it is enough." There is a lesson here worth taking. Do not rush. Walk at a steady, unhurried pace and rest often. Treat the fog hours as rest hours if you need to. Travel mostly in the clear noon window and along the roads. Hurrying makes you sweat, sweat makes you wet, wet makes you cold, and cold makes you careless. Let the land set your pace.

## Part Eight: A Model Day

- **Before dawn, while the sky is still clear:** wake, check the stars for bearings, eat a hot breakfast, and record the date.
- **Dawn:** the fog condenses. Set up your fog-collector and fill your water bottles.
- **Morning, in fog:** travel only along a known road, or rest, repair gear, and tend your feet. Rope up if you move.
- **Noon, in the clear window:** put on glacier glasses. Climb a low swell, take bearings, map, plan the next road, check your shadow, and write in your log. Move quickly and efficiently across open country toward the next road.
- **Mid-afternoon:** the fog gathers in the hollows. Reach your road or campsite before it thickens.
- **Evening, grey and still:** make camp on gravel, away from walls and roads. Cook a hot, fatty dinner. Change into your dry sleeping layers. Do your foot care. Update your log with rations and position, and note anything strange.
- **Night:** sleep. If you hear tapping, let it be. It is not for you.

## Part Nine: The Exit

- **Before you arrive,** know how you will leave and where your extraction point is.
- **On arrival,** fix your exit point relative to at least one straight road, and take bearings to it from several directions during your first noon window.
- **Set a turnaround day based on rations, keeping a full reserve.** Since distances stretch, assume the walk back will take one and a half to two times as long as the walk in.
- **Set warning signs that mean you leave immediately:** a companion missing in the fog, worsening altitude symptoms, shadows lagging more than a half-step, the tapping sounding persistently near, or anyone showing an unusual wish to enter the finished houses.
- **Leave with nothing** you did not bring: no copper pin, no carved pebble, no stone, no plant, not even a handful of grus. Check your boot treads again on the way out, this time to make sure you are not carrying the tableland away.

## Kit List

**Clothing**
- Merino base layers, 2 sets
- Merino mid-layer
- Synthetic insulated jacket
- Breathable hardshell jacket and trousers
- Warm hat, balaclava or buff, liner gloves, insulated mittens (with spares)
- One permanently dry sleeping set
- 4–5 pairs of wool socks, plus waterproof or neoprene socks
- Boots and tall gaiters
- Glacier glasses

**Shelter and Sleep**
- Low, sturdy, well-ventilated tent
- Gravel/snow stakes and anchor bags
- Synthetic sleeping bag rated well below freezing, or down with a vapor-barrier liner
- Closed-cell foam pad plus insulated inflatable pad

**Navigation and Time**
- Compass (tested on arrival)
- Paper maps or blank paper for sketching, pencils
- Waterproof logbook
- Mechanical watch with date, plus a backup
- Star chart
- Whistle, one per person
- Rope for fog travel
- Colored ribbon on your own stakes (optional, removed on exit)

**Food and Water**
- All food: dense, fatty, 4,000–5,000 kcal/day, plus a reserve
- Liquid-fuel or cold-rated stove, fuel, windscreen, pot
- Mesh fog-collector and water bags
- Water filter or purification

**Health**
- Acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine
- Pulse oximeter
- Foot balm, blister kit, general first aid
- High-SPF sunscreen, lip balm
- Waste bags for packing out all human waste

**Biosecurity**
- Cleaning brush for boots and gear before departure and on exit
- Sealed food containers

**Mind**
- Companion
- A small source of music or a recorded voice, for the tent only
- A clear, pre-agreed set of rules, written in the front of your logbook:
  1. Do not chase the tapping.
  2. Do not touch the tools.
  3. Do not enter the finished houses. Do not light the hearths.
  4. Do not walk through the lone doorway.
  5. Do not drink from the rosette crowns.
  6. Do not stack stones.
  7. Leave nothing behind. Take nothing away.
  8. Count the days.

## A Last Word

The Tableland of Unfallen Walls may be one of the gentlest strange places a traveler can visit. Nothing here hunts you. Nothing here hates you. The builders are careful, patient, and busy with work that has nothing to do with you. The danger is almost entirely in how easily a cold, tired, oxygen-starved person might wander a little closer to the tapping, rest a little longer in a doorway, or pick up a warm stone, and slowly be drawn into a pattern that runs on a timescale of centuries.

Go well prepared. Walk slowly. Keep your log. Stay on the roads. Watch your shadow. Let the stone meet stone out there in the fog without you.

And when you leave, know that the city you passed will keep moving across the old stone, rising and falling, long after you are gone, with its hearths laid and waiting for someone who is not you. It knows exactly how much time it has, and it is enough.
