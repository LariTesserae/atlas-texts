# Preparing for the Held Sea of Ammaret

## First, the Honest Summary

Ammaret is not dangerous the way a war zone or a predator-filled jungle is. Nothing there wants to hurt you. The people are gentle, the animals are few and slow, and the water is as warm as your own blood. That gentleness is the danger. Ammaret is soft, dim, drowsy and kind, and it will let you drown politely if you stop paying attention.

You will be an air-breathing animal living as a guest inside another people's breath, in a place with no direct route to the sky. Everything you prepare should serve three goals:

1. **Never run out of air between two pockets of it.**
2. **Never let the drowsiness make your decisions.**
3. **Take as little as possible from a place where everything is scarce and carried by hand.**

If you cannot freedive, cannot stay calm in enclosed spaces, or dislike deep water, do not go. Visit the rim and look at the mist. That alone is worth the climb.

---

## Part One: Are You the Right Person?

Test yourself honestly before you begin training.

- **Claustrophobia.** Much of your time will be spent in small stone domes with water for a floor and no door except the water. Spend a night in a small tent zipped shut. Then imagine that the only exit is underwater. If your chest tightens, take that seriously.
- **Thalassophobia.** You will hang in dim, glowing water with forests swaying around you and no visible bottom or surface. Some people find this the most beautiful thing they have ever seen. Others panic without warning. Find out in a controlled setting first, such as a deep, clear lake with a buddy and a boat.
- **Sense of self.** Visitors report feeling like they are dreaming the whole time. If you have a history of dissociation, sleep disorders, or trouble keeping hold of time, the vent-air may affect you more than it affects others.
- **Physical condition.** You need good cardiovascular fitness, healthy ears and sinuses, and no seizure disorder, heart arrhythmia, or recent lung problems. A punctured eardrum here is a serious, trip-ending injury, and there is no ear surgeon.

## Part Two: Training (Begin Months Ahead)

### Freediving

This is the core skill, and it is not optional. Take a proper freediving course with an instructor, not self-taught breath-holding in a bathtub. You need to learn:

- **Equalization.** You will descend past 40 feet on the rope and move up and down constantly between bells at different depths. Learn the Frenzel technique, not just pinching your nose and blowing. Your ears will be the first thing to fail you.
- **Relaxed static apnea.** Aim to hold your breath calmly for at least two to three minutes. Most passages in the city's heart take seconds, but you want a huge margin. Your surplus is your safety.
- **Dynamic apnea.** Practice swimming horizontally underwater on one breath. That is exactly what moving between bells is.
- **Shallow water blackout.** Learn what it is, why it gives no warning, and why you must never hyperventilate before a breath-hold. Hyperventilating does not add oxygen. It suppresses the urge to breathe, so you pass out before you feel you need air. In Ammaret, blacking out in a passage means dying in a passage.

### Overhead-Environment Thinking

Talk to cave divers, even if you never take up cave diving. Once you are inside the city, you are in what they call an overhead environment. Swimming straight up will not reach air. It will reach rock. Absorb their habits:

- Always know where your last air was.
- Turn back when a third of your capacity is gone. Do not wait for half.
- Never enter a passage you have not seen someone else use.
- Silt and panic kill more people than darkness does.

### Warm-Water Practice

Train in warm pools if you can. Body-temperature water feels wonderful and quietly deceives you. You lose the edge of alertness that cold water gives, and you do not notice yourself tiring. Learn how your body feels when it is relaxed rather than lulled.

### Altitude Acclimatization

The Held Sea sits in the crown of a mountain high enough for fierce sun and thin air. You will climb for days. If possible, spend time at altitude beforehand, and learn the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, confusion, breathlessness at rest. Those symptoms will blur into the vent-air drowsiness later, so know your baseline.

## Part Three: The Most Important Section (Pressure)

The poetic description will not warn you about this, so read it twice.

**The air in the bells is compressed.** A bell sitting 60 feet underwater holds air at nearly three times surface pressure. That air was carried down in bladders from the vents and is held in place by the weight of the sea. When you breathe inside a bell, you are filling your lungs with dense, pressurized gas, like a scuba diver does.

This creates two real dangers.

### 1. Never Hold Your Breath While Rising

If you fill your lungs in a deep bell and then swim upward to a shallower one while holding that breath, the air in your lungs expands. Rise far enough and it can tear your lungs and force bubbles into your blood. This is an arterial gas embolism, and it can kill you within minutes.

- **When moving from a deeper bell to a shallower one, exhale slowly as you rise.** Let a thin stream of bubbles escape. This is the opposite of what your instincts will tell you.
- Moving between bells at the same depth, or downward, is fine on a held breath.
- Ask your hosts which bells are higher and which are lower. The terraces climb the caldera slopes, so the depth differences can be significant.

### 2. Decompression, and Leaving Slowly

If you live for days in deep bells, your body tissues will absorb the gas you breathe, as a saturation diver's do. If you then rise quickly to the surface, and worse, to a surface at high altitude where the air pressure is already low, that dissolved gas can form bubbles in your joints, spine, and brain. That is decompression sickness, "the bends."

- Stay in the **shallowest bells** you reasonably can.
- **Plan your departure as a staged ascent.** Spend your last day or two in progressively shallower bells before going up the rope.
- Rest at the rim for at least a full day before you descend the mountain. Descending is actually safer than climbing higher, but avoid heavy exertion immediately.
- Know the symptoms: joint pain, tingling, itching skin, unusual fatigue, confusion, weakness. If you have them after leaving, descend the mountain gently. Lower altitude means higher air pressure, which helps.
- If you can bring a dive computer or depth gauge from your home world, do so. It is the single most useful object you can carry.

### The Vent-Air Itself

Treat the "thin, drowsy" air with suspicion. That feeling could come from low oxygen, excess carbon dioxide, mild narcosis at depth, or trace volcanic gases. The faintly sour smell of the bells deserves attention. Hydrogen sulfide smells of rot at low concentrations, then deadens your sense of smell at dangerous ones.

- **If a bell's smell suddenly stops, leave.** A smell that seems to have gone away may mean your nose has stopped detecting it, not that the gas has cleared.
- A headache that worsens, rapid breathing, or confusion inside a bell means you need fresh air, meaning a newly filled bell. Ask a breath-carrier.
- If your world allows it, a small **pulse oximeter** and a **personal gas monitor** (for O₂, CO₂, and H₂S) are worth their weight many times over. Keep them in a waterproof case.
- Respect the breath-carriers' knowledge. When they say a bell is "going stale," believe them.

## Part Four: The Climb and the Rim

### The Ascent

- Budget several days through cloud-forest. Expect constant damp, mud, and fog. Everything you own will be wet before you reach the water, and it will stay wet.
- Carry drinking water, or purification for streams on the lower slopes. Above the last goat paths, sources may vanish.
- Pace yourself for altitude. Arriving exhausted means making your first dive exhausted.

### The Rim Itself

The rim is fresh volcanic glass, and it will cut you.

- **Wear sturdy boots with thick soles.** Glassy basalt and burst bubble-hollows have edges like broken bottles, and some patches are still warm.
- **Bring tough gloves** for scrambling. Do not grab the rock bare-handed to steady yourself.
- **Leave the small stones in the bubble-hollows alone.** No one remembers why they are there, which is a reason for more care, not less. Do not move them, pocket them, or add your own unless someone invites you to.
- The light at the surface is diffuse and pewter-grey, but you are at high altitude. Protect your eyes and skin with a hat and covering rather than sunscreen (see below), especially if you wait at the rim for a guide.

### Arrange a Guide Before You Descend

Do not go down the rope alone. Find someone of Ammaret, or someone who trades with them, to meet you at the rim. Approach the descent as you would a stranger's home: you need to be invited and escorted in. The lampmoss trails that show where people walked fade within an hour. You cannot rely on them to find your way back.

## Part Five: Gear

Pack light. Everything you bring must be carried underwater, stored in tiny shared spaces, and never really dry out.

### Essential

- **A low-volume freediving mask.** Underwater vision without a mask is a blur, and in the dim glow you will want every detail. A mask with a flexible nose pocket also makes equalization easier. Bring a spare, since a cracked mask is miserable and irreplaceable.
- **A thin rash guard or light skin suit**, long-sleeved and long-legged. It is not for warmth. The water is body-temperature, and a real wetsuit would overheat you. It protects against sharp new rock, frond abrasion, and the heat near vents.
- **A weight belt with a quick-release buckle.** The water is denser than ordinary water, so you will float more than you expect. Locals use stones. If you use theirs, learn how to drop them instantly.
- **Short fins, or none.** Long freediving fins will tangle in tallstem and churn up the delicate growth. Many visitors find it better to pull themselves along ledges and ropes as locals do. Ask your hosts.
- **A dive computer or depth gauge** (see Part Three).
- **Waterproof dry bags**, small and several, rather than one large one.
- **A thin cord** for tying your belongings to a ledge peg in the bells. Anything you drop into a bell's pool may sink out of reach.

### Medical Kit (Waterproofed)

- **Ear drops** (an alcohol/acetic acid mix) to prevent swimmer's ear. Constant warm immersion is ideal for ear infections, and an ear infection ends your ability to equalize.
- **Wound care for glass cuts:** antiseptic, bandages that stick when wet, skin glue, and a broad-spectrum antibiotic. You have no idea what lives in mineral water with no fish, so treat every cut seriously.
- **Burn gel.** You should not go near the vents, but minor burns happen.
- **Skin balm** for maceration. After days of warm immersion, your skin will wrinkle, soften, and split, especially on your hands and feet.
- **Altitude sickness medication**, used carefully and discussed with a doctor beforehand.
- **No decongestants for diving.** They can wear off mid-dive and cause a painful, dangerous "reverse block" in your ears or sinuses. If you have a cold, do not dive. Wait at the rim.

### Clothing for the Bells

Bring one or two sets of quick-drying clothes. Nothing ever dries fully in the saturated air. Wool that stays warm when damp is good. Cotton will sour and rot. Tie back long hair, because it drifts like smoke underwater and will catch on everything.

## Part Six: What Not to Bring

- **No fire of any kind.** No matches, lighters, candles, or stoves. Flame in a sealed bell burns the air that someone swam up from the deep floor to give you, and volcanic gases can be flammable. This is the most serious taboo you could break without meaning to.
- **No sunscreen, insect repellent, perfume, or scented soap.** These wash into still water that no river flushes, and they linger in enclosed air where everyone breathes them. Cover up with fabric instead.
- **No bright lights.** A small, dim, red-filtered torch for emergencies is reasonable. Shining a white beam through the forests or into a bell would feel like shouting in a temple, and it would ruin everyone's night-adapted eyes, including yours.
- **No loud or rhythmic devices.** No music players or ticking watches. Rhythm has cultural meaning here: it "belongs to things that are going somewhere." A tapping foot or a hummed marching tune would come across as strange, perhaps rude, perhaps restless in a way that unsettles people.
- **No blades for cutting growth.** Carry a small safety cutter for emergency entanglement only. Do not hack through tallstem to clear a path. This is a garden that people have left to itself for a very long time.
- **No foods with strong smells**, and nothing that crumbles into the water.

## Part Seven: Living in the Bells

### Breath Is a Gift

Every breath you draw in Ammaret was carried by hand, in a bladder made from the skin of a named and mourned eel, from the glowing red vents of the deep floor. You are consuming someone's labor every minute you are there. Behave accordingly.

- Keep your visit **short**. A few days is generous. Weeks would be a burden.
- **Stay calm and still.** Exertion burns air. Locals move slowly for a reason, and you should too.
- **Do not crowd a small bell.** Ask where you should sleep.
- **Bring gifts for the breath-carriers.** Offer something useful and non-polluting from outside: fine cord, a good tool, dried food if they eat such things, or something small, beautiful and inert. Ask your guide what would be welcome. Be prepared for the answer to be "nothing," and accept it gracefully.

### Food and Water

The description does not say what the people eat. That in itself is telling: food is probably scarce, local, and not something you should casually share in.

- **Bring your own sealed, compact food** for the whole stay, plus a margin, in something that won't leak or crumble into a bell's pool.
- **Do not drink the sea.** Iron, sulfur, and that "something almost sweet" could mean dissolved minerals, and possibly metals your body does not want. Ask how the people drink. Condensation on bell walls, perhaps? If you can't get a clear answer, carry what you need.
- **Stay hydrated.** Warm immersion makes you urinate more and sweat without noticing. Dehydration also worsens decompression risk.
- Ask quietly about how waste and bodily needs are managed. Every culture has an answer, and it is better to know it than to guess.

### Sleep

You will sleep more than you expect. Let yourself, but:

- **Sleep only in bells your hosts designate as fresh.**
- **Tell someone when you are going to sleep**, so that if you don't wake easily, someone notices.
- **Set yourself a reference point.** Keep a small waterproof notebook and write the date, where you are, and what you did each time you wake. Visitors lose track of time here. The notebook will be your anchor to your own continuity, and it will also be a lovely record of a dream you actually had.

### Movement Between Bells

- **Always move with a guide** until you truly know a route.
- **Watch someone swim a passage before you swim it.** Ask how long it is.
- **Stay in the dense heart of the city.** The long gaps at the edges are for the young and strong of Ammaret, people who grew up breathing this way. You are neither.
- **Exhale when rising** (Part Three, again).
- **Lampmoss shows your trail for about an hour.** That is useful when you are lost and have just come from somewhere. It is useless after a long sleep.
- If you feel the urge to breathe partway through a passage, **keep going if the far side is closer.** Decide which way before you start. At the entrance, know which side is nearer to air and which is your fallback.

## Part Eight: Etiquette and Reverence

- **Speak softly.** Voices pool and blur in the curved bells. Murmur, and let pauses hang.
- **If someone sings a held tone, be still.** Don't join unless invited. Don't hum along to fill the silence. The silence is part of it.
- **Don't touch the soft stone.** New pillow lava that a mason is shaping may be one small stage of eleven years' work. Keep your hands off walls you do not know, even if they look finished.
