# Preparing for Anhel

## First, What It Will Actually Be Like

You will be damp from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave. Not rained on, since there is no rain. You will simply be wet the way a glass of cold water is wet on a summer table. The air in the band where Anhel lives is saturated, and your coat, your hair, your eyelashes, and the backs of your hands will bead over within an hour. Nothing you bring will ever fully dry.

You will be cool. Never cold enough to see your breath clearly, never warm enough to take off a layer and feel relief. It is the temperature of a cellar or a church in spring, forever.

You will be quiet, or you will feel very loud. You will walk along a path that runs sideways across a steep mountain wall. Above you is bare black rock, below you is a white floor that is not a floor, and you are confined to a ribbon of green perhaps forty paces tall. You will not be able to step off that ribbon in either direction without leaving the livable world.

And you will be the only thing in the valley that is changing. That is the most important thing to prepare for, practically and emotionally, and it shapes almost everything below.

---

## Arrival

**Arrive on the Rim Road, ideally at or near one of the four great cairns.** They are landmarks, they are public ground, and they mark places where people expect travelers to pass. Arriving at a random point on the slope means landing on someone's terrace, in someone's moss, or between two houses in the carefully measured gap that the Anheli do not break.

**Do not arrive above the damp band.** The ridges are young basalt, all blades and columns, and descending them is genuinely dangerous: fractured rock, sheer scarps, and talus that will cut you. You would also be arriving dry, in the one zone where nobody lives, and walking down into the city from above, which is not how anyone comes.

**Never, under any circumstances, arrive below the line.** If your method of travel has any imprecision, bias it upward. Landing in the cloud is the one mistake here that may not be survivable, and it is certainly not recoverable in any way the Anheli can help with. Nobody will come in after you.

## Clothing: Dress for Wet That Never Ends

The ordinary instincts of rainy-weather dressing are partly wrong here, because the problem is not water falling on you. The problem is water appearing on you, and on everything, from the air.

**Wool, and more wool.** Wool keeps a good share of its warmth when damp, and you will be damp. The Anheli live in felted goat wool for a reason. Merino base layers, a thick wool mid-layer, wool socks, and a wool hat are ideal.

**No cotton. No down.** Cotton wets through and stays wet, pulling heat off your body steadily in exactly this kind of cool, windless, saturated air. This is how people get hypothermia at temperatures that don't seem dangerous. Down collapses when wet and will become a clammy sack within days. If you want synthetic insulation, choose something that works while wet.

**The outer layer is tricky.** A fully waterproof shell will keep condensation off your wool, but in air this saturated your own sweat won't escape it either, and you'll be wet from the inside. Better options:
- a heavy felted or boiled-wool cloak or coat, like the locals wear;
- oiled wool, or a waxed canvas outer that you can open freely;
- a light shell you put on only when sitting still.

You're managing a slow, constant wetness, not keeping out a storm.

**Bring more socks than seems reasonable.** A fresh pair every day, with a system for keeping the clean ones sealed. More on feet below.

**Consider buying or trading for local clothing once you arrive.** Anheli felt is made for exactly this air, and wearing it will also make you look less alien.

---

## Hands and Feet: The Rock Is Sharp Forever

This is the physical hazard people will underestimate. Stone in Anhel does not weather. Every edge of every broken rock is as sharp as the moment it fractured. Talus slopes are fields of blades. The basalt is glassy. Children here are taught to touch rock with the heel of the hand, never the fingertips, and you should learn this on the first day.

**Hands:**
- Bring leather palm-guards, or sturdy leather gloves with reinforced palms. Cut-resistant gloves of the kind used for glasswork or butchery are worth considering for any scrambling.
- Once you arrive, ask for or trade for proper Anheli palm-guards. They will fit how the locals actually climb.
- Train yourself not to steady yourself on rock with your fingers. Use the heel of your hand, and look before you reach.

**Feet:**
- You need soles that are both **puncture-resistant** and **soft and grippy.** The goats here have soft, wide hooves suited to wet rock, and that tells you what works: soft rubber that molds to wet stone, not hard aggressive lugs that skate on it.
- A stiff midplate or puncture-resistant insole will protect you from shards on the talus.
- **No hobnails, crampons, or metal-shod soles.** They would be slippery on wet basalt, noisy in a place that prizes quiet, and they would scratch carved stone that has kept its crispness for centuries (see "Marks Are Permanent," below).
- Learn to walk flat-footed on talus as the children do. Place your whole foot and weight it deliberately. Don't hop, don't skid, don't scramble.
- Gaiters will help keep your socks drier when you walk through moss and fern.

**Foot care is not optional.** In constant damp, feet macerate: the skin softens, wrinkles, splits, and becomes vulnerable to fungus and infection. Prolonged cold wet feet can progress to immersion foot (trench foot). Every night:
- take your boots off and dry your feet as thoroughly as you can;
- apply foot powder or an anti-chafing balm;
- put on dry socks for sleeping.

Treat a pale, numb, or wrinkled foot as a warning, not an annoyance.

## First Aid for a Place Where Nothing Dries

Cuts are likely. Cuts in constant damp heal slowly and get infected easily. Pack:

- **Fine tweezers and a magnifier** for glass-like basalt slivers.
- **Antiseptic** and plenty of it.
- **Waterproof, breathable dressings** and hydrocolloid bandages.
- **Antifungal cream and powder**, for feet, groin, armpits, and anywhere skin folds and stays wet.
- **Butterfly closures or skin glue** for clean slicing cuts, which the rock will give you.
- A **course of broad-spectrum antibiotics** if your world allows you to carry them, discussed in advance with a physician.
- Something for **gut upset**, since you'll be eating unfamiliar food.
- An **emergency blanket and a hypothermia plan.** Know the signs: shivering that stops, clumsiness, confusion, slurred speech. Know that the treatment is dry insulation and warmth, not "pushing on."

The mountains are young and high-walled. You don't know the valley's altitude. If you are arriving from low elevation, allow for the possibility of mild altitude effects: headache, breathlessness on the stairs, poor sleep.

## Water: The Most Serious Thing You Will Touch

In Anhel, water is not a river you draw from. It is combed out of the air by walls, nets, and ribs of stone, and every household depends on the surface and air around it. The spacing between houses exists because crowded houses rob each other's water. The spacing is marked in stone and it is not broken.

You are a problem in this system. You drink. And your body, your tent, and your gear are all cool, wet surfaces that will condense water that would otherwise have run into someone's gutter.

**Bring as much of your own water as you reasonably can**, or a way to make your own. At the very least, never assume you may draw from a cistern. Ask. Every cistern belongs to a house.

**Purify what you drink.** Condensate is fairly clean, but cisterns and basins hold frogs, moss, runoff from goat-cropped slopes, and whatever else lives in standing water. Filters or tablets are cheap insurance.

**Do not camp in the gaps between houses, and never next to a fog-net.** A pitched tent in the empty stretch between two homes is, to the Anheli, a new house appearing inside the measured distance. It violates the one spacing rule the whole city is built on, and it literally steals condensation from the nets. If you need shelter, ask a warden where travelers are permitted to sleep. The open meeting shelters or granary grounds are likely candidates.

**Never block, divert, step in, or dam a channel.** The ten thousand small troughs along the contour are one unified system. Moving a stone in a trough to make a better footing, or letting your pack sit in a gutter, is tampering with someone's water supply.

**Offer to work.** Wringing the nets into clay jars in the midmorning is daily labor that someone must do. Carrying, mending, and wringing are the most honest ways to repay what you drink. Learn to do it gently. The nets are felted wool on wooden frames with scarce bronze fittings, and they must be kept mended.

## Food

The Anheli eat tubers, climbing beans, bitter greens, a drooping-headed grain, a cress-like herb from the flooded terraces, goat, and snail broth. The granaries are communal, and the whole system is balanced to the number of mouths it has always fed.

- **Bring your own food for most of your stay**, especially anything calorie-dense. You will burn more than you expect staying warm in wet clothing on steep stairs.
- **Accept offered food graciously but modestly.** Being fed from the granary is being taken into the "keeping." It is an honor, not a transaction, and it also draws down a store.
- **Cook snails thoroughly** and eat only snail dishes prepared the local way. Snails can carry parasites. The Anheli have eaten them for generations; your gut has not. Well-boiled broth is a different matter from anything underdone.
- **Wash greens** with purified water, or eat them cooked.
- **Fuel is scarce.** Hearths burn shrub-wood and dried dung, and there's no forest here. If you bring a stove, bring a small, quiet one with its own fuel, and use it only where you have permission. A roaring pressure stove would be both a wasteful and a loud intrusion.
- **Pack out your food waste** unless you are told where it belongs. Nothing here is surplus, and scraps may be precisely someone's compost or someone's goat fodder.

## Sound: Learn to Be Quiet Before You Go

The quiet of Anhel isn't just an absence. The cloud swallows sound, there is no wind and no birdsong, and the dominant sound of the whole city is dripping. The Anheli speak low by habit and tell children the cloud is listening.

You will feel conspicuously loud. Practice before you go:
- **Speak at a murmur.** Practice conversation at the volume you'd use in a hospital at night.
- **Leave behind anything that beeps, chimes, alarms, or plays music.** Silence your watch.
- **Avoid crinkly gear.** Stiff plastic bags and noisy shells rustle in a way that will carry.
- **Do not whistle, do not call out across ravines, do not shout.** This includes emergency whistles, except in a true emergency. Ask a warden what the local signal for distress is. There must be one, and you should use theirs.
- **Soft soles again**: a clattering boot on stone stairs will be heard houses away. The goats pass with "a sound like someone brushing a sleeve." Aim for that.

If you are someone who fills silence, prepare for the discomfort of not doing so. After a few days you will likely find you've started to hear the texture of the place: the irregular ticking of a thousand drips, the wet tearing of grazed moss, a chisel tapping somewhere far down the slope. The quiet becomes spacious rather than oppressive, but only if you stop fighting it.

## The Cloud

This deserves its own section because it will pull at you.

**Do not enter it.** Not a few steps, not a wade, not "just to see." The description is blunt: nobody has gone more than a few steps in and come back willing to talk about it. Take that at face value.

**Do not follow the waders.** You may see one of those tall, wrong-looking elk walk slowly down past the lowest terraces and sink into the white chest-deep, neck-deep, gone. You may see one walk out days later, matted with pale "floor-silt." It will be one of the strangest and most beautiful things you have ever seen, and you will want very badly to know where it goes. Let it go.

**Do not touch the surface.** Not a hand, not a stick. The swifts dip their wingtips; you are not a swift.

**Do not throw anything into it, and tether everything you might drop.** Lens caps, notebooks, a bronze token you were gifted. Near the lowest terraces, anything that rolls away goes down into the cloud and does not come back. Beyond the loss, dropping things into the cloud may carry meanings you don't understand.

**Do not ask about what is at the bottom.** It "is not a question the city asks aloud." Respect that completely. Don't speculate in conversation, don't ask children, don't press the wardens. If someone raises it with you privately, listen far more than you speak.

**No drones, no probes, no weighted lines, no measuring instruments dropped over the edge.** Even if your world has the technology to answer the question, you do not have the right to answer it here.

**Keep well back from the edge on steep slopes.** The lowest terraces are soaked and slick, and a slip on a steep spur may not stop until the white takes you. Use a rope or a hand-line on anything exposed, and walk the lowest terraces only where locals walk them.

## Change Is the Thing They Fear, and You Are Change

The Anheli have no word for weather. The day is the same word every day. Their stories are about keeping. Their one real fear is that the cloud might someday move, even a little.

Everything about your behavior should be governed by a simple principle: **leave nothing different.**

### Marks are permanent

This is the unique hazard of Anhel. Nothing weathers here. The oldest carved lintel still has crisp corners. That means:
- **A scratch you make on stone will be there, sharp and fresh, for as long as the city exists.** An initial carved idly into a wall, a scrape from a boot nail, a chip knocked off a step by a dropped pack, a chalk mark (which may not wash away in a place with no rain) would be permanent in a way they wouldn't be anywhere else.
- Treat every stone surface like a museum object.
- **Never move or touch the carved distance markers on the Rim Road**, or the carved mark at each lowest terrace that shows the cloud's height. Those markers are the city's constitution and its thermometer. Brushing against one is bad; shifting one could be catastrophic to someone's peace of mind.
- Don't stack stones, build cairns of your own, or rearrange anything. The four great cairns are landmarks. A fifth, however small, is a lie in the landscape.

Moss, at least, recovers. Footprints in it will fill back. But stay on the stairs and paths wherever you can. The moss has settled at its depth for a reason.

### Biological hygiene

This ecosystem is sealed and balanced "as if counted." You could unbalance it. Before you travel:

- **Clean your boots, gaiters, trekking poles, and pack thoroughly.** Scrub out every seed, soil crumb, and fleck of mud from your home world. Consider disinfecting.
- **Be especially careful about frogs.** Frogs sit in every cistern and basin. Amphibian fungal diseases have devastated frog populations in worlds that have them, and they travel on wet gear and hands. Don't handle the frogs, and don't dip your hands or gear into basins. Disinfect anything that's been in water elsewhere before you come.
- **Bring no seeds, no plants, no fresh produce, no live food, no pets.** A single invasive weed on these terraces could be a disaster generations in the making, and the Anheli would have no concept of how to fight it.
- **Handle no goats, hares, or snails unnecessarily**, and never feed wild animals.
- **Don't touch the tree-ferns by the doors.** Each one was planted by the first person to live in that house, and none has ever died. They grow too slowly for anyone to see them change. Do not be the first thing that ever hurt one.

### Heat and fire

Your campfire or stove warms the air, however slightly, in a place whose entire life depends on air staying precisely cool and saturated. Physically, a small stove is insignificant. Symbolically, a big blaze drying out the rock around it would look like exactly the kind of thing they dread. Keep any heat small, brief, and sanctioned.

### What you say

Be careful telling stories of change. Floods, droughts, seasons, glaciers retreating, sea levels rising, storms, a world where clouds race across the sky. To the Anheli these may range from incomprehensible to deeply distressing.

You don't have to lie about where you come from. But lead with things that fit their frame: the keeping of houses, the mending of things, the tending of gardens. Let them ask before you describe a world where the ground changes under people's feet. Never make a joke about the cloud rising or falling.

If you find yourself discussing seasons, understand you are describing something like magic, or like horror.

### Technology and trade

Metal is scarce. There's a little bronze, cast in a few molds and traded along the rim. It's used for chisels, needle points, and net fittings. This means **your steel is enormously valuable, and potentially disruptive.**

- A single steel knife, left behind, could reorder the prestige and trade of a whole spur.
- A packet of steel needles could change who mends nets best, and who owes what to whom.

Think hard before gifting metal. If you want to give, give things that fit the existing economy or that get consumed:
- help and labor;
- a song sung quietly, a story told low;
- perhaps food that will be eaten;
- perhaps salt, since there is no sea and nothing here suggests a salt source, though ask discreetly first whether it is precious or even welcome.

Accept Anheli gifts (a clay vessel, a felted item, a flaked-obsidian blade) with care. Obsidian is sharper than steel and brittle. Wrap it.

**Leave most of your electronics at home.** Humidity will kill them, desiccant packs will be saturated within a day, and a glowing screen or camera flash will be alien in a way that follows you around the rim. If you must bring a camera, seal it in dry bags with fresh desiccant, keep it silent, and ask before photographing people or homes.

For writing, bring a **pencil and waterproof paper.** Ink will run on anything in Anhel, and paper that isn't treated will turn to pulp.

## Social Conduct Along the Rim

Anhel is a city, not a village. It has common law, wardens for each spur, communal granaries, and a single road connecting every house to every other. Behave as you would entering any city with strong civic order, except that the order here is spread along miles of mountainside.

- **Present yourself to the warden of whatever spur you arrive on.** Ask where you may walk, sleep, and draw water, and what you must not do. Rules probably vary slightly by spur. Ask at each one.
- **Approach houses along the Rim Road.** Doors face the cloud, so you'll often be approaching from above or from the side. Pause somewhere visible and wait to be noticed rather than walking straight up to the door or onto a terrace.
- **Respect the distance.** The measured gap between houses is sacred practice as much as practical necessity. Don't loiter, rest, or set down your pack in the middle of a gap as though claiming it. Pass through.
- **Walk the rim in the flow of the road.** It's the city's only continuous street, often a narrow stair on a steep face. Step aside for goats, who come home at dusk without being called and have more right to the path than you do. Step aside for anyone carrying jars or baskets of soil.
- **Witness, don't interrupt, the morning check.** Each morning someone from every house walks to the lowest terrace, looks at the cloud against the carved mark, nods, and goes back up. It may be the single most important act in Anheli life. If you're invited to watch, stand back and quiet. Don't comment. Certainly don't say "it looks the same," as though there were any question. Just nod, if they nod.
- **The ravens are watching.** They sit in pairs on house-peaks with "patient ownership." Don't chase or feed them. Whatever their standing is in Anheli thought, assume it is not nothing.
- **Children** will likely be curious and hushed. Don't encourage them to shout, don't do anything loud to entertain them, and don't tell them the cloud isn't listening.

## Time and the Mind

The light rises grey-gold at the same angle every morning, brightens to an even ordinary daylight, and fades at the same hour. Nothing grows perceptibly. There are no seasons. Every day is the same word.

This is harder on a visitor than it sounds.

- **Keep a written log, dated by your own count.** You will lose track faster than you believe. Without seasons, weather, or even varying day-length, days fold into each other.
- **Set your departure date before you arrive**, and commit to it with someone outside Anhel if possible. There's a real risk of simply... staying. Not because anything traps you, but because nothing ever gives you a reason to decide.
- **Expect a range of emotional responses.** Some travelers will find Anhel profoundly restful: a world with no bad news, no weather, no surprises, where the only work is keeping. Others will find it airless and eerie, a place where they are the only moving thing, where even the animals balance "as if counted." Many will feel both in alternating hours.
- **Bring something from home that changes.** Perhaps a book you haven't read, or letters to open on particular days. Novelty is the one thing Anhel does not provide, and a small private supply of it may keep you steady.
- **Low grey light and constant damp can weigh on mood.** If you're prone to seasonal depression, consider bringing a vitamin D supplement and planning a short stay.
- **You may sleep strangely.** The dripping never stops. Some find it lulling. Others lie awake counting ticks. Earplugs may help, though some travelers report that silence is worse.

Understand, too, that you will age a little while you are there, will wear through socks, will grow your hair, will gain calluses on your palms. You will go home changed. The Anheli will not see it, because you'll be gone, but you might carry a peculiar tenderness for anything that stays.

## Packing List Summary

**Clothing**
- Merino/wool base layers (several)
- Thick wool sweater or felted mid-layer
- Wool or felted cloak/coat, or oiled wool outer; light breathable shell for rest stops
- Wool hat
- Many pairs of wool socks, sealed in dry bags
- Gaiters

**Hands and feet**
- Puncture-resistant soft-rubber-soled boots (no metal)
- Leather palm-guards and/or cut-resistant gloves
- Foot powder, anti-chafing balm, antifungal cream

**Water and food**
- As much water as is practical, plus purification
- Calorie-dense food for the length of your stay
- Small quiet stove with own fuel (use only with permission)
- Waste bags to pack everything out

**Safety and health**
- First-aid kit weighted toward cuts: fine tweezers, magnifier, antiseptic, waterproof dressings, closures
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics if permitted
- Gut medicine
- Emergency blanket
- A short rope or hand-line for exposed terraces
- Tethers for anything you might drop

**Gear**
- Dry bags and desiccant
- Pencil and waterproof notebook
- Silent watch
- Quiet, non-rustling pack
- Earplugs (optional)

**Leave at home**
- Cotton and down clothing
- Hobnails, crampons, metal-soled footwear
- Drones and probes
- Seeds, plants, fresh produce, pets
- Loud or chiming electronics
- Surplus steel to give away

**Before departure**
- Clean and disinfect all gear, especially anything that's touched water
- Fix your departure date
- Practice speaking softly

## A Last Word

You will likely spend your days walking a path that curves forever around a white lake, among soft grey goats and silent frogs, while swifts draw lines across the cloud and close them again. At dusk the goats will come home on their own. In the morning someone will look at the mark, nod, and go back up to wring the nets.

The whole purpose of your preparation is that, when you leave, that is still all that happens. No new scratch in the stone, no new weed on the terrace, no fungus in the basins, no steel knife rewriting the rim, no child who's learned to shout. The line kept.

If you manage that, the Anheli may think of you, if at all, not as a change but as a brief, quiet exception. That is the best a visitor can hope to be here.
