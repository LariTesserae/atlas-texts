# Preparing to Visit the Terraces Above the Cloud Line

## First, What You're Actually Walking Into

Most travelers prepare for this place as if it were a mild, pleasant highland, and in one sense it is. Nothing here will freeze you, burn you in a storm, or chase you. No predators, no venomous insects, no weather events. The temperature sits like a warm bath that has cooled a little.

But the plateau makes three demands, and preparation is really about meeting them:

1. **Everything is wet, always, and nothing will ever dry.** This includes you.
2. **The ground is new stone.** It is sharp, hard, slick, and unforgiving, and there are sheer drops with nothing but cloud below.
3. **The place is in balance, and you are the most disruptive thing that has arrived in a long time.** You are a walking ecosystem entering a landscape with no animals, a loud body entering a silent one, and a hurried person entering a place where nothing hurries.

The first two are about keeping yourself intact. The third is about keeping the place intact, and it matters more.

---

## 1. Biosecurity: The Most Important Preparation

This plateau has no animals. No insects, no mites visible in the moss, no birds, nothing in the pools. The flowers stay open forever because nothing has ever pollinated them. That makes the place unusually vulnerable to what you might carry in.

Consider a single fruit fly, a few aphids, a pregnant spider in a boot seam, or a seed stuck in Velcro. On an island with no predators, competitors, or parasites, one introduction could spread without check. Worse, an introduced pollinator could start the trees fruiting, change which plants spread, and rewrite the plateau's green map of its water. You would have ended something that has held still for centuries.

### Before you go

- **Clean every piece of gear**, and inspect it the way you would for a sensitive island reserve. Turn pockets inside out. Pick out boot treads with a stiff brush and a probe. Check seams, hook-and-loop fasteners, pack mesh, and laces.
- **Freeze soft goods** (clothing, bags, sleeping gear) for several days if you can, or heat-treat them, to kill hidden eggs and mites.
- **Wear new or thoroughly laundered clothing.** Don't wear anything you have recently worn through fields, forests, or gardens.
- **Deal with your own passengers.** Get checked for lice. Make sure you haven't been anywhere with bedbugs. Don't bring a pet, ever, for any reason.
- **Bring no fresh food.** No fruit, vegetables, nuts in shells, seeded bread, flowers, soil, or potted anything. Seeds hide in unlikely places: poppy seeds on a roll, a berry, a tomato.
- **Bring no wood.** No untreated wooden tool handles, walking sticks, or souvenirs from elsewhere.

### While you're there

- **Pack out all your waste**, including human waste. Use sealed waste bags. Ask your hosts what they do; they may have a system, but assume it isn't meant for foreign bodies. Everything on this plateau drains into drinking water or over the western rim into the cloud.
- **Don't wash in the pools.** Soap, sunscreen, skin oils, and your microbial film will enter water that "stays as it is." Pools that never drain also never flush.
- **Don't handle the flowers.** You would only be the first pollinator the plateau has ever had.

Your gut and skin microbes can't be left behind. The residents presumably live with their own, so this is less alarming than it sounds. The goal is to avoid adding anything *new and multicellular*.

## 2. Living Permanently Wet

The air sits at the edge of saturation. Stone weeps. Your skin will be damp within an hour of arrival and will stay that way. Your pack will be damp the next morning, and so will your sleeping bag, and so will the inside of your tent. There is no sunny afternoon when you can spread things out to dry. Plan for **managing wetness**, not avoiding it.

### Clothing

- **Choose fabrics that work wet**: merino wool, or synthetics designed to stay comfortable damp. Avoid cotton entirely. Denim will become a cold, chafing weight, and in this humidity even a mild evening will feel clammy in wet cotton.
- **Wear light layers.** It is warm, so you don't need insulation for cold. You need layers that reduce the clammy feeling and protect your skin from stone.
- **A rain jacket is nearly useless for its usual purpose.** There's no rain to shed. In this humidity it will simply trap sweat. At most bring a very light, highly breathable shell for the rare breeze, or none.
- **Bring more socks than seems reasonable.** Seal them in waterproof bags and treat them as a consumable.

### Your body

Constant damp is hard on skin:

- **Trench foot and maceration** are a real risk on longer stays. Your feet will sit in damp boots for days. Change socks at least twice daily. At night, wipe your feet as dry as you can, apply a barrier balm, and sleep with them bare in a dry-bagged sleeping area if you can manage it.
- **Fungal infections** thrive in warm damp. Bring antifungal cream; powders turn to paste here.
- **Chafing** will find every seam. Bring anti-chafe balm and use it before problems start.
- **Wounds heal slowly in constant moisture.** More on that below.

### Your equipment

- **Put everything in dry bags**, and keep the important things double-bagged with desiccant packs. Know that the desiccant will saturate within days, so bring spares.
- **Electronics** will suffer from condensation, especially if you move them between a sealed bag and the open air. Bring only what's sealed and rated for immersion, or leave it at home. Lenses will fog constantly.
- **Paper will fail.** Bring waterproof notebooks and pencils; ink runs. A mechanical waterproof watch is more reliable than anything with a screen.
- **Anything that rusts will rust.** Carbon steel knives, cheap zippers, and uncoated metal will all corrode. Prefer stainless, titanium, and plastic.

## 3. The Stone: Feet, Hands, and Edges

This is the main physical danger, and it is easy to underestimate because the landscape looks so calm.

The basalt is young and unweathered. Flow edges are as sharp as broken glass or bottle-shards. Lava-skin wrinkles can shred fabric and skin. The whole surface is either bare wet stone, which may carry an invisible algal film, or moss-covered, which is slick and hides what lies underneath. Nothing has been ground into soft footing. There is no dirt, no sand, and no forgiving scree slope to slide down safely. You are either on solid stone or falling onto it.

### Footwear

You need boots that are:

- **Durable.** Fresh lava destroys light trail shoes quickly. Look for a thick, cut-resistant sole and a protective rand around the toe and sides.
- **Grippy when wet.** Soft, sticky rubber, like approach or canyoneering shoes, is better than hard lugs on slick stone.
- **Quiet.** This is where the plateau asks something unusual. Hard soles and especially metal cleats or hobnails will ring on the basalt with a faint glassy note, and in this silence that ring carries. Soft rubber is both safer and more polite.
- **Well-draining.** They will get soaked, so let them drain rather than hold water like buckets.

Break them in fully before you go. Blisters on constantly wet feet are miserable and slow to heal.

### Hands

- **Bring gloves**: thin leather or cut-resistant synthetic, the kind you would use for scrambling. You will reach for the rock to steady yourself, and the rock will cut you.
- **Knee protection** is worth considering if you expect to sit, kneel, or scramble. Soft pads under trousers are enough.

### Wounds

- **Bring a proper first-aid kit** with wound-closure strips, antiseptic, waterproof dressings in several sizes, and a way to irrigate cuts. Expect minor lacerations. Keep them covered and clean, and watch them; wet wounds soften and resist closing.
- **Don't bleed into the pools** or the channels. Treat those as someone's drinking water, because they are.
- **There is no medical care as you know it.** The residents may have their own knowledge, but plan to be self-sufficient. Your real evacuation route is whatever brought you here, so know how to reach it quickly.

### The eastern cliff

Several hundred meters of vertical, water-sheened columns, ending in cloud. The rim has no railing, no gentle approach, and no slope to catch you. The rock at the lip is exactly as wet and slick as the rock everywhere else.

- **Stay well back from the edge.** A few body lengths is not too cautious. The view is just as extraordinary from a safe distance.
- **Don't lie on your belly to peer over** unless you are anchored. People do this to see the columns drop into white, and it is where accidents come from.
- **Nobody is coming to get you from below.** No one knows what's down there.

### The western cloud margin

On the western side, the town's lowest terraces meet the cloud-sea directly. It is tempting to walk into it. **Don't.** Inside the cloud you'll lose all visual reference within a few steps, the stone is still sharp and wet, and you have no idea whether the ground continues or drops. The cloud looks like a floor, but it isn't one. If you are curious, reach an arm into it from a secure spot and let that be enough.

## 4. Light, Sun, and Seeing

You're above the clouds. The sun hits you directly, and the cloud-sea below reflects it back up. The wet stone and the pools add their own glare.

### Sun protection

- **You'll be lit from below as well as above.** A wide-brimmed hat protects your scalp and the top of your face, but not the underside of your chin, nose, ears, or neck. This is the same way people burn on snowfields and glaciers.
- **Cover up rather than relying on sunscreen.** Long sleeves, a neck gaiter, and a brimmed hat do most of the work. Sunscreen will run in the humidity. Worse, whatever washes off you ends up in water that never drains. If you use it, choose a mineral formula and apply it carefully, away from pools and channels.
- **Wear sunglasses with good UV protection.** Polarized lenses help twice over: they cut glare off the wet stone, and they let you see into the pools and spot the edges of submerged rock.

### Flat light

With light coming from above and below, shadows are faint. This is beautiful, and it also makes it harder to read terrain. The crisp edges and small drops that shadows usually reveal disappear into an even, glossy sheen. You may misjudge a step or put your foot on a sharp edge you didn't see.

- Slow down on unfamiliar ground.
- Use your eyes and your feet together. Test footing before committing weight.
- Consider trekking poles with **rubber tips**, never bare metal. They help with balance and let you probe ahead, and rubber tips keep them quiet and stop them scratching the stone.

### Night

The sun still sets. With little wind and no animals, night here may be profoundly dark and profoundly quiet. Bring a headlamp with a dim red setting. A bright white beam will feel like shouting, and it wrecks your night vision on terrain where you need it. Better yet, **don't move around at night at all.** Pick your sleeping spot in daylight and stay there.

## 5. Water, Food, and What You Owe Your Hosts

### Water

The plateau's water is condensate, which is almost distilled. It's probably very clean, though that isn't a promise; treat it or filter it if your stomach is sensitive. The bigger concerns are these:

- **It's someone's water.** The cisterns and channels are the town's plumbing, built and maintained by hand. Ask before drawing from them. Some pools may belong to a household. Assume nothing is free for the taking until someone tells you otherwise.
- **It's mineral-poor.** Drinking only condensate for days, especially while you sweat in the humidity, can leave you short on electrolytes. **Bring electrolyte tablets or salts** and use them daily. Headaches, cramps, and lethargy are early signs.
- **Draw water gently**, with a cup, not by plunging a bottle into a pool and stirring it up.

### Food

**Bring all your own food, and bring more than you think you need.**

Consider what this place can grow: moss, ferns, and a few rare, widely spaced trees whose flowers are never pollinated and may never fruit. There are no animals, so there is no meat, egg, milk, or honey. Whatever the residents eat, and it's worth wondering about, it cannot be plentiful. A foreigner eating their food is taking from a supply that may barely sustain them. If they offer, accept a small, polite amount. Don't ask.

Don't forage. Many ferns are toxic, some seriously, and you can't know which are safe here. Even if some are edible, you'd be stripping plants that took decades to find their foothold.

Choose food that:

- Contains **no viable seeds** and no fresh produce (see Biosecurity).
- Is **sealed and moisture-proof**, since anything open will go soft, then moldy, alarmingly fast.
- Needs **no cooking**, or can be cooked with a small, quiet stove. Fire is a bad idea: wood is scarce and precious, and a stove's roar or smoke would be intrusive. The warm climate makes cold rations entirely pleasant.
- Is **low-odor**. With no wind, smells linger and don't disperse. Strong food smells will hang around you and your camp.

Pack out every wrapper, crumb, and scrap.

## 6. Shelter and Sleep

The ground is solid basalt. **Tent stakes won't go in.** Your options:

- **A freestanding tent**, weighted with your pack or anchored with cord to your own gear. **Don't pry up stones for anchors.** A loose rock may be part of a wall, a channel lip, or a platform somewhere.
- **A bivy sack**, which may be simplest. There's no rain and little wind, so what you really need is a moisture barrier underneath and some privacy.
- **A hammock is a bad idea.** Trees here are rare, each "an event in the landscape," and their roots cling over the stone like poured wax. Don't hang your weight from them.

**Don't sleep on moss.** It looks like an ideal mattress, soft and springy, but the moss cushions may be centuries old and "no one alive has seen it spread." One night of your weight could crush something that took generations to grow and will take generations to recover. Sleep on bare rock with a thick, closed-cell or inflatable pad and a waterproof groundsheet.

Better still, **ask the residents** where a visitor may rest. They may have a place for guests, or at least a view on where your presence would be least disruptive.

Accept that your sleeping bag will be damp by morning. Synthetic insulation copes with this far better than down. Given the warmth, a light synthetic quilt or even a sleeping-bag liner may be enough.

## 7. Sound and Motion: How to Behave

On the plateau, you are loud and you are fast. That describes you now, before you've done anything wrong.

A voice sounds loud here. A footstep rings. You can hear a single drop fifty paces off. In a landscape where the only other movement is water, a person walking briskly stands out the way a siren would in a library.

### Practice before you go

It may sound silly, but spend some time rehearsing:

- **Speaking softly.** Practice speaking at a level that would carry across a small room, and no more.
- **Moving slowly.** Walk at half your normal pace for an hour and notice how hard it is. You'll need that pace here, for safety on the stone and out of courtesy.
- **Being still.** Sit for thirty minutes without looking at a screen, speaking, or fidgeting. The plateau asks this of you constantly.

### On arrival

- **Leave behind anything that beeps, chimes, buzzes, or clicks.** Put your devices on silent, or better, don't bring them. No speakers. No drones, which would be the first flying thing many people here have ever seen.
- **Keep zippers, buckles, and gear quiet.** Pad anything that clanks. Tape your trekking poles, and tape metal items that knock together.
- **Walk softly** on bare basalt, and softer still on the stairs.

## 8. The Town and Its People

### Recognizing the town

At first, you might not realize you're in a town. The houses are far apart, the streets are long lines of fitted columns running across open rock, and a single path may pass no doorway for a long while. Don't mistake that spaciousness for emptiness or abandonment. **Assume everything built is in use and cared for.** A platform with nothing on it may be a public space. A stretch of stone that seems empty may be the space that belongs to a house.

### What not to touch

The town is built without mortar. The fitted hexagons stay in place because they fit well, and because no one disturbs them.

- **Don't move, lift, pry at, or sit on loose-looking stones.** You can't tell which are structural.
- **Don't step in, on, or across the channels** if a crossing is provided, and don't let anything fall into them. The channels are the town's circulatory system: they carry water from roof gutters to cisterns, terrace to terrace, all the way to the western rim. A blocked or cracked channel is real damage, and repairing it means someone kneeling with a chisel for a long time.
- **Don't lean on the weeping walls, put your hands into the cisterns, or wade in pools.**
- **Stay on the laid paths** whenever there are any. They protect the moss, spare you the sharpest stone, and keep you out of private spaces.

### Approaching people

The residents are few, slow, and quiet, and each house stands alone. That tells you a lot.

- **They seem to value space.** Approach from a distance, in the open, where you can be seen coming. Don't come up behind someone or appear suddenly around a wall.
- **Stop at a respectful distance** and let them decide whether to close it. A distance that seems formal to you may feel intimate to them.
- **Match their pace.** If they're slow to respond, wait. Silence here is not rudeness. Don't fill it.
- **Don't interrupt work.** Someone mending a channel or sitting by a pool is doing something, even if it looks like nothing. Wait, or pass by quietly.
- **Learn a few gestures of greeting** if you can, and if you can't, keep your hands visible, your voice low, and your movements slow.

### Gifts

Think carefully before giving anything. Your gift could change their world more than you intend.

- **Don't give powered tools, machines, or anything that makes noise.** These would be disruptive, and their way of life may depend on things being as they are.
- **Don't give seeds, plants, animals, or wood,** for all the reasons above.
- **Think about what they lack.** Bronze exists here, yet copper and tin don't come from young basalt. So either they have trade with somewhere, or they have ancestral stock they can't replace. Metal may be precious. So may salt, which condensate doesn't provide and a fern diet wouldn't supply. A small, modest gift of something like that might be deeply appreciated. It might also be inappropriate in ways you can't predict. **Ask, or watch, before you give.**
- **Labor may be the best gift.** If they're willing and you're careful, you could help carry, hold, or fetch. Take direction and do exactly what you're shown, slowly.

Above all, **don't try to speed anything up.** Don't suggest improvements, efficiencies, or innovations. They have "found the one way to live here." That is their knowledge, and you're a guest.

## 9. The Stillness and Your Mind

People seldom prepare for this part, and it's the part that most affects them.

### What happens

Most people's senses are always sampling motion: birds at the edge of vision, insects, leaves stirring, wind, traffic, other people. You don't notice this until it stops. On the plateau it stops. The only things that move are water and the occasional distant person.

Visitors report some of the following:

- **Heightened awareness of their own bodies.** You may hear your heartbeat, your breathing, the blood in your ears. If you have tinnitus, it may become loud.
- **A feeling of unreality.** The landscape seems too still, too clean, too perfectly held, as if it were painted or paused. This can be euphoric or unsettling, and sometimes both.
- **Loss of time sense.** The temperature barely changes. Shadows are faint. The cloud never moves. Many visitors lose track of hours, then days. Some find this restful. Others find it frightening.
- **Restlessness.** Sometimes this includes a strong urge to make noise or do something just to break the stillness.
- **Deep calm.** Many people, once the first adjustment passes, find something they didn't know they were missing.

### What to do

- **Keep a waterproof mechanical watch and a simple log.** Write down the time you wake, the time you sleep, and what you did each day. This anchors you.
- **Set your departure date before you arrive,** and keep it. The stillness can make it hard to decide to leave, or hard to stay. A fixed date spares you the decision.
- **Keep your first visit short.** A day or two is plenty. Come back longer if you want to.
- **Travel with someone** if you can. A companion to speak quietly with is a great comfort, but choose someone who can also be silent.
- **Have a calm practice ready.** Breathing exercises, meditation, or simply something to do with your hands (a small sketchbook, a bit of knotwork) will help if the stillness starts to press on you.

## 10. The Question Nobody Can Answer

The plateau has no animals. The simplest explanation is isolation: a young stone island above a cloud-sea that nothing winged has crossed. But the description leaves it open: *"Perhaps something else explains it."*

You are an animal.

This isn't cause for panic. People live here, and apparently live well. But a sensible traveler notices when something unexplained points at them. So:

- **Pay attention to your body.** Unusual fatigue, headaches, or symptoms that don't match the obvious causes (dehydration, electrolytes, sun, exertion) are worth noting. If something feels wrong and you can't explain it, leave.
- **Ask the residents, gently, if the moment comes.** How long have they lived here? Have others come? What happened to them? They may know things that aren't visible.
- **Keep your exit ready.** Know exactly how to reach your departure point, and don't let yourself get more than half a day from it.

---

## 11. Leaving

- **Take nothing.** Not a flake of basalt, a sprig of fern, or a scrap of moss. The stone is sharp and new, and their town is built from it. Every piece belongs where it is.
- **Pack out everything you brought:** waste, wrappers, spent desiccant, used dressings, all of it.
- **Check your gear before you go**, the same way you did when you arrived. Moss spores and fern fragments may have hitched a ride. Clean your boots and clothes before you step into anywhere else.
- **Say goodbye quietly,** if you've been welcomed. Don't make a ceremony of it unless they do.

## Packing List Summary

**Clothing**
- Merino or synthetic layers, no cotton
- Many pairs of socks, dry-bagged
- Long sleeves and trousers for sun and stone protection
- Wide-brimmed hat and neck gaiter
- Durable, sticky-soled, quiet boots, well broken in
- Thin leather or cut-resistant gloves
- Optional soft knee pads

**Protection and health**
- Polarized UV sunglasses
- Mineral sunscreen, used sparingly and away from water
- Comprehensive first-aid kit with wound closure and waterproof dressings
- Antifungal cream, anti-chafe balm, foot barrier balm
- Electrolyte tablets
- Water filter or treatment
- Any personal medications, with extra, sealed

**Gear**
- Dry bags, several sizes, with spare desiccant
- Freestanding tent or bivy sack
- Thick sleeping pad and waterproof groundsheet
- Light synthetic sleeping bag or quilt
- Trekking poles with rubber tips
- Headlamp with dim red setting
- Waterproof mechanical watch
- Waterproof notebook and pencils
- Sealed waste bags for all waste, including human waste

**Food**
- All your own supply, plus reserve
- Sealed, moisture-proof, no viable seeds, no fresh produce
- Low-odor, no-cook or quiet-cook

**Leave behind**
- Pets, plants, seeds, fresh food, wood
- Anything loud, metal-soled, or powered
- Speakers, drones, alarms
- Any hurry you brought with you

## A Last Word

People who return from the Terraces tend to describe the same moment. They're standing somewhere on the plateau, maybe on a terrace, maybe near the eastern rim (a safe distance back). They've finally stopped moving, and they hear one drop fall into a pool fifty paces away. Then they realize it's the only thing happening, anywhere, for as far as they can see.

Everything in this guide exists to get you to that moment safely and to let you leave without having changed it. The place asks nothing of you except care, slowness, and quiet. If you can give it those, it gives you something back that is hard to find anywhere else.
