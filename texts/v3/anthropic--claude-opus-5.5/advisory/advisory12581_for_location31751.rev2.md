# Preparing for the Breathing Quarter

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

The Breathing Quarter is not a ruin. It is a working machine that happens to be empty of people. Everything that makes it beautiful also makes it dangerous: the heat, the vents, the resin, the wind, the music. Nothing here is hostile. It simply ignores you, and you are the only wet, soft, oxygen-dependent thing in it.

Three things will try to hurt you:

1. **The air.** It is hot, bone-dry, and probably chemically wrong.
2. **The breathing.** Twice a day it brings a gale, a deafening sound, and a surge of fresh vent gas.
3. **The ground.** It hides hot fissures under plants, is overhung by a ceiling that sheds stone, and is scattered with small explosives.

Everything below follows from these.

---

## Before You Go Down: Watch It for a Day

Do not descend on your first day. Spend a full cycle on the salt flat above.

- **Finding the crack.** It is marked only by shimmering air, which is nearly invisible at midday when the whole flat shimmers. A cheap thermal camera will find it instantly, and so will looking at dawn, when the flat is cool and the crack is not. Once you find it, drop a GPS pin and plant a tall, bright marker. Salt flats offer no landmarks, and you do not want to lose the only way out.
- **Timing the breathing.** Sit near the crack and note when the outflow starts: when grit begins streaming upward, the smell of scorched sugar arrives, and you may hear the city faintly through the rock. Note when it stops. It should happen roughly at the morning and evening temperature transitions, but you need the real times for this place, this season. Your whole plan depends on them.
- **Sampling the air first.** Lower a multi-gas meter on a line into the throat before you send yourself. Leave it there through one breathing and look at the peaks.

## The Air

### Heat
The heat pools and rises from glowing rock, and "skin tightens within minutes" is a warning. Expect temperatures well above body heat, possibly far above, with strong radiant heat coming from *below* as well as from the air.

- **Cooling vest** with phase-change packs rated around 15–18°C. Ice melts too fast. Bring spare packs and keep them in an insulated bag.
- **Clothing:** loose, full-coverage, light-coloured, natural or flame-resistant fibres. No synthetics that melt. Reflective outer layers on the lower legs help, because the heat comes up from the floor.
- **Boots** with thick, heat-rated soles, like the ones foundry and wildfire workers use. The floor is warm everywhere and hot near the fissures. Thin trail runners will soften.
- **Gloves** at all times. Sun-warmed stone is one thing. Vent-warmed stone is another.
- **Work-rest cycles.** Decide your exposure limit before you go, and stick to it when the place is enchanting you. A wearable core-temperature sensor, or at least heart-rate monitoring, is worth carrying. If your heart rate stays high while resting, you leave.

### Dryness
Nothing here has ever been wet, and it will pull moisture out of you aggressively.

- **Water:** bring all of it, because there is none to find. Plan on at least a litre per hour of exposure plus a large reserve, with electrolytes in every bottle. Drink on a schedule. You will not feel thirsty in time.
- **Eyes:** wraparound sealed goggles. Preservative-free eye drops. No contact lenses; they will fuse to your eyes.
- **Nose and mouth:** saline nasal gel, heavy lip balm, and a fabric buff you can breathe through, dampened if you can spare the water. Expect nosebleeds.
- **Skin:** a thick occlusive balm on hands and face before you descend. Cracked fingertips are a real risk, and cracked skin plus resin is miserable.

### Gas
Treat this seriously even though the description sounds gentle. The plants feed on sulphur compounds and hydrocarbons seeping from the fissures. That means some of these are likely present:

- **Hydrogen sulphide**, which smells of rotten eggs at low levels and paralyses your sense of smell at dangerous ones. The pleasant scorched-sugar smell could mask it. Do not trust your nose here.
- **Carbon dioxide and heavy vapours.** These are denser than air and **pool in low places**. The streets are sunken channels, knee- to waist-deep. They are ideal gas traps.
- **Low oxygen near the vents.** Glowing rock sits next to hydrocarbon seeps and nothing is on fire. One likely explanation is that the air right at the vents lacks the oxygen to burn. It would also lack the oxygen to keep you conscious.
- **Flammable vapour.** If oxygen *is* adequate somewhere, you are walking through a place where fuel meets ignition.

Equipment:

- **A four-gas meter** (oxygen, H₂S, carbon monoxide, flammable-gas limit), ideally with CO₂ as well. Clip it at chest height and carry a second one at knee height, or check low regularly. The street floors may read very differently from your face.
- **A half-face respirator** with combination acid-gas and organic-vapour cartridges plus particulate filters, for resin dust, grit, and pollen. Understand its limit: a filter respirator does nothing for low oxygen.
- **An escape set.** A short-duration supplied-air escape hood or bottle, carried by every person, every time.
- **No open flame of any kind.** No lighters, stoves, matches, or smoking. You are surrounded by oil-soaked plants and hydrocarbon seeps. Use intrinsically safe (non-sparking) lamps if you can get them.

**A rule of thumb the city gives you for free:** the tallest, lushest plants grow directly over the vents. Lush growth marks the richest gas, the hottest ground, and the worst air. Admire it from a distance.

## The Breathing

This is why you came, and it is the most dangerous hour of the day.

### What happens
- A strong updraft pulls toward the crack in the ceiling at the far end, drawing from every street at once.
- **Fresh gas surges up from the fissures.** Expect your gas readings to spike exactly when you are least able to hear alarms or talk.
- The sound becomes overwhelming, louder than a shout, in an enclosed stone room that throws it back at you. Realistically that means hearing-damage levels, plus infrasound from the deep drones.
- Grit, pollen, and resin dust are all airborne at once.

### How to prepare
- **Double hearing protection:** foam earplugs *and* over-ear muffs. This is not optional. An hour of this unprotected could cost you hearing permanently.
- **A vibrating gas alarm**, or one worn where you can see it, because you will not hear a beep.
- **Hand signals**, agreed and practised on the surface. At minimum: *I'm fine / something's wrong / gas / out now / follow me / stop*. Bone-conduction radios may work, but do not rely on them.
- **Tether everything.** Hats, maps, cameras, and loose fabric will be pulled toward the crack. Anything you lose is gone.
- **Expect your body to react.** Infrasound can cause nausea, pressure in the chest, dread, or tears for no reason. Knowing this in advance helps a great deal. If someone in your party panics, the plan is simply to sit down together and wait.

### Where to be
- **Not in the throat or near the crack.** During the breathing the exit becomes a chimney of hot, gritty, gas-laden air at gale force. Never plan to enter or leave during it. If you arrive late and it has started, wait on the surface, upwind.
- **Not in the sunken streets.** They carry the heaviest drones and the pooled gas.
- **Not inside the hollowed terraces.** They resonate like drum bodies, and being inside one is like being inside a speaker.
- **Ideally in an open plaza or on a solid, low roof-terrace near the centre, away from any big vent basin.** The central stepped platform is probably the best seat in the house, since it was likely designed to be. Test your gas levels there before the breathing starts, sit, brace, and let it happen.

Almost no one alive has heard what you are about to hear. Keep your plugs in anyway.

## The Ground and the Ceiling

### Fissures
- The streets are often knee-deep in plants, so **you cannot see what you are stepping on.** Carry a trekking pole or probe and test ahead in the overgrown quarters.
- Gaiters or taped trouser cuffs keep grit and seeds out and give some protection against brushing hot rock.
- The upward-cast shadows will scramble your depth perception. A small white light, used sparingly, restores it when you need to judge a step or a gap.

### The ceiling
- Wear a **helmet.** Sandstone flakes drift down constantly, and larger pieces occasionally come with the booms.
- Stress is highest around the temperature transitions, which is when the breathing happens. Do not linger under visibly sagging sections of the lid, especially then.
- Take the booms seriously but do not flee blindly. A panicked run through a street full of plants and fissures is more dangerous than the ceiling.

### The architecture
- The roof-bridges are single stone slabs, centuries old and wind-worn. Dry-stacked work can be extremely stable or very quietly unstable. Test before you trust, cross one at a time, and skip bridges that would not be worth dying for, which is most of them.
- Stair treads are dished bowls, often full of loose grit. Treat them like scree.

### The seed pods
- They pop constantly in the warm hours, sometimes in chains, and throw hard fragments. Your goggles and helmet cover most of the risk. A **face shield** is worth it if you plan to work close to the plants for photography or study.
- The first hundred cracks will make you jump. By the second hour they will sound like rain. Stay aware anyway; a chain burst beside your face is still a chain burst.

## The Resin

It is beautiful, and you will want to touch it. Know what it is: a hydrocarbon oil fixed by plants living on vent gas. Assume it is sticky, flammable, possibly irritating to skin, and likely to carry whatever the plants absorbed.

- Keep it off your skin. Your gloves will get coated; bring spares and a sealed bag for fouled ones.
- Keep it away from rope, which it stiffens, and from lenses, which it ruins.
- Resin-soaked clothing is a fire hazard for the rest of the trip, including on the surface. Bag it separately.
- If you collect a sample, take a small one: a single fallen bead, sealed in glass. The city has been making it for no one for ages. It will not miss a bead, but it should not become a quarry either.

## Getting In and Out

- **Rope access.** The slot and throat may require a proper rope descent. Salt is a poor anchor, so bring a deadman anchor or use a vehicle, and plan it with someone who knows desert rigging.
- **Protect the rope.** Grit and hot stone abrade and weaken rope quickly. Use rope protectors at every edge and inspect the rope after every trip. Do not leave a rope hanging in the throat through a breathing; the hot, gritty gale will degrade it.
- **Keep a surface team.** At least one person stays on the salt flat with water, first aid, communications, and the ability to haul. Agree on a hard turnaround time. If you are not back by then, they call for help rather than coming in after you alone.
- **Buddy system below, always.** Heat illness and gas exposure both cloud judgement before they cause collapse. Your partner will notice you slurring before you do.

## Gear Checklist (Condensed)

**Protection:** helmet, sealed goggles, face shield, double hearing protection, respirator with acid-gas/organic-vapour/particulate cartridges, escape air supply, heat-rated boots, gaiters, spare gloves, flame-resistant clothing.

**Environment:** cooling vest and spare packs, large water supply with electrolytes, eye drops, nasal gel, lip balm, skin balm, breathing buff.

**Detection:** two gas meters (chest and knee height), core-temperature or heart-rate monitor, thermal camera for navigation and for spotting hot ground.

**Movement:** probe pole, rope system with edge protectors and anchor, tethers for all loose equipment.

**Light:** intrinsically safe headlamps with red and white modes, spare batteries kept cool. Heat shortens battery life and can make lithium cells dangerous.

**Other:** laminated hand-signal card, sealed sample containers, bags for resin-fouled gear, a lint roller or brush for seed decontamination, first aid with burn dressings and heat-illness supplies.

## A Suggested Day

1. **Pre-dawn:** arrive at the crack while the flat is cool and it is easy to find. Check the anchors and rig the rope.
2. **The morning breathing:** wait it out on the surface, upwind. Watch it exhale.
3. **Late morning:** descend once the outflow has fully settled and your meters confirm the throat has cleared.
4. **Midday:** explore. This is when the three blades of daylight are most useful, the streets are lit from above as well as below, and you can watch the vines turn to follow the light. Keep to roofs and plazas where you can, and dip into the streets only after checking gas at knee level.
5. **Before the evening breathing:** rehydrate, change cooling packs, and take your position on the central platform or an open plaza. Check the gas one last time. Hearing protection in.
6. **The evening breathing:** sit, listen, and do not move unless your alarm tells you to.
7. **After:** wait for the air to settle, check your meters, and climb out into the cooling desert night. The salt flat will feel like heaven. It will also get cold quickly, so have warm layers waiting on the surface.

## Leaving It as You Found It

- **Seeds.** The pods fling seeds everywhere, and they will be in your cuffs, boot treads, hair, and pack seams. On the surface, before you leave the flat and certainly before you cross back through the multiverse, brush down everything and check thoroughly. These plants have spent ages waiting for a warmth that almost never comes. Somewhere, it might come: a car engine bay, a boiler room, a geothermal field in your home world. Do not be the one who carries it there.
- **Water.** Do not spill it. Nothing in this place has ever been wet. A splash will not destroy anything, but it is the one thing you can bring that is truly foreign here.
- **The instrument.** Do not block the holes, lean gear against the pierced walls, or wedge anything into the openings. The music will end on its own, sealed in amber, and it should not end early because of a visitor.
- **The carvings.** Do not touch the reliefs. The wind has polished them to suggestion over centuries, and skin oil, resin-smeared gloves, and grit ground in by fingers do damage that no wind would.
- **The dust.** The plazas hold undisturbed dust that may not have been walked on since the builders left. Your footprints will be the first marks in an age. Think about whether you want to cross them, or walk the edges instead. Either is defensible, as long as it is a choice.

## What It Will Actually Be Like

You will be uncomfortable the whole time. You will be hot in a way that has direction, rising up your legs from the floor. Your lips will hurt. Your goggles will fog with sweat, then clear as the air drinks it. Your respirator will make your breathing loud in your own head, and you will be glad of it, because it is the only sound for a while.

Then the silence will get to you. It is not the silence of a dead place. The plants rustle, the pods crack like knuckles, and the ceiling ticks above you like a cooling engine. It is the silence of *no one*. You will walk down a straight street whose end you cannot see in the red dimness and realise you have not been this alone in your life. Some people find that peaceful. Some find it frightening. Many find it both, alternating every few minutes. Prepare for that. Talk to your partner, and do not wander off to be alone with it.

The upward shadows will make every doorway look like a mouth, and your mind will try to put people in the rooms. It will not find any. Room after room will be empty, swept, and finished, and the orderliness of that emptiness is stranger than a scatter of bones would be.

Then the breathing starts. You will feel it before you hear it: a pressure, a pull on your sleeves toward the far end of the city. Then one thin whistle, and an answer. Then the floor under you begins to hum. Within minutes you will be sitting inside a chord so large that it has weather in it. Your chest will vibrate, and your eyes may water, from grit or from something else. You will not be able to hear your own voice, and you will not want to use it.

When it ends, the silence afterwards will sound completely different. You will know what the holes are for now, and every wall will look like it is waiting.

Go carefully. The place does not need you, will not remember you, and will not help you. Given the right preparation, it will let you listen.
