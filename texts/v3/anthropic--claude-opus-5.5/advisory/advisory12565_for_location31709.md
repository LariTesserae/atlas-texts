# Preparing for the Standing Roar

## The Short Version

You are going to a place that is extremely high, extremely bright, extremely loud, and extremely windy, all at once and without a moment's break. Any one of these can be managed with ordinary mountaineering sense. The difficulty is that they act on you together and never let up. The altitude dulls your judgment. The noise takes your voice and your hearing. The light takes your eyes. The wind takes anything you don't hold on to. The constancy that makes the place beautiful also means there is no lull to recover in and no calm hour to wait for.

The one mercy is that the place is perfectly predictable. There are no storms, no weather windows, and no surprises from the sky. Whatever you plan for on the first day will hold on the last. So plan thoroughly, because conditions will not improve.

Plan to stay one to three days. That seems to be what the people who came before you did. There is nothing to be gained by staying longer, and a lot to be lost.

## 1. Getting There: Arrive Low, Walk Up

This is the most important decision you will make, and you make it before you arrive.

**Do not open your crossing onto the plateau itself.** "Every breath has to be taken twice" means you are somewhere well above 5,000 meters, possibly much higher. An unacclimatized person dropped there suddenly may be confused within hours and in serious danger within a day. High-altitude cerebral and pulmonary edema can kill fast, and there will be no one to help you.

Instead:

- **Arrive in the lower valleys** on the approach side, where the old route begins. You will know you're on it when you reach the cairns at the crest of the lower slopes.
- **Ascend in stages** over days, following standard acclimatization practice: gain a few hundred meters of sleeping altitude per day at most, with rest days.
- If you have to arrive higher, arrive **far back on the shelf, away from the Throat**, with supplemental oxygen, and be ready to descend immediately.

**Medical preparation:**
- See a physician familiar with high-altitude medicine before you go. Discuss acetazolamide for prevention, and carry dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies.
- Carry a **pulse oximeter**. You won't be able to trust how you feel, for reasons covered below, so trust the numbers.
- Build aerobic fitness for months beforehand.
- **Get a baseline audiogram** (a hearing test). You'll want to compare against it afterward.
- Tell your doctor if you have migraines, anxiety disorders, tinnitus, vertigo, heart rhythm problems, or photosensitive seizures. All of these interact with what you're about to experience.

**The cure for altitude illness is descent.** Before you set foot on the shelf, know exactly how to get back down the approach route, and memorize the line of cairns. Rescue is not coming. No aircraft could operate in that thin, violent air, and a signal fire would be blown flat.

## 2. The Light: Protect Your Eyes From Below

Most people prepare for glare from above. Here the glare also comes from beneath. The cloud-floor is a mirror miles wide, throwing sunlight up under your hat brim, under your glasses, and into the undersides of your eyelids. Under a nearly violet sky at this altitude, the ultraviolet load will be ferocious.

**Eyes:**
- Wear **full goggles or glacier glasses with side and bottom shielding**, rated for high UV. Ordinary sunglasses let reflected light in from below and will not protect you. Snow blindness (photokeratitis) is painful and incapacitating, and it appears hours after the damage is done.
- Bring a **spare pair**. If the wind takes your only pair, you are in real trouble.
- Goggles do double duty, because they also keep the wind out. Without them your eyes will stream, then dry out, then burn.
- Carry **lubricating eye drops**.
- Use **anti-fog treatment**. In the wet band, condensation will bead on your lenses constantly.

**Skin:**
- Use high-SPF mineral sunscreen, and apply it where you normally wouldn't: **under your chin, under your nose and inside the rims of your nostrils, beneath your jawline, the undersides of your ears, and your eyelids**. Faces here are lit from beneath. Burns in those places are common and miserable.
- Carry SPF lip balm and reapply often, since the wind strips it off.
- A brimmed hat is nearly useless. The light comes from below, and the wind will take the hat anyway. Wear a **fitted balaclava or neck gaiter** that you can pull up over your lower face.

**Depth perception:**
Shadows here are faint and seem to come from nowhere. That kills your ability to read the shape of the ground, much like the "flat light" mountaineers know from overcast snowfields. Small ledges, grooves, and dips will look flat until you trip on them. Walk deliberately. Use trekking poles to feel ahead. Be especially careful near the edge (see Section 6).

You will squint. Everyone does. Your face will ache from it by the second day.

## 3. The Wind: Tether Everything

**Direction first.** The wind pours up the escarpment face, funnels through the Throat, and blows *onto* the plateau, away from the edge. Near the Throat it will push you inland, not over the cliff, which is a small comfort. But along the rest of the escarpment lip the updraft rises vertically, and the airflow at any edge is turbulent and unpredictable. **Do not trust the wind near the drop.**

**Strength.** Near the mouth of the Throat, the wind can knock a standing adult flat. Farther back on the shelf it is still strong, constant, and exhausting. Leaning into it all day tires you in ways you won't notice until you're wrecked.

**Practical rules:**

- **Leash everything.** Gloves, goggles, hat, camera, water bottle, map, and food bag all need to be clipped to you. Nothing loose has survived on this plateau, and your belongings will not be the exception. Anything that gets away is gone for good.
- **Wear fitted clothing.** Loose fabric will flap until it drives you mad or tears. Choose a snug windproof shell with hood drawcords and cuffs you can cinch tight.
- **Watch for the lee trap.** When you step behind a rock rib, the wind you've been leaning against vanishes and you will stumble forward. The reverse happens stepping out, when you're shoved sideways. Anticipate both.
- **Breathe away from the wind.** In a strong headwind it's hard to inhale, and at this altitude you need every breath. Turn your head aside, or pull the gaiter up to take the edge off.
- **Expect a rain of small life.** The wind carries winged ants, gnats, midges, seeds, and ballooning spiders trailing silk. They will hit your face, catch in your hair, and fly into your mouth if it's open. They're harmless but relentless. Keep the gaiter up and your goggles on.
- **Approaching the Throat means crawling, or being roped and anchored.** The granite is polished and loose rock is almost nonexistent, so bring **cams, nuts, or slings for natural anchors** in cracks and around rock ribs. Don't count on finding a boulder to tie off to. Never remove or disturb a cairn to use as an anchor or windbreak.
- **Use a low-profile shelter.** A standard tent near the Throat will be shredded. Use a **bivy sack or very low, heavily guyed shelter**, pitched in the lee of a rib far back on the shelf. Follow the cairn-builders' lesson: stay low, flat, and anchored.
- **Cooking is hard.** Stoves struggle in thin air and gale-force wind. Bring a stove designed for high altitude and a windscreen, cook in the deepest lee you can find, and carry extra fuel. Water boils at a much lower temperature here, so bring food that only needs rehydrating, not real cooking.

## 4. The Sound: The Thing You Most Need to Prepare For

Everything above is familiar mountain danger. This is not.

### Your hearing is at risk the whole time

If you cannot hear your own voice a mile from the Throat, the ambient noise even there is probably at a level where unprotected hearing is damaged within minutes to hours. Near the lip it will be well beyond the threshold of pain.

The dangerous part comes after a day or two, when **your mind stops presenting the roar to you**. It will feel like silence. It is not. Your inner ear is still taking the full load. The adaptation is psychological, not physical. You will feel fine while you are being deafened.

**Protection:**
- Wear **foam earplugs and over-ear earmuffs together** at all times on the plateau, including while sleeping. Bring dozens of plugs. They'll get lost, dirty, and wet.
- Even doubled protection has a ceiling. At extreme levels, sound reaches your inner ear **through your skull bones** and no plug can stop it. So **limit your time near the Throat to short visits**, minutes rather than hours, and retreat to the far shelf to rest your ears.
- Watch for ringing in your ears, muffling, or a feeling of fullness. Treat these as warnings and back off.

### The note you feel rather than hear

The deepest layer of the roar is felt in the breastbone and teeth. Very low-frequency sound like this can cause:
- nausea and a queasy, seasick feeling
- a vague, sourceless dread or unease
- a sense of pressure behind the eyes, or even slight blurring of vision
- a feeling that your heart or chest is being tuned to something outside you

None of this can be blocked by earplugs. Know in advance that it is coming, so that when you feel inexplicably frightened or ill near the Throat, you can recognize it as physics and not premonition. Combined with altitude hypoxia, it makes your judgment and emotions unreliable. **Make every important decision before you approach the Throat,** including how close you'll go, how long you'll stay, and when you'll turn back, and then stick to it.

### Communication without sound

Your voice is gone. So is your companions'. Shouting a warning will do nothing. Plan for this as carefully as you would plan for a dive.

- **Agree on hand signals** before you leave, and practice them at home until they're automatic: stop, come here, go back, danger, I'm unwell, I'm fine, look there, rope up, descend now.
- **Carry a small whiteboard or waterproof notebook** for anything complicated.
- **Use touch.** A tap on the shoulder, a squeeze of the arm, tugs on a rope between you. Divers and climbers already use rope-tug codes, and the same logic works here.
- **Signal with light, the way the birds do.** A signal mirror is the best long-range tool on the plateau. The light is abundant and the air is clear. Agree on a few flash codes.
- **Try the rock.** The grazers communicate by stamping. Palm-to-granite contact carries vibration, and you may be able to tap simple signals to a companion nearby.
- **Electronics:** ordinary radios and phone calls are useless. Throat-contact microphones or bone-conduction headsets *might* work at close range, but test them in extreme noise before relying on them. Text-based devices work fine.
- **Make alarms visual or tactile.** Use vibrating watches and flashing lights, not beeps.
- **Keep each other in sight constantly.** You won't hear a companion fall, cry out, or get into trouble behind you. Agree on regular visual check-ins, a thumbs-up every few minutes that must be returned.

### A strange comfort

There is one voice you *can* hear on the plateau: your own humming, carried through the bones of your skull. When you feel isolated inside the roar, try it. It's a small private sound, and for many people it is steadying.

### Sleeping

The roar travels through the rock you lie on. A thick sleeping pad insulates you a little from both the cold and the vibration. Wear earplugs and muffs, and an eye mask if the starlight is too bright. The stars there are "hard and many and very near." Expect poor sleep the first night. Many people find that by the second night the roar becomes almost lulling.

### Coming back down: "going deaf in reverse"

Prepare for this as seriously as for the ascent. When you descend into the ordinary quiet of the valleys, it may sound **hollow, eerie, even terrifying**, as if the world is holding its breath or something is about to happen. You may startle easily, feel anxious, or find ordinary sounds too sharp or strangely thin. You may also discover, in the quiet, ringing in your ears that the roar was hiding.

- **Descend gradually** and spend a night or two partway down.
- **Bring a small white-noise source** (a fan app, a recording of wind or surf) to ease the transition at night.
