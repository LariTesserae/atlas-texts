# Preparing for a Visit to the Works at Hollin Bottom

## The Short Version

Hollin Bottom isn't dangerous the way a cliff or a predator is. It's dangerous the way a working mill yard, a flooded mine entrance, and a hospital ward are dangerous: industrial hazards, contaminated water, hidden voids, and moving parts that don't know or care that you're there. On top of that, you'll be inside a single living organism that heals its wounds, keeps a pulse, and slowly absorbs whatever stays still long enough.

Most visitors will be fine if they move carefully, touch little, take nothing, keep their visit to a few hours, and leave the moment the rhythm changes.

The rest of this guide explains why, and how.

---

## Before You Go

### Medical preparation

- **Get a tetanus booster.** This matters more than anything else on the list. The Bottom is full of rusted iron, much of it hidden under moss at shin and hand height. You'll brush against edges you can't see.
- **Assume the water is poisoned.** This was an industrial site that needed a lot of water, possibly a dye works. Dye works historically used arsenic, chromium, lead, and aniline compounds, and that legacy is probably still in the ground. The plant concentrates iron, and it may concentrate other things too. The warm, red-brown water that wells up around your boots should be treated as contaminated.
- **Consider your lungs.** The air is warm, saturated, and full of moss and fungal spores. Warm standing water that becomes airborne, as in the chimney steam and the fine spray from drips, is the classic setting for respiratory bacteria. If you have asthma, bring your inhaler and keep it in an outer pocket. A well-fitted respirator (P100/FFP3) is worth carrying even if you don't wear it the whole time.
- **Know the contraindications.** Think twice, or don't go in, if you:
  - have a pacemaker or other implanted electronic device. The cable at the eastern edge is carrying something unknown, and the ground is dense with iron, so magnetic fields are unpredictable.
  - are pregnant. Lead is present, at minimum in the cable sheathing.
  - have a heart condition, severe anxiety, or panic disorder. The infrasonic hum and the heartbeat-like knock affect the body directly, as described below.
  - are strongly claustrophobic. The Bottom isn't dark or narrow, but it is *full*, and many people find that harder than they expect.

### Information to gather

- **Talk to the oldest people in the nearest village, and specifically ask when the last Silence was.** The knock stops roughly once a generation, for a few days. If the last Silence was thirty-five or more years ago, you're in the window where another could begin. That doesn't mean you shouldn't go. It means you should keep your visit short and keep listening.
- **Ask whose land the pasture is and get permission.** This is rural, grazed, working country. Close every gate you open. Don't cross fields with stock in them without asking.
- **Ask what the villagers call the place and how they talk about it.** Local habits often encode hazards, such as "don't go past the tanks" or "nobody touches the cable." Listen for those.
- **Tell someone in the village when you're going in and when you expect to be out.**

### Go with a companion

Ideally, one person goes in and one stays on the lip of the basin with a line of sight to where the first person entered. The basin is small enough that your partner can hear a shout, but only between knocks and above the hiss. Agree on signals in advance: whistle blasts carry better than voices here. One blast means "I'm fine," three means "come to me / something's wrong."

## What to Wear

The main problem is that you move from cool, wet pasture into a warm, dripping greenhouse and back again. Dress in layers you can manage.

- **Close-fitting clothing with nothing loose.** No scarves, dangling straps, drawstrings, lanyards, or loose sleeves. Tie long hair back and tuck it under a cap. This matters most in the engine house, where the gear train, part iron and part grown wood, is meshing and turning with no guards. A beam a meter thick doesn't slow down for a snagged coat.
- **Base layer of wool or quick-drying synthetic, not cotton.** You'll be soaked in sweat and condensation within minutes. Cotton stays wet and chills you badly on the walk out.
- **A light waterproof shell for the pasture**, which you'll probably take off and stow once inside. Inside it's too warm, and everything is wet anyway.
- **Tall waterproof boots, knee-high wellingtons or better.** The root mat is half a meter thick and saturated, and each step presses warm water up around your feet. Gaiters over the top keep water and debris from going in. Stiff soles and ankle support help on the uneven lattice. Avoid anything with deep lugs that will clog with root fiber.
- **Gloves in two layers.** Nitrile underneath against contaminated water, sap, and lead. Leather or heavy work gloves on top against rust, rivets, and resin.
- **A bump cap or lightweight helmet.** Most of the Bottom's volume lies between your knees and a few meters above your head, with iron hidden in it: stanchions, pipe flanges, a flywheel's rim. You'll hit your head at least once.
- **Safety glasses.** Rusty water falls constantly from leaf tips. You'll want it out of your eyes. Treat them with anti-fog, or they'll be useless within five minutes.
- **Foam earplugs or earmuffs, used with judgment.** The noise is mill-yard loud, not deafening, and you need to hear the knock and notice if it changes or stops. The best approach is to wear moderate protection most of the time and remove it periodically to check the rhythm. Don't wear noise-cancelling electronics. They may deal badly with the infrasound and the local fields, and you don't want your ears depending on a battery here.

## What to Carry

**Essential:**

- **A probing stick**, a stout walking pole or ash staff about shoulder height. This is your most important tool. Before you trust your weight to any patch of mat, press the pole into it. The Bottom contains an old pump shaft, settling tanks, narrow-gauge rail that dives underground, and almost certainly other pits and sumps overgrown so completely that the surface looks solid. The mat is springy by nature, so you're feeling for *give that doesn't stop*.
- **A whistle** on a short cord tucked inside your collar, not dangling.
- **Drinking water, more than you think.** You'll sweat heavily in the glasshouse air, and there is nothing inside you can safely drink.
- **A headlamp.** It's daylight, but there are deep green-shaded pockets, and the engine house is enclosed.
- **A timepiece you trust, and not a mechanical watch.** The iron, the magnetic beetles, and the humming cable can magnetize a mechanical movement and ruin it. Use a cheap digital watch. You'll want to time the knock.
- **A dry bag** for phone, camera, and anything electronic. The humidity is total. Expect condensation *inside* devices and fogged lenses, and assume anything unsealed may fail.
- **A pencil and waterproof notebook.** Pens fail on wet paper.
- **A small first aid kit:** antiseptic, dressings, and something to irrigate a wound with clean water.
- **A complete change of dry clothes and a warm layer, left at the lip of the basin**, not carried inside, in a sealed bag.
- **A stiff brush and a bag for your boots**, also left at the lip, for decontamination on the way out.

**Useful:**

- **A length of light rope** tied off at the edge, if you intend to go far toward the center. The basin is only a stone's throw across, but inside you can't see more than a few meters, and every direction looks the same: stem touching stem, leaf over leaf.
- **A respirator**, for time near the chimney or the engine house.

**Leave behind:**

- **A compass, or at least don't trust one.** Between buried iron, heaps of iron nodules, magnetic beetles, and the live cable, it will lie to you. Navigate by sound instead (see below).
- **Knives, saws, and machetes.** You won't need to cut a path, and you shouldn't. See "What Not to Do."
- **Anything you'd hate to lose.** Things set down in the Bottom tend to become part of the Bottom.

## Navigating Inside

You can't see far and your compass won't help, so use what the place gives you:

- **The knock is your centre.** It comes from the engine house at the heart of the basin. With practice you can feel its direction as much as hear it.
- **The chimney is north.** Look for the steam above the canopy, or stand still and smell for coal smoke on the air.
- **The cable is east.** The hum you feel in your back teeth gets stronger as you approach it.
- **Uphill is out.** It's a basin. If you're lost, go toward wherever the ground tilts up and the air feels cooler. You'll reach the embankment.

Move slowly. Probe every step onto new ground. The going is less like walking through a wood and more like crossing the rigging of a ship that happens to be alive. Stems thick as your wrist have fused into lattices, walls, and floors. Use them, but test them. Most are structural. Some are just very convincing.

## Timing Your Movements Around the Knock

About every forty seconds, the knock comes, and the whole mass shivers. This is more than a sound. The floor you're standing on moves.

- **Get into the habit of counting.** You'll probably do it without meaning to anyway. Visitors almost always do. By the time you've been inside for ten minutes, you'll know the rhythm in your body.
- **Don't step onto uncertain ground on the count.** Make your moves between knocks, and when you feel one due, plant your feet, get a hand on something solid, and let it pass.
- **In the engine house, only move between knocks.** Watch several full cycles from the entrance before you go any further in: the squeeze of the ribs, the rise of the beam, the pause at the top, the fall, the slam of the pump rod. Know exactly where every moving part travels before you step anywhere near it.

---

## The Engine House: Specific Rules

This is what most people come to see. It's also the most dangerous place in the Bottom.

1. **Stay out of the path of anything that moves.** The beam, the connecting rods, the pump rod, and the gear train are all live machinery. The fact that they're made of wood doesn't make them softer. The beam is a meter-thick limb swinging with the force of a pumping engine.
2. **Keep away from the pump shaft.** The rod plunges down into it. It's deep, it's wet, and you won't get out.
3. **Don't stand pressed against the ribbed walls.** The walls contract with each cycle, a squeeze that runs around the ribs from one side to the other. Nobody has recorded anyone being caught, but don't put yourself between two ribs, and don't put a hand in a gap that closes.
4. **Don't touch the gauges.** Don't tap the glass to see if the needle moves. The plant grew around them and keeps them working. You don't know what they're measuring, and you don't want to be the thing that changes the reading.
5. **Keep your visit here short.** It's the warmest, loudest, and most intense part of the Bottom, and the part where the infrasound is strongest. Ten or fifteen minutes is plenty.

## The Cable

At the eastern edge a lead-sheathed cable, thick as a man's arm, runs out through the embankment. Where it's bare it's warm and buzzing.

- **Treat it as live.** Nobody knows what it carries, where it goes, or what's at the other end. If you touch it at all, do it with dry, gloved hands, briefly, and never while standing in water, which is to say anywhere in the Bottom. Honestly, you'll learn as much from standing near it and feeling the hum as from touching it.
- **Don't damage it, dig at it, or try to follow it under the pasture.** Whatever it's feeding has been fed without interruption for a very long time, except during the Silences. You don't want to be the one who interrupts it.
- **Lead: wash your hands before you eat or drink anything afterward.**

## What Not to Do

**Don't cut anything.** Cut a stem and sap wells out under pressure, pulsing faintly in time with the knock. The Bottom is one organism, and it will heal the cut, but you'll have wounded it, and you'll have released sap of unknown chemistry onto your skin. There's no need. The ways through already exist.

**Don't take the nodules.** The rust-red iron lumps that ripen from the bark and fall into heaps in the moss are almost irresistible. They're heavy, strange, and beautiful when broken, layered like pearls. But they're the product of years of the plant's work. You don't know what else they contain besides iron. And removing anything from a closed system this old, this self-maintaining, feels like the kind of thing that has consequences. If you must, photograph one.

**Absolutely do not take the seeds or the seed-casings.** The hexagonal casings near the chimney split open and scatter seeds that never take root in the Bottom, because there's no room. That tells you nothing about what they'd do somewhere with room. A plant that fills every niche, absorbs machinery, grows replacement parts to spec, and concentrates metal from the ground is the last thing you want to carry into another world. This is the most important rule for a multiverse traveler, and the reason for the boot decontamination below.

**Don't disturb the beetles.** The small bronze-green beetles living between iron and wood eat the resin the plant uses to seal its joints. They're part of how the place works. Also note that they're magnetic, so they may cling to your gear. Check yourself before you leave.

**Don't go near the heron.** If you see it standing at the edge of a settling tank with its head tilted toward the knock, stay where you are and watch. It will leave on its own time. The settling tanks themselves are brick-walled pits of deep, dark, warm water with mossy, unreliable rims. Stay back from them.

**Don't linger near the chimney.** The coal-smoke smell is uncanny, since nothing is burning, and you don't know what's in the steam. Pass by upwind if you can.

**Don't sleep, sit still for long, or leave things behind.** Nothing in the Bottom has room around it, and everything eventually gets grown over. The process takes years, not hours, so you're in no danger on a short visit. But the principle holds. Keep moving and keep your gear on you.

## If the Knock Stops

Once a generation or so, the knock stops for a few days. Nobody knows what happens in the Bottom during that time, because nobody has gone in.

**If you're inside and a knock doesn't come, start counting.** Forty seconds is normal. Fifty is unusual. At sixty seconds with no knock, stop what you're doing and walk out calmly by the nearest route uphill, probing as you go. Don't run. Running on the mat is how you find a shaft.

Signs that it's more than a missed beat:
- the hiss drops away and only the dripping continues
- the canopy stops its faint shivering
- the steam from the chimney thins
- the hum in your teeth fades

Don't wait to confirm. Don't go deeper to see what's happening. The plant is adjusting something deep in its workings, and afterward some iron part somewhere will have been replaced entirely with wood. You don't want to be anywhere near a process like that while it's running.

If the knock resumes while you're on your way out, keep going anyway. Note whether it's faster or slower than before, and tell the villagers. They'll want to know.

## The Psychological Side

People underestimate this part.

**The hum.** The low tone near the bottom of hearing is infrasound, or close to it, and infrasound affects the body directly. Common reactions include unease, pressure in the chest or ears, mild nausea, a sense of being watched, or a sudden low-grade dread with no cause. If you feel any of these, know that they're physical, not a warning from your intuition, and treat them that way. If they become strong, step back toward the edge, where the hum is weaker (except near the cable).

**The rhythm.** You'll count the knock without meaning to. After a while you may find your breathing or even your heartbeat drifting toward it. It's not a machine's rhythm; it's closer to breathing. Some people find this soothing, and some find it very disturbing. Either is normal. It can linger after you leave. Some visitors report hearing it, or feeling for it, for days, especially when falling asleep.

**The fullness.** The Bottom isn't dark and isn't narrow, but nothing in it has space around it. Every surface is occupied by something growing on something else. After an hour, the absence of *empty* can press on you in a way that's hard to name. It helps to plan short breaks at the edge, where you can see the sky.

**Being inside something.** At some point, usually in the engine house with its ribbed walls squeezing in time, most people realize they're standing inside a body. Expect that moment. Let it come, and don't let it make you hurry.

**Silence.** There are no birdsong, no insects, and no rustling in the undergrowth, because there is no undergrowth. Only the dripping, the hiss, the hum, and the knock. You may find yourself talking to your companion more than usual, or to yourself. That's fine.

---

## How Long to Stay

Two to three hours inside is plenty for a first visit, including a careful approach to the engine house and a return. That's enough time to feel the place without accumulating exposure to the water, the air, the heat, and the hum. If you want to come back, come back another day.
