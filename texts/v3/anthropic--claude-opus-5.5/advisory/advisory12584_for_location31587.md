# Preparing to Visit the Warm Throat

## The Short Version

The Warm Throat will not attack you. Nothing there wants anything from you, with one important exception covered below. What can hurt you is the environment: dry heat that never lets up, bad air that can settle in a one-door room, darkness total enough to hide open fissures of glowing rock, a floor that is alive in its top layer, and a fine organic dust you'll breathe the whole time. Its mood can hurt you too. Visitors disagree about whether it is the most frightening or the most peaceful place they've been, and you won't know which it will be for you until you're inside.

Prepare as you would for three things at once: a sauna, a cave, and a sacred site belonging to people who are no longer around to tell you the rules.

---

## 1. Understand the Heat Before You Meet It

The description is careful: it isn't a furnace, but it "stops being air and becomes pressure." My guess is that the chamber air sits somewhere around or above body temperature, perhaps 40–55°C, and climbs during the mountain's exhalation. The ash-moths' survival band is near the vents, so the room's baseline is set by creatures adapted to heat you are not.

What that means for your body:

- **You cannot cool off by radiating heat.** If the air is warmer than you, you absorb heat. Your only defense is sweat, and in air this dry sweat evaporates almost as fast as it forms. You won't feel soaked. You'll feel fine until suddenly you don't. Dry heat is deceptive in exactly this way.
- **Set hard time limits.** Plan sessions of 15–20 minutes, then go back out through the cleft to cool down. Have a partner outside timing you who is authorized to call you out. Don't negotiate with yourself in the dark about whether you feel okay.
- **Learn the early signs of heat illness** and agree on them with your partner: headache, a sudden stop in sweating, confusion, nausea, clumsiness, a racing pulse, or irritability and a sense that everything is fine. The dark and the quiet will mask confusion, so assume you'll be the last to notice.
- **Pre-hydrate for a day beforehand** and carry electrolytes. Drink outside the chamber, not inside (see Section 6).
- **Your eyes will dry out almost at once.** Bring sealed goggles, the kind used for swimming or dust, not open-sided glasses. Preservative-free lubricating drops are for use outside afterward.

### Time Your Visit to the Mountain's Breath

This is the most useful preparation you can do. The chamber runs on a roughly five-hour cycle: about an hour of rising heat, a peak, then easing. Enter just after the peak, as the heat starts to fall and the living carpet begins flowing back down the walls. That gives you the longest cooler window.

You don't need instruments to read the cycle. The room tells you in two ways:

1. **The red seam at the back.** A dull, forge-cooled glow means the heat is low. When it brightens toward a smolder, the heat is rising.
2. **The moths themselves.** Put a gloved palm flat on the wall at chest height and hold it there. If you feel the slow creep moving *upward* past your hand, the heat is rising, and you should begin working your way out. If it's moving *downward*, you're in the good part of the cycle.

The moths are a living thermometer made of tens of millions of bodies, and they are more sensitive to that temperature band than any device you can carry. Trust them.

Consider spending a full cycle at the cleft mouth before your first real entry, just feeling the exhalation strengthen and fade against your face. Knowing the mountain's rhythm in your body makes the inside much less disorienting.

## 2. The Air: The Danger That Doesn't Announce Itself

The gas is described as nearly waterless and smelling of sulfur and struck flint. Volcanic exhalations can carry hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, and carbon dioxide. The description suggests the air is breathable: generations of moths live there, people came and went, and the hot gas probably rises and escapes up the chimney and out the cleft by convection. Still, bring a **portable multi-gas meter** (O₂, CO₂, H₂S, SO₂) and clip it at chest height. Carry a second sensor at ankle height on a lanyard or pole, because CO₂ is heavy and pools low.

Things to know:

- **Hydrogen sulfide deadens your sense of smell at dangerous concentrations.** If the rotten-egg smell suddenly seems to fade, that isn't reassurance. Leave.
- **The exhalation peak likely means more gas as well as more heat.** Another reason to time your entry after the peak.
- **Don't lie down or put your face near the floor or the vents,** however tempting it is to get close to the moths or the water mound.
- **Agree on the exit rule beforehand:** if the meter alarms, you leave without discussion and without finishing what you were doing.

## 3. Breathing the Dust

You will breathe shed scales the entire time. The taste of "chalk and pepper" on your tongue is your warning. Insect scales and chitinous dust are known allergens and respiratory irritants, and the pale fungal-like filaments in the drift of dead bodies are almost certainly shedding something microscopic as well.

- **Wear a respirator** with P100/FFP3 particulate filters, and ideally an acid-gas cartridge for sulfur compounds. Breathing through a respirator in dry heat is hard work, which is one more reason to keep sessions short. Practice wearing it in a sauna at home, or somewhere equally hot, before you go.
- **If you have asthma or serious allergies,** talk to someone qualified, carry your medication, and seriously consider limiting yourself to the cleft.
- **Afterward,** expect some throat irritation. Coughing that lasts, or any fever in the following weeks, deserves medical attention, and you should tell whoever treats you where you were. Cave-dust fungal infections are a real phenomenon in many worlds.

"Smells faintly sweet for days" is lovely writing and also a sign that this dust gets into your skin, hair, and clothing and stays there. See Section 9.

## 4. The Dark, and What It Hides

### Protect Your Night Vision; It's the Whole Experience

The two things worth seeing here are the dull red seam and the blue-white ripples of the moths' warning light. Both are extremely faint. Full dark adaptation takes 30–40 minutes, and one glance at a white light resets it. Given your heat limits, you can't afford to spend half a session adapting inside, so:

- **Pre-adapt before you enter.** Wear a double eye patch or fully blacked-out goggles for the last 30–40 minutes of the approach while your partner guides you. Take them off in the cleft. Your eyes will then be ready as you step into the chamber.
- **Use only dim red light**, and only when you need it: to read the gas meter or to check your footing near a vent. Tape over every indicator LED on your equipment.
- **Consider a thermal camera.** It's the one tool that suits this place. The moths are warm bodies on warm stone, and a thermal imager would let you watch the tide and the heat of the vents without emitting any light. You could see the room's whole architecture of heat without disturbing its darkness. A low-light camera on a long exposure might capture the blue ripples, but go in expecting to see them only with your own eyes.

### The Floor Is the Real Hazard

The chamber floor is crossed with fissures. Most are sealed, and a few are open, venting hot gas, with at least one glowing red. The floor is also covered in a moving layer of moths, over a drift of dead ones that is soft and felt-like and many meters deep in places. So:

- **You cannot see where you're putting your feet.** The carpet could be covering an uneven lip, a soft patch in the drift, or a crack.
- **Carry a probing staff**, a walking pole with a wide rubber foot, and test the ground ahead of each step. Move slowly. Nothing in this room rewards speed.
- **Stay near the walls and away from the back of the chamber** where the deepest fissure glows. The moths cluster at the lips of the vents, so a thick patch doesn't mean safe ground. It may mean the opposite.
- **Wear thick, heat-resistant soles.** The floor stays warm all the time, and near the vents it may be hot enough to damage thin rubber. Leather mountaineering boots with solid soles are a reasonable choice.
- **Use a guideline.** It's only one room with one door, but in total darkness, with your ears full of that silk-on-silk pressure, people lose their sense of direction quickly. Run a light cord from the cleft mouth and keep a hand on it, or clip it to your belt. Your partner outside holds the other end.

## 5. Walking on Them

This is the part most people underestimate. You're going to step on living animals, thousands of them over a single visit. They part "slowly... like thick fur," but not all of them will move in time, and some will die under your boots.

Make peace with this before you go, or don't go. Here's how to reduce the harm:

- **Take few steps.** Choose a spot near the entrance or along a wall, walk there slowly, and then *stand still* or sit on your heels. You'll see more by staying put anyway. The tide comes to you.
- **Lift your feet cleanly and set them down slowly,** giving the moths time to part. Don't shuffle or drag.
- **Never step on the mound beneath the water drop.** That's the most crowded and most important spot in the chamber, and the ring of stones marks it off. Stay out of the ring.
- **Remember your weight.** The drift beneath is centuries of compacted dead, banded by season. Every footprint compresses that record. Walk where others seem to have walked, if you can tell, and keep your path short.

### Seal Yourself

Things will try to climb you, less out of aggression than because you're a large, warm, slowly moving surface, and some of your warmth is wet.

- **Tuck trousers into boots and tape or gaiter the join.** Do the same at the wrists over your gloves.
- **Wear loose, long-sleeved natural fabric**: cotton or light wool, closely woven. Avoid synthetics, which can soften near hot rock and which hold dust statically. Light colors don't matter in the dark, but a smooth weave sheds scales more easily.
- **Wear a close-fitting head covering.** Moths clinging to the ceiling may drop, especially near the peak of the heat.
- **Threaders.** A few hundred pale, blind, forearm-long hunters move through the carpet at ankle and wall level. Nothing suggests they're interested in people, and a creature built to take thumb-sized prey probably can't do much to you through a boot. But a sudden, finger-thick, many-legged body sliding across your ankle in total darkness will make most people jump, and jumping in this room is how you stumble near a vent. Expect it. Decide in advance that you'll stay still when it happens.

## 6. You Are a Walking Well

This is the exception I mentioned at the start, and it comes from thinking about the room rather than from the description itself.

Every creature in this chamber lives on water that arrives one drop every hour or two. Tens of millions of moths pile up and strain toward a single point on the ceiling, and the mound shivers when a drop lands. Then you walk in, a large bag of water, exhaling humid breath, sweating, with wet eyes and a wet mouth.

The description says the chamber "asks nothing of you," and in spirit I believe that. Still, I'd expect the moths near you to notice you. Some may drift toward your face, your breath, the corners of your eyes. So:

- **Don't expose moisture.** Your sealed goggles and respirator matter here for this reason as well as the dust. Your exhaled breath will be partly captured, and your eyes will be covered.
- **Don't drink inside.** Opening a bottle or spilling water in this room is like tossing coins in a crowd. At best it causes a disruption. At worst you'll trigger something you don't understand. Drink outside.
- **Don't pour water as an offering.** You may be tempted, especially once you notice the blackened, slick-topped stones around the drop site and the empty bowl in the niche. I suspect those stones and that bowl are exactly what they look like: the ancient visitors may have brought water here, poured it near the drop, and left it in the bowl. It's a moving idea, and you shouldn't copy it. You don't know what those people knew, whether the practice stopped for a reason, or what a sudden flood of water would do to a balance that has held for centuries on a drop an hour. Leave the gesture to them.

## 7. The Hands

Of everything in the Throat, the handprints on the eastern wall are what visitors remember most, and you should approach them with the most restraint.

Twenty or thirty old prints appear only when the tide rises. The moths flow over the whole wall except inside those shapes, so the hands appear as bare, pale outlines. The threaders won't cross them either, and even the blue light stops at their edges. Nobody knows why.

My advice:

- **Plan to see them, and time it.** They're visible only when the tide is *up* on the eastern wall, which means during or near the hotter part of the cycle. That conflicts with your safety window, so you'll need to find a compromise. You might position yourself near the eastern wall toward the end of the easing phase, before the tide fully drops, or make one short, well-timed entry near the peak specifically for this, with your partner holding the guideline tight.
- **Don't touch them.** Skin oils, sweat, and grit could degrade whatever pigment or substance makes them work. If the moths avoid them because of something in the pigment, your hand could wear it away. If they avoid them for some other reason, you don't want to find out what that reason is.
- **Don't press your own hand onto the wall beside them.** The urge to do this will probably be strong. It's an old human instinct, and the people who made these prints felt it too. Resist it. You don't know whether the moths would begin avoiding your mark as well, or what it would mean if they did, and you'd be adding yourself to something that isn't yours.
- **Just look.** Twenty or thirty open palms, outlined in warm living gray, surfacing in the dark every five hours for longer than anyone remembers. Stand there long enough to see the tide reveal them, and possibly long enough to watch a blue ripple come up to a print's edge and stop. Many visitors find this is the moment that unsettles them most, or else the one they treasure.

## 8. Respect the Threshold

There's a line of upright flat stones at the cleft mouth, a fired bowl fused to the rock in the niche, a ring of stones by the drop site, and the hands. That's everything the people who came here left behind. They clearly thought this place mattered and marked it with care.

- **Don't move, straighten, or collect any of it.** The bowl is fused to the rock anyway.
- **Pause at the line of stones.** You don't need to know what it meant to show some respect for it. Treat it as a doorway. Some travelers simply stop there for a breath before going in, and that feels right.
- **Take nothing out**: no moths, no threaders, no scrapings of the gray-green film, no dust from the drift, no filaments. Beyond the ethics, you're a multiverse traveler, and this is a sealed ecosystem built on a chemosynthetic film and a decomposer network nobody has studied. See the next section.
- **Leave nothing behind**: no markers, no cairns, no dropped gear. Anything you drop in the carpet is probably gone for good anyway.

## 9. Decontamination and Biosecurity

The dust "smells faintly sweet for days." That's poetic, and it also means you're carrying living material from this world on your body.

- **Set up a decon point outside the cleft,** well away from it on bare scoria: a sealable bag for outer clothing, a brush, and water for rinsing (drink separately).
- **Strip your outer layer there** and bag it. Brush down your boots and wash them thoroughly before moving on to other places. Moth or threader eggs, filament spores, or fragments of the vent film could all be riding in the tread.
- **Wash your hair, skin, and especially your ears and nostrils** as soon as you can.
- **Don't bring this material to another world.** For a multiverse traveler this is the most serious long-term risk the Throat poses. A decomposer that binds dead bodies into felt, or a film that feeds on sulfur chemistry, could do real damage in the wrong ecosystem. Quarantine your gear.

## 10. The Approach Itself

Don't neglect the walk up.

- **The scoria is sharp, loose, and rolls underfoot.** Wear ankle-supporting boots and gloves for scrambling, and use trekking poles.
- **The ground is warm around the clock.** Stones feel sun-warmed even at midnight, so the whole upper mountain is a heat load, not only the chamber. The lee flank has no wind, which means no cooling breeze, and possibly still air where gases can linger in hollows. Keep the gas meter running on the approach, especially in the "shallow basins."
- **Carry all your water.** There's none on the mountain. Budget generously: you'll be sweating heavily on the approach, during sessions, and while cooling down between them.
- **Nothing grows and nothing moves,** so there's no shade, no landmarks except rock shapes, and no life to orient by. Navigate carefully, and pay particular attention to the way back down in the dark
