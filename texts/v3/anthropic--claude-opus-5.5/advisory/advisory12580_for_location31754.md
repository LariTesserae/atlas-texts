# Preparing for the Blown Table

*A traveler's guide for an ordinary human body*

---

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Most dangerous places are dangerous in one way. The Blown Table is dangerous in several ways that interlock, and the pieces that protect you from one hazard tend to expose you to another.

- Earplugs protect your hearing, but hearing is how you read the weather.
- Shafts protect you from wind, but they are holes you can fall into.
- The warm wind keeps you alive, but it soaks you, dries you, and blasts you with seed all day.
- The warmth feels safe, until it stops.

You are not going to a harsh place that you can outlast with enough gear. You are going into the working parts of a machine: a planetary-scale organ pipe driven by a continent's rising heat. The people who live there survive by reading the machine. Your preparation should aim to make you a competent, humble, temporary reader of it. It should not aim to make you armored against it.

The most important sentence in this guide is this: **your survival depends on noticing when the sound changes.** Almost everything else follows from that.

## Part One: The Body You Bring

### Hearing: Protect It, but Don't Go Deaf to the Weather

The account says it plainly. One hour exposed gives you ringing ears. One week risks permanent loss of high frequencies. Take that literally. The sound levels on open ground are probably in the range where unprotected exposure causes damage in minutes, not hours.

**What to bring:**
- **Foam earplugs, and many of them.** Bring at least fifty pairs. They will get wet, gritty, and full of spore, and you will want to swap them often. Dirty plugs in a warm, humid environment are an ear-infection factory.
- **Custom-molded silicone plugs**, as a more comfortable daytime option.
- **Over-ear protective muffs** that fit under a hood, to layer over the plugs. With both, you might get 30–40 dB of reduction. That may still not be enough in the booming fields, so limit time in the open.
- **Ear-drying drops** (alcohol/acetic acid) and antibiotic ear drops. Your ear canals will be wet for days.
- **A personal noise dosimeter**, or a sound meter on a ruggedized device, so you know your actual daily dose instead of guessing.

**The problem:** protection cuts the high frequencies first, and some of the plateau's warnings live partly in those frequencies. The "sudden thinning," when the booming stops and only the shriek remains, is a change in the balance of the sound. With plugs in, you may still notice it, because the booming is felt in the body. But you need to train yourself to feel it rather than hear it.

**The solution is to read the chord with more than your ears:**
- **Bring a spectrum-analyzer tool**: a waterproof device with a microphone and a live frequency display. Before you go, study whatever recordings or descriptions you can find of the chord patterns: the dissonant cluster, the clean low octave, the rising slide, the thinning. On the Table, check the display regularly. Think of it as a barometer you read with your eyes.
- **Train your body as an instrument.** The low booms are felt in the belly and teeth. That part of the signal reaches you no matter what is in your ears. The absence of that felt pulse, when the ground stops thrumming against your sternum, is one of the clearest danger signs you will get. Practice paying attention to it.
- **Palm to stone.** When you stop, put your hand flat on bare rock. The locals do this for messages, but it also tells you what the plateau's voice is doing below the threshold of hearing.

**Infrasound you cannot block.** The largest shafts produce tones below hearing. Prolonged infrasound exposure can cause nausea, a sense of dread, pressure in the chest, visual blurring, and unexplained anxiety. Expect to feel uneasy for no reason. Know that it is physical, not intuition, and don't let it push you into bad decisions. Anti-nausea medication helps.

**After you leave,** see an audiologist, ideally with a baseline test taken before the trip for comparison.

### Altitude

"Thin, and it hurts the lungs." Walls thousands of meters high rising from lowland forest put the plateau at an altitude where many people get sick. The three-day climb works as partial acclimatization, and you should use it that way. Do not rush the ascent.

- **Get a medical consultation** before the trip and discuss acetazolamide for prevention. Also discuss emergency medication for high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema (HAPE/HACE), such as nifedipine and dexamethasone, and learn exactly when to use them.
- **Know the warning signs:** headache that won't respond to painkillers, loss of coordination, confusion, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough.
- **The treatment for altitude illness is descent, and descent here is days away** down a narrow crack in stone. You cannot be carried down easily. Plan your route so you are never more than a day or two from the stair you came up. If you develop serious symptoms, start down immediately. Don't wait to see if it improves.
- **A pulse oximeter** is cheap, light, and very useful.
- Hydrate more than you think you need. The wind dries you even as the fog wets you.

### Fitness

Plan for:
- **A multi-day climb** ending in a narrow scramble up a crack, hauling yourself over a lip into a wall of wind. Scrambling competence matters. If you have never done exposed scrambling, train for it before you go.
- **Walking bent over, crouched, or crawling** for long periods. Much of your movement on the Table will happen below the wind, and your thighs and back will pay for it. Train with loaded squats and low carries.
- **Bracing against gusts.** Core strength keeps you from being knocked over when a rotor passes.

### Lungs and the Air Itself

The air is full of seed, spore, husk, insect wing, and fluff from the lowlands, whipped around in threads. For anyone with allergies or asthma, this is serious.

- Bring antihistamines and inhalers if you use them. If you have ever had a severe allergic reaction, bring epinephrine auto-injectors, more than one.
- **A breathable buff or light mask** keeps the worst debris out. Heavy respirators will be miserable at altitude, so choose something that filters particulates without much breathing resistance.
- Expect to cough a lot for the first days.

### Skin, Feet, and the Wet

This is a place of constant fog and condensation, at warm temperatures, in high wind. That combination causes:
- **Trench foot and maceration.** Bring many pairs of socks and foot balm, and dry your feet every time you are sheltered.
- **Chafing** everywhere. Anti-chafe balm is not optional.
- **Fungal infections.** Bring antifungal powder and cream.
- **Windburn and UV burn.** Thin air means strong sun when the cloud breaks. Use sunscreen and lip balm, and cover up.
- **Wind chill while wet.** The air may be warm, but a soaked body in constant high wind loses heat fast. You can get cold on a "warm" day. Watch for shivering, clumsiness, and apathy in yourself and your companions.

### Eyes

Every day, tornadic threads of seed and grit will cross the plateau. Wear **sealed, anti-fog goggles** constantly in the open, and bring a spare pair, since goggles get scratched or blown away. Pack eyewash and saline. A seed husk scratching your cornea three days from help is a real emergency.

## Part Two: The Clothing You Wear

Your clothing needs to do three things: shed wind, handle wet, and hold warmth instantly when the silence comes.

**Principles:**
- **No down.** It will be soaked within hours. Use synthetic insulation, or wool, which stays warm when wet.
- **Nothing flappy.** Loose fabric in this wind thrashes, wears out, and makes noise you can feel. It can also snag on stone or catch the wind like a sail. Choose fitted, cinchable gear.
- **Layers you can add in minutes.** The silence can drop the temperature thirty degrees in an hour. Your warm layer must be on the outside of your pack, reachable without unpacking.

**Suggested system:**
- Synthetic or merino base layers, several sets.
- A windproof, highly breathable shell: jacket and trousers with cinch cuffs and a snug, adjustable hood.
- A synthetic insulated jacket and trousers for silences and nights, packed where you can grab them fast.
- A waterproof shell as well. It is less useful for rain, which is rare, than for lying in the wet moss.
- Gloves that let you feel stone. Thin, grippy gloves work best, with warmer overmitts for cold.
- Sturdy, fast-drying boots with good ankle support. The ground is pocked with holes and covered in knee-deep moss that can hide them.
- Kneepads. You will kneel and crawl constantly.

**Color and visibility:** Wear a strong color and carry reflective panels. You cannot shout to a partner, and fog comes in fast. They need to be able to see you.

## Part Three: Shelter

### The Knee-Height Rule

*Nothing standing survives.* Anything above knee height will be taken, unless it is inside a shaft.

That rules out any conventional tent. Pitch one in the open and you will lose it within the hour, and possibly hurt yourself chasing it.

**Bring instead:**
- **A low bivy sack** with plenty of anchor points, or a tarp you can stake flat to the ground in a very low profile.
- **Long, sturdy stakes and rock anchors,** since much of the surface is bare stone. Cord to tie to rocks, and slings to wrap around outcrops.
- **A sleeping pad that doesn't mind wet**, such as closed-cell foam. It doubles as a windbreak when propped against a rock.
- **A synthetic sleeping bag** rated for well below the temperatures you expect, in case the silence comes at night.

### Using the Old Walls

The spiral and circular windbreaks were built so that there is always a lee somewhere, whichever way the wind blows. **Use them.** They are the best surface shelter on the Table. When you settle into one, think about where the wind will come from in the next few hours: morning east, noon south, evening west, night north. Choose your spot for the wind that is coming, not the one blowing now.

Some walls may still be in use. If you find signs of recent habitation, such as ash, trimmed turf, stored seed, or tools, treat it as someone's home. Don't camp there uninvited.

### Shafts: Refuge and Trap

The shafts are the Table's greatest shelter and its greatest hazard.

**Why they're valuable:**
- Out of the wind, warm, wet, and in some cases **quieter**. The sealed-bottom shafts with still pools have "the roar passing overhead like weather on another world." These are your refuges for your ears as much as your body.
- They are where the locals run during silences, and where the listeners run too.

**Why they're dangerous:**
- **Falls.** Shaft rims are "fuzzed with green." Moss and vines cover the edges, and the edges may be undercut. In fog, you can walk off one without seeing it. Smaller holes, fist-sized and thumb-sized, can break an ankle. The knee-deep hair-moss can hide them.
- **Probe the ground.** Carry trekking poles (keep them low; they are fine) and test ahead in thick moss and near any green ring.
- **Not every shaft is a shelter.** Distinguish the *sounding* shafts from the *sealed* ones. A sounding shaft is open below and booms with the wind. Down inside it, you would be sitting in a vast organ pipe, possibly in resonant pressure that could be physically harmful. It may also have strong internal drafts. A sealed shaft, with a still black pool at the bottom, is the kind that shelters. If a shaft booms, do not go down it. If it is quiet and green and still, it may be safe.
- **Bad air.** Still air in deep, enclosed spaces with rotting organic matter can pool carbon dioxide or other gases. If you descend a deep shaft and get a headache, feel suddenly breathless, or feel oddly drowsy (distinct from your baseline altitude symptoms), climb out. A small gas monitor is worth its weight if you plan to spend time in deep shafts.
- **Getting in and out.** Some shafts can be scrambled; others need rope. Bring a light rope, harness, belay device, and ascenders if you intend to descend. Practice ascending a rope before you go. Climbing out of a hole with nothing but grip strength, after days of altitude and wet, is how people get stranded.

### Your Silence Plan

Twice a year or so, the wind stops. The temperature crashes. Then a front returns from an unexpected rim and flattens everything standing.

Every time you stop for the night, and ideally every few hours during the day, know **where your nearest safe shelter is**: a sealed shaft or a deep spiral wall. Know **how long it would take you to reach it**, and **what you would carry**.

**Signs it's coming:**
- The booming fields fall away and only the high shriek remains. This is the thinning. You will feel it as the pulse going out of your chest.
- The listeners unfold their ears and turn in slow circles. If they run for the shafts, **you run too.** Don't finish packing. Grab your warm layer and go.
- A sudden, eerie stillness in the grass. The waves stop flowing.
- Locals moving purposefully toward shelter, or drum messages you can feel but can't read, repeated urgently.

**When it arrives:**
- Get in shelter immediately. Put on all insulation. Get into your sleeping bag if you can.
- Expect the silence to be psychologically awful: pressure in the skull, ears ringing into the vacuum, a sense of wrongness. Breathe. It passes.
- **Do not leave shelter when the wind stops, thinking it's calm.** The return front is the killer. It comes all at once, possibly from any direction, strong enough to flatten anything standing. Stay put until the chord has fully re-established, with steady booming and a settled pattern, and ideally until you see locals moving again.

If possible, find out from anyone who knows the Table what season the silences tend to come in, and time your visit away from them.

## Part Four: Water and Food

### Water

*The water is in the ground's fur.* You can squeeze clear, cold water from the hair-moss, and there are still pools at the bottom of sealed shafts.

- **Filter or treat everything anyway.** The water is fed by lowland fog carrying lowland biology, and it sits in living mats alongside rotting insects and seed. It may be clean. It may not be. Your gut is a stranger here.
- Bring a **squeeze filter** that you can load by wringing moss directly into it. Bring **purification tablets** as backup.
- A **fine mesh fog-catcher** that you can pin low to the ground can supplement your supply overnight.
- Carry a day's water. Moss is common but not everywhere, and drying winds can empty patches.
- Don't strip large patches of moss. It is the Table's water supply for everyone and everything. Take from many places lightly.

### Food

**Bring your own.** The plateau produces almost nothing of its own, and what it gets arrives unpredictably.

- High-calorie, compact food that needs no cooking. Altitude and constant bracing against wind burn a lot of energy, and altitude also kills appetite, so bring food you actually like.
- If you want hot food, you will need a stove that can be shielded. Cooking in the open is nearly impossible; you'll want to cook in a wall's lee or down in a shaft. Bring a windscreen.
- **Fire hazard.** The seed drifts are fermenting heaps, "sprouting from the top and rotting from the bottom." That material may be flammable. Heaps like that can even self-heat. Never light a flame near one. Never make an open fire unless you are with locals who know how. Wind-driven fire across dry grass could be catastrophic, and it would destroy people's homes, gardens, and plugs.
- **Local foods:** The locals roast insects that rain onto the rim each morning, and gather seed. If they offer you food, the social value of accepting may be high. But lowland insects can be toxic, and you don't know which seeds are safe. If you eat, eat small amounts of what they eat, prepared the way they prepare it, and watch for reactions. Don't forage on your own.
- **Don't trap the round brown birds.** They are few, they are part of the locals' food supply, and a stranger's hunting could matter to a population of a few hundred people.

## Part Five: Navigation

The Table is nearly flat, often fogbound, and empty to the horizon. It is easy to get lost and very hard to find your way back to the one narrow crack you came up.

### Before You Leave the Rim

- **Mark your arrival point obsessively.** Take photographs in several directions. Note the shape of nearby outcrops. Record coordinates if any positioning system works in this world. Note which rim the wind came from when you arrived and at what time.
