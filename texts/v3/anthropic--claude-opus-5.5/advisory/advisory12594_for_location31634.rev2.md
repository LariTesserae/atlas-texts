# Preparing for the Underleaf

The Underleaf is not dangerous the way most places are dangerous. Nothing hunts you, nothing poisons you, and nothing falls on you. The hazards are subtler. It is hot in a way that doesn't let you cool. It is lit in a way that flattens your eyes. It is quiet in a way that swallows your companions. And it is changeless in a way that works on the mind the way warm water works on a sleeper's hand.

Prepare for four things: the crossing to get there, the body's struggle in the heat, the practical problems of a place that forgets, and the harder problem of leaving.

---

## 1. The Crossing Above

The first danger isn't the Underleaf. It's the white plain around it.

You'll approach across salt-crust in full sun. There will be no shade, no water, and brutal glare. Treat this as a desert crossing:

- **Carry more water than you think you need for the approach and the return.** Budget the return as though you'll get nothing below. The cups are drinkable, but you shouldn't rely on them, for reasons below.
- **Protect your eyes.** Salt flats reflect enormous amounts of light. Bring wraparound sunglasses or glacier glasses. Snow blindness happens on salt too.
- **Cover everything.** Wear a wide-brimmed hat, long sleeves, and a neck cloth. Salt dust is abrasive and gets into cracked lips and skin. Bring lip balm and a barrier cream.
- **Keep your boots sealed.** Salt eats leather and stitching.
- **Time the approach for early morning.** Arrive at the lip before the plain becomes an oven.

The lip is crumbling. Test your footing before you trust it. From above, the canopy looks like a pond, and it may look solid enough to stand on. Don't assume it is.

Before you go through, **fix the location of your entry point in as many ways as you can.** Record GPS coordinates. Build a cairn or plant a tall, bright flag on the lip. Take compass bearings to distant landmarks. Note where you are along the crescent: inner curve, outer curve, near which horn. You'll understand why in section 5.

## 2. Getting Down

"You push through it and it closes behind you." Below that is a hundred and fifty meters of vertical lattice. There is no floor near the roof. You are climbing down through galleries, catwalks, and braided stems.

- **You need real climbing competence, or a guide who has it.** The lattice is dense and the stems are strong, so this probably isn't technical climbing. But it is sustained, three-dimensional downclimbing in heat, for a long time.
- **Bring a harness, a light static rope, and slings.** The stems are smooth, faintly translucent, and possibly slick with condensation. Slings girth-hitched around a stem give you anchors without cutting anything.
- **Wear gloves with tacky palms,** like gardening or rope-work gloves with rubberized grip. Bare hands on smooth, damp stems may slip, and gloves also spare the plant your skin oils.
- **Choose soft-soled, grippy shoes for the lattice** rather than stiff hiking boots. Pack those for the plain.
- **Go slowly.** You'll be climbing in glasshouse heat from the first meter. Descents cause more accidents than ascents, and fatigue makes them worse.

Remember the climb back up. It will be harder. You'll be tired and possibly dehydrated, and you'll be searching for an exit you can't see (again, section 5).

---

## 3. The Body in the Heat

This is the most serious physical danger, and it's easy to underestimate because the place feels so gentle.

The air is hot, saturated, and absolutely still. The description says water doesn't evaporate: the cup-pools don't lower and the beads on the leaves stay put. **You should assume your sweat will not evaporate either.** Sweat cools you only by evaporating. In a place where evaporation may be suspended, or at least crippled by humidity and dead air, your main cooling system may not work. People die of heatstroke this way in conditions that feel merely "muggy."

Prepare accordingly:

- **Acclimatize beforehand** if you can. Spend a week or two exercising in hot, humid conditions such as a greenhouse or a sauna under supervision.
- **Bring a way to move air.** A simple folding hand fan may be the most important object in your pack. A small battery fan is better if your equipment works there. Nothing in the Underleaf moves the air, so you'll have to do it yourself.
- **Monitor yourself.** Bring a thermometer. Learn the signs of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, dizziness, cramps, confusion, and the sweating that stops. Agree with your companions that any of these means an immediate halt and rest.
- **Move slowly and rest often.** Plan your days around half the exertion you'd manage elsewhere.
- **Take electrolytes.** Plain water isn't enough when you're sweating constantly. Bring salt tablets or electrolyte powder. The plain above is made of salt, but you should bring your own, measured.
- **Dress minimally but protectively.** Wear loose, thin, light-colored synthetic or fine merino. Cotton will soak and stay soaked. You don't need warmth at all. You'll never be cold here.
- **Expect nothing to dry.** Bring several changes of socks and underwear in sealed bags. Bring anti-fungal powder and chafe balm. The Underleaf may have no rot and no fungus of its own, but you brought your own microbes, and they will love this climate. Check your feet, armpits, and groin every day.

Sleeping is easy. The root-felt is springy and warm. You don't need a sleeping bag, only a thin sheet, and perhaps not even that. A lightweight hammock of your own lets air reach your back. Do not use the one that's already there.

## 4. Water and Food

**Food:** Nothing here fruits. There is nothing to eat. Bring everything, and bring food that doesn't need cooking (more on fire below). It's possible your food won't spoil here. It's equally possible it will, since it isn't the plant. Pack as though it will, and be pleasantly surprised if not.

**Water:** The cup-plants hold clear, sweet, blood-warm water, and people have drunk it. But consider:

- **It may not refill.** The water doesn't evaporate, but nothing says it replenishes. There is no rain and no flow. The leaf-bowl left by a past traveler "still holds a little water," which suggests water here is *preserved*, not renewed. Every pool you drink may simply be gone. Drink sparingly, from many pools rather than draining one.
- **Some pools are inhabited.** The clear shrimp live in some of them. Choose empty pools. Don't drink from a pool while something in it is watching you. You don't need to know why that's good advice.
- **Bring a filter or purification tablets anyway,** out of habit and humility.
- **Blood-warm water won't cool you.** Plan for this. Don't expect a refreshing drink.

**Fire: don't.** Don't light a stove, a candle, or a cigarette. The heat is already bad. More importantly, in a place where the air doesn't move and mist banks keep their edges, **smoke may simply hang where you made it, possibly for a very long time.** Anything you release into the air should be assumed to stay: smoke, spray, strong scents. Eat cold food.

**Waste:** You can't dig; the floor is root, all the way down. Bring sealable bags and pack out everything, including human waste. The place may forget your mess. Don't make that experiment.

## 5. Light, Time, and Seeing

### The shadowless light

The light comes from every direction. Nothing casts a shadow. This sounds beautiful, and it is, but it has consequences:

- **Your depth perception will degrade.** Shadows are one of the brain's main cues for shape, distance, and the edges of things. On a lattice of pale stems a hundred meters up, you may misjudge where a catwalk ends or how far the next handhold is. **Probe before you step.** A telescoping trekking pole is good for the galleries.
- **Everything will blend.** The moths vanish into the leaves. The stems are the color of the light. Your companions will too, if they wear green or tan. **Wear a strong, unnatural color,** like safety orange, red, or magenta, so that each of you stays visible against the green-gold.
- **Expect visual fatigue.** Some people get headaches from shadowless environments, the way some do in whiteouts. Bring something with dark, hard edges to rest your eyes on: a book with black print, a dark cloth. Mildly tinted sunglasses may help.

### No night, possibly

The light comes from the sun through the roof. But the description suggests the dark belongs to the upper world, not to this place. Prepare for **the possibility that it never gets dark.**

- Bring a **sleep mask.**
- Bring a **watch with a date,** preferably mechanical as well as digital, in case one fails. Without day and night, time will blur quickly.
- Keep a written **log of sleeps and meals.** It anchors you, and later it will be evidence that time passed.

### Navigation is genuinely hard

There's no sun to navigate by, because the light has no direction. The roof looks the same everywhere from below. Distance doesn't dim anything, so far things look deceptively near. And **the place forgets marks.** Scratch a stem, and the scratch is gone. Footprints in the root-felt presumably vanish. You can't blaze a trail.

So:

- **Bring a compass,** but don't trust it until you've tested it against a known bearing inside.
- **Bring an altimeter.** Knowing your height above the floor helps you find the roof again.
- **Map as you go** with bearings, paces, and sketches of distinctive gallery structures.
- **Use the one thing that lasts.** The knots past travelers tied from braided tendril have held; the plant didn't absorb them. Things made *of the plant, shaped by hands*, seem to stay. The hammock stayed. The leaf-bowl stayed. So **learn to braid and knot tendril before you go,** and mark your route by tying small, distinctive knots in the hanging filament. Don't cut anything; use loose tendril and tie gently. This is the most important navigational technique you'll have. Make your knots unlike anyone else's so you don't follow someone else's decades-old trail by mistake.
- **Don't count on caches.** Whether imported objects persist is uncertain. The seeds did. Bodies didn't. Keep essentials on your person.
- **Don't treat the knot-arches as signposts.** Some past travelers bound stems into doorways "leading from one part of the gallery to another identical part." Don't assume they lead anywhere. Don't assume they don't.

### The mist banks

Some fog banks have edges. You can put your arm in and pull it out beaded. Don't walk into one you can't see through, especially on high galleries. They might hide a drop, and in a shadowless world you can't read a floor through haze. Treat them as rooms with no known floor. If you must pass through, go roped to someone outside.

## 6. Sound and Companionship

Voices don't echo. They soak into the tendrils and vanish. Speaking feels like shouting in a library.

- **You'll lose each other easily.** A companion around a column may not hear you call. Agree on visual signals and check-in intervals. Line of sight is long, which is your advantage.
- **Bring a whistle,** but test how far it carries on the first day before trusting it.
- **Consider radios,** tested at the lip, with low expectations.
- **Travel in pairs at minimum.** Solo visits are for people who have already been once and returned.
- **Expect to whisper.** Most visitors do. The silence is heavy, and you'll find yourself lowering your voice without meaning to. This is fine, but it makes calling for help harder. Practice using a loud, clear voice once so you know you still can.

The faint ticking inside the stems is harmless as far as anyone knows. The rare deep note, like a finger on a glass rim, is unexplained. When you hear it, note the time and your direction if you can. Don't go looking for the source.

## 7. The Other Inhabitants

**The moths.** Pale and nearly invisible, they're everywhere. You'll brush past many. Don't catch them, don't collect them, and don't try to find out whether they die. If one takes off, stand still until it settles.

**The shrimp.** Clear, small, hanging in the cup-pools, turning to keep its face to you. It's the only thing that seems to watch. Let it. Don't touch it. Don't put your hands in its pool. Drink elsewhere. Some visitors find it unnerving, and some find it companionable. Either reaction is fine, but don't provoke it.

**The large pale thing on the high galleries.** Many-limbed, the size of a dog or a deer, it moves without hurry. It has been seen perhaps a dozen times, never eating, never resting, never leaving. If you see it:

- Stay still, or move slowly out of its path.
- Don't follow it.
- Don't block a gallery it's traveling.
- Don't photograph it with a flash.
- Write down what you saw as soon as you can, before the place smooths the memory.

No one has reported it harming anyone. That isn't the same as saying it's harmless. Give it the courtesy you'd give anything old and unknown.

---

## 8. The Traces: Etiquette

People have been here, and a few of their works have stayed. Treat them as you'd treat a shrine you don't understand.

- **Don't untie the knots.** They may be the only permanent things in the Underleaf that aren't the plant. They're also someone's words.
- **Don't disturb the seed circle.** Walk around it. Nothing inside is different, and you don't need to test whether that stays true with you in it.
- **Don't drink from the leaf-bowl.** It's been holding that little water for a long time.
- **Don't lie in the hammock.** You don't know that it's empty.
- **Don't take anything out.** Not a leaf, a filament, a seed, or a bead of water. You probably couldn't. A cutting would likely be forgotten, or might not be. You don't want to learn which.
- **Don't damage the plant to see it forget.** Everyone wants to try this. The place allows it, but it's a poor way to meet somewhere. And if the Underleaf can forget a scratch, you should wonder carefully what else it forgets.

If you want to leave something, tie a knot. That seems to be the only kind of mark the Underleaf consents to keep.

---

## 9. Your Own Wounds

Don't assume the forgetting applies to you. Your body isn't the plant. A cut is still a cut, and in this heat and humidity it's more likely to fester, not less. Bring a good first-aid kit: antiseptic, waterproof dressings, blister treatment, and treatment for heat illness and dehydration. Clean wounds promptly.

It's also possible that the place's forgetting touches you in ways you won't notice, like fatigue that doesn't accumulate or hair that doesn't grow. See the next section.

---

## 10. The Mind

This is the real danger, and the one people least prepare for.

The Underleaf is warm, bright, damp, safe, and perfectly still. Nothing breaks. Nothing rots. Nothing is ever cold or in shadow. The beads of water never fall. It is the first morning of a garden, held forever. Something in a human being answers that powerfully, and it isn't entirely a healthy answer. People who stay a long time say the changelessness is exactly the problem. There are no bodies and no graves, and the traces suggest that some who came didn't go.

Protect yourself:

- **Set a hard departure date before you enter,** and tell someone in the upper world. Make them promise to come looking, or to raise an alarm, if you don't return.
- **Bring things that change.** You'll be the only thing in the Underleaf that does, and you need proof that change is still real:
  - a watch or clock that visibly ticks
  - a journal, written in daily
  - photos of people and places you love
  - a small growing thing, like a sprouting seed in a sealed jar, so you can watch it put out a leaf
- **Keep counting.** Sleeps, meals, days. When you catch yourself not caring what day it is, that's the signal to leave.
- **Name your reasons for returning** before you go, and write them down. Read them each "morning."
- **Watch your companions for stillness.** If one of you starts sitting for long periods, speaking less, or stops writing, becoming lit on both sides like everything else, take them out. Don't wait for them to agree.
- **Leave while you still want to stay a little longer.** Don't wait until you're ready. You may never be.

---

## 11. Leaving, and After

**The climb out.** Follow your knots back to the roof. Push up through it. It closed behind you, and it may take some force to part. You'll come out into the white plain. The glare, the dry heat, the moving air, and the *shadows* will feel violent. Have your sunglasses and water ready in an outer pocket before you break the surface. Your skin, soaked for days, may crack in the dry air. Bring moisturizer and cover up fast.

**The weeks after.** People who leave say they sometimes wake in the dark in the upper world and miss the Underleaf so badly they can't breathe. Take that seriously:

- Expect disorientation, low mood, and trouble sleeping in darkness.
- A **dim nightlight** by your bed helps some people. So does a humidifier, or a warm, damp room.
- Tell someone what you're feeling. It is a kind of grief.
- Don't plan an immediate return. If you go back, go deliberately, after enough time to know whether it's desire or withdrawal.
- Keep your journal. On bad nights, read the entries where you wrote *I want to go home.* You meant it then.

## Packing List Summary

**For the plain:** plenty of water, glacier glasses, sun-protective clothing and hat, sealed boots, barrier cream, lip balm, a marker flag or cairn materials, GPS.

**For the descent:** harness, light static rope, slings, tacky gloves, grippy soft-soled shoes, trekking pole.

**For the heat:** hand fan (essential), battery fan, thermometer, electrolytes, light synthetic or merino clothing, several changes of socks and underwear in dry bags, anti-fungal powder, chafe balm, thin sheet, personal hammock.

**For sustenance:** all your food, no-cook only; water purification; waste bags.

**For seeing and time:** brightly colored outer layer, sleep mask, mechanical and digital watches, compass, altimeter, notebook and pencils, tinted glasses, something dark to rest your eyes on.

**For finding your way:** tendril-knotting practice beforehand, a distinctive personal knot, map sketches, bearings.

**For each other:** whistle, radios, agreed signals, a partner.

**For the mind:** departure date, an outside contact, photos, journal, a sprouting seed in a jar, your written reasons to return.

**For after:** nightlight, moisturizer, patience.

**Do not bring:** anything that burns, sprays, or smells strong, or any intention to take something home.

---

## A Last Thought

Go to the Underleaf the way you'd go to someone sleeping peacefully: quietly, gratefully, without touching more than you must, and without lying down beside them. It will be the most beautiful place you've ever stood. That is the reason to go, and it is also the thing to prepare for.
