# Preparing for the Rising Country

## A Traveler's Briefing

The Rising Country will not try to kill you in any single dramatic way. It will kill you through an accumulation of small wrongnesses, each one survivable alone: thin air, bad air, cold air over hot ground, darkness that isn't night, ground that won't hold still, sound that doesn't carry, and no water anywhere. Most dangerous of all, it will quietly impair the judgment you need to manage the rest.

Prepare for it as a combination of a Himalayan expedition above 8,000 meters, a volcanologist's fieldwork on an active lava field, and a sterile-protocol visit to a place where life has barely begun. Then add one principle that most expeditions never face: **there is no "down" to retreat to.**

---

## 1. The Air: Your First and Constant Problem

### How high you are

The description gives clues. Without the ash the sky would be "blue-black" and full of "hard stars," and every breath feels half-taken. That places you somewhere between the high Himalaya and the edge of what an unpressurized human can survive. Assume **7,000–8,500 meters** until you measure it. You could be higher.

Bring a **barometric altimeter** and a **pulse oximeter**. Check the pressure the moment you arrive and plan everything from that number.

### Arrival is the deadliest moment

Mountaineers acclimatize over weeks, climbing high and sleeping low. A multiverse traveler steps from sea-level air straight into near-stratospheric thinness. At these altitudes, the time of useful consciousness for an unacclimatized person can be minutes, and at the upper end much less. Hypoxia does not feel like suffocation. It feels like mild confusion, mild euphoria, and a sense that everything is fine.

- **Arrive already breathing supplemental oxygen.** Put the mask on before you cross, not after.
- **Pre-acclimatize if you can.** Spend real time at genuine altitude on your home world (a staged high-mountain program), or at minimum do extended hypoxic-tent training. This won't make the Country comfortable. It will make it survivable for longer and give you a margin if your oxygen system fails.
- **Carry redundant oxygen.** Bring two regulators, spare masks, and more cylinders than your planned stay requires. Oxygen is your time budget. When it runs low, you leave, whatever you are in the middle of.

### Treat altitude illness without descent

On an ordinary mountain, the cure for pulmonary or cerebral edema is to go down. Here the whole plateau is at extreme height. You cannot walk out of it.

- Carry a **portable hyperbaric chamber** (a Gamow-style bag). It is the only "descent" available to you.
- Carry the standard medications: acetazolamide for prevention, dexamethasone for cerebral edema, and nifedipine for pulmonary edema. Know the doses before you go, because you will not think clearly enough to read instructions there.
- **Your real exit is your portal.** If anyone shows signs of HACE (confusion, loss of coordination, severe headache) or HAPE (breathlessness at rest, a wet cough), the correct response is to leave the Country entirely.

### Fix your decisions in advance

Hypoxia erodes judgment first, so decide everything important on your home world and write it down: turnaround times, oxygen reserves at which you abort, gas-meter readings at which you retreat, and conditions under which you do not approach a fissure. Laminate the list and carry it where you can see it. When you are up there and it feels reasonable to stay a little longer, **it isn't. That feeling is the hypoxia.**

## 2. The Poisoned Air: Gas in the Hollows

One sentence in the description should frighten you more than the fire: *"in the hollows between boulders the heat pools and shimmers."*

Active volcanic ground releases carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and sometimes carbon monoxide. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and collects invisibly in low places. The warm, sheltered, shimmering hollow that looks like the only comfortable spot on the plateau is exactly where a lethal gas pocket would sit. People and animals have died in such places on Earth by kneeling down to rest.

The pale sulfur-and-salt "dew" crystallizing around the vents confirms that sulfurous gases are present.

**Prepare:**

- **Carry a multi-gas detector** (O₂, CO₂, SO₂, H₂S, CO) clipped at chest height, plus a second one you can lower on a cord into hollows, doorways, and fissure edges before you go near them. Test it and calibrate it for low pressure before the trip.
- **Filter the gases your oxygen mask won't.** Supplemental oxygen raises your oxygen levels but does not remove sulfur dioxide or ash. You need an integrated system: a sealed full-face mask fed by your oxygen supply, with particulate (P100) and acid-gas filtration. This is specialized equipment. Test it at simulated altitude, because a mask that works at sea level may leak or choke at low pressure.
- **Stay on the heights.** Ridge crests and wind-exposed ground clear gas fastest. Hollows, basins, enclosed structures, and the lee sides of large boulders collect it.
- **Never enter a low, enclosed space without testing it first.** That includes the corbelled hut (see §11).

## 3. Cold Air Over Hot Ground: Dressing for a Thermal Sandwich

Your body will be caught between two opposite environments. The air is bitterly cold, probably well below freezing, and a thin, fast wind strips heat from exposed skin. Meanwhile the ground ranges from comfortably warm, through hot, to kiln-hot at the edges of the wide chasms.

### Boots

Your boots must do contradictory jobs. They need insulation against cold air, soles rated for hot surfaces (volcanologists' boots use heat-resistant rubber that won't melt or soften on hot rock), and aggressive tread for glassy, fractured stone.

There is a real trade-off in sole thickness. Thick soles protect you, but you will need to *feel* the ground: tremors, grinding, the vibration of distant rockfalls that you won't hear. Choose a stiff but not excessively thick sole, and test in the field whether you can feel a heavy object dropped nearby.

### Clothing

- **Layer for cold** with high-altitude down or synthetic insulation, windproof on the outside.
- **Choose a smooth, ash-shedding outer shell.** Volcanic ash is powdered glass. It clings to fleece and brushed fabrics and grinds away at fibers, zippers, and skin. Ripstop nylon sheds it better.
- **Carry heat-resistant gloves over your insulated mitts** for handling rock near fissures and vents. "Warm as a hand" is the pleasant end of the range, not the whole range.
- **Bring a face shield or radiant-heat hood** if you plan to approach the wide chasms. The heat from them is radiant, like a furnace mouth, and it can burn exposed skin faster than you'd expect even while the air around you is freezing.

### Don't be seduced by the warmth

The ground's warmth will feel like comfort, a hearth in a frozen world, and you'll want to lie on it. Remember the local rule: *the warmth looks like comfort and comes from a wound.* Warm ground is ground with heat and gas moving beneath it, and it is where new fissures are likeliest to open. Enjoy it through your boots and your seat pad. Don't sleep on the warmest patch you can find.

## 4. The Ground Itself: Walking on Something Unfinished

### Fresh rock is sharp

Nothing here has been weathered. Every edge is a fresh fracture, and obsidian breaks into edges sharper than surgical steel. Expect cuts through gloves, clothing, and boot uppers.

- Wear gloves with cut-resistant palms.
- Pack extra wound care: irrigation, closure strips, sutures if you're trained, and antiseptic.
- Treat the obsidian curtains as blades, not walls. Don't brace yourself against them.

### Nothing is settled

*"Boulders balance in positions that gravity has not yet had time to argue with."* Every boulder is a potential trap. Don't climb on precariously perched stones, don't shelter beneath overhangs, and don't pass below slopes steeper than about 30° if you can avoid it.

- **Wear a climbing helmet at all times.**
- **Carry a probe pole or trekking pole** and test the ground ahead, especially over thin crusts. The underside bubbles on tilted slabs show that some rock cooled against voids that are no longer filled.

### Fissures

- **Hair-thin fissures** are the insidious ones. You can step across them without noticing, and they "wander." A hair-thin crack under your foot today could be something else tomorrow. Note them and keep your weight moving.
- **Chasms** of a body's width are lethal, from the fall and from the heat. Keep a respectful distance, never approach alone, and if you need to look in, do it roped and belayed, wearing a heat shield, with your gas meter lowered first.
- **Listen for the tearing-cloth sound.** A new fissure announces itself sharply and briefly. When you hear it, stop and feel for where it came from before moving.

### Rope

Bring a rope and basic glacier-travel skills. The logic is similar to crossing a crevasse field: travel roped in pairs or small teams, spaced apart, so that one person falling through a crust or into a newly opened gap can be held.

### Shaking

When the plateau shudders, get low, get away from tall rock and slopes above you, and wait. If you're near the northern ridges, which "shed," move away from them as soon as the shaking stops, because avalanches may follow.

## 5. The Dark: Seeing by the Ground

This is perhaps the least intuitive part of the Country. There is no sky-light. The ash ceiling hides the sun and stars, and whatever illumination exists comes up from below in dull orange.

### What underglow does to your eyes

- **Lighting from below inverts every shadow cue** your brain uses. Ridges and hollows can visually swap. Depth perception will be poor, and distances will be hard to judge.
- **The light is monochrome orange-red,** so color distinctions mostly vanish. You'll have trouble telling a patch of the dark living film from wet stone, or a thin fissure from a dark vein in the rock.
- **The light pulses** with the vents over minutes, which disrupts your dark adaptation and your sense of what's out there.

### Bring your own light

- A strong **headlamp**, and a second one.
- A **handheld floodlight or wide-beam torch** to light terrain from above and restore normal shadow cues so ridges look like ridges again.
- A **red or low-intensity mode** for moments when you want to preserve what night vision the underglow allows.
- **Many spare batteries, kept warm** inside your clothing, because cold air drains them fast.
- **Chemical light sticks or small LED markers** to mark a route, a hazard, or a companion's position.

A thermal imager would be extraordinarily useful. It would let you see which ground is hottest, where gas-warmed hollows lie, and where new fissures are forming beneath a crust. If you can bring one, bring one.

## 6. The Ash-Fronts: Standing in Blindness

Without warning, a plume crosses the plateau and "the world goes fully black." This is literal zero visibility, full of glassy grit, while you stand on shifting, fissured ground.

### Protect your body

- **Wear sealed goggles** (not ordinary sunglasses) whenever you're outdoors. Ash is abrasive glass. It scratches corneas, and rubbing your eyes makes it worse. Carry saline eyewash.
- **Keep your mask filtered and sealed.** Inhaled ash damages the lungs, and at altitude your lungs are already your weakest system.
- **Seal your electronics.** Ash is abrasive and, when damp, can be conductive. Keep devices in sealed pouches and cameras in housings, and don't change lenses outside.

### Protocol for when a front hits

1. **Stop.** Don't try to walk out of it. You cannot see the ground, and the ground may be opening.
2. **Get low** and brace against stable ground, not against a boulder that might roll.
3. **Stay tethered** to your companions. Clip in and keep physical contact.
4. **Watch your gas meter.** Plumes may carry more than ash.
5. **Wait it out.** Feel the warmth of the ground and breathe through your filter.
6. **When it passes, stop and re-survey before moving.** The description is clear that the terrain may have changed while you couldn't see it: a boulder upright before may now be lying on its side. Your mental map just expired.

### Warning signs

- **The obsidian curtains ring** when the ash-wind strikes them. If you hear that thin, glassy tone, the wind is moving hard and fronts may be coming. On "the worst nights," whole slopes of them sing together.
- **Watch the vents.** Learn their rhythm, because a change in it may precede a plume.

## 7. The Quiet: A World That Won't Carry Your Voice

Thin air carries sound poorly. That sounds peaceful, but it is a hazard.

- **You won't hear danger coming.** A rockslide half a mile away arrives as a soft hiss. You'll *feel* many events through your feet before, or instead of, hearing them. Train yourself to pay attention to the ground under you.
- **Your companions won't hear you** either. Shouting will be feeble and tiring at altitude. Carry **radios** for everyone, preferably with voice-activated headsets that fit under your mask and hood. Agree on simple signals: a whistle, a light flash, rope tugs.
- **Listen to the rock.** The ticking of cooling and heating rock is constant background. A change in it, or in the deep groan you can hear by putting your ear to a boulder, may tell you something is shifting nearby. The description says the mountain has a voice. Learn to listen to it the way sailors listen to the hull.

## 8. Navigation: Learning a Pattern, Not a Map

*"Anyone who crossed this country would have to learn it as a pattern rather than a map."*

### Your instruments will struggle

- **Satellite positioning** almost certainly doesn't exist in this universe.
- **A magnetic compass** may be unreliable. Basalt is rich in magnetite, and freshly cooled volcanic rock can distort local fields. Test it early against a known bearing.
- **Maps don't survive.** The terrain rises a hand's width a day, sometimes in lurches. Fissures open and close. Ridges shed.

### What works instead

- **An inertial or step-counting navigation device**, or at minimum a disciplined log of distance and bearing.
- **A persistent beacon at your arrival point** (see §13), which you can home in on by radio.
- **Learning the patterns** the description names. Keep a field notebook of them from the moment you arrive:
  - The ground rises in the east.
  - The ground falls in the south.
  - The northern ridges shed stone.
  - The vents breathe every few minutes.
  - The big fissures stay open, and the small ones wander.
- **Reading the big chasms and major vents as landmarks.** They change most slowly.
- **Using the built stones as reference points.** They drift with the ground beneath them, but they are distinct, recognizable, and not re-formed daily. Just remember that they are moving and tilting too.

**Take time-lapse photos** of your surroundings from fixed points. They'll show you how the land moves, which is both practical and one of the wonders of the place.

## 9. Water and Food: There Is Nothing Here

There has never been water here. There is no snow to melt, no ice, and no stream.

- **Carry all the water you'll need, plus a reserve.** At altitude, in dry wind, you lose water quickly through breathing, and dehydration worsens altitude illness. Budget around 4–5 liters per person per day. That weight will limit your stay, and that's fine, because your stay should be short anyway.
- **Keep water from freezing** in the cold air. Insulated bottles, or bottles carried inside your clothing, work. Resting a bottle on warm ground sounds clever, but only if you're sure the ground isn't hot enough to damage it and isn't pooling gas.
- **Do not touch, taste, or collect the mineral dew.** It's a crust of sulfur compounds and salts, likely acidic and potentially toxic. It is not water.
- **Bring high-calorie, easy-to-eat food.** Appetite collapses at altitude, so bring things you'll actually want to eat: fats, sweets, warm drinks. Eating through a mask is awkward. Plan short, gas-checked "mask-off" breaks on windy ridges for eating and drinking, and keep them brief.
- **Water boils at a lower temperature** at this altitude, perhaps around 75°C, so cooking takes longer and some foods won't cook properly. A pressure-cook system or ready-to-eat meals are better. Don't try to cook on a fissure, however tempting that seems.
