# Preparing for The Pans

## The Short Version

You are going to a place that is hot, humid, blindingly bright, and very quiet in its terrain but deafening in its life. The ground is ancient, slick where it's wet, and almost featureless for navigation. The water you see is often a mirage, and the real water belongs to the animals that live by it. Nobody will come for you if something goes wrong. The two big dangers are not what they seem. It isn't the snakes or the lizards. It's **heat combined with getting lost**, and **the afternoon storm that finds you as the tallest thing on the plain.**

Go slowly, carry your own water and shade, walk on rock instead of mud, and plan your days around the land's rhythm. Do that and this will be one of the most extraordinary places you ever stand in.

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Your intuition will likely mislead you about this place in three ways.

**It looks empty but it is crowded.** From a distance the plain reads as barren red rock and sky. In fact every pan, gnamma, seep, and overhang is densely occupied. Nearly all of your ecological impact, and most of your encounters with animals, will happen where life is concentrated. Those are also the places you will be most drawn to. Assume every pool, overhang, and patch of green already belongs to something.

**It looks flat but it isn't.** The rises are under head height, spread over kilometers. You'll feel tilt without seeing direction. This matters for navigation, because the horizon offers almost no landmarks. It also matters for weather, because sheet water will move across the rock in ways you can't predict by eye.

**It's wet but it's a drought land.** Three days after rain the place feels almost tropical. Water is standing everywhere and the air is thick. But this is a brief interruption. The water is evaporating, not draining, and it is getting saltier and scarcer every day. The later in the dry-down you arrive or stay, the more your trip becomes a desert trip.

---

## Timing Your Visit

The description places you three days after rain, with "weeks before the land forgets." Within that window the character of the place shifts.

- **Early (days 3–10):** The pans are broad and shallow. The moss is vivid green, the shrimp are hatching, and the frog chorus is at full strength. Water is abundant but spread thin, so walking is wetter and the rock is slicker. Afternoon storms are more likely to renew everything. Insects and humidity are at their worst.
- **Middle:** The shorelines are retreating and the sedge rings show their bands. Wildlife begins concentrating, which makes this the best time for watching animals. Walking gets easier.
- **Late dry-down:** The last pools are crowded and frantic. Salt crusts form, and the pans are pale, dazzling floors. The clay may crack on the surface while it is still soft below. Heat intensifies with less evaporative cooling. Water sources become unreliable and often undrinkable.

Decide in advance how long you're staying. Build your water plan around the assumption that local sources are shrinking and worsening from the day you arrive.

## Heat, Humidity, and Light

This is the single most important section.

### Heat and humidity together
Humid heat is more dangerous than dry heat because sweat doesn't evaporate well. You will feel sticky rather than cooled, and you can overheat while believing you're coping. The pans breathe water into the air, so the humidity near them is highest exactly where you'll want to spend time.

- **Acclimatize if you can** by spending a week or two in hot, humid conditions before the trip.
- **Plan to rest through midday.** From roughly two hours after sunrise until mid-afternoon, move as little as possible. The animals do the same thing for the same reason.
- **Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.** Carry electrolyte salts, because you'll sweat heavily. Plain water alone can leave you depleted.
- **Learn the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke** in yourself and any companions: headache, nausea, confusion, stopping sweating, clumsiness. Confusion is especially dangerous here because navigation depends on clear thinking.

### Carry your own shade
Shade is rare, short, and **occupied**. The cool overhangs belong to wallabies, bats, nesting birds, and almost certainly snakes and wasps. The lone trees are few and far apart, and they are exactly where you should *not* be during a storm.

Bring portable shade:
- A lightweight tarp with a few trekking poles or a collapsible frame. You can't count on anchoring it to trees, and pegs won't go into rock. Bring cord and plan to weigh the corners with loose stones.
- A reflective trekking umbrella works well here for walking in the heat.

### Light from above and below
Every pan is a mirror and every wet rock face flares. Sun will reach you from underneath as well as above.

- **Polarized sunglasses with side protection**, or glacier-style glasses. This is not optional. Hours of reflected glare cause eye strain, headaches, and potentially photokeratitis. Bring a spare pair.
- **A wide-brimmed hat with a dark underside**, since a pale brim reflects glare up into your face.
- **Sunscreen under your chin, nose, and ears, and on the backs of your legs.** Those are the places reflected light burns that you won't think to protect.
- **Long, loose, light-colored sleeves and trousers**, in a quick-drying synthetic or linen.

## Navigation: The Place Where People Get Lost

The land feels featureless: low swells, silver water, red rock, a lone tree, repeated to the horizon. Distances are hard to judge. By midday the mirages float false water between real pans and blur the difference. A person could walk for an hour toward a "pan" that doesn't exist, turn around, and fail to recognize anything.

### Don't trust your compass blindly
The ground here is ironstone and laterite, which is iron-rich rock. **Magnetic compasses may be deflected**, possibly badly and inconsistently, especially close to the ground or near outcrops. Test your compass on arrival against the sun, or against a known bearing, and don't rely on it alone.

### Use the land's own compasses
- **The termite mounds are aligned north–south.** Their flat faces point east and west and their thin edges point north and south, to minimize sun exposure. They are a natural compass rose built into the landscape. Learn to read them early.
- **The sun and stars.** The sky is enormous and clean after rain. Know how to find direction by the sun's path and by the night sky. If you don't know which hemisphere's sky this resembles, figure it out on the first night.

### Build your own landmarks
- On arrival, climb the largest nearby whaleback and study the view. Note the bluish domes on the horizon, lines of termite spires, lone trees, and which pans flash white because they have already dried.
- **Record waypoints obsessively.** Use GPS if it works in this world, plus sketches and written notes: "tree with forked crown, 20 minutes past third termite line."
- Consider small removable markers, such as a ribbon on a pole. Remove every one of them when you leave.
- Pans change shape daily as they shrink. **Don't use shorelines as landmarks.** Use rock domes, trees, and termite mounds, which change on the scale of centuries.

### Rules for yourself
- Never walk toward water you can't confirm is water.
- Set a turnaround time and keep to it.
- Don't split up at dusk (see "Noise" below).
- Assume there is no rescue. Satellite messengers may not function in this world. Leave your itinerary with whoever manages your crossing, and move with conservative margins.

## Weather: The Afternoon Storm

Clouds build every afternoon. Most days nothing comes, but some days a short, hard storm drums across the rock.

**Lightning is the real risk.** On a plain where nothing but a few trees and termite mounds rises above head height, *you* are a prominent object.

- Watch the clouds from early afternoon. If towers are building dark and tall, get off the whaleback tops and away from lone trees and termite mounds. Don't shelter in a rock overhang mouth, because current can arc across the gap.
- Choose a lower area, but know that low areas collect sheet water. You'll get wet, and that is acceptable.
- If lightning is close, crouch on your insulated sleeping pad with your feet together and head low. Spread out from companions by several meters.
- Carry a proper rain shell. The storm will be cold relative to the heat, and being soaked afterward in wind can chill you surprisingly.

After the storm, **every sheet of water starts moving again**. It won't be deep enough to sweep you away, but it makes the granite very slippery and can refill channels you'd planned to cross dry.

## Walking on This Ground

The ground is ancient, rounded, and deceptive.

### Footwear
- The **granite is slick where water runs**, and the seep lines carry mats of blue-black algae that are nearly frictionless when wet. You need soft, sticky rubber soles of the kind used for approach shoes or canyoneering.
- The **laterite is hard and abrasive**. It rings under a heel and will wear down soft soles and skin. Pick shoes that balance grip and durability, and bring a spare pair or a repair kit.
- Shoes will get wet. Choose ones that drain and dry quickly, and bring several pairs of socks. Your feet will spend weeks in warm, wet conditions, and fungal infection and maceration are real concerns. Dry your feet every evening and consider foot powder.
- **Gaiters** keep grit and clay out of your shoes and help against snakes.

### Hazards underfoot
- **Exfoliation sheets:** the curved slabs lying around the whalebacks rock and clack when stepped on. They may shift or crack underfoot. Avoid stepping on them where you can. Apart from the risk to your ankles, that hollow clack carries across the plain and startles everything nearby.
- **Algae-covered seeps:** treat every dark stripe on the rock as if it were ice. Also, these are living mats. Walking on them damages them.
- **Crusted pan floors** in the late dry-down may look solid but can break through into soft clay.

### The pans: don't walk in them
The clay under the water is "soft as custard and warm as blood." Its depth is unknowable, and it can suck at your legs and hold your shoes. More importantly, the pan floor is a seed bank and an egg bank. Shrimp eggs, some decades old, lie in that clay, and frogs lie cocooned beneath it. Walking across a pan damages a nursery that has been waiting years.

- **Walk around the pans on the rock.** This will take longer, so plan for it.
- If you truly must cross wet or soft ground, use **trekking poles** to probe ahead, step slowly, and keep to the edges.
- Trekking poles are generally useful here for balance on slick granite and for probing.

## Water: Abundant-Looking, Complicated in Practice

### Carry most of what you need
You may see water in every direction, but plan as if you'll find **none that's safe**. How much you need depends on how you're supplied across the multiverse. If you can't carry enough for your whole stay, establish a cache or a resupply route before you rely on local water.

### If you must use local water
- **Gnammas are the best option.** They hold clear, tea-colored water, stained by tannins and not necessarily harmful. They also contain dead insects, grit, and anything that has drunk from them, including birds, wallabies, and parrots by the hundred.
  - Draw from the cooler lower layer if you can do it without stirring the silt.
  - **Filter, then disinfect** with chemical treatment or UV, or boil it.
- **Never drink from near the blue-black algae mats.** These are likely cyanobacteria, which can produce toxins that filters and boiling don't remove. An activated-carbon stage may reduce this risk but won't eliminate it. Avoid that water entirely.
- **Don't drink pan water.** It's full of sediment and life, and as the pans shrink it grows saltier. Late in the dry-down it may be brine.

### Water ethics
The gnammas are the plain's lifeline. The parrots, wallabies, and finches depend on them, and so do the pale star-leaved rosettes growing submerged inside them. Those plants have to flower before the pool dries or they will die.

- **Take only what you need and never take a pool's last water.** Late in the dry-down, a gnamma may be the only water for an entire population of animals.
- **Never bathe, swim, or wash in a gnamma or pan.** It will be tempting, especially in a basin "wide enough to lie down in." Your sunscreen, repellent, soap, and skin oils are pollutants in a closed system that never drains. Carry water away from the source to wash, and scatter wastewater on bare rock far from pools.
- Don't disturb the silt, and don't dip containers that have held anything else.

## Wildlife: Respect and Precautions

### Snakes
"Snakes follow the frogs." Expect them at pan margins, in the sedge rings, around gnammas, and in overhangs, especially at dusk and night when frogs are active.

- Wear gaiters and long trousers. At dusk and night, **use a headlamp and watch every step**, particularly near water.
- Never put your hands into crevices or under rock sheets without looking first.
- Learn **snakebite first aid**. If these snakes resemble the Australian elapids that this landscape recalls, the standard response is a pressure-immobilization bandage: firm, broad elastic bandaging of the whole limb, plus a splint, while keeping the victim still. Carry at least two broad elastic bandages. Don't cut, suck, or apply a tourniquet.
- Understand that your evacuation options may be very limited. Prevention is everything.

### Monitor lizards
They're generally not aggressive, but they are strong, have sharp claws, and may run *up* a person if frightened, mistaking you for a tree. Give them space and never corner one. Keep food sealed and away from your camp.

### Insects
- **Midges** rise in humming columns from the pans, and after rain there will almost certainly be **mosquitoes**, even though they aren't mentioned. Bring a **head net**, strong repellent (picaridin or DEET, applied far from water), and treated clothing.
- Be careful about disease: you don't know the pathogens of this world. Minimizing bites is your best protection.
- Expect wasps and bees at the water and in overhangs.
- Cuts and scrapes infect easily in hot, wet conditions. Clean and cover every scratch from the laterite promptly.

### The rest
- **Wallabies** are harmless and shy. You'll hear their quick thumps on the stone before you see them. Keep still in the evening and they'll come to drink.
- **Parrots** at the gnammas are loud and startle easily. Watch from a distance and don't approach a drinking flock, since each disturbance costs them water and energy in the heat.
- **Bats** stream out at dusk. It's a wonderful sight. Don't enter their overhangs.
- **Nesting birds:** late in the dry-down, waders nest in shallow scrapes on the pan floors, lined with shell and grit and nearly invisible. Watch where you walk on dried pans. If a bird is calling agitatedly or feigning a broken wing near you, you're near a nest. Back away.

### The small, ancient things
The **shield shrimp and fairy shrimp** are among the oldest lineages anywhere. Look closely at a pan's shallow edge in the first weeks and you'll see them: translucent, pink, swimming upside down, and plowing the clay with shields like tiny horseshoe crabs. Watch them, maybe photograph them, but don't collect or disturb them.

## Camping and Sleeping

### Where to camp
- **On a dry rise**, away from pans. That keeps you away from snakes, mosquitoes, and the peak of the noise, and it protects the pan margins.
- **Not in overhangs**, because they are occupied.
- **Not under a lone tree**, for lightning reasons.
- **Not in hollows or low channels**, since sheet water may flow through after a storm.

### What to sleep in
- You'll be on rock, so **stakes won't hold**. Bring a freestanding tent or a bivy with fine mesh, and cord to tie out to heavy stones.
- **A hammock won't work.** Trees are too far apart.
- Bring a thick sleeping pad for comfort on stone and for lightning insulation.
- Nights will be warm and humid. You need ventilation and bug protection more than insulation.

### Human waste
**There is no soil to dig into.** The ground is bare rock, a thin crust, or clay pans that must not be contaminated. You cannot bury waste here, and nothing drains away. **Pack out all human waste** in sealed waste bags, and pack out all toilet paper. Urinate on bare rock far from any water, spreading it out so it dries rather than pools.

### Fire
Don't make one. Fuel is scarce, the trees are centuries old, and every scrap of organic material matters in soil this poor. Bring a stove.

## Noise: Plan for It

This is something most travelers underestimate. The frog chorus begins at dusk and "goes on until nearly dawn." It's a physical pressure that drowns out your voice, and it carries for kilometers.

- **Earplugs**, several pairs, if you want to sleep at all. Foam plugs, or custom molded ones if you're sensitive. Some people may want noise-canceling earbuds.
- **You will not be able to hear companions at dusk and night.** Agree on signals before dark: light flashes, touch signals, whistle blasts (which may still be swallowed by the noise). Don't separate after sunset.
- **You won't hear approaching weather or animals** as well at night. Rely on your light and eyes.
- Allow yourself at least one night of *not* sleeping through it. Lie awake and listen for the deep *bonk*, the metallic ticking, the endless machine trill, and the bleating. Notice the distant pans' choirs rising and falling out of phase. This may be the strangest and most memorable part of the whole visit.

The noon silence is the counterpart. On still, hot days, when birds and frogs have gone quiet, listen for the clay beginning to tick and crack. You'll only hear it if you're still.
