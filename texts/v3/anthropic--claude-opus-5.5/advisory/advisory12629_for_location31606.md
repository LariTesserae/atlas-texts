# Preparing for the Hollow of Unceasing Voices

## The Short Version

The Hollow will not try to kill you. Nothing there has noticed that humans exist. What will hurt you is ordinary: sound pressure, bad air, cold water, slick stone, and total disorientation. They arrive together and never let up. The place is less like a jungle than a factory floor at the bottom of a mine, in the dark, in the rain, forever.

Prepare for four things above all:

1. **Your ears.** The noise is the environment.
2. **Your lungs.** Guano and sulfur springs make the air chemically hostile.
3. **Your orientation.** No sky, no walls, no light, and no silence you can use to think.
4. **The place itself.** It is a sealed, ancient, finely tuned system, and you are its first contaminant.

---

## What It Will Actually Feel Like

The first impression will not be sight. There is nothing to see. It will be pressure.

Even through hearing protection, the Hollow arrives as a physical force. You will feel it in your sternum, in your sinuses, and in that ultrasonic ache behind the teeth that no earplug touches, because some of it is reaching you through bone. For the first few minutes your brain will try to sort it into parts: crickets, water, whistling. Then it will give up, and the whole thing becomes one roar, like standing behind a jet engine made of insects.

Then comes the smell. It is ammonia strong enough to make your eyes stream, then mushroom, then a wet-penny iron. Near the springs it becomes rotten eggs. Through a good respirator you will smell little of it, and that is the point.

Then the cold drops start: single, heavy, unpredictable, landing on your helmet with a *tock* you feel rather than hear. The ground under your boots is soft and warm, and it moves.

After a while, if you are well prepared, something unexpected happens. You start to hear the *geography*. The roar leans one way. It is drier in one direction and more liquid in another. A pillar nearby is singing one note, and another farther off answers a fifth higher. This is where the wonder lives. You can reach it only if the physical assault is handled well enough that you have attention left over.

## 1. Hearing: The Primary Hazard

### The problem

The Hollow is described as literally deafening, and you should believe it. A dense chorus of millions of animals, amplified by resonant stone, easily exceeds levels that cause permanent hearing damage within minutes to hours. Worse:

- **It never stops.** Workplace noise limits assume you get to go home. Here there is no recovery period. The "quiet" between Heavings is still overwhelming.
- **Much of the energy is ultrasonic.** Your ears can't hear it, but intense airborne ultrasound can still cause headaches, nausea, fatigue, and a feeling of pressure or dread.
- **Some of it is structure-borne.** The stone hum reaches you through the ground and your skeleton, so earplugs cannot block it.

### What to bring

- **Double protection, worn at all times:** high-attenuation foam or custom-molded earplugs *plus* over-ear muffs. Helmet-mounted muffs are ideal, since you will be wearing a helmet anyway.
- **Put both on before you transit.** Do not step into the Hollow bare-eared, even for a second, "just to hear it." The first unprotected moment could cost you a slice of hearing permanently.
- **Spare plugs, many of them.** Foam plugs get wet, filthy, and lost in the dark. Bring several dozen pairs in a sealed bag.
- **A dosimeter**, meaning a wearable noise-exposure meter, so you know your actual accumulated dose rather than guessing.
- **Earmuffs with electronic level-dependent pass-through** are worth considering. They can let quiet sounds through while clamping loud ones, which becomes important during the silences (see below).

### Plan your exposure

Even with the best protection (roughly 30–40 dB of attenuation, and bone conduction caps what is physically achievable), you will likely still be receiving a significant noise dose. Keep visits **short**: hours or a day or two, not weeks, unless you bring a portable acoustic shelter. That means a small tent or pod with dense, damped walls you can retreat into to rest your ears. It is heavy, but for any multi-day stay it may be the single most important piece of equipment you carry.

**Get a baseline hearing test before you go and another after.** If you notice ringing, muffling, or a "full" feeling in your ears, that is damage happening. Leave.

### Communication

You will not be able to talk to companions. Shouting is useless, and you'll be wearing a respirator anyway. Plan for this in advance:

- **Throat-microphone or bone-conduction intercoms.** These pick up vibration from your throat rather than sound from the air, and they are designed for exactly this kind of environment.
- **A tactile backup code:** squeezes, taps on the shoulder, tugs on a connecting line. For example: one tug means stop, two means come to me, three means emergency or leave now. Practice until it is automatic.
- **Light signals** for when you can see each other.
- **Stay within arm's reach, or tethered.** A companion ten meters away in the dark and the roar is effectively gone.

## 2. Air: The Silent Hazard

The Hollow's air is the danger most likely to be underestimated, because it doesn't announce itself except as a bad smell, and smell stops working.

### The threats

- **Ammonia from the guano.** In poorly ventilated caves with deep guano, ammonia can reach levels that burn the eyes and airways.
- **Hydrogen sulfide at the springs.** The rotten-egg smell is a warning, but H₂S **deadens your sense of smell at higher concentrations**. If the smell fades while you're near a spring, that may be the gas getting stronger, not weaker. H₂S is heavier than air and pools in low spots.
- **Carbon dioxide and low oxygen.** The fog that settles into hollows during the Heaving marks where heavy, humid air collects, and that same air can carry CO₂ and sulfide. Treat every low basin, especially near springs, as a possible gas trap.
- **Fungal spores.** On Earth, bat guano is the classic source of *histoplasmosis*, a serious lung infection from spores kicked up as dust. The Hollow's guano is alien, but the principle holds: disturbing it throws biological material into the air you breathe.
- **Airborne larvae.** During the Heaving the lower air fills with billions of weak, soft-bodied fliers. Without protection you would be inhaling them.

### What to bring

- **A full-face respirator** with combination cartridges: P100 particulate filtration plus ammonia/methylamine and acid-gas protection. Full-face rather than half-mask, because it also protects your eyes from ammonia and falling debris. Choose an anti-fog model; the temperature swings between cold rain and warm spring air will fog everything.
- **Plenty of spare cartridges.** Ammonia saturates filters faster than you would expect.
- **A multi-gas monitor** with audible, visual, **and vibrating** alarms, since you won't hear the beep. It should cover at minimum oxygen, H₂S, CO₂, and ammonia. Clip it low on your body, because the heavy gases are the dangerous ones.
- **Know the escape rule:** if the monitor alarms, go *up*. Uplands and rises are safer than hollows.
- **Emergency escape supply:** a small self-contained breathing cylinder or escape hood for a few minutes of clean air, in case you blunder into a pocket of bad air.

A respirator does not supply oxygen. If the air is oxygen-poor, filters won't save you. That's why the monitor matters.

## 3. Darkness and Seeing

### Lights

Nothing in the Hollow has eyes. Your light will not disturb the wildlife, and you can use it freely. Rules:

- **Three independent light sources, minimum.** A helmet headlamp, a handheld torch, and a backup. Caving rule: if you have two, you have one.
- **Waterproof everything.** It rains constantly, the humidity sits near 100%, and you will slip into pools.
- **Batteries for twice your planned stay.** Cold drains them, so keep spares warm, inside your clothing.
- **A wide floodlight mode** and a **long-throw spot mode.** The dark is "too big for a ceiling," and most lights will simply vanish into it. Your beam will die in empty air, and you should expect that to feel unsettling.

### What you'll see

Where your light lands, you will see flowstone like poured wax in cream, ochre, and rust; rimstone terraces stepping away into blackness; pillars furred with roosting bodies that shift and ripple; and translucent calcite draperies glowing where your beam passes through them.

### What you should make time to see *without* light

This is the Hollow's secret gift to a human visitor. The bacterial mats glow faintly blue-green, and after each Heaving the rising larvae carry tiny sparks up into the dark and go out one by one as they're eaten. Nothing in the entire world can see this except you.

To see it, you need **dark adaptation**: about 20 to 40 minutes in complete darkness with no light at all, not even a glance at a screen. Plan for it. Settle somewhere safe on high ground within sight of a spring field before a Heaving, sit, switch everything off, and wait. You may be the only witness this spectacle will ever have. It's worth an hour.

Be aware that in total darkness combined with overwhelming noise, your brain will start inventing things: shapes, figures, flashes, maybe voices in the roar. This is normal sensory-deprivation hallucination. Know in advance that it will happen so it doesn't frighten you.

## 4. The Ground

### Guano

The "real ground" of the Hollow is guano, meters deep in places, soft, warm, and alive. Treat it like snow over unknown terrain or like a bog:

- **Carry a probing pole**, a trekking pole or a longer staff. Test before you step, especially in the central column forests where deposits are deepest.
- **Expect to sink**, possibly to the knee or deeper. Hidden pits and voids may lie under the surface.
- **Wear tall waterproof boots with gaiters**, sealed at the top. The guano is full of crawling life, and it will try to get into your boots.
- **Tuck and seal everything:** trousers into gaiters, sleeves into gloves.

### Flowstone and rimstone

Flowstone is glossy, wet, and extremely slippery. Rimstone dams are fragile ridges of calcite a hand's width high that took millennia to grow.

- **Footwear with soft, grippy rubber soles** (caving boots or wellies with good tread), not hard hiking lugs.
- **Avoid stepping on rimstone walls.** You would break in one second what took thousands of years to build, and the broken edges are sharp. Where you must cross a terraced slope, step carefully *in* the pools rather than on the rims, or pick your way along rubble.
- **Mineral slurry marshes:** avoid them. They are unknown depth, unknown chemistry, and slick.

### Falling things

You will be walking beneath millions of roosting animals. In the column groves, a steady rain of droppings, dead insects, and calcite-laden drip will fall on you.

- **A caving helmet** is mandatory, for impacts, lights, and muff mounting.
- **A hooded waterproof shell** that you're happy to throw away or thoroughly decontaminate afterward.

## 5. Water, Cold, and Wet

The Hollow's temperature isn't given, but deep caves tend to sit steadily cool, and the warm springs only warm their immediate vicinity. You will be **constantly wet from cold drip, in still, humid air, for as long as you stay.** This is a classic setup for slow hypothermia.

- **Synthetic or wool base layers.** No cotton anywhere.
- **A fully waterproof outer layer**: a caving oversuit or PVC suit is ideal, since it also takes the guano abuse.
- **Dry clothing sealed in a waterproof bag** for sleeping only. Never wear it while moving.
- **Watch for early hypothermia**: shivering, clumsiness, irritability, bad decisions. In the noise and the dark, you and your companions may not notice each other's decline. Check on each other on a schedule.

### Drinking water

Water is everywhere, but none of it is obviously safe.

- **Rimstone pool water** is heavily mineralized ("slick between the fingers") and may contain unknown dissolved compounds, plus whatever has fallen in from above.
- **Spring water** is sulfurous and carries the bacterial communities of the deep earth. Don't drink it.
- **Ceiling rain** is probably your best source, being condensation, essentially distilled. Rig a tarp or funnel into a container in a "wet district" and let it collect. Even so, **filter and chemically treat it**, because it has fallen through an atmosphere full of guano dust and flying life.
- **Bring enough water for the whole trip if you can.** For a short visit, that's the simplest option. If you must rely on local water, bring a test kit and a good filter.

### Food

Bring everything. Nothing in the Hollow is food for you. Snails and shrimp that live on chemosynthetic bacteria in sulfur springs may accumulate compounds you don't want, and the risk is simply not worth it. Keep food sealed. Cave crickets as long as your hand, in carpets across whole slopes, will find crumbs.

## 6. The Heaving: Protocol

The Heaving is the Hollow's "day," and it is the most dangerous and most spectacular time to be there.

### Recognizing it coming

The rhythm drifts and doesn't repeat, so you can't schedule it exactly. The signs:

- **The great chord slides upward.** Even through double hearing protection you will likely feel this, a rising shift in the drone that you sense in your chest. Consider bringing a **simple spectrum analyzer** (a phone app with a decent microphone works) and watching the dominant low frequency. When it starts climbing, the Heaving has begun.
- **Warm, sulfurous air rolls outward** from the springs. Your gas monitor may spike.
- **The drip becomes drizzle.**
- **Fog forms in the hollows.**

### What to do

- **Get to high ground before it starts.** The springs surge and flood across the mat fields, and warm, gassy air pools in low areas. Uplands "no taller than a house" are enough to lift you out of the worst of it.
