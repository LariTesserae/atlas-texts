# Preparing for the Held Basin

## The Short Version

The Held Basin will not try to kill you. It will just be indifferent to you in about six different ways at once. The ground cuts, the air is full of glass, and the sound goes through your body, not just your ears. Everything valuable is overhead, supported by something you can't see and can't trust your instincts about. You cannot talk to anyone. You will probably lose some hearing. You will probably be moved by it anyway.

Plan a short trip, pack light, learn to sign before you arrive, and let the residents and the animals tell you what to do.

---

## 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

Most visitors misjudge the scale and the medium.

**Scale.** This is not a city you walk through. It is a valley-sized volume of air with a few thousand people in it. You will spend long stretches seeing no one, then look up and see a dwelling hanging a kilometer away like a seed pod. Distances deceive: the next Throat is a pencil-line on the horizon, and walking there on glass is a full day or more.

**Medium.** The environment is sound. It isn't background noise. It is the structure holding up the city, the source of the water, the weather system, and the way predators hunt. Every protective instinct you have assumes sound is something you can get away from. Here you can't, only into rarer and quieter pockets.

**Tempo.** The place is dim, warm, and unchanging for long periods. Then a retuning hits and everything moves at once. Prepare for both boredom and sudden emergency.

---

## 2. Before You Go: Your Body

### Get a baseline hearing test
Get a full audiogram, including high frequencies, before you leave. Keep the results. You will want to know what the basin took from you, and residents have no reference point, since for them middle-aged deafness is normal.

### Know who should not go
Seriously consider not going, or only visiting the far edges, if you have any of the following:
- **Inner-ear or balance disorders** (Ménière's, vestibular migraine, BPPV). The infrasound and the shifting lattice will be brutal.
- **Migraines**, especially if pressure-triggered. The ultrasonic ache behind the eyes is constant near Throats.
- **Cardiac arrhythmias or pacemakers.** A place loud enough that you can't feel your own heartbeat is not a place to test implanted electronics or a fragile rhythm.
- **Epilepsy.** Rhythmic beats and the geometric halos are a plausible trigger combination.
- **Pregnancy.** The effects of sustained, whole-body infrasound are unknown, and "unknown" is the wrong answer.
- **Anxiety or panic disorders.** Low-frequency sound tends to produce dread with no object. You may feel afraid for no reason, for hours.
- **Respiratory conditions.** See the dust section below.

### Train your balance and your nerve
You'll be riding woven rafts across open air, sometimes tens or hundreds of meters up, with no rail. Practice on slacklines, paddleboards, and wobble boards. Get comfortable kneeling low and shifting weight slowly. If heights make you freeze, know that about yourself first. Freezing on a raft is survivable. Lurching isn't.

### Consider your weight
This is unusual advice, but it matters. Everything above the floor is built to the load its nodes can bear. The residents are probably lean, and they build as light as possible. A heavy visitor with a heavy pack may be unwelcome on some rafts or in some dwellings, and it's their call, not yours. You don't need to change your body for a trip. But understand that here, mass is a real social and physical constraint, and pack accordingly.

## 3. Before You Go: Language

**Learn to sign.** No local sign-language course will exist in your home world. What you can do is become fluent in *thinking* in a visual-spatial language. Learn any natural sign language to a working level. You'll start from zero when you arrive, but you'll know how signing grammar works: space as reference, classifiers, facial grammar. That will let you pick up the local language several times faster than someone who has only ever spoken.

Practice specifically:
- **Height, direction, and relative position.** Local vocabulary is dense here.
- **Emergency and needs signs.** Help, stop, hurt, water, fall, wait, "I don't understand," "show me."
- **Numbers and pointing precision.**

**Develop a tap code.** Residents communicate by touch and by tapping on rafts. Invent a small, simple personal code, such as one tap for yes, two for no, and a rapid series for alarm. Teach it to your guide on day one.

**Bring a writing surface.** A small slate or wax tablet with a stylus works well. Don't rely on paper: fog shelves soak it. Don't rely on a screen, for reasons covered below. Drawing matters more than writing. Sketches of heights and directions will get you further than words the residents can't read.

**Accept the loss of your voice.** You will not speak to another human for most of your trip. For some people that feels like relief. For others it feels like drowning. Try a silent weekend at home and find out which you are.

## 4. Protecting Yourself from the Sound

The chord has three parts, and each needs a different answer.

### The audible middle: the vast unwavering chord
- **Double protection:** high-attenuation foam or custom-molded plugs *plus* over-ear muffs. Wear both near Throats at all times.
- **Bring many spares.** Plugs get dirty with glass dust. Put in a dirty plug and you'll scratch your ear canal.
- **Custom-molded plugs** are worth the cost. You'll wear them for most of your waking hours.
- **Understand the limit:** close to a Throat, sound reaches your inner ear through your skull. Plugs reduce the damage. They don't stop it. The only real protection is **time and distance.**

### The felt bottom: infrasound
Nothing you can wear blocks this. It goes through your sternum, your teeth, and your organs.
- **Expect** chest pressure, a vibrating voice box, nausea, possibly blurred vision (eyeballs have resonant frequencies in the infrasonic range), fatigue, and unexplained dread.
- **Remind yourself it's physical.** When you feel doom, name it: *this is the sound, not a premonition.* That really helps.
- **Eat bland food** in the first days and carry anti-nausea medication.
- **Stay loose.** Clenching your jaw against the vibration in your back teeth will leave you with headaches and sore teeth. Let it buzz.

### The high end: the ache behind the eyes
- Expect headaches and possibly tinnitus. Bring painkillers, but don't use them to push through to a longer stay near a Throat. The ache is your warning system.
- Watch the ground. When glass flakes tremble and walk, you're in a strong ultrasonic field. Move along.

### Plan your exposure like a diver plans depth
- **Near a Throat** (where speech is impossible): minutes, not hours, unless a resident takes you there for a reason.
- **The between-spaces**, where chords cross and beat: this is where you'll live. It's tolerable for days, with protection.
- **Cancellation pockets:** your decompression chambers. Know where the nearest one is at all times. Ask your guide to show you several early on.

### Sleep
Sleep will be your biggest problem. You can't sleep in a pocket every night, since those are common land and shared. Bring:
- A sleep mask (the halos and the lanterns flicker)
- Your best plugs
- A **dense, heavy-ish pad** or folded blanket between you and whatever you lie on. It dampens a little of the direct conduction. Weigh this against the weight rule above.
- Realistic expectations. You'll sleep badly. Build rest days into your plan and don't make critical decisions after a bad night.

## 5. Protecting Yourself from the Ground

The floor is a few-centuries-old sheet of volcanic glass, and nothing has dulled it. **Treat every surface as a broken bottle.**

- **Boots:** thick, stiff, puncture-resistant soles with a steel or composite midplate. A normal hiking boot will be sliced. The ridge crests really can cut a sole, so step *over* them rather than on them.
- **Gloves:** cut-resistant, like the ones used for handling sheet metal or glass. If you slip, you'll put a hand down, and you don't want that hand bare.
- **Knee and shin protection** for scrambling on ridges and clinker.
- **Long, tough trousers.**
- **Clinker slopes** on the cinder cones shift and ring underfoot. Test before you commit your weight, and don't climb them after a retuning, when loosened debris is still settling.
- **Fissures:** they breathe warm air. Don't put your face in them. Carry a **small gas monitor** (CO₂, SO₂, H₂S) if you can. Warm volcanic vents often carry gases that pool in low places. The damp mossy runnels are beautiful and a good place to find water, but approach them upwind and don't linger in the deep ones.
- **Heat:** the floor is warm everywhere. That's pleasant at night and tiring by day. Your boots should breathe. Treat your feet like a soldier would: dry socks and foot powder.

**First aid for cuts:** you'll get cut, so pack for it:
- Irrigation syringe and saline, to flush glass fragments out of wounds
- Fine tweezers and a magnifier
- Wound closure strips, and sutures if you know how to use them
- Antiseptic, antibiotic ointment, and a course of broad-spectrum oral antibiotics
- Heavy-duty tape for patching boots

## 6. Protecting Yourself from the Air

The basin's defining trait is that **its dust never settles.** It's all still in the air. Much of it is fine volcanic glass, which means microscopic shards.

- **Respirator:** at least a P100/FFP3 half-mask, with plenty of spare filters. Wear it whenever the haze is visibly thick and during *every* post-retuning dust fall. Inhaled volcanic glass is a slow, permanent injury. This may matter more than any other item on this list.
- **Sealed goggles**, with anti-fog treatment, because you'll be walking through fog shelves. Plain glasses won't do. Glass dust in the eye is miserable, and you'll be far from an eye doctor.
- **Eyewash** bottles.
- **Cover your food and water** from dust. Wipe cups before drinking.
- **The fog bands** are damp and cool compared with the clear air between them. Walking up through a shelf feels like wading into a cold lake. A light, water-resistant layer is useful.

## 7. Protecting Yourself from Above

On the floor, the danger is things falling on you. The residents' first rule exists for this reason:

> **Never stand at a node on the glass floor. Things fall on nodes.**

The problem is that you can't see nodes. Here is how to find them, roughly from most to least reliable:
1. **Ask.** Residents know. Have your guide point out safe ground wherever you plan to stop.
2. **Look up.** If dust, water beads, or anything else hangs in a row directly above you, you're under a node column. Move.
3. **Read the ribbing.** The glass floor carries concentric patterns around each Throat. Once someone explains it, you may learn which bands of ribbing sit under nodes. Don't trust your own reading until a local has confirmed it.
4. **Watch the lizards.** They're deaf, calm, and have lived here for generations. Wherever they bask is probably safe from falling things and warm besides.

**Wear a helmet** on the floor, and especially after a retuning. Something as ordinary as a plum-sized ball of water dropping from forty meters will hurt. A clod of soil could kill you.

## 8. Moving Through the City

### Hire a guide. Seriously.
You can't read halo figures. You can't feel node positions. You can't sense a retuning coming. You don't know where the pockets are. A resident guide is the difference between a visit and a rescue. Arrange one before or immediately after you arrive, and follow their lead without argument.

### Rafts
- **Stay low.** Kneel or sit, spread your weight, and move slowly. The raft sits on many nodes. Sudden shifts push it off some of them.
- **Keep your hands in.** Don't grab at passing held objects. They're somebody's water, someone's seeds, a mute's bait, or just not yours.
- **Tethering:** wear a simple harness, but think before you clip in. Tying yourself to a raft that is falling is worse than not being tied at all. Follow your guide's practice. They probably tether only to things anchored to the ground, like the old vine ropes.
- **Don't push off hard.** Residents nudge from node to node. You'll want to shove. Don't.

### Dwellings
- Ask before stepping onto one, and accept "no" graciously. Your weight could push their home down a tier.
- Don't touch the resonant plates hanging beside the doors. They are tuned to hold the house at its height. Touching one is like fiddling with someone's gas line.
- Don't ask the pitch of their node. Ever. Think of it as asking about someone's blood.

### Navigation on your own
If you walk the floor without a guide (not recommended):
- **A magnetic compass may be unreliable** over volcanic rock. Bring one, but trust it loosely.
- Use the **glass roads.** They are laid along the ribbing, built precisely, and almost empty. They're your safest routes.
- **Learn the Throats' bearings.** Each Throat has a characteristic halo, visible only from certain directions. You can photograph or sketch them early with your guide, which gives you a crude navigational chart to work from.
- **Keep a Throat in sight.** They're the only fixed landmarks. Everything else floats and moves.

## 9. Retunings: Your Emergency Plan

Retunings are the weather. They come without schedule, take minutes, and reorganize the entire lattice. Nodes move, and everything held either drifts to a new position or falls.

**Early warning signs** (in order of when you're likely to notice them):
1. **Animals.** Birds move a quarter-hour before you'll feel anything. Nodewren flocks changing course, grids breaking up, sudden commotion on every level: **treat that as your alarm.**
2. **Mutes gathering** near one Throat. That's the Throat that's about to shift.
3. **Pressure in your skull** and restlessness in the residents.
4. **The tone begins to slide.**

**If you're aloft:**
- Get to a **tethered vine-field**, the floating terraces tied to the ground by living ropes. That's where residents bring their children. Go there too.
- Stow and lash everything. Anything loose will either fall or drift away.
- Don't try to ride out a retuning on an open raft unless your guide is doing it.

**If you're on the floor:**
- **Get out from under things.** Move to open glass, away from overhead fields, dwellings, and forests.
- Helmet on. Respirator on. The dust that has been aloft for decades is about to come down.
- **Don't shelter in fissures.** Falling debris funnels into them, and so might gas.
- After the shift, **your old "safe" spot may now be under a node.** Re-check before settling.

**Afterward:**
- Expect fine grey sediment everywhere and a rain of seeds, water, insects, and fruit, sometimes also animals or objects from households.
- It's beautiful. Thousands of seedlings sprout in the dust. Walk with care. You're stepping on the basin's only topsoil, and it won't last.
- Mind the mutes. They'll be busy and bold.

## 10. The Animals

- **Nodewrens:** harmless, beautiful, and your best retuning indicator. Watch how they roost. Birds straddling two nodes are telling you something about the local structure.
- **Insect swarms:** near Throats, clouds of insects so dense they shimmer. A light mesh face-net, one that fits over your respirator and goggles, will keep them out of your mouth and eyes when you pass through.
- **Floor lizards:** calm, deaf, and harmless. They're good guides to warm, safe ground. Don't disturb them.
- **Blind fungus-eating mammals in the pockets:** *never pick one up.* They panic when taken out of the quiet.
- **Pale fungi and floor succulents:** very fragile. The fungi can't tolerate vibration, so don't bring vibrating devices into the pockets and don't stamp around. Walk softly.
- **Mutes:** they probably won't target you, since you're far too heavy to hang at a node. But a mute that cancels one node under a small raft or a lightly built perch could tip it. If you feel that sudden **hollow ear-pop** and see things falling nearby, grab the raft and stay low. Keep anything small and precious tied down. Mutes steal toys.

## 11. Food and Water

- **Carry your own water** on the floor. It's dry except for the fissure runnels. Bring a filter and purification tablets. Runnel water flows through moss over volcanic rock and may carry dissolved minerals.
- **Fog harvesting:** bring a light mesh net. Watch how residents harvest trembling water spheres at the nodes, and ask before taking any. Held water may belong to someone.
- **Food:** bring light, calorie-dense supplies. Local foods, like gas-filled fruit and grass seeds, may be fine, but try small amounts first and only foods residents eat in front of you.
- **Don't pick fruit that's drifting.** A drifting fruit is spreading its seeds, and animals might be counting on it.

---

## 12. Your Gear Will Suffer Too

Constant vibration across the whole spectrum is hard on equipment.

- **Screws back out.** Use threadlocker or tape on anything that matters.
- **Sensitive electronics may misbehave.** Motion sensors, microphones, spinning drives, and fine mechanisms can all be thrown off by sustained acoustic energy. Don't count on your phone as a navigation device. Microphones are useless here.
- **Things rattle and walk.** Anything with a loose part will buzz. Glasses frames, zipper pulls, buckles: tape them down or you'll go mad.
- **Hard cases** protect gear from the dust, but seal them.
- **Choose soft, snug clothing.** Loose fabric flaps and buzzes near the Throats, and it will drive you slowly insane.
- **Lights:** a headlamp with a **red** setting. At night the basin is almost fully dark, and the animals signal with whatever light they make. A white beam is rude and possibly disruptive to that. Use it only in emergencies.
- **Recording:** don't expect audio recordings to capture anything meaningful. If you want to take something of the sound home, a vibration sensor pressed to the glass would come closer.

## 13. Etiquette

The residents' rules, plus a few that follow from them:

- **Never touch a Throat.** You won't get close enough to be tempted. Near one, you won't be able to do anything but survive.
- **Never cut the vine ropes.** They're the oldest living things in the basin and anchor its safest refuges.
- **Never build or bring anything heavier than your node can bear.** For visitors, this means packing light and not overloading someone's raft or house.
- **Never ask about someone's node pitch.**
- **Cancellation pockets are common land.** Anyone may go there to rest, speak, or grieve, and no one may be turned away. That includes you. It also includes the person who arrives after you. Don't monopolize one.
- **Speak aloud only in pockets.** Using your voice there is a privilege. Keep it gentle. Someone may be grieving next to you.
- **Don't cling to your voice.** Trying to shout through the chord marks you as a stranger who hasn't adapted.
- **Touch matters.** A hand on the shoulder carries meaning in its pressure. Until you learn the local meanings, don't initiate touch. When someone touches you, pay attention to *how.*

**Gifts:** anything you bring should be **light** and **quiet.** Fine thread, very good needles, strong lightweight cord, lenses, small durable tools, seeds from your world (only if residents are willing and you're sure they won't cause ecological harm). A heavy gift here is unwelcome, not generous.

## 14. The Mind

Residents who come from elsewhere say the sound gets into your thinking. Your thoughts start to fall into the chord's intervals, and after a month you can't remember what silence was for. Believe them.

- **Keep a journal** in writing or drawing. Record your moods. Look for slow change: a flattening, a loss of interest in your own inner voice, or odd music-like patterns in your thinking.
- **Visit pockets regularly** to reset. Speak aloud in them to yourself. Sing if you like. Remind yourself what your own voice sounds like.
- **Set a hard limit** of one to two weeks for a first visit. Set it before you arrive, tell your guide, and keep it even if you feel fine. *Especially* if you feel fine and don't want to leave.
- **Watch for the mood.** The basin is dim, the light has no direction, noon looks like late afternoon, and the sound breeds dread. Seasonal-depression lamps won't help. Time spent at the basin's edge, in open light, will.

---

## 15. Leaving, and After

- **Walk out slowly.** Leave the basin by its edges. Let the chord fade over hours, not minutes.
- **The silence will be strange.** You may hear your own heartbeat for the first time in days, and it may frighten you. Your voice will sound wrong. Your inner monologue may feel too loud, or too empty. This is normal and usually passes.
- **Tinnitus** may follow you home. Get a second audiogram within a few days and another after a month. Compare them with your baseline.
- **Chest X-ray** if you spent much time in dust or had any cough.
- **Check old cuts** for embedded glass. Volcanic glass can stay under the skin and surface weeks later.
- **Expect to miss it.** People who visit places like this often describe a longing afterward. Ordinary quiet feels thin, and the world seems unanchored, as if nothing is holding it up.

---

## A Final Note on Attitude

The basin is a place where everything is held and nothing settles. Visitors do best when they adopt that stance themselves. Travel light, touch lightly, and don't try to put your full weight anywhere until someone who lives here tells you it will hold. Watch the birds. Rest in the quiet places and share them. Don't fight the sound, but don't let it into you for too long.

Somewhere under all of it is a black floor that has never worn down, waiting for the city to land. You're a brief, heavy, noisy thing passing through a place that has been carefully tuned for centuries. Go gently, and leave nothing behind but a few bootprints in the dust after a retuning, which the lattice will lift away within days.
