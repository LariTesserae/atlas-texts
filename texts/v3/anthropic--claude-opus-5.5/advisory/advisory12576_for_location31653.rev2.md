# Preparing for the Seaward City

## A Traveler's Briefing

### The short version

If you remember nothing else:

1. **Never touch a seam with bare skin.** Wear real boots and carry gloves.
2. **Don't trust your eyes about distance or direction.** Count seams, count paces, and carry twice the food and water your plan requires.
3. **Water doesn't go where you expect.** Seal everything. Never sleep in a seam or in a wet ring.
4. **Stay away from the western shore at night.** Treat the eastern forest as a separate expedition.
5. **You are inside something that is paying attention.** Take nothing, break nothing, leave nothing, and be quiet.

Everything below expands on those five points.

---

## 1. What It Will Actually Feel Like

Before the practical matters, adjust your expectations.

Most travelers arrive braced for danger and find a place that is mostly *calm*. The Seaward City is not hostile. It is vast, dim, quiet, and slightly wrong in ways your body notices before your mind does. The hazards are small and constant: a cut hand, a twisted ankle on dew-slick stone, a walk that runs six hours longer than planned. Beneath them is a slower pressure from the light, the distances, the loneliness, and the low hum of the stays.

The most common failure here is not injury. It is **disorientation followed by overconfidence**. A traveler sees a stay that looks close, sets out with a liter of water at mid-morning, and is still walking at dusk, when the fog comes in waist-deep and hides the ground.

The second most common failure is emotional. People leave early because the place gets inside them: the dim, endless noon, the figures who never walk toward each other, the sense of a city thinking to itself in its amber windows. Some travelers find it the most peaceful place they have ever been. Others find it unbearable by the third day. You won't know which you are until you're there, so plan for both.

## 2. Hands and Feet: The Stone

The lay is fused, glassy stone, set in plates whose seams are **sharp as broken pottery**. Nothing has had time to wear down. This is your most immediate physical hazard.

### Footwear
- **Stiff, puncture-resistant soles.** Think of mountaineering approach shoes or boots with a plate in the midsole. Thin trail runners will be sliced through at the seams within days.
- **Soft, sticky rubber with fine siping.** Every morning the entire lay is beaded with dew, and faceted glassy stone under a film of water is treacherous. Hard lugged soles skate on it. Soft climbing-style rubber grips far better.
- **No crampons, microspikes, or hobnails.** They will slip on the glass. They will also scratch the stone, and scratching a surface that is *part of a living system* is exactly the sort of thing you should avoid (see Section 10).
- **High ankles.** You will step down into seams you didn't see.
- **Gaiters.** Seams are overgrown with ferns and moss cushions that hide the cutting edge underneath. Gaiters keep that edge off your shins.

### Hands
- **Carry cut-resistant gloves and actually wear them** whenever you climb, scramble, steady yourself on a wall, or reach into vegetation. The reflex to put out a hand when you slip is the reflex that will open your palm.
- Buildings grow out of the ground seamlessly, but that doesn't mean their edges are soft. Assume every edge is sharp until your gloved hand has tested it.

### Walking technique
- **Use a trekking pole** to probe seams before stepping. The fern-and-moss "green grout" looks soft. It may be a few centimeters deep, or it may hide a crack that takes your leg to the knee.
- **Cross seams deliberately, at right angles, one foot at a time.** Don't walk along them.
- **Be extra careful in the first two hours after dawn**, when the stone is wettest. Slow down. There is nowhere you need to be quickly.

### First aid for cuts
The climate is warm and damp, and cuts will get infected if you neglect them.
- Wound-closure strips, tissue adhesive, and plenty of antiseptic
- Non-adherent dressings and waterproof tape
- A broad-spectrum antibiotic course, if your home medicine allows it
- Clean every cut the same day, however small. Glass-edge cuts are deep and narrow, and they hide debris.

## 3. Eyes and Head: The Light

### The dimness
Noon looks like late dusk. Your eyes will adapt, and faster than you expect, because the light is constant and soft. Plan around it:
- **You won't need sunglasses.** Instead, bring **lubricating eye drops.** People here squint, strain, and forget to blink.
- **Bring a headlamp with a red mode and a very low white setting**, and use it sparingly. The lanterns open only in the *dimmest hour*. A white beam swept across a wet ring may close every flower in it, and it will certainly announce you to everything alive nearby. Your dark-adapted eyes, plus the glowing avenue-threads and the lanterns, will usually be enough to see by at night once you stop overriding them.

### The lensing
This is the subtler problem. The stays bend light, so the sun smears or splits, the horizon lifts into a bowl, and distances expand and contract. Your visual system keeps trying to reconcile what it sees with what your feet and inner ear report, and the mismatch produces something close to **motion sickness**.
- **Pack anti-nausea medication**, and try it at home first to learn how drowsy it makes you.
- **Don't stare at the sky.** The description is accurate: the ache comes from interpretation, not brightness. When you feel it, look at the ground near your feet for a few minutes. The plates close by are the one part of the landscape that doesn't lie much.
- **Expect headaches in the first days.** Keep hydrated and rest often.
- **Photographs will look strange,** and panoramas even stranger. Don't use them to judge distance afterward.

### The circadian problem
With a barely perceptible dawn and a dim noon, your body clock will drift. On a stay of more than a few days this matters.
- **Bring a reliable watch**, and a second one of a different mechanism (one mechanical, one quartz, say). It is prudent in any strongly lensed place to compare them now and then against each other and against your own sense of time. If they start disagreeing, take note.
- **Keep a strict sleep and meal schedule** set by the watch, not the sky.
- Travelers prone to low mood in winter should consider a small **light-therapy lamp**, used inside a tent or closed shelter so it doesn't spill onto the landscape, and **vitamin D supplements** for longer stays.

## 4. Finding Your Way

This is where the Seaward City most often gets people into trouble.

### What doesn't work
- **Judging distance by eye.** A stay that looks an hour away may take half a day. Assume every visual estimate is wrong, in a direction you can't predict.
- **Navigating by the sun.** It smears, splits, and shifts depending on which stays lie between you and it.
- **Following water downhill.** Water here doesn't run downhill. It runs toward stays. A traveler looking for low ground by following runoff will be led to the nearest column instead.
- **Old maps.** The city moves. New plates and new stays appear. Any map older than a few years is partly fiction.
- **Magnetic compasses.** Nobody has confirmed whether they work here. Bring one, but don't rely on it until you've tested it against landmarks you trust.

### What does work
- **Counting seams.** This is the residents' method, and you should adopt it. Carry a tally counter or a knotted cord. Record routes as sequences: *"From the arcade with the low note: 214 seams along the pale avenue, then left at the plate with the fig, 37 seams to the stepped hall."*
- **Pacing.** Your stride on flat stone is consistent even when the view isn't. Measure your pace length before you go.
- **The avenues.** They are straight, faintly pale by day, and threaded with light at night. They are the most reliable linear features in the city. If you're lost, find an avenue and follow it. It goes somewhere.
- **The daily fog.** In the late afternoon it rolls in from the sea and flows *inland* along the avenues. Wherever it's coming from is west, and wherever it's going is east. For about an hour each day, the fog is a compass.
- **Sound.** The exhalation of new land comes from the far west. Each arcade moans on its own note, which makes them good landmarks you can recognize in the dark or the fog.
- **The grain of the stone.** The fine crystalline lines in the plates all run in one direction, at least locally, and the grain changes direction along the avenues. If that holds across a region, it could serve as a compass. Test it before trusting it.
- **Age of the ground.** Seams still glowing orange, or plates that still steam, mean you are near the western edge. Softening, crazed, gravel-edged stone and slack stays mean you are near the eastern margin.

### Rules for any walk
- **Carry at least twice the food and water your route seems to need**, and set a firm turnaround time.
- **Don't set out toward a stay after midday** unless you're prepared to spend the night out.
- **Mark your arrival point** by seam count and bearing from at least two stays, plus distinctive nearby features. Stays grow and move slowly, and new ones sprout, so describe your anchor point in several independent ways.

## 5. Water

It is damp here but not wet. There are no rivers, and water behaves oddly.

### Sources, best to worst
1. **Morning dew.** At dawn every plate is beaded with condensation. This is your cleanest source. Spread a clean waterproof sheet or collection tarp in the evening, shaped so that it funnels into a *sealed* container, and position the funnel on the stay-facing side, because the water will creep that way whatever you do. Collect early, before it migrates.
2. **Rain.** Brief, heavy, warm bursts every few days. The collection tarp works for this too.
3. **Seam water.** It has run through moss and roots, so filter and purify it.
4. **The black pools in the wet rings.** These are standing water full of plant life, snails, and whatever lives in the dark, in the most biologically active zone in the city. Use them only as a last resort, after filtering *and* boiling or chemical treatment.

**Filter and purify everything anyway.** Nobody knows what minerals leach out of the lay.

### Containment
- **Keep all water in sealed containers.** An open cup or pot may develop a slow drift at its surface or lip toward the nearest stay. The effect is weak, but it is real, and spills will not stay where they land. They will leave you in a thin, determined line.
- Use bladders or bottles with positive seals. Don't leave water standing overnight in an open vessel.
- Nobody has observed the pull acting on fluid inside a sealed container or inside a body. You don't feel heavier near the stays. Still, be attentive. Report any unusual sensation (drifting tears, odd sinus pressure, sweat that won't drip straight) and use it as a reason to back away from the stay.

## 6. Weather, Fog, and the Daily Rhythm

### Climate
It is warm and humid day and night. Dress for a dim tropical lowland:
- **Lightweight, quick-drying long sleeves and long trousers**, for protection from cuts more than from sun
- A **light waterproof shell** for the rain bursts
- **At least three pairs of socks**, rotated and dried. Foot fungus is a real risk in damp boots.
- **Foot powder** and a small towel

### The fog
The late-afternoon fog is beautiful: white rivers flowing down the avenues and pouring into the seams at dusk. **It is also the most dangerous hour to be walking**, because knee- to waist-high fog hides the ground, and on this ground that is exactly what you can't afford.
- **Plan to be stationary**, or at least on a known avenue, when the fog arrives.
- If you're caught on open plates, stop. Probe with your pole, stay put, and wait for the fog to break and drain at dusk.
- Watching the fog from a raised terrace or bridge is one of the great sights of the city. Get somewhere high and sit.

### Rain
The rain falls straight, then runs sideways across the ground in sheets toward the stays. **Don't be between a large expanse of plate and a stay when it rains** if you're sitting or sleeping on the ground. You'll be in the path of the runoff.

## 7. Shelter and Sleeping

### Where not to sleep
- **In a seam.** However soft and mossy, a seam is a gutter by dawn and a channel in rain.
- **In a wet ring.** It is damp, full of life, loud with infrasound, and home to the large grazer before dawn.
- **In a hollow left by a broken plate**, or near a seam-fig that is actively splitting a plate. The city reabsorbs broken plates over weeks. Nobody knows what happens to things left lying in such a hollow while that happens, and you should not be the one to find out.
- **Near the western edge**, at all.
- **Inside a stepped hall or house with a lit window**, unless someone has clearly invited you. You can't tell from outside whether a building is inhabited or simply *running*.

### Where to sleep
- **Open, flat, unbroken plate**, away from seams, and not directly downstream (stay-ward) of a large surface
- **Beneath an arcade**, which gives some shelter from rain and makes it easy to find again by its note. The wind's moaning keeps some people awake, and others find it the best sleep of their lives.

### Kit
- A **waterproof bivy or small tent** with a solid, cut-resistant floor. Put a thick groundsheet under it, because the seam-edges can slice a thin floor even on "flat" plate.
- A **sleeping pad**, for comfort and insulation from the damp, if not the cold. The stone holds a faint warmth that many travelers love.
- **Earplugs** are optional. The ticking of the stone, the dripping at dawn, and the occasional distant exhalation from the west are usually more soothing than disruptive. Keep some just in case.

## 8. The Stays

The stays are the heart of the place, and nearly everyone wants to go right up to one. You can, with care.

### Known effects near a stay
- **Infrasound.** A deep pressure in the chest, strongest at the base. Low-frequency sound like this can cause unease, anxiety, a sense of being watched, nausea, and mild visual disturbance in some people. If you feel dread near a stay, the likely cause is the sound, not the stay "wanting" anything. Still, respect it and step back.
- **Intensified lensing.** Close to the base, the distortion is at its worst. Expect vertigo. Sit down before you lose your balance.
- **Wet ground.** The rings around the base are the wettest, most overgrown part of the city: black pools, moss ridges, pale seam-figs, fleshy spiral vines.

### Guidelines
- **Limit time at the base** to short visits, especially at first. An hour is plenty.
- **People with heart conditions, pacemakers, epilepsy, or severe anxiety, and anyone pregnant,** should keep well back. Nobody has any evidence of harm, but nobody has any evidence of safety either.
- **Don't climb the stays**, even where the spiral vines seem to offer a ladder. The vines are fleshy, the surface is matte and unknown, and the stays rise beyond sight.
- **Don't touch the stays without gloves**, and ideally don't touch them at all.
- **At night the lanterns ring the base like a necklace**, moths spiral upward in the same direction as the vines, and glass snails move through the moss like tiny travelling lights. This is worth staying up for. Watch from the edge of the ring, headlamp off.

## 9. The Two Edges

### The seaward edge: look from a distance, and never at night
New land is laid here: sea turning flat, oily, and pale, stone rising in slow smoking tongues, seams glowing orange.

**Hazards:**
- Steam burns and hot stone. The new plates stay hot for days.
- Possible volcanic-type gases. Bring a gas detector if you can, and stay upwind.
- Unstable, unfinished ground.
- The sea itself. Don't swim, wade, or approach the water.

**Protocol:**
- View the edge **by day, from well-cooled plates**, several hundred meters back at the very least.
- **Leave at dusk.** The laying happens mostly at night. Watch for the warning signs (flat, oily, then pale water) and **retreat at once** if you see them nearby.
- The exhalation of new land can be heard across the whole lowland. You don't need to be close to experience it. Many travelers say it's more moving heard from a quiet plate far inland, lying on warm stone.

### The inland edge: a different journey
Past the oldest plates, the stays go slack, the stone crumbles into gravel and gullies, and the jungle takes over.

**This calls for different preparation.** It is dense, warm, dripping lowland forest, with collapsing ground underneath. Prepare for it as you would any unfamiliar tropical jungle:
- Insect and leech protection, jungle footwear, and extra water treatment
- Caution in gullies, where collapsing gravel slopes are common
- Awareness that slack stays are an unknown. Don't shelter near them, in case anything is still coming down.
- Navigation by conventional means once the pull is gone. That may actually be *easier* than in the city.

Don't wander into the forest casually from the city side. Plan it as a separate trip.

## 10. The City Itself: Etiquette for a Living Place

This is the part that matters most, and it is the hardest to pack for.

The Seaward City is not a ruin. It *runs itself*. It absorbs broken plates, grows arcades around trees, lays new ground, and grows new stays. Many of its amber windows hold processes, not people. It is the only city most travelers will visit where *the city itself* is the host.

You don't know what it notices. Behave accordingly.

- **Take nothing.** Not stone chips, not seeds, not snails, not a lantern-flower. The city includes its plants and animals in its plans. You don't know what role a given fig or snail plays.
- **Break nothing.** Don't pry at seams, chip edges, or scratch the lay. If you scratch the surface by accident, leave it. Don't try to "fix" it.
- **Leave nothing.** Carry out every scrap, including food waste and, yes, human waste, in sealed bags. Given how the city reabsorbs broken plates, anything you leave behind may be *incorporated*. Nobody knows what that would mean, for you or for the city.
- **Don't carve or mark anything.** Not the stone, not the figs. Mark your routes in your notebook, not on the landscape.
- **Don't try to read the inscribed surfaces by touch or rubbing.** Photograph or sketch them. The script changes from district to district, and nobody living here reads all of it. If you have an interest in languages, a notebook of transcriptions is one of the most respectful souvenirs you can take away.

### The residents
The people here are few, solitary, and unhurried, and they "almost never seem to be going toward each other." That is probably not unfriendliness. It is the shape of life here.
- **Don't approach people directly or quickly.** If someone is walking an avenue, let them pass. If someone stands in an arcade looking out, stay at a distance they can see and give them time.
- **Let them come to you.** If someone does approach, be quiet, keep your hands visible, and follow their lead.
- **Keep your voice low.** Sound carries in the stillness, and loud voices are unmistakably foreign.
- **Don't knock on lit windows**, and don't try to enter buildings. Remember that most lit windows have no one behind them.
- **Bring a notebook and pencil** for drawing and gesturing. Expect no shared language.
- **Ask permission before photographing people**, with a gesture, and take no for an answer.
- **Learn to count seams the way they do.** Some travelers find that this, more than any word, shows residents that you are trying to live in their place on its terms.

### The large animal
Some speak of a dark, deer-sized or larger grazer with three-toed, wide-set tracks, seen in the wet rings before dawn.
- **Don't track it. Don't wait for it. Don't try to photograph it with flash.**
- If you see one, stand still. It may be standing at the base of a stay, head raised, listening to the stone. Don't interrupt. Very few people ever see one.
- If you find its tracks in the morning damp, look, sketch, and move on.

### Other animals
- **Waders** stand so still that you'll pass a dozen before noticing one. Give pools a wide berth so you don't flush them. Watching one fly, constantly correcting its curve toward the stay, is one of the strangest small sights here.
- **Glass snails** are delicate and translucent. Watch where you put your feet in seams at night.
- **Moths** are harmless and drawn to the stays, not to your light. Let them be.

### Plants
- **Eat nothing that grows here.** Nothing is known about toxicity. Bring all your own food.
- **Don't pick the lanterns.** They are part of the night ecology, feeding snail-light and perhaps more.
- **Handle seam-figs gently, if at all.** They are the city's slow erosion, and the city tolerates them for reasons of its own.

## 11. Health and Mind

### Physical
- A full first aid kit weighted toward **lacerations** and **infection**
- **Anti-nausea medication** for lensing sickness
- **Pain relievers** for eye-strain headaches
- **Electrolytes**, because you'll sweat in the humidity without noticing
- **Antifungal cream** for feet and skin folds
- A **personal medical card** in case you need help from someone you can't talk to

### Psychological
This is the part travelers most often underestimate.

The Seaward City is enormous, dim, and sparsely peopled. The few figures you see walk alone. The horizon curls upward. Windows glow with something you can't identify. Some travelers feel deep peace. Others feel intense loneliness, dissociation, or dread, and many feel both on different days.

- **Keep a journal.** Write every day, by the watch.
- **Bring something from home** that you can hold and look at.
- **Travel with a companion if possible**, or keep a schedule of check-ins with someone outside.
- **Know your exit.** Before you go, decide what would make you leave early: persistent dread, sleeplessness, feeling that time has come loose. Then honor that decision.
- **Give yourself long, idle hours.** The city rewards stillness. Many of its best moments (the dew creeping, the fog rivers, the lanterns opening, the moths spiraling up) only happen in front of someone who has stopped moving. Travelers who try to "see everything" tend to leave tired and oddly empty. Travelers who sit on one plate for a whole afternoon tend to leave changed.

## 12. Packing List

**Feet and hands**
- Stiff-soled, puncture-resistant, sticky-rubber boots with high ankles
- Gaiters
- Cut-resistant gloves (two pairs)
- Trekking pole(s) with rubber tips
- Three or more pairs of quick-dry socks, plus foot powder

**Clothing**
- Lightweight, quick-drying long sleeves and trousers
- Light waterproof shell
- Soft hat (for comfort, not sun)

**Navigation**
- Tally counter or knotted cord for seam-counting
- Notebook and pencils (waterproof paper if possible)
- Magnetic compass, for testing only
- Two watches of different mechanisms
- A measured pace length, memorized

**Light**
- Headlamp with red mode and very low white setting
- Spare batteries or a small solar or crank charger (charging will be slow in this dim sun)

**Water**
- Sealed water bladders or bottles, enough for twice your planned route
- Collection tarp or clean sheet with funnel
- Filter plus chemical or boiling treatment
- Stove and fuel, if you plan to boil

**Shelter**
- Waterproof bivy or small tent with a tough floor
- Heavy groundsheet
- Sleeping pad
- Earplugs (optional)

**Food**
- Everything you'll eat, plus a generous reserve
- Sealable waste bags for all food scraps and packaging
- Sealable human-waste bags

**Health**
- Laceration-focused first aid kit: closure strips, tissue adhesive, antiseptic, dressings
- Antibiotics (if available)
- Anti-nausea medication
- Pain relievers
- Eye drops
- Electrolytes
- Antifungal cream
- Vitamin D (longer stays)
- Small light-therapy lamp (optional, longer stays)
- Gas detector (only if you'll visit the seaward edge)

**Mind**
- Journal
- A comfort object from home
- A written exit plan

## 13. A Suggested First Day

**Arrival.** Stop. Don't walk anywhere yet. Sit on the plate where you arrived and let your eyes adjust for twenty minutes. Look at the ground near you, then gradually further out. Note your nearest stays. Record your arrival point in at least three independent ways.

**Dawn.** If you arrived at night, wake with the grey. Watch the dew on the plates beside you begin to creep. Put your ear to the stone and listen to the hiss. Collect your water.

**Mid-morning.** When the plates dry, walk to the nearest avenue, counting seams. Follow it for a short, measured distance and back. Learn how wrong your distance estimates are.

**Noon.** Rest in the shade of an arcade. Listen for its note. Look briefly at the smeared sun, then look away before the ache starts.

**Afternoon.** Find a terrace or bridge you can climb safely, with gloves on. Get up high before the fog.

**Late afternoon.** Watch the fog come in from the sea and run the avenues. Now you know which way is west.

**Dusk.** Watch the fog drain into the seams and the avenue-threads begin to glow. Walk back to camp along a known route while you still can.

**Night.** Headlamp off. If a stay is close, walk carefully to the edge of its wet ring and watch the lanterns open. Look for snail-light in the seams and moths circling upward. Sit until you're tired.

If you're lucky, somewhere in the dark to the west, the sea will go pale, and you'll hear the long breath of new ground coming into the world.

Sleep on warm stone, and in the morning, count your seams.
