# Preparing to Visit the Crown of the Stack

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

The Crown is not dangerous the way a jungle or a battlefield is dangerous. Nothing here wants to hurt you. It is dangerous the way the inside of a working machine is dangerous: indifferent, enormous, and running. You are a small soft thing on a narrow shelf beside a bottomless chimney, inside a sound too big to hear.

Most of your preparation comes down to three facts:

1. **There is no floor.** Anything that leaves the gallery is gone forever.
2. **There is no hearing.** Every warning, conversation, and cue you normally get through your ears is gone.
3. **There are two climates, and they meet in a line a few meters wide.** Inside is saturated and skin-warm. Outside is thin, freezing, and violent.

Everything else follows from these.

---

## Arrival

Whatever method you use to cross between worlds, **your arrival point must be on the gallery itself, against the wall.** Arriving even a few meters off would put you in the updraft over an unmeasured drop. If your method has any positional uncertainty, don't come. This place does not forgive imprecision.

Arrive already wearing your harness, and clip in within the first ten seconds. The sound will hit you immediately, and it will be disorienting. You do not want to be fumbling with a carabiner while your inner ear figures out what is happening.

If you can choose your timing, plan to arrive in daylight. The Crown is open to the sky, so it follows a real day. Arriving in the dark, into that roar, beside that void, is an experience that has broken sturdier people than you.

---

## The Sound: Your Primary Hazard

### What it will do to you

The note is mostly low-frequency, and much of it is probably infrasound, below the range of hearing. Infrasound doesn't just enter your ears. It enters your body. Expect some or all of the following:

- Pressure in the chest and sinuses, as though the air itself were heavier.
- Teeth, jaw, and eyeballs humming. Some frequencies in this range can make vision shimmer or blur at the edges.
- Nausea, especially in the first hour.
- **Unexplained dread.** Low-frequency sound is known to produce anxiety, a sense of being watched, and a feeling that something is wrong. Know this in advance. When the fear comes, and it probably will, you can name it: *this is the note, not a threat.*
- Fatigue out of proportion to your exertion. Your body is being shaken very gently, constantly, all over.

After about an hour, the description says, you stop perceiving it as sound and start perceiving it as a condition, like altitude. That is adaptation. It is not safety. Your body is still absorbing the vibration.

### Protection

- **Double hearing protection:** foam earplugs under sealed earmuffs. This will not stop the infrasound, which comes through bone and flesh, but it will cut the audible harmonics riding on top of it, and those are what damage hearing.
- **Limit exposure.** Plan visits in blocks of a few hours, with rest breaks at the rim (see below) if you can manage the transition, or at least with time spent pressed flat against the wall, where the vibration may feel different.
- **Plan for aftereffects.** Expect tinnitus and a strange, hollow sensitivity to ordinary sound for days after you leave. Get a hearing test before and after.
- **Anti-nausea medication** that doesn't cause drowsiness. You cannot afford drowsiness here.

### The loss of hearing as a sense

This is subtler and more important than the discomfort. You rely on hearing more than you realize:

- You won't hear your own footsteps, so you'll lose a surprising amount of your sense of balance and placement. **Look at your feet.** Every step.
- You won't hear metal groaning before it gives way.
- You won't hear a companion shout a warning.
- You won't hear your gear slipping, a buckle unclipping, or a zipper opening.
- You won't hear anything approaching from behind, including whoever polishes the top rungs of the ladder.

You have to replace hearing with sight and touch, deliberately, all the time.

## Communication

Speech is impossible. Before you arrive, your party must agree on and practice:

- **A hand-signal vocabulary.** At minimum: stop, go, danger, look, help, I'm okay, I'm not okay, clip in, unclip, up, down, turn back now. Practice until it's reflexive.
- **Touch signals.** Taps on the shoulder work when someone's back is turned, and squeezes work on a shared line. Climbers' rope-tug codes are a good model: one tug for "attention," two for "okay," three for "come to me."
- **Writing.** Paper will be soaked within minutes. Bring waterproof slates or plastic cards with grease pencils. Pre-print cards with common phrases.
- **Light signals.** Use them sparingly, for reasons explained under "The Frogs."

Phone vibration alerts will be lost in the vibration of everything. Any alarm you carry, including gas monitors and timers, **must be visual.**

## Falling, and the Rule of Tethers

### Your body

- Wear a full climbing harness at all times.
- Use a **double lanyard**, so you are always clipped with at least one arm while moving the other.
- **Test the railing before trusting it.** It is centuries-old iron in a condensation chamber. Clip to it if it holds, but where possible also clip to the wall's structural fittings: pipe brackets, rivet-heads that are clearly sound, the ladder. Distribute your trust.
- **Walk close to the wall.** The gallery is bolted to the wall, and the grating near the railing is farthest from support. It is also closest to the void.
- The grating is wet, mossy, and in places slick with snail trails. Those polished lines are beautiful, and they are ice. Wear soft-rubber, deeply lugged boots that you've already broken in on wet metal.
- Test each section of grating before putting your full weight on it, especially where rust plates have lifted.

### Everything else

Here the Crown is stranger than an ordinary cliff, because the draft goes *up*:

- **Heavy things fall** into the void and are gone.
- **Light things rise.** Hats, wrappers, paper, loose fabric, and hair will be lifted over the rail and carried up the column. They circle in the vortex and eventually stick to the walls, where they become permanent litter in a garden that has been untouched for centuries.
- **Things walk.** The description says loose objects crawl across the grating under the vibration. Set down a cup, turn away for ten minutes, and it may have migrated to the edge.

So: **everything is tethered or stowed.** Every tool, bottle, camera, and notebook gets its own lanyard. Hoods get cinched. Long hair gets braided and tucked. Nothing is ever set down unsecured, not even for a moment. Pack out every scrap, including food crumbs, because they will rise.

Your harness straps and lanyard tails will flutter upward in the draft. Tape down loose ends so they can't snag or unclip.

## The Warm Climate: Inside the Breath

The air is at skin temperature and saturated. Sweat won't evaporate, so your body cools poorly. Nothing will ever dry. The walls sweat cold water, the air hangs warm, and you will be damp within minutes and stay damp for the whole visit.

### Clothing

- Wear quick-drying synthetics, and no cotton at all. Cotton will become a cold, heavy rag.
- Choose light, loose-fitting clothes that won't chafe when soaked.
- Wear gloves with good wet grip. The rust flakes are sharp-edged and the plates peel "the size of hands."

### Your skin and feet

- Plan for prolonged wet exposure. Bring antifungal powder, spare socks in a drybag, and barrier cream for feet and chafe points.
- On long visits, check your feet daily. Trench foot is possible in warm conditions, not just cold ones.

### Your lungs

The breath comes up from an unknown furnace. It smells of hot metal, wet ash, and bread. That is lovely, and also a warning. You don't know what's burning.

- Carry a **gas monitor** (carbon monoxide, oxygen level, and ideally sulfur compounds) with a *visual* alarm, clipped where you'll see it.
- Wear a **respirator** with particulate filters. The air carries rust dust, spores, dried bird droppings, and insect fragments. Droppings in warm, damp, enclosed spaces can harbor fungal diseases, and you are standing inside a centuries-old swift colony.
- If the monitor ever alarms, or if the smell ever changes, go up to the rim or leave. Don't wait to find out why.

### Your blood

- Get a **tetanus booster** before you go. You will be handling rusted iron in a biologically rich environment, and cuts are likely.
- Bring a real first-aid kit: wound irrigation, antiseptic, waterproof dressings.

### Electronics

Everything must be sealed and waterproof, and everything will still suffer. Condensation will form inside any device that cools. Bring backups for anything important, and don't count on fine-tuned equipment surviving.

## The Cold Climate: The Rim and Beyond

The ladder climbs roughly eighty meters from the gallery to the rim, and there the world changes in a few steps.

### The climb

- Treat it as a via ferrata. Stay clipped continuously, moving one lanyard at a time.
- The lower rungs are furred with moss and wet. Test every rung.
- The upward draft pushes against your body. That may feel helpful, but it's uneven, and it will tug at anything loose.
- Your hands will tire faster than you expect, because the vibration is constantly working the small muscles. Rest often, clipped in.
- **The upper rungs are polished bright.** Someone or something climbs this ladder. No one has seen who. Be courteous. Don't linger at the top, don't block the ladder, and don't leave anything on it. If you ever meet whoever it is, you cannot speak to them, so move aside, show open hands, and let them pass. It seems wise not to follow.

### The transition

This is the part most likely to kill an unprepared visitor. You will climb out of skin-warm saturated air, soaked to the bone, into freezing, thin, high-altitude wind. **Wet clothing in that wind will freeze almost instantly.** Hypothermia can set in within minutes.

- Carry a **dry insulating layer and windproof shell** in a sealed drybag, and never open that bag inside the Crown.
- Stop just below the frost line, where there's a clean stripe between orchids and frost, and change there. Strip the wettest layers if you can, towel off quickly, and put on the dry kit.
- Wear a hat, a face covering, and insulated gloves. Your damp skin will be very vulnerable to frostbite.

### The altitude

The Crown sits "far above the clouds," and the air outside is thin. Expect hypoxia at the rim: headache, breathlessness, poor judgment. You're climbing hard, and possibly with lungs already irritated by the damp.

- If the altitude is significant, bring **supplemental oxygen** for rim visits.
- Acclimatize beforehand if you can, somewhere high in your own world.
- Know the signs of altitude sickness, and turn back at the first confusion.

### The wind

Outside, the wind moves sideways, hard. Stay low, stay clipped, and don't stand upright on the rim edge. The rim's iron will be coated in frost and ice, so you'll want microspikes or crampons for the outside, carried separately.

### What you're climbing for

From the rim you'll see the cloud sea and the other stacks, each breathing its plume, each with its green crown. If you're very still, you'll feel their notes through the iron: a slow chord that never resolves. It is worth the climb. Budget time just to stand there and feel it. You won't be able to reason your way into feeling it, so give it time.

Then get back down before the cold gets into you.

## Living With the Garden: Conduct and Ethics

This is an ecosystem built over centuries, and it is extraordinarily fragile in specific ways. The rule of the Crown is that nothing is made anew. That applies to damage, too: anything you break will be broken for a very long time.

### The rust is the soil

Don't peel the rust plates, however tempting they are. Behind each one is the thin substrate that everything grows in. Handhold only on bare metal, brass, rungs, and fittings. Don't grab at plants for balance; the tree ferns and figs are rooted in shallow crust, and your weight could strip a patch of wall that took a century to grow.

### The snails

A snail on the gallery is "a landmark for a generation." Step over them, never on them. Don't move them, even to save them from your path. Walk around. Their trails are slippery, and they are also the only straight lines in the garden. Try not to scuff them.

### The spiders' threads

The rising silver lines can be hundreds of meters long and strong enough to hold a live bird. Don't cut them. Don't walk through them if you can avoid it. They may be sticky enough to foul your face, respirator, or lanyards. If one catches you, free yourself slowly and carefully, damaging as little of it as possible. Keep your tools and tethers away from them; a tangled lanyard in a rising thread is a hazard to you both.

### The swifts

They have never landed. Their nests are felted cups hanging on the upward-streaming vines. Don't touch the vines, don't disturb the nests, and don't reach into the column. From the gallery the birds look like ash on the breath. Let them.

### The moths

They navigate by the note, and they cluster around the gauges. Don't touch the gauges. Don't brush the moths away. They are blind and reading the room through their bodies, and a sudden movement or a foreign vibration source may confuse them. Avoid leaning your whole body against the pipes they gather on. You will be a strange dampened patch in the resonance they read.

### The frogs

This is the subtle one. The frogs speak in light, flashing their throat sacs orange on the long evenings. Here the wall's conversation is visual, because the roar has taken all the sound.

**Your headlamp is a shout in their language.** A sweeping white beam across the wall in the evening is the equivalent of someone walking through a quiet library with a bullhorn.

- Use **dim red light** only, pointed at your own feet or hands.
- Turn it off when you don't need it.
- In the evenings, the best thing you can do is stop, sit, clip in, go dark, and watch.

Don't touch the frogs. Translucent amphibians have delicate, absorbent skin, and the sunscreen, insect repellent, and oils on your hands could harm them. Some amphibians are also toxic to people. Don't find out.

### The machinery

- **The valve wheel** turns a fraction of a degree a year. It is no danger to you, but don't obstruct it, hang anything on it, or brace yourself against a spoke. The ferns around it have grown bent to accommodate it over decades. Respect the arrangement.
- **The hatch** no longer opens. Don't try it. Whatever the hatch leads to, and whatever keeps it shut, it has been undisturbed a very long time. The Crown's machinery "continued without anyone." You don't know what depends on it staying that way.
- **The maker's mark**, a circle with a line through it, a stack or an eye, is worth photographing. Brush the moss aside gently with a fingertip, not a blade, then let it settle back.

### Food and water

- **Water:** The condensation is abundant, but it runs over rust, droppings, and rot. Filter it *and* purify it. Expect a heavy iron taste.
- **Food:** Bring all your own. The figs are tempting. They ripen and fall *up* for a while before gravity wins, which is enchanting. But this is an alien world's fig, grown in rust and the updraft of an unknown furnace. Don't eat them. If you must, a single small taste after a long wait is the most you should risk. They also belong to the garden, not to you.
- Pack out every crumb. Crumbs rise.

## Sleeping in the Crown

If your visit lasts more than a day:

- Sleep **clipped in**, with your harness on and short lanyards to two separate anchors.
- A portaledge or a hammock slung between sound wall fittings, over the gallery rather than the void, will be more comfortable than wet grating. But the updraft may make a hammock sway. Test it.
- The roar may make sleep impossible. It may also, for some people, become a strange comfort, like sleeping in a moving train. Bring a sleep aid you've tested at home, one that doesn't leave you groggy.
- Keep your dry kit sealed. Always.
- Wake with a visual alarm. You can't hear any other kind.

## Timing: The Seven-Year Slackening

Every seven years or so, the draft slackens for a season. The heat drops, the note falls, and for a few weeks the Crown goes almost quiet. You can hear rain on the iron, the drip of condensation, and a frog calling aloud.

This is the easiest time for a human to be here. It is also the most fragile time for everything else.

- The swifts must land for the first time in their lives, and they cling to the vines, bewildered. They will be exhausted and vulnerable.
- The threads sag, and the rising seeds settle.
- The whole garden is between states.
