# Preparing for the Striking Room

## The One Thing to Understand First

Everything in this room survives by one habit: **it moves in the gap and holds still at the blow.** The mites do it. The tenders do it. The moths are shaken loose by it and use it. The room will try to teach you the same habit whether you want to learn it or not. You should learn it, deliberately, *before* you go, so that you are following a rule you chose rather than a rhythm that has chosen you.

The second thing to understand is less obvious. **You are a cool surface.**

The mineral vapor condenses on anything cooler than the air. The chamber is kiln-warm, almost certainly hotter than your skin. That means the vapor will do to you what it does to a dropped coin. It will begin, slowly, to grow on you: on your clothes, your eyelashes, the wet surface of your eyes, and the inside of your lungs. The boots at the foot of the ladder are full of needles for a reason. Plan your whole visit around this fact.

## Before You Go

### Who shouldn't go

- **Anyone with a heart rhythm condition, a pacemaker, or a history of arrhythmia.** The strike is felt in the sternum, and the room entrains heartbeats. You do not want your heart negotiating with an iron hammer.
- **Anyone with epilepsy or photosensitivity.** The light is dim, but the shard-sparks are rhythmic and strictly periodic.
- **Anyone with asthma, COPD, or other lung disease.** See the section on dust.
- **Anyone who struggles with heat.** That includes people on diuretics or other medications that affect sweating or hydration.
- **Anyone who finds repetition soothing in a way they can't easily set down.** Be honest with yourself about this one. The people most at risk here are the ones who like it.

### Training at home

1. **Learn the interval.** If anyone has measured it, get the number, since it's the only number in the ledger. If no one has, plan to spend your first minutes at the top of the crack timing it. Then practice with a metronome set to that interval until your body knows it: move in the gap, freeze before the blow. Practice freezing mid-step, mid-reach, mid-climb.
2. **Practice your own counter-rhythm.** This is just as important. Pick something that doesn't fit the interval, such as a count, a song, or a breathing pattern, and practice keeping it while the metronome runs. In the room it becomes your anchor. You aren't trying to fight the beat. You're trying to remember that you have a beat of your own.
3. **Agree on hand signals with your partner.** Speech will not work down there. Keep the set small: *OK, not OK, look, stop, leave now, I'm fine but I need to leave, you need to leave.* That last one matters, because you may be the one who needs to be told.
4. **Hydrate for two days beforehand.** Arrive with your body already full.

### Go with a partner

This isn't optional. One of you watches the other at all times. Watch for these warning signs:

- stops flinching at the blow
- stands still through a gap
- forgets to drink
- smiles without reason
- is breathing in time with the room

Any of those means the visit is over.

## Equipment

### Respiration (the most important item)

The dust is ground finer by every blow, and some of the crystal grows as **hairlike fibers**. Treat it as you would asbestos or silica. Surgical masks and cloth are useless here.

- **The minimum** is a tight-fitting full-face respirator with P100/FFP3 particulate filters. Fit-test it first.
- **The better choice** is a supplied-air system, a small SCBA. The vapor is not dust, and a particulate filter will not stop it. It condenses on cooler surfaces, and your airway is one. A few breaths probably won't hurt you. An hour of breathing it might leave something growing inside you. Supplied air also sets a hard time limit, which is a feature: when the bottle runs low, you leave, and no one has to argue about it.
- Carry a spare filter or a spare bottle, and know how to change it by feel.

### Eyes and face

- The full-face mask covers this, or use sealed goggles plus a face shield.
- Every strike throws shards of new crystal "impossibly sharp." They will be flying at face height near the engine.
- Leave contact lenses at home. The dryness will glue them to your eyes.

### Hearing

- **Double protection:** foam earplugs under over-ear muffs.
- This will not stop the blow. Nothing will. The thud travels through your bones. What it will stop is the ringing that "no longer seems to belong to your ears." Your hearing is a finite resource, and this room spends it quickly.

### Head

- A climbing or construction helmet. Needles snap across the whole ceiling at each strike, and dust and moths come down with them.

### Hands and arms

- **Cut-resistant gloves** (ANSI A7+ or equivalent) for the crack, which has edges "sharp enough to take skin." Wear a second, grippier pair over them for climbing.
- Long sleeves in a cut-resistant fabric, or arm guards.

### Body

- **An inner layer** that is loose, light, and moisture-wicking.
- **An outer layer** that is cheap, tough, and disposable, such as a coverall you will cut off and throw away at the top. Vapor will deposit on it, and mites will cling to it. It will not come home with you.
- **About cooling vests:** it's tempting to wear an ice vest in kiln heat. Be careful. Anything much colder than the air becomes a magnet for deposition. If you use cooling, keep it *inside* insulation, so your outer surface warms to near ambient and the vapor has less reason to choose you. Make yourself less of a target, not more.

### Feet and legs

- **High, sealed boots** with stiff, puncture-resistant soles.
- **Gaiters** taped or strapped over them. The dust is ankle-deep and flows like silt. Things live in it, and you do not want either the dust or the things inside your boots.
- Knee pads for the crack.
- **Do not take your boots off down there, for any reason.**

### Hydration

- A sealed bladder with a bite valve, filled with electrolyte solution. Carry more than you think you need. Don't use open bottles, which would collect dust and deposition.
- Sip on a schedule, not on thirst. Your partner enforces this.

### Light

- A headlamp with a low setting, plus a spare. The room's dimness makes shards and eel-ripples hard to judge. Use enough light to see your feet, but don't flood the chamber. You don't know what bright light does to anything here.

### Timekeeping

- **A vibrating timer set to an interval that is *not* the room's.** Something slightly off works well, like a buzz every 47 seconds against the hammer's beat. It is a small, stubborn reminder that there is another time.
- **A hard-stop alarm for your total visit.** Set it before descent and do not extend it once you're below.

### Rope

- The crack's edges are glass-fresh and will cut ordinary rope through under load. Use **edge protectors** at every contact point, or a steel cable or chain handline.
- Anchor at the surface, not in the crack. You don't know how long any given crystal face will last.

### Tethers

- Tether everything: headlamp, spare filter, water, tools, camera. "The room accepts offerings but doesn't keep them." Anything you drop is gone. It will be furred within hours and part of the wall within a day.

## The Descent

The crack slants down through salt-white stone with amber banding. It is new, and nobody knows *why* it is new. It may have been split by the propagating shock of a strike. If so, the same deposition that closes the chamber could narrow the crack over hours or days. **Your exit is not guaranteed to stay the size it was.** This is one more reason to keep the visit short.

- Descend slowly, taking contact on gloves and pads, never bare skin.
- Mark the route with reflective tape at each turn. Retrieve it on the way up.
- The heat builds as you go down. Stop partway, check each other, and drink.
- You will feel the blow in your chest before you hear it. Use the descent to confirm your trained interval against the real one. By the bottom, you should already be freezing on the beat.

## Inside: How to Move

### The rule

- **Move in the gap.** The mites tell you when it's safe. Their chittering rises after each strike and **cuts off precisely before the next.** When the chittering stops, stop. Plant your feet and brace. Keep your hands off the walls.
- **The blow comes.** The floor jumps. Shards fly. Needles snap everywhere. Moths fall.
- **The ringing fades. The mites swarm. Move again.**

This is the one place where it is wise to trust the animals completely. "They have never been wrong about the timing, because the timing has never changed."

### Where to stand

- **Near the entry, against a wall with no overhang**, where there is less crystal to snap above you. Look first, and don't lean.
- **Keep away from the anvil-bed and the engine.** Shards are thickest there. The eels also gather most densely near the point of impact, and "the ones nearest the hammer grow the largest." You don't know what they do to a foot.
- **The ember-moss by the vents is the only soft thing in the room**, and the mites avoid it. That's tempting. But the vents are where the heat is strongest and the vapor is densest, so it's a poor place to linger. Look at it, then step back.

### Don't touch

- **The walls.** Every edge is new. If you brush a wall during a strike, you'll be cut through the glove.
- **The engine.** Nothing about it is yours to adjust. Do not climb it. The tenders scrape off whatever grows on the machine, and you don't want to find out whether they count you as growth.
- **The lamp.** It has no wire. It simply runs. Leave it that way.
- **The hammer's path.** This needs no explanation.

### Never try to stop the engine

Not out of curiosity, pity, or scientific interest. Without the hammer, the room fills "like a fist within weeks." The crack you came down may fill too. Everything living here is built around that interval. So, possibly, is something larger. You can't know what depends on it.

## The Inhabitants: How to Share the Room

- **Interval-mites:** they are everywhere, harmless as far as anyone knows, and your best clock. They will get on you. Before you climb out, brush down during the gaps, and plan to shed them with your outer layer at the top. Don't carry them out. They eat fresh crystal and would starve anywhere else.
- **Glass-moths:** they will fall past you and possibly onto you. Their wings are mineral plates, probably sharp at the edge. Don't swat them. Let them slide off your helmet and shoulders. Their chiming as they fall is one of the loveliest sounds you will ever hear. Listen, but listen carefully, since this is exactly the kind of beauty that keeps you standing one more cycle.
- **Dust-eels:** watch for the ripples. At each strike you'll see their backs break the surface. Keep your weight on your feet so you don't kneel or fall into the dust. Stay out of the dense zone near the anvil.
- **Tenders:** watch them from a distance. They are the room's clockwork conscience. Nothing suggests they care about you, and you should keep it that way.
- **Thread-root:** it hangs high, unchanging, and swaying. Leave it alone.

## The Remains

The catwalk, the cabin, the ledger, the tools, and the boots.

- **Don't climb the catwalk.** Its bolts have endured an unknown number of shocks. Its railings are furred with needles and can't be gripped. Even if it holds, it puts you at mid-height, in the band where needles snap and moths fall.
- **Don't try to free the ledger.** Its pages are fused into a single block. You'd shatter it and learn nothing more than you already know from looking: the same figure, over and over.
- **Photograph the boots, and think about them.** They stand neatly side by side. Whoever wore them took them off calmly, as though settling in. Their owner isn't in them. You don't know what happened, but the room's rule suggests an answer: everything left here is grown over, broken free, and worn down to nothing. The boots are the most important safety sign in the chamber, and nobody wrote it.

## How Long to Stay

**Short.** Twenty to forty minutes on the floor is plenty for a first visit. Less is better. Base the limit on the lowest of these:

- your air supply, minus the whole climb out plus a margin
- your water
- your partner's judgment
- your hard-stop alarm

Watch for these signals to leave early:

- Your lips split, or your eyes feel gritty behind the seal.
- You notice fine amber fuzz on your outer layer, especially in folds and on cooler surfaces like buckles and fittings. The room has started to accept you.
- You realize you haven't been counting your own counter-rhythm for a while.
- **You stopped flinching.** This is the real warning. The description says it plainly: "The room isn't welcoming you. It is... fitting you into the equilibrium." Once the blow feels natural, the room is winning. That moment is your cue to leave.

When your partner signals *leave now*, you leave. Don't negotiate with your hands. Negotiate at the top.

## The Ascent

- Climb in the gaps. Freeze on the blow, even partway up the crack, where the shock still travels through the rock.
- Check your rope protectors as you go.
- Collect your route markers.
- The sound will follow you, fading in layers: the chittering, then the chiming, then the ringing, then only the blow. Keep counting your own rhythm the whole way.

---

## Decontamination at the Top

Set this up *before* you descend.

1. With your respirator still on, strip the outer coverall and cut it off rather than pulling it over your head. Bag and seal it. It stays in this world, or gets buried.
2. Brush and vacuum your boots and gaiters with a HEPA vacuum. Check the tread for needles.
3. Only then remove the respirator. Wipe it down and bag the filters.
4. Rinse your eyes with saline. Moisten your nose with saline spray. Put balm on your lips.
5. Drink slowly and steadily.
6. Check each other for crystal on the eyelashes, at the hairline, at the corners of the mouth, and in the ears.
7. **Take nothing out.** Not a shard, not a moth wing, not a pinch of dust. You don't know whether the crystal keeps growing when it's carried away, or what it would grow from.

## Aftercare

The description is honest about what comes next: "For days afterward you wake in the night having stopped breathing, waiting for it."

Treat it like a real physiological aftereffect, because it is one.

- **Don't sleep alone for the first few nights.** Tell whoever is with you what to watch for: long pauses in your breathing, followed by a start.
- **Consider a gradual taper.** Some travelers find it helps to fall asleep to a recording of the interval played quietly, getting fainter and slowing slightly each night, until it's gone. The idea is to let the rhythm unwind rather than be torn out. Stop at once if it makes things worse.
- **Try irregular sound.** Rain, surf, and wind are all good, because none of them keeps time. Remind your body what non-periodic sound is like.
- **See a doctor** for:
  - apneic episodes that last more than a few nights
  - palpitations or heart rhythm changes
  - a persistent cough, or chest tightness
  - ringing that doesn't fade within a week
- **Get a hearing test** afterward. Get one beforehand too, so you have a comparison.
- Give it time. The room exerts a pull because it is perfectly, terribly complete. Some people find themselves wanting to go back. If you catch yourself planning a second trip mostly to feel the stillness of it again, talk that over with someone before you pack.

---

## A Last Thought

What you are visiting is not a ruin or a machine. It is a balance, one that has held so long nothing inside it remembers anything else. It is beautiful: the amber light, the sparks across the ceiling, the moths spiraling down through the shards, the mites' chittering rolling outward in waves and stopping, all at once, exactly on time.

Go down knowing that the room will try to make you part of it, because that is all it knows how to do with anything. Your job is to be the one thing in the chamber that leaves on its own schedule.

Keep your boots on. Keep your count. Come back up.
