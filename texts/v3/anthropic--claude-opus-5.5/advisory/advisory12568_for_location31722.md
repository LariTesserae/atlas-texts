# Preparing for the Held Country

*A traveler's briefing*

---

## The Short Version

The Held Country is a mountain-altitude bog the size of a continent, under a permanent cloud ceiling, where everything that stops moving is slowly absorbed. It is warm, dim, wet, quiet, and gentle, and it will not help you. Nothing here is trying to hurt you. The danger comes from how completely the place is organized around rules that don't include you.

You will be wet the entire time. You cannot light a fire, cannot use the sun or stars, cannot eat the plants, cannot be rescued, and cannot rest in one place for long. Your footprints may outlast your grandchildren.

If you accept all of that, it may be one of the most peaceful places you ever stand.

---

## 1. The Body Problems

### Altitude

The plateau sits two to three miles up, roughly 3,000 to 5,000 meters. The warmth and humidity will fool you. The air feels thick, wet, and heavy, like a greenhouse, and nothing about it says "high mountain." But the oxygen is thin.

- **Acclimatize before you go.** Spend a week or more at comparable altitude in your home world, or have a staged-entry plan. Arriving directly from sea level at 4,500 meters is how people get high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema, and there is no descent available. The only way down is the edge, which is a miles-tall cliff into cloud.
- **Bring acetazolamide or your equivalent, plus dexamethasone for emergencies.** Know the signs of HAPE and HACE: breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, confusion, loss of coordination. In the dim, shadowless light, it will be harder to notice your companion stumbling.
- **Your exit is your descent.** Know exactly how to reach your extraction point, and treat severe altitude illness as a reason to leave the world immediately.

### Saturation

The humidity is effectively 100% at all times, and at night the fog is "almost liquid." Every consequence of this matters.

- **Sweat will not evaporate.** You lose your main cooling mechanism. Even at mild temperatures, a heavy pack and a springy, exhausting surface can cause heat strain. Move slowly. Walk the way the grazers do.
- **Your feet are the most vulnerable part of you.** Warm-water immersion foot can develop within two to three days of constant wet in warm conditions. It is painful, disabling, and far from help. You need:
  - Many pairs of socks in sealed bags. Wool or wool blends are best because they stay warm and don't disintegrate.
  - Silicone-based or zinc-oxide barrier cream, applied every night.
  - A disciplined routine of removing footwear, drying your feet as much as possible (a small absorbent towel wrung out constantly), airing them in the morning when the fog lifts, and inspecting for maceration and white, wrinkled, painful skin.
- **Skin folds, groin, and armpits will develop fungal infections.** Bring antifungal powder and cream in quantity. Wear loose clothing.
- **Nothing will dry.** Quick-dry fabric is meaningless here. Choose fabrics that remain tolerable when wet (merino, loose synthetics) over fabrics meant to shed water. Pack spare layers in waterproof dry-bags, and keep at least one fully dry set sealed for sleeping or emergencies.
- **Mold will colonize your gear.** Everything organic you carry (paper, leather, cotton, food packaging) will grow something within days. Bring nothing that can't tolerate it.

### Temperature

It's warm, "bathwater" in the sponge, and the mat radiates held heat. Hypothermia is unlikely but not impossible. At altitude, soaked, exhausted, and immobile after an injury, you could still chill overnight. Bring one lightweight insulating layer that works wet, such as synthetic fill or fleece.

### Light and Eyes

This will affect you more than you expect.

- Daylight is roughly as bright as a room with the curtains drawn, green-tinted, and **shadowless.** Shadows are one of the main ways your brain judges depth and surface shape. Without them, a leaf and the space behind it "seem to sit at the same distance." Expect eye strain, headaches, clumsiness, and misjudged steps. Trekking poles are essential partly because they let you test the ground your eyes can't read.
- At night it is fully dark except for the lichen glow. Let your eyes adapt. Use a **red, dim, low-held light** only when necessary. A bright headlamp in dense fog creates a white wall of backscatter and blinds you. Held at knee height and pointed down, a weak light shows the path. Worn on your forehead, it shows only fog.
- You may experience circadian drift. There is a day and night cycle, but no sun disc, no dawn colors, and no visible sky. Keep a watch, and treat the fog's descent and lift as your real clock.

## 2. The Ground

### Walking on the Sward

The surface is deep, springy moss over a thin skin, and beneath it lies the sponge, a warm, waterlogged layer "several yards thick." This is bog walking, and bog walking is exhausting.

- **Expect to cover a fraction of normal distance.** Every step sinks and rebounds. Plan for perhaps 5 to 10 miles on a good day on established paths, and far less off them.
- **The sward may not hold everywhere.** If you can push an arm down through it, a heavy human with a pack might occasionally break through into the sponge. Being waist-deep in warm, fibrous, waterlogged material with no firm bottom is a real hazard, especially alone. Carry trekking poles with wide baskets and probe ahead in soft-looking or unusually lush areas. Consider broad-soled footwear or light bog-shoes. The grazers' broad feet are a hint.
- **Walk the pale paths.** The silver-green tracks are where grazers have pressed and compacted the sward for centuries. They are firmer, tested, and they bend away from dangers like the edge. They are also, ethically, where you should walk. See below.

### The Stem-Forest

The clubmoss towers grow so close that you must turn sideways between them.

- **A wide pack will be a torment.** Use a tall, narrow pack that hugs your spine, with nothing strapped to the sides.
- **Don't try to cut your way through.** It would be slow and destructive, and it's pointless. The land has no stake in your convenience, and a hacked trail is exactly the kind of mark you shouldn't leave. Go around, or follow the grazers, whose passage keeps the forest open.
- **Water-spider webs** will be strung across your way, heavy with dew. You will walk into them constantly unless you learn to see them. They're harmless, but each one is a spider's night of work and its morning water. Duck when you can.

### The Weave

Below the sponge, the living root is "braided so tight a knife would skid off it." You will never reach it by digging, and you shouldn't try. It is the body of the country.

## 3. The Eyes

The round, dark pools are the most beautiful things you'll see and the most dangerous.

- **Do not swim.** They are fathoms deep and root-walled, with no bottom you can find. The water is tea-dark and clear. You'd be putting sunscreen, soap residue, skin bacteria, and foreign microbes directly into the country's circulatory system. The root-eels live in the channels that open into them.
- **Do not stand at the rim.** Once a year, somewhere in the country, an eye's wall "softens and collapses inward." The odds that it's the one you're standing at are tiny, but the edges of these pools are root over water, and some rims may be undercut. Admire them from a few body-lengths back, on firm sward.
- **Leave the ring-flowers alone.** They grow nowhere else, and they mark the eyes' slow tides like a record book.
- **Leave the offerings exactly where they are.** A pale pebble on a leaf at the waterline is not a curiosity. It is a message you are not the recipient of.

## 4. The Rule of Stillness

This is the defining fact of the place, so think about it carefully.

**The mat grows over whatever does not move.** A feather is threaded with root-hair in a week. A carcass is covered in a season.

### What It Means for Sleep

A single night's sleep will not bury you. But:

- **Anything you leave in one place for several days will begin to be colonized.** Rotate your campsite every night. Every morning, pick up your pad, your pack, and your tarp, and shake and inspect them. Check the underside of anything that sat on the sward. Pull off root-hairs before they take hold.
- **Sleep like the animals: in shifts.** If you travel in a pair or group, and you should, one person stays awake and moving while others sleep. This isn't because anyone will be engulfed overnight. It builds the habit of motion, keeps watch in a disorienting dark, and makes sure someone notices if a sleeper isn't waking properly, such as from altitude illness.
- **Sleep off the ground if possible.** A short hammock or raised sleeping arrangement keeps you out of the saturated sward and reduces contact. The towers are close-set, so a hammock would have to be short. A thick closed-cell pad on the sward, moved nightly, also works.
- **The bent houses are "dry-ish."** They may be the best sleeping places in the country. But they were made by someone, over generations, with patience and hands. Enter as a guest. Sleep near the door. Take nothing, move nothing, and leave the floor as you found it. If grazers are sheltering inside, let them have it. They were invited, or at least tolerated, long before you.

### What It Means for Injury

This is the real danger. **An immobilized human is, to the Held Country, a still thing.**

A broken ankle here isn't just an injury. It means you can't keep moving. If you are alone, it means lying in warm, wet moss, far from help, while root-hair begins to find your clothes and eventually any open wound. Colonization would be slow, over days to weeks, but so would any rescue, because:

- **There is no evacuation.** The cloud ceiling sits a hundred feet above the tallest growth. No aircraft could navigate beneath it. There is no road, no river to float out on, no downhill.
- **Therefore: do not travel alone.** Carry a comprehensive wilderness first-aid kit, including splinting materials, wound-closure strips, and occlusive dressings to keep wounds sealed from the constant moisture and whatever grows in it. Know how to improvise a drag-litter from your gear. Have an extraction plan that doesn't depend on walking.
- **If you die here, you will be taken.** Within a year you would be a slight swell in the sward, "flowering a little brighter than the moss around it." Some would find that beautiful. Your family might not. Leave instructions and expectations with them before you go.

## 5. Water and Food

### Water

You'll never lack it.

- **Fog condensate is the cleanest source.** String a tarp or fine mesh at night and collect the beads in the morning. That's the water-spider method, and it's a good one.
- **Sponge water** runs "clear and faintly sweet" when squeezed. The sweetness suggests dissolved sugars or organic compounds, which could feed microbes your gut has never met. Filter and treat it.
- **Don't draw from the eyes.** Treat them as the country's arteries, not your well.

### Food

**Bring all of it.** This is non-negotiable.

- The plant life is clubmoss, fern, moss, and lichen analogues. In most worlds, many ferns are toxic or carcinogenic, contain enzymes that destroy vitamin B1, or are simply indigestible. Clubmosses contain alkaloids. You have no way to test safely, and poisoning at altitude with no rescue could be fatal.
- The translucent frogs may carry skin toxins, and your skin oils and sunscreen will harm them. Don't handle them.
- The grazers are the only large potential food source. Killing one would be ethically dubious in a place you're visiting, practically difficult, and pointless without fire to cook it.
- **No fire. None.** Not because the peat-like mat would easily burn (it's saturated) but because if any part of it did, you'd be igniting the body of a continent. Deep peat fires can smolder for years. Bring a sealed, contained stove only if you absolutely need hot food. Use it on a non-flammable base, never directly on the sward, and understand that it will run poorly in thin air.
- **Pack calorie-dense, moisture-sealed food.** Every package must be airtight, or it will be moldy in days. Plan for high caloric needs, because bog-walking at altitude burns a great deal of energy.

### Waste

- Urine is probably fine on the sward, away from eyes and any visible channel openings.
- Solid waste will be absorbed by the mat within weeks. You can't bury anything here, so deposit it well away from eyes, pale paths, stone rings, and bent houses. If you're on medication, consider packing out waste in sealed bags. You're depositing unknown chemistry into a single interconnected capillary system.
- **Pack out every piece of trash.** Anything you drop will be threaded with roots and carried into the body of the land. Nothing is buried here; things are *taken up*. Don't make the country take up your wrappers.

## 6. Navigation

This will be one of your hardest problems.

- **No sun disc. No stars. No moon.** Celestial navigation is impossible.
- **No landmarks.** The land is nearly flat, the swells run up to a hundred miles crest to crest, and everything is "green on green on green." There are no rocks, rivers, or peaks, and at night the fog is total.
- **A compass may or may not work.** It depends on whether this world has a magnetic field. There's almost no rock to cause local interference, so if the field exists, a compass may be unusually reliable. Test it at your arrival point before committing to it.
- **Satellite positioning won't exist.** Assume none.
- **Use dead reckoning:** compass bearing (if available), pace counting, and a watch. Record everything in a waterproof notebook with a pencil, since ink will run and paper will rot.
- **Read the fog.** It pools in the troughs between swells, so in early morning or evening the low mist reveals the shape of the land that your eyes can't see in full daylight.
- **Follow the pale paths and the herds.** The grazers have walked these routes for centuries. Following a herd at a respectful distance is probably the safest way to move across the country. They avoid the edge, they know the firm ground, and they gather on crests at night.
- **Your arrival point will vanish.** Any marker you leave will be overgrown in weeks. Mark it with something the land won't swallow quickly, like a tall, bright, synthetic pole, and record precise bearings and pace-counts from it. Better still, have an extraction method that doesn't depend on returning to an exact spot.
- **Don't travel in the night fog.** You'll lose the path, and you won't be able to see the eyes until you're in one.
