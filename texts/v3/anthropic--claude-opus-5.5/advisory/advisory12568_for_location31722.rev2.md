# Preparing for the Held Country

## A Traveler's Briefing

---

## First, What It Will Actually Be Like

Forget the romance for a moment. Here is the lived experience.

You will arrive in a warm, dim, dripping green place where nothing is dry, nothing casts a shadow, and the ground gives under every step. You will be two to three miles above sea level, and your body will notice before your mind does. Within an hour your clothes will be damp all the way through, and they will stay that way until you leave. The light will feel like late afternoon in a closed room for your entire stay. The silence will be deeper than any you have heard. When you finally notice the sounds under it, the ticking in the ground and the hum in the fog, you will not be able to stop hearing them.

The place is not hostile. Nothing here hunts you, and the air is breathable and the water is sweet. But the Held Country has a single, patient appetite for things that stop moving, and almost every serious danger comes from that fact, or from altitude, wetness, or disorientation. It is a gentle place that can kill you softly, mostly by letting you rest.

Prepare accordingly.

---

## I. The Body: Altitude and Endurance

### Acclimatize before you go

The plateau sits at roughly 10,000 to 16,000 feet. The warmth and the lush growth make this easy to forget. The description reads like a lowland jungle and feels like one to the skin, but your lungs will be operating on perhaps 55 to 70 percent of the oxygen they're used to.

- **Spend at least one to two weeks at comparable altitude beforehand**, ideally somewhere that also has similar humidity if you can find it. A high cloud forest would be ideal training.
- **Know the symptoms of altitude illness**: headache, nausea, breathlessness at rest, confusion, a wet crackle in the lungs, loss of coordination. In the Held Country, confusion is doubly dangerous, because the landscape already confuses.
- **Bring altitude medication** (acetazolamide or your world's equivalent) and dexamethasone for emergencies. Discuss with a physician first.
- **There is no "going down."** On an ordinary mountain, the cure for altitude sickness is descent. Here, the only way down is a miles-high cliff into cloud. **Your exit method is your only evacuation route.** If you develop cerebral or pulmonary edema, you must be able to leave the world entirely, quickly. Plan for that before anything else.

### Train your legs for moss

The sward is deep, springy moss. Walking on it is like walking on a mattress, or on wet sand that doesn't quite hold. It is far more tiring than it looks, and it works muscles in the ankles, calves, and hips that pavement never touches.

- Train on soft sand, deep snow, bog, or thick foam mats with a loaded pack.
- Practice **walking sideways** under load. In the stem-forest the columns grow so close together that you'll often have to turn and edge between them. Your pack profile matters enormously (see gear).
- Build **slow, sustained endurance** rather than speed. Distances here are vast and the pace is unhurried. Hundreds of miles of near-flat ground, with no reason to rush and every reason not to.

### Heat in saturated air

The Held Country is warm and at or near 100% humidity, especially at night when the fog becomes "almost liquid." **Sweat will not evaporate.** Your body's main cooling method stops working.

- Exert gently. Overheating creeps up without the warning of a dry, hot sensation.
- Walk in the **morning**, when the country exhales and the fog lifts. That is the most open, breathable part of the day.
- Hydrate with electrolytes. You will lose salt even though you never feel sweaty, only wet.

## II. The Wet

This is the single biggest practical challenge, and the one visitors most underestimate.

**Nothing dries. Ever.** Every surface beads with water every night. The morning "lift" is an exhale, not a drying wind. Anything you bring must function while soaking wet, indefinitely.

### Clothing

- **No cotton at all.** Cotton wet is cotton useless: heavy, chafing, cold if the temperature ever dips, and quick to rot.
- **Merino wool and quick-drying synthetics.** Wool stays tolerable when wet and resists odor. It will not dry, but it will be *less* miserable.
- **Loose-fitting layers.** Tight clothing plus constant moisture equals chafing, and chafing becomes open skin within days.
- **Skip heavy rain gear.** It barely rains, and a waterproof shell in 100% humidity at warm temperatures just becomes a sweat-bag. A very light, breathable shell for the evening fog is enough. Accept being damp.
- **Many, many socks.** Carry more pairs than you think reasonable, sealed in dry bags. Change them at least twice a day.

### Feet

Trench foot, known elsewhere as immersion foot, is a genuine threat. Weeks of wet feet cause tissue breakdown, pain, fungal infection, and eventually serious injury.

- **Boots:** lightweight, fast-draining, with mesh or drain ports. Heavy waterproof leather boots will fill and never empty. Think jungle boots, canyoneering shoes, or wading boots with good ankle support. The moss gives unevenly and ankles roll.
- **Every rest stop:** remove boots, wring socks, massage feet, apply a barrier balm (zinc, lanolin, or silicone-based).
- **Antifungal powder and cream.** Use them preventively, not just when symptoms appear.
- **Inspect your feet every evening** by headlamp. Whitened, wrinkled, numb, or painful skin means stop and treat before it becomes a crisis.

### Skin, generally

- Antifungal and barrier products for groin, armpits, and between toes and fingers.
- Keep fingernails short.
- **Any cut must be cleaned and sealed immediately.** Wounds heal slowly in constant moisture. And given the land's tendency to thread root-hair into still things, you do not want an open wound sitting idle. See Section III.

### Gear protection

- **Dry bags inside dry bags.** Seal everything that must stay dry: medications, electronics, spare socks, food, documents.
- **Paper will rot.** Maps and journals should be waterproof paper or synthetic. Pencils work wet and pens usually don't.
- **Electronics** need full waterproofing plus desiccant packs, which you will need to replace. Assume condensation will find its way inside eventually. Carry redundant non-electronic tools for anything essential.
- **Eyeglasses** will fog constantly and bead with water. Contact lenses, with scrupulous hygiene given the fungal environment, or daily disposables may be better. Otherwise bring anti-fog treatment and several soft cloths in a sealed pouch.

## III. The Rule of Stillness: Your Most Important Briefing

> *The mat grows over whatever does not move.*

This is not metaphor. A feather is threaded with root-hair in a week. A dead animal is covered in a season.

A healthy, moving human will not be overgrown in an afternoon. But understand the implications fully.

### Sleeping

The fauna here sleeps in shifts or on the move. You cannot sleep walking. So:

- **Move camp every night.** Never sleep in the same spot twice in a row.
- **Inspect your groundsheet and sleeping gear every morning.** Look for fine pale threads, like cobweb or mold, adhering to the underside. Brush them off, shake everything out, and don't lay things down in the same patch again.
- **Consider a hammock** strung between clubmoss towers, off the sward. Epiphytes grow on everything here and will eventually creep along your cords, but that takes longer than contact with the living mat. Check the lines each morning.
- **Bent houses** (see Section VIII) are the closest thing to shelter the country offers. They are dry-ish, with compressed-moss floors. They are also someone's work, and grazers sometimes shelter in them. If you use one, be a courteous guest and don't stay more than a night.
- **Sleep in an insulated pad or cot rather than directly on moss.** This is less for warmth and more to keep a barrier between you and the living surface.
- **Roll, shift, stir.** You will do this naturally. Just don't take strong sedatives that leave you motionless for twelve hours.

### Your gear

Anything you set down and leave, whether a pack, a stove, a cache of food, or a marker flag, will be colonized. Caches are unwise beyond a few days. **If you leave something to find later, it may not be findable.** Things are not buried here. They are taken up, sideways and downward.

### Your exit point

**If your return route is anchored to a fixed physical location, such as a marked spot, a placed beacon, or a planted device, understand that it will be overgrown.** A beacon left for a week will be furred with root. A month, and it may be a green lump indistinguishable from the sward. A season, and it will be going into the sponge.

- Carry your exit on your person if at all possible.
- If you must use a fixed anchor, return to it regularly and clean it, or place it on something that moves, or on stone. A stone ring is the most durable surface in the land, but read Section VIII before considering this, because it is likely disrespectful.
- Always have redundancy.

### Injury: the real danger

This is where the Rule of Stillness turns lethal. **A person who cannot walk is a person the land has begun to claim.**

- **Never travel alone.** An immobilized solo traveler, with a broken ankle in the moss or unconscious from altitude illness, will lie still in warm, wet, living growth. That is the one thing you must never do here.
- If a companion is injured, **keep them moving**: turn them, shift them, move them onto a hammock or off the mat entirely, and brush off any growth every few hours.
- **Bring a lightweight litter or drag-sled** for moving an injured person across moss. This is non-negotiable for any party of more than a few days' range.
- Keep wounds covered and dressed. A still, damp wound on a still body is an invitation.
- Have a hard evacuation threshold agreed in advance. For example: "Any injury that prevents independent walking means we leave the world within 24 hours."

### Psychological stillness

Here is a subtler danger. The moss is soft and warm, the light is dim, and the whole country hums and ticks in a slow, drowsy rhythm. There will be moments when **you want very much to lie down and stay.**

Watch yourself. Watch your companions. Exhaustion, altitude, and low light together breed a heavy lethargy. Someone who keeps sitting down, who stops talking, who says "just a few more minutes," needs attention: food, water, oxygen check, and a reason to get up. Treat unexplained lethargy as a medical sign, not a mood.

## IV. Navigation

The Held Country is one of the most disorienting landscapes imaginable.

- **No sun disc.** The sun is only a vague brightening in the cloud. You cannot navigate by it.
- **No shadows.** Light is diffuse and shadowless.
- **Nearly flat.** The swells run a hundred miles crest to crest, too gentle to notice by eye.
- **Nightly fog** so thick it is "almost liquid."
- **Everything looks the same.** Green on green on green, with columns in every direction.

### Tools

- **Compass:** Test it, but don't trust it until verified. You don't know this world's magnetic field. Bring one anyway, and check it against other methods on the first day.
- **Inertial or step-counting navigation.** A pedometer plus a bearing gives you dead reckoning. Log everything.
- **GPS-equivalent:** almost certainly unavailable. There are no satellites you know of, and nothing overhead but cloud.
- **Altimeter/barometer:** Useful for detecting the long swells. The crests are slightly higher, and fog pools in the troughs. It will also give you advance warning of the evening lowering.

### Reading the land

- **The pale paths are your roads.** Silver-green tracks of regrown moss, left by centuries of animals walking in lines. Follow them. They are the easiest walking, they go somewhere, and critically, **they bend away from the edge.** The herds' long memory is your safety margin.
- **Fog pools in troughs.** When the evening cloud descends, watch where it gathers first. That's low ground, and low ground is where the eyes are.
- **Stone rings sit on crests.** If you find one, you're on high ground.
- **Listen to the grazers.** Their evening hum carries through the fog and marks where the herd is gathering, often on a crest.

### Depth perception

Shadowless light makes a leaf and the space behind it "seem to sit at the same distance." Expect to misjudge distances constantly, to walk into stems, and to misstep. **Use a trekking pole**, or better, two, to probe ahead and feel the ground. Your eyes will lie to you, so let your hands and feet do some of the seeing.

### Don't travel at night

At night, the country is fully dark except for faint lichen-glow, submerged in dense fog, and dotted with unmarked, bottomless pools. Make camp before the cloud comes down. Don't walk in the dark unless you absolutely must.

## V. Terrain Hazards

### The eyes

Round, dark, still pools. Perfectly clear for several fathoms, then dark. Dropped stones don't audibly land.

- **Do not swim in them.** Walls of root, no bottom you can reach, unknown depth, and water the color of steeped tea going down and down. There is no easy way out if you tire or cramp.
- **Keep back from the edges**, especially in troughs and at dusk. Their rims are ringed with pale flowers, which is a useful warning sign. If you see those flowers, slow down and probe.
- **The walls collapse.** Rarely, about once a year somewhere across the whole continent, but when it happens a section of weave softens and falls inward. The odds are small, but don't camp on an eye's rim.
- **The whumph.** If you feel a deep soft thud come up through your soles, that's a collapse, possibly a hundred miles away. It's not a threat to you, but it's startling. Tell your companions about it in advance so no one panics.

### The sponge

Below the moss is a waterlogged layer, warm as bathwater and several yards thick. The sward normally supports you. But in places, such as heavy grazing zones, trough bottoms, or near eyes, it may be thinner.

- Probe with your pole where the moss looks different or feels softer.
- If you break through to the sponge, **spread your weight**, lean back, and crawl out. Don't thrash. It's warm and it won't freeze you, but being stuck and still is the one thing you don't want to be.

### The stem-forest

Clubmoss towers thirty to forty feet tall, too close together for anything wider than a sideways person.

- Pack slim: tall and narrow, not wide.
- Expect to be draped in hanging moss, filmy ferns, and lichen as you pass. Check your hair, collar, and pack straps afterward. You're carrying a small ecosystem out with you.
- The stem-forest thickens toward impassable where grazers haven't passed. **If it's getting denser, you're off the herds' routes.** Turn back to a pale path.

### The edge

See Section X. It's important enough to have its own section.

## VI. Food, Water, and Fire

### Water

The one easy thing. Water is everywhere. Squeeze the sponge and it runs clear and faintly sweet. Webs, leaves, and stems drip constantly.

- **Filter or purify anyway.** It may be perfectly clean. It may harbor microbes your immune system has never met. A lightweight filter plus chemical backup costs you almost nothing.
- **Eye-water** is tannin-dark. It's probably drinkable after filtering, but the sponge is easier to access and doesn't require leaning over a bottomless pool.
- You will never need to carry much water, and that's a major weight saving.

### Food

**Bring all of it.** This is essential.

- Everything here is plant, and nothing is known to be safe for human digestion. The biochemistry of these clubmosses, ferns, and root-mats is unknown, and many earthly ferns are toxic or carcinogenic. **Do not forage.**
- **Do not eat the frogs, snails, grubs, or anything else.** The small translucent frogs in the leaf-cups may be toxic. Many earthly frogs are. And beyond safety, see Section IX on ethics.
- **Food will rot fast** in warm, saturated air. Pack everything in sealed, moisture-proof packaging. Dehydrated, vacuum-sealed, or canned food works. Open packages will mold within a day or two.
- Plan calories carefully. Walking on moss at altitude burns far more energy than you'd expect.

### Fire

**Effectively impossible, and you shouldn't try.**

- There is no dry fuel anywhere. None.
- Even if you could, the Held Country is a continent of organic mass. Burning it would be, at best, vandalism. At worst, if some pocket of dried fiber caught deep in the weave, it would be something much worse.
- **Bring a sealed stove with its own fuel** for heating food or water if you want hot meals. Use it carefully, off the moss, on a heat-reflective base.
- Honestly, consider eating cold. It's warm enough.

## VII. Light, Dark, and the Mind

### Daylight

Midday is about as bright as a room with the curtains drawn, green-tinged and shadowless. For a short visit, this is just atmospheric. For a long stay, weeks or more, expect:

- **Disrupted sleep rhythms.** There is no sharp dawn or dusk, only the slow lowering and lifting of fog.
- **Low mood,** similar to seasonal depression.
- **Vitamin D deficiency** on longer trips. Bring supplements.
- A small **light therapy device** is worth its weight for extended expeditions. Use it in the morning inside your shelter.

### Night

Fully dark, except for the faint blue-green glow of lichens on stems and stones.

- **Headlamp with a red-light mode.** Red preserves your night vision and disturbs the fauna less. The fog-moths navigate by the lichen-glow, and a bright white beam may draw or confuse them.
- **Let your eyes adapt.** Give it twenty or thirty minutes without artificial light and the lichen-glow will become visible, "like a sky seen from underneath." Many travelers find this the most beautiful thing in the country.
- Bring spare batteries, sealed. Cold isn't the problem here. Moisture kills battery contacts.

### Sound

The silence is profound. Then you'll begin to hear the root-eels ticking through the mat, faint and dry and everywhere, like a clock slightly out of tune. Some people find this soothing. Some find it maddening after a few days.

- Earplugs for sleep, if you're the maddened kind.
- Be aware that in deep silence, your own tinnitus, heartbeat, and breathing become loud. This is normal.

### Disorientation and dreaminess

Shadowless light, no horizon, soft ground, and low oxygen together produce a floaty, unreal feeling. Time goes strange. Expect:

- Losing track of how long you've walked.
- Difficulty judging how far away things are.
- A sense that you're not quite awake.

Keep a written log with times. Talk to your companions and check in on each other's clarity. Agree on simple cognitive checks, such as counting backward or naming the date, and use them if someone seems off.

## VIII. The Fauna

Nothing here is described as predatory toward humans. But "not predatory" isn't the same as "harmless," and you are the stranger.

### The grazers

Low, broad, heavy animals in herds of dozens, with ferns growing in their bristles.

- **They are big and they move in herds.** In the evening fog, you may not see them until they're on top of you. Their hum is your warning. If you hear it close, get off the pale path and let them pass.
- **Don't stand between a herd and its evening gathering spot.** They gather on crests, often around stone rings.
- They are probably gentle. Don't test it. Give them room, move slowly, and don't approach.
- They sometimes shelter in bent houses. If a grazer is in one, it's theirs for the night.

### The walking birds

Long-legged and soft-feathered, flightless, stepping carefully through the stem-forest.

- Watch the male's display if you're lucky enough to see it: wings fanned wide in the dim light, a pale patch that seems to hold the light.
- Don't approach nests in bent-house eaves.
- **Don't use flash photography.** In a world this dim, a camera flash would be a shocking, blinding event for animals whose courtship depends on faint glows.

### The root-eels

You'll hear them, not see them, except at the eyes. Don't reach into channels.

### The fog-moths

Pale, slow, in drifts of thousands at dusk. Harmless, and beautiful.

- They may land on you. Let them.
- Keep bright lights off during their rising.
- If you're prone to insect phobias, prepare yourself mentally. It can be a *lot* of moths.

### The water-spiders

Their webs catch dew, not prey. **Walk around them.** Each web represents a night's water for its maker. It's a small courtesy but a real one.

### The frogs, snails, and flatworms

**Don't touch the frogs.** Translucent frogs living in epiphyte cups may well be toxic through the skin, and your hands carry oils, salts, and microbes that could harm them. Look closely. That's the pleasure here.

### Everything else

"Every square yard, looked at closely, is inhabited many times over." Bring a **hand lens.** It will be the most rewarding item in your pack.

## IX. Ethics, Etiquette, and What You Leave Behind

The Held Country is ancient, slow, and inhabited, perhaps by someone as well as something. Your behavior matters more here than almost anywhere.

### Your footprints last for centuries

"Where many feet have passed, the moss grows back paler, and these pale tracks last for centuries."

Think about that. **Walk off-path and you are drawing a line that will outlive you by hundreds of years.** Every step you take cross-country is a permanent mark on a record of "every animal that ever walked in a line."

- **Stay on existing pale paths wherever possible.**
- If you must go off-path, walk in single file so you make one line, not many.
- **Don't wander in circles or zigzag near camp.** Plan your camp movements.

### Stones: the rarest thing there is

Stone is the most precious material in the Held Country. Every pebble took thousands of years to work its way up through the mat.

- **Do not take stones.** Not from the rings, not from the eye-edges, not lying loose in the moss. Not one. Not "just a small one."
- **Do not move stones in the rings.**
- **Do not touch the offerings,** the single pebbles on flat leaves or pressed into eye-walls.
- **Should you bring a stone?** Think carefully. Placing an outside stone in a place whose entire culture, if that's the word, seems organized around rare local stone is a strange act. It might be meaningless, confusing, or offensive. The safest choice is to leave no stones at all.

### The bent houses

These took generations of patient hands to shape. They are living, growing architecture, and possibly still tended.

- If you enter one, **touch nothing on the structure.** Don't pull at the growth.
- **Do not add your handprint** to any living lintel, however tempting. Those handprints were pressed in by the makers when the growth was young, and time has stretched them into something meaningful. Yours would be graffiti.
- Leave it as you found it. Don't leave gear. It will be absorbed, and you'll have left foreign material growing into someone's home.
- If you see signs of recent tending, such as fresh ties or fresh stone rings, **you are near the makers.** See below.

### The makers

"Whoever made them isn't here, or is here so rarely and so quietly that the difference doesn't matter."

You might meet them. Almost certainly you won't. But prepare as though you might.

- **They are quiet and patient beyond human measure.** They built houses by bending growth over generations. Loudness, haste, and bright light would likely be alarming.
- If you sense or see anyone, **stop. Be still for a moment, but only a moment. Remember the Rule. Be quiet, show open hands, and let them decide whether to approach.**
- Do not follow. Do not photograph without some sign of permission. Do not offer gifts of stone, because you don't understand what stone means here.
- They may have deliberately stayed hidden for a very long time. The most respectful thing may be to let them continue.

### Biosecurity

You are bringing foreign microbes, fungal spores, seeds, and possibly insects into a closed continental ecosystem that has been isolated for ages.

- **Clean all gear thoroughly before arrival.** Boots especially: scrub the soles and remove every seed and soil particle.
- **Wash and ideally sterilize clothing.**
- **Do not bring plants, seeds, or soil.**
- Consider your own health. Don't travel while sick with anything transmissible.
- **When leaving, clean again.** Moss spores, fern spores, and lichen fragments will be in your hair, your pack seams, your boot treads. Don't carry the Held Country into the next world, either.

### Waste

Here, "nothing is buried. Things are taken up."

- **Pack out everything non-biological.** Plastics, foils, and synthetics won't be absorbed cleanly and may persist, working their way through the mat for millennia like the pebbles, but as pollution.
- **Human waste** will be taken up by the land. It's organic. But your gut microbes and any pathogens are foreign. Dispose of waste well away from eyes and water channels, and preferably off paths. Carry a trowel for the sward, and a pack-out system if you're especially careful.
- Don't use soap in or near eyes. Keep any cleaning products biodegradable and minimal.

## X. The Edge

Hundreds of miles out in any direction, the land simply stops. There is no slope, only a sheer cliff of braided root dropping miles into cloud.

### Why it's dangerous

- **It will not announce itself.** In fog and diffuse light, on flat ground, you may not see the rim until you're at it.
- **The rim may be soft.** It's the outermost edge of a mat that seeps water constantly. There's no reason to assume it's firm right up to the drop, and it may overhang.
- **Nothing lives near it.** The walking birds avoid it. The grazers never go. The pale paths all bend away. Everything alive here has decided the edge is not a place to be.

### How to know you're near

- **It gets warmer and louder.** There's a continuous soft hiss of falling water becoming spray. If you hear a hiss that grows as you walk, slow down.
- **The pale paths curve away.** If you've left the paths and are walking straight on while the paths bend, you're heading toward the rim.
- **Upward air currents.** Mosses swaying where there should be no wind.

### If you choose to see it

Some travelers will want to stand at the edge and look down into the white. That's understandable. If you must:

- **Rope up.** Anchor to something substantial well back from the rim, such as a large clubmoss tower or several of them, and belay.
- **Approach on your belly** for the final stretch, spreading your weight.
- **Go in the morning,** when visibility is best.
- **Don't go alone. Never go in fog.**
- **Know that no one who falls here is recoverable.** Miles down, into cloud, onto unknown ground, if there is ground.
- **Turn back once you've seen it.** You do not need to stay long.

## XI. The Flowering

Roughly every thirty-some years, a single plant flowers across the whole continent at once. For three weeks, the Held Country turns a faint dusty violet, and the air smells of wet pepper and honey.

**If you can time your visit for it, you are seeing something most living things here see only once or twice in their lives.** It is worth planning around.

But prepare:

- **Pollen.** A whole continent flowering at once means enormous quantities of pollen in the air, in saturated conditions. If you have *any* hay fever, asthma, or pollen allergies, **bring antihistamines, a rescue inhaler, and a good respirator mask** (N95 or better). Even non-allergic people may develop reactions to a novel pollen in such concentration.
- **Unknown pollen chemistry.** You've never breathed this. Watch for respiratory irritation, rash, or eye inflammation in yourself and companions.
- **The smell may be overwhelming** after days. Pepper and honey is lovely in small doses.
- **The fauna may behave differently.** A once-a-generation event likely changes grazer movement, bird behavior, and moth hatching. Be more alert.
- **Do not pick the flowers.** Don't collect seed. This is a generational event, and the seed that falls into the sward is the next thirty years.

## XII. Packing List

### Essential

- **Exit method on your person**, with redundancy
- **Altitude medications** (acetazolamide, dexamethasone) and a pulse oximeter
- **Full medical kit** with emphasis on wound sealing (skin glue, waterproof dressings), antifungals, antibiotics, and blister care
- **Waterproof, sealed food** for the full trip plus reserve
- **Water filter** plus chemical purification backup
- **Lightweight drag-litter or sled** for moving an injured companion
- **Two trekking poles** for probing and balance
- **Hammock with bug net and tarp**, or insulated sleeping cot/pad
- **Nested dry bags** for everything critical
- **Desiccant packs**, replaceable
- **Many pairs of wool/synthetic socks**, sealed
- **Fast-draining boots** with ankle support
- **Wool and synthetic clothing layers**, loose-fitting
- **Headlamp with red mode**, plus spare sealed batteries
- **Compass, altimeter/barometer, pedometer**, and a waterproof notebook with pencils
- **Whistle** for communication in fog
- **Climbing rope and harness** if you intend to approach the edge
- **Sealed stove and fuel** (optional), with a heat-reflective base

### Strongly recommended

- **Hand lens**, which will be your favorite item
- **Earplugs**
- **Anti-fog treatment** or contact lenses
- **Foot barrier balm** and antifungal powder
- **Vitamin D supplements** and light therapy for long stays
- **Respirator and antihistamines** if visiting during a flowering
- **Biodegradable soap**, minimal
- **Trowel** and waste pack-out system
- **Grooming kit**: comb, small brush, mirror. You'll want to check your hair, beard, and back for hitchhiking growth.

### Leave behind

- Cotton anything
- Heavy waterproof boots
- Anything you'd be upset to have absorbed
- Seeds, soil, or organic material from elsewhere
- Stones
- Flash photography equipment, or at least the habit of using it

## XIII. A Daily Rhythm That Works

A pattern past visitors might recommend:

**Morning (the lift):** Wake as the fog rises. Check yourself, your companions, and your gear for any growth, brushing it off and shaking everything out. Change socks. Treat feet. Eat. Pack everything off the ground. This is the best walking light, so start moving.

**Midday:** Walk the pale paths. Rest often, but briefly. Sit, don't lie. Change socks at rest stops. Drink from the sponge, filtered. Look closely at things. Log your bearing and distance.

**Afternoon:** Begin watching for a camp site. Choose it on a crest or gentle rise, away from eyes, off the path, near but not in any grazer gathering place. Pitch before the cloud begins lowering.

**Evening (the lowering):** The fog comes down and everything beads. Settle in. Turn off white light. Wait for your eyes to adapt, and watch the lichens light and the moths rise. Listen for the grazers' hum. Final foot check. Sleep.

**Night:** Stay put. If you wake, shift position. Let the eels tick you back to sleep.

**Every morning, without exception:** move on.

## XIV. A Last Word

You will want to stay longer than is wise. People always do in places this quiet.

The Held Country does nothing to you except wait. It is warm, and soft, and patient. Everything in it keeps moving not from fear but from long habit, a slow agreement with the land's single rule. You'll feel that agreement settle into your own body after a few days: an unhurried restlessness, a readiness to rise and walk on.

Honor it. Walk on the old paths. Touch nothing that took a thousand years to arrive. Keep your hands off the stones and your prints off the lintels. Look closely at the frogs in their leaf-cups and the dew on the spiders' webs. Lie with your ear to the moss once, briefly, and listen to the clock of the country ticking in the weave.

Then get up, before the land starts to think you've decided to stay.
