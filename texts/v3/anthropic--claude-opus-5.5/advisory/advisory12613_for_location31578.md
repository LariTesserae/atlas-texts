# Preparing to Visit the Quickening Hollow

## First, What You're Actually Walking Into

It helps to name the danger before packing for it, because the Hollow's danger is quiet and friendly. Nothing there wants to hurt you, and nothing will attack you. What it will do is slowly and politely take things from you: your heat, your moisture, your sense of time, some of your balance, and a certain amount of your composure. The creatures that crowd around you are affectionate in the way a draft through a cracked window is affectionate.

Treat the visit as a **cold-water immersion that happens to be dry**, carried out inside a **cave with active rockfall**, in the presence of **something that is probably thinking**. Prepare for all three.

---

## 1. Getting to the Tundra

The Hollow's entrance is easy to miss. It is a sag in the moss with concentric frost rings and nothing more. Before you go:

- **Arrive in daylight, in calm weather.** You need to see the rings, which read best at low sun angles when the frost throws shadows. Flat grey overcast makes them nearly invisible.
- **Mark the depression** with something that won't blow away and won't hurt the moss: a weighted flag on a stake driven into the ground *outside* the outermost ring. Don't stake inside it. The rings are part of the organism.
- **Leave a surface cache.** Put dry clothes, a stove, fuel, a thermos, and a sleeping bag in a sealed bag at your marker. You will come up colder than you expect, and you don't want to warm up with gear you carried down into the wet.
- **Ordinary tundra hazards still apply.** Depending on where this tundra is, that may mean bears, sudden weather, bog, and a very long way to help. Someone should know where you are and when to expect you back. Ideally that person stays at the surface.

Because the rings are widening, the entrance may sit slightly differently on a return trip. Record GPS coordinates or a triangulated landmark for the center, not the edge.

---

## 2. Clothing: Dressing for a Place That Drinks You

Two properties of the Hollow make it worse than ordinary cold.

**It's saturated.** Mist hangs in layers, dew forms on every surface, and rime feathers grow on anything that stays still. Wet insulation fails.

**It pulls heat toward itself.** Heat isn't just escaping from you; it is being drawn off, and the pull is strongest on whichever side faces the weave. Standing near a wall is like standing next to an open freezer door.

So dress as if for a winter dive:

- **Vapor-barrier base layer.** A thin non-breathable liner next to the skin, or directly over a very thin wicking layer. Your own sweat is the enemy, and the mist will get in otherwise. You'll feel clammy, but you won't lose insulation.
- **Synthetic insulation, not down.** Down collapses when damp, and it will get damp. Pile, fleece, or synthetic fill all keep working wet.
- **A waterproof, smooth, closed outer shell.** Smooth matters because rime forms on texture and the springtails settle in fibers. Cuffs, collar, and hem should cinch shut. A hood with a drawstring is essential; see the fauna section.
- **Insulated, waterproof boots with good soles.** The floor is woven root with the sheen of wet horn. Think of it as wet, polished, slightly uneven wood. Microspikes or crampons are tempting, but **don't use them**. You would be puncturing a living floor, and possibly something like a nervous system. Use soft, grippy rubber soles, ideally with a siped or felt tread of the kind used on wet river rock.
- **Two pairs of gloves**: a thin liner for fine work and an insulated waterproof overmitt. You'll want to touch things. Consider what you're willing to touch bare-handed; the section on the hum covers this.
- **Face protection.** A balaclava or neck gaiter will frost up from your breath, and the Hollow seems to want your breath. A fleece gaiter over a thin windproof layer works.
- **Pack a spare base layer and socks in a dry bag** in case something goes wrong down there.

Note that your breath-fog drifts to the walls and disappears into them. You'll feel a faint, constant draw on your exhalations, and some people find this disturbing. It isn't dangerous, but notice it and expect it.

## 3. Your Heat Budget

This is the most important section.

In ordinary cold, you lose heat at a rate you can roughly predict. In the Hollow the rate rises with proximity to the weave, stillness, and how many animals are on you. Each cricket on your sleeve is a tiny heat siphon, and together they are not tiny.

**Plan your visit as a fixed heat budget, spent deliberately:**

- **Eat heavily before descending**, fat and complex carbohydrates, and bring more. Calorie-dense food that doesn't freeze solid, such as nut butter, chocolate, or fat-heavy bars, should be carried inside your layers.
- **Carry a hot drink in an insulated flask.** Don't plan on drinking the Hollow's water, however clean it looks (see below).
- **Chemical heat packs** in pockets, boots, and against your core. Be aware that the organism may pull at them too, and they may exhaust faster than rated.
- **Stay moving.** Stillness is when the animals gather and the draw is strongest. If you want to observe something for a long time, and you will, keep shifting your weight and flexing, and step away from walls periodically.
- **Keep your back away from the weave.** Don't sit on the floor or lean against anything. If you must rest, use an insulated closed-cell foam pad, and move it often, because it will be drawn cold from underneath.
- **Set a hard time limit and set an alarm**, for reasons explained under time distortion. For a first visit, 45 minutes to an hour inside the chamber is plenty. Many people will want to stay far longer. That wanting is part of the risk.

**Know the signs of hypothermia and check them on yourself honestly:** shivering that won't stop, then shivering that *does* stop (worse), fumbling hands, slurred thoughts, apathy, and, in this place especially, **a growing sense of calm contentment and a desire to sit down and watch the light**. That last one might be wonder. It might be early hypothermia. In the Hollow you can't easily tell which, so leave.

Go with a partner if you can, and agree beforehand that either of you can call the exit and the other obeys without argument.

## 4. The Descent

The throat is about twelve body-lengths of angled, woven, wet root. It isn't dug, and nothing about it is engineered for you.

- **Bring a rope**, a static line of 30–40 meters. Don't anchor it by driving anything into the moss or root. Anchor it at the surface to a deadman: a buried stuff sack of rocks, or a stake well outside the rings. Use it as a handline.
- **Descend feet-first, facing the slope**, like downclimbing a steep bank. Roots offer holds, but grip broad braids rather than fine rootlets, and don't haul on anything thin.
- **Keep your pack small** or lower it separately. The passage is "held open around an absence," and you want to take up as little of that absence as possible.
- **About the breathing cycle.** The weave swells and settles over roughly sixty hours. The throat may be slightly narrower at one point in the cycle than another. If you're claustrophobic or large-framed, it would be worth observing the throat on a couple of short scouting visits to learn when it's widest. At minimum, note its width on the way down so you know if it has changed when you return.

---

## 5. Hearing: Protect It

You won't expect a cave under frozen ground to be loud. It is, and some of the noise is dangerous.

- **The cracking** sounds like rifle shots when it's close, and in an enclosed, reverberant vault that is a genuine hearing hazard over an hour. Bring **electronic earmuffs or electronic earplugs**, the kind shooters use. They pass through quiet sounds, so you can still hear the crickets and the bell-tones, but they clamp down on impulse noise. Foam earplugs will work in a pinch, but you'll lose the music, and the music is half the point.
- **The hum** is infrasonic or close to it, felt in the diaphragm. Low-frequency sound at high amplitude is known to produce unease, nausea, a sense of presence, pressure in the chest, blurred vision from eyeball resonance, and sometimes dread or awe with no source. Expect some of this. If you're prone to panic attacks or migraines, know that this may trigger them. Knowing the cause helps a great deal: "that's the hum" is a steadying thought.
- **Don't press your ear to the filament for long.** The description calls it "so strong it feels like pressure." A few seconds is enough to hear the counting. Longer risks your inner ear and your equilibrium, and possibly something harder to name.

## 6. Balance and the Slow-Fall Zones

Near the densest junctions, the chamber's heart where the light is nearly continuous, things fall at about half speed.

What this means for your body:

- **Your inner ear may not agree with your eyes.** If falling objects and drips slow down but your own body doesn't feel lighter, you get a vestibular mismatch similar to motion sickness. If your body *does* feel lighter, your stride will overshoot and you'll misjudge steps on a wet, uneven floor. Either way, **move slowly and deliberately near the heart**, with small steps and knees soft.
- **Take anti-nausea medication before the descent** if you're prone to motion sickness, one that doesn't cause drowsiness. Drowsiness in this cold is dangerous.
- **Don't drop things to test it.** It's irresistible, but a dropped item in the heart of the Hollow is litter inside a mind, and retrieving it means kneeling on the weave. If you want to watch the effect, watch the water drips. They are already doing the experiment for you.
- **Tether small gear.** Clip lanyards to anything you'll handle: light, camera, notebook, pen. Slow-falling objects tumble and drift unpredictably in the mist.

---

## 7. Light: Bring Your Own, Use It Gently

The chamber is dim but not dark. Near the heart you can read by the plant's glow. Near the raw granite it is almost black.

The Hollow's light isn't decoration. It appears to be the organism's cognition, or at least its circulation, made visible. Pulses meet, cancel, combine, and produce new colors that head off in new directions. Shining a bright white torch across that is roughly like shouting into someone's face while they are trying to work something out.

- **Bring a headlamp with a red or very dim amber mode**, and use it mostly pointed at your own feet and hands.
- **Carry a brighter lamp for emergencies and for the raw-stone area**, where you need to see footing and there's little of the organism's light to disturb.
- **Let your eyes adapt.** Give it ten to twenty minutes in the dim before deciding you need more light. Much of the Hollow's beauty only appears once you're dark-adapted: the rose pulses, the cold-lanterns in the pools, the faint seams near the unfinished edge.
- **Flash photography: don't.** There's no evidence it harms anything, but there's no evidence it doesn't, and you'd be strobing a thinking thing. Use a camera that handles low light well, or better, a notebook.
- **Cold kills batteries,** and this cold is compounded by the heat draw. Keep spare batteries inside your layers against your body, and assume your electronics will last half as long as rated.

---

## 8. Water and Air

- **Don't drink from the bladders or the pools.** The bladder water looks so clean it "looks like nothing at all," and you will be thirsty, because cold air and heavy breathing dehydrate you. But the bladders are part of the organism's circulation, and the pools hold living cold-lanterns in standing wave-patterns. Puncturing a bladder is wounding it. Drinking from a pool is disturbing an ecosystem and taking in whatever lives there. Bring your own water, insulated and warm.
- **Breathing:** the air is saturated and near freezing. It won't hurt you, but cold, wet air irritates airways, and people with asthma should bring their inhaler and keep it warm so the propellant works. Breathing through a gaiter pre-warms the air.
- **Foggy glasses and goggles** will be a constant fight. Anti-fog treatment helps a bit. Contact lenses may be easier if you're comfortable with them in the cold.

---

## 9. Around the Unfinished Stone: Rockfall

The far wall is raw, freshly fractured granite with sharp edges, which roots are actively prying apart. Cracks widen as you watch, and the stone opens with reports ranging from a knuckle-pop to a gunshot.

This is an active rockfall zone. Treat it like the foot of a crumbling cliff.

- **Wear a helmet.** A climbing helmet is ideal and works for the descent too. Wear it the whole time, not just near the stone. The ceiling is also unfinished.
- **Keep your distance from the raw granite**, a few body-lengths back. Fresh fractures shed sharp flakes, and a cracking stone can spall fragments outward with real force.
- **Don't touch the fresh edges.** They will cut, and cold hands don't notice cuts quickly. Blood in this place is also heat and moisture, and you don't know what the Hollow does with it.
- **Stay out from directly under the oculus.** Something might come down, and the bladder-moths make their papery-roar flights through it.

---

## 10. The Fauna: Friendly, Unafraid, and Very Interested in Your Heat

Nothing here is aggressive, but everything here is drawn to you, and they are heat carriers. Their affection has a cost.

- **Rime-crickets** will colonize your sleeves and shoulders if you stand still. Tiny and harmless, they will find gaps. Cinch cuffs and collar.
- **Springtails** settle in fibers, collars, and hair. Expect to take some out with you unless you're careful. A **smooth hood cinched tight** keeps them out of your hair.
- **Weave-moles** may press against your boots. Don't step on them. They surface from the floor itself, so watch where you place your feet, especially when a bulge travels across the weave toward you. The description suggests they are the Hollow's stitches, doing its maintenance. They matter.
- **Cold-lanterns** in the pools: don't wade, don't touch the water, don't stir the ripples. They navigate by the standing-wave patterns, the "roads" of the hum. Your hand in a pool would be like a boulder dropped across a highway.
- **Bladder-moths** hang on the ceiling. If a colony lifts off near you, stand still and let them pass. The roar is startling but harmless.

**Gentle shedding:** every few minutes, step away from the weave and gently brush off your sleeves and shoulders, returning the animals to the root. Don't swat. Crickets on the weave make it glow briefly; you'll see the heat you've been giving away being received.

**Decontamination before leaving:** at the base of the throat, stop and do a thorough brush-down, especially collar, cuffs, hood, and boot tread. Don't carry these creatures to the surface. They almost certainly can't survive there, and if they can, that's worse.

---

## 11. Conduct: You Are a Guest Inside Something

It's worth deciding your ethics before you arrive, because on arrival you'll be dazzled.

**Take nothing.** No root samples, no rime, no water, no animals, no rock chips. You don't know what's structural, what's sensory, and what's thought.

**Leave nothing.** Pack out every wrapper. Don't spit or urinate inside; go before descending. You don't know what the organism would do with foreign organic matter, and it drinks everything.

**Don't climb toward the oculus.** The upper chambers are barely formed, a loose gauze of filaments like the first threads of a cocoon. That is the Hollow's most delicate work, and it can't be undone.

**Speak softly or not at all.** A raised voice returns from every side, and sound in this place does work: the hum moves water, and the drip-notes fall in structured intervals. You don't know what your voice does to that structure. Some visitors might want to sing back. That's a real choice with unknown consequences. If you must, do it quietly, briefly, and on a later visit, not your first.

**Accept that you are feeding it.** Your heat is being taken and distributed through the tissue. Every visitor contributes to its growth, and the frost rings on the surface are widening. Whether that's fine, wonderful, or something to worry about, you won't know. But you should go down knowing that visiting is not neutral.

## 12. The Filament

Everything in the Hollow grows along a single glassy thread, thinner than a hair, running from stone floor to oculus. It has no seam, and you can't find where it ends. The light bends toward it. Beneath the hum, very close to it, there is something like counting.

This is the most compelling thing in the chamber, and it's where you should be most careful.

- **Don't touch it with bare skin.** A hair-thin, glassy strand under tension may be sharp enough to cut, like a monofilament or a glass fiber. And you don't know what it is. It may be a seed, a spine, an instrument, or a message.
- **Don't pluck, tap, or pull it.** If the organism treats it "the way a nerve treats a spine," you'd be striking a spine. If it's under tension, you'd be making it sing at a frequency no one has heard.
- **It sits in the slow-fall region**, where the densest junctions are. Your balance will be off. Approach slowly, stand wide, and keep your hands close to your body.
- **Listen briefly.** A few seconds, ear near it but not touching, is enough to hear the counting. Then step back.
- **Watch your own mind afterward.** Some people will leave the filament with the sense that they've half-understood something important. Write it down immediately in plain words before it fades or grows. Then set it aside until you're warm and on the surface.

---

## 13. Time

Every rhythm in the Hollow is slow except the cracks. It breathes over sixty hours, fills its bladders over days, and changes the key of its music over weeks. The light-pulses take minutes to cross the ceiling. The frost-glyphs last minutes.

Minds adjust to the tempo of their surroundings. In a place this slow, dim, and hypnotic, with an infrasonic hum and chorusing crickets, your sense of elapsed time will drift. "A few minutes" of watching the rose pulses branch can easily be forty.

- **Use a vibrating alarm** on a device kept warm inside your layers, set at intervals: a warning at 30 minutes, a firm "begin leaving" at 45, and "leave now" at 60.
- **Obey it as if someone else set it.** The version of you who set it was warm, clear-headed, and not in love with the ceiling.

---

## 14. Leaving

- **Leave with reserves.** Start up the throat while you still feel fine. The climb is where cold hands and fatigue become dangerous.
- **Brush down at the base of the throat** as described above.
- **Check the throat's width against your memory from the descent.** If it has narrowed, go now, calmly.
- **Climb steadily, not quickly.** Sweating on the ascent soaks your layers just before you meet the surface wind.
- **At the surface, go straight to your cache.** Change into dry clothes, eat, drink something hot, and get into the bag if you're shivering hard. Don't stand around looking at the frost rings until you're warm. They'll still be there. Wider, maybe.

---

## 15. Afterward

Expect some aftereffects:

- **Deep fatigue.** You spent far more calories than you realized.
- **Ringing ears**, if you skimped on hearing protection.
- **Lingering sensation of the hum**, a phantom pressure in the chest or a feeling of vibration when you lie still, sometimes for a day or two. Low-frequency exposure does this.
- **Altered sense of pace.** Ordinary life may feel frantic and shallow for a while.
- **Dreams** of branching light, bell-tones in intervals you almost recognize, or counting. Harmless, as far as anyone knows. Write them down anyway.
- **An urge to go back.** Notice it. Wait at least a few days before acting on it, and never go back alone because of it.

---

## Packing List Summary

**Surface cache:** dry clothes, sleeping bag, stove, fuel, thermos, food, first aid, emergency shelter.

**Worn:** vapor-barrier base, synthetic insulation, smooth waterproof shell with cinching cuffs, collar, and hood; insulated waterproof boots with soft grippy soles; liner gloves and waterproof overmitts; balaclava or gaiter; climbing helmet; electronic hearing protection.

**Carried:** static rope and deadman anchor materials; red/dim headlamp plus a brighter backup; spare batteries in an inner pocket; insulated flask of hot drink; calorie-dense non-freezing food; chemical heat packs; closed-cell foam sit pad; vibrating alarm; tethered notebook and pencil (ink freezes); non-drowsy anti-nausea medication; inhaler if needed; small first aid kit including blister care and a thermal blanket; dry bag with spare base layer and socks.

**Leave behind:** crampons and spikes, sampling kits, flash, anything you'd be tempted to drop "just to see," and any intention of climbing through the oculus.
