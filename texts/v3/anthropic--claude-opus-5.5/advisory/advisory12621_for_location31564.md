# Preparing for Ossary: A Traveler's Guide

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

Ossary is not dangerous the way a war zone or a volcano is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a hospital ward, a cave system, and a factory floor are dangerous, all at once and without pause, for as long as you stay. Nothing there will try to kill you. It is simply a place built for birds, maintained by people who have adapted over forty generations, and your body has had none of that adaptation.

Everything comes down to three things you can't escape: **the wet, the dust, and the noise.** Prepare for those and the rest is manageable. The wonder takes care of itself.

---

## The Ascent

You'll arrive at the foot of the escarpment on the brine flats, which are hot, glaring, and thick with midges in the afternoon. Arrive in the morning if you can, before the Column really starts moving.

The practical way up is the cable-lifts that carry fertilizer down. They almost certainly carry passengers or can be persuaded to. Expect to ride in or beside cargo that smells powerfully of ammonia. **Put your respirator on at the bottom, not the top.** The climb takes you up through the Column itself, and for part of the ride you'll be inside a rising river of insects. Close your mouth, wear eye protection, and don't panic when they cover you. They are midges. They don't bite, but they get in everything.

The altitude, just under a mile, is not enough to cause real altitude sickness in most people. You might feel slightly winded on stairs for a day or two, and Ossary is almost entirely stairs.

## Respiratory Protection: Non-Negotiable

This is the single most important section.

You'll be breathing air that carries:
- **Guano dust**, dried, powdered, and airborne, especially near the Works and the older roosts
- **Ammonia** from decomposing droppings
- **Lime and kiln dust** from the rendering and burning industries
- **Insect fragments**, the endless fine organic rain from the Column
- **Fungal spores** associated with bird and bat droppings

On Earth, histoplasmosis and cryptococcosis are serious fungal lung infections caught from exactly this kind of environment: bat caves, old chicken houses, pigeon lofts. Ossary is the largest bird and bat roost imaginable. Assume the risk is real.

**Bring:**
- A proper half-face respirator with P100 filters, not a paper mask. Bring enough replacement filters to change them every few days, because humidity degrades filter performance quickly.
- A lighter N95-type mask for casual wear in the "cleaner" districts.
- If you have asthma or any chronic lung condition, think seriously about whether to go at all. If you do go, bring rescue inhalers, a spare, and a course of oral steroids with medical guidance.

You'll notice Ossarans probably don't wear masks, or wear only cloth wraps. They've grown up in it. Some of them likely have the chronic coughs and scarred lungs that come with it and consider it normal. Don't copy them.

**Insect allergy warning:** On Earth, people living near lakes with massive midge (chironomid) hatches often develop allergies to them: sneezing, asthma, hives. Insect proteins can also cross-react with shellfish allergy. If you're allergic to shrimp or crab, be very cautious about chitin-mill products, including anything made with midge flour, and about the airborne dust near the mills. Bring non-drowsy antihistamines, and an epinephrine auto-injector if you have any history of severe allergy.

## Your Skin, Your Eyes, and the Weeping Stone

The walls weep water that is "slightly bitter and slightly slick." That is how alkaline water behaves. The stone is limestone soaked in centuries of lime-burning and ammonia-rich guano, so the condensation is almost certainly basic, like very diluted lye. Slick-feeling water on your fingers is actually a thin film of your own skin oils being saponified.

It won't burn you on contact. Over days of constant exposure, though, it will:
- Dry and crack your skin, especially your hands
- Irritate any cuts or scrapes
- Sting your eyes if it drips into them
- Cause dermatitis where wet clothing rubs

**Bring:**
- A heavy barrier cream (petroleum- or silicone-based) for your hands, face, and neck. Apply it in the morning and again at midday.
- Mildly acidic or pH-balanced soap. A small bottle of diluted vinegar for rinsing hands and wiping skin is a surprisingly good idea, since it neutralizes the alkaline film.
- Close-fitting glasses or light goggles with anti-fog coating. You'll be in dripping stairwells and falling dust constantly. Contact lenses are a bad idea: dust, alkaline drips, and humidity make for eye infections.
- Saline eyewash, and plenty of it.
- Thin nitrile or neoprene gloves for handling railings, climbing, or touching walls. Those violet and copper-green bacterial films are probably harmless, but you don't want them in a cut.

**Treat every scrape seriously.** Clean it, disinfect it, and cover it. Constant wet plus a dense animal population means infections take hold easily. Bring antiseptic, waterproof bandages, and a broad-spectrum antibiotic course if you can get one prescribed for travel.

## Materials: What Survives Ossary and What Doesn't

This is easy to overlook. Ossary's chemistry will quietly destroy some of the things you bring.

**Alkaline conditions attack:**
- **Aluminum.** It corrodes in basic solutions. Leave aluminum water bottles, cookware, and trekking poles behind, or expect them to pit and discolor.
- **Wool and silk.** Both are protein fibers and degrade in alkali over time. Your nice merino base layers will felt, weaken, and eventually fall apart.
- **Leather.** It will stiffen, crack, and discolor with constant wet alkaline exposure. Leather boots are a poor choice.

**What holds up:**
- Synthetic fabrics like polyester and nylon
- Stainless steel
- Rubber and neoprene
- Most hard plastics
- Glass

**Electronics:** Everything needs to be fully waterproof, not just splash-resistant. The humidity never lets up. Keep devices in sealed drybags with silica gel packets, and replace the packets often, because they'll saturate. Wipe contacts and ports with a dry cloth daily. Expect lenses to fog when you move between districts.

**Paper:** Notebooks will go limp and moldy (or whatever passes for moldy there) within days. Bring waterproof paper, or buy local. Ossaran bookbinding uses nest-glue specifically because it's water-resistant, and a locally bound journal might be both the most practical and the most beautiful souvenir you could get.

## Clothing

The climate is **warm, humid, and relentlessly damp**, never cold. You'll never be dry. Nothing you wash will ever fully dry on its own. Plan for that.

**Principles:**
- Lightweight, quick-drying synthetics
- Loose-fitting, because tight wet fabric chafes badly
- Multiple complete changes (at least three), carried in drybags
- Long sleeves and trousers despite the warmth, for protection from drips, dust, and the occasional panicked insect or bird

**Headwear matters more than anything else you wear.** Hundreds of millions of birds are overhead, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: droppings fall constantly. You need a wide-brimmed, waterproof hat, ideally one that sheds liquid easily and can be rinsed. Many Ossarans probably wear hooded oilcloth or something similar. A brimmed hat also keeps dust out of your eyes and gives you some shade during bright spells.

**For the lower Guttering and other rain-heavy districts**, carry a light oilcloth or waterproof poncho. An umbrella is questionable. In a dense crowd on narrow stairs, with birds and bats moving through the air, it might be more hazard than help. Watch what locals do.

**Footwear is critical.** The streets are ramps and stairs, all angled, all wet, many coated in bacterial films that "peel away in sheets like wet paper," and some crawling with snails. You need:
- Synthetic or rubber boots with **aggressive, soft-rubber soles**, the kind made for wet rock or boat decks
- Ankle support, because you'll be on uneven stairs all day
- Something that drains or dries, plus several pairs of synthetic socks
- Antifungal foot powder and cream. Constant wet feet can lead to maceration and fungal infection, and in extreme cases immersion foot. Dry your feet completely every night, no matter how tired you are.

Consider bringing slip-on crampons or ice-cleats with small studs for the oldest wards. You'll find out quickly whether the locals think this is sensible or ridiculous.

## Water and Food

**Do not drink the weeping water.** It's alkaline, full of dissolved minerals, and carries whatever has been washing down the walls, which includes centuries of guano. Ossarans have a water supply somewhere, likely treated, likely from the Throats' condensation systems or deep cisterns. Ask what locals drink and how it's treated. Bring:
- A reliable filter *and* a chemical or UV purification method as backup
- Electrolyte tablets. You'll sweat constantly in the humidity without noticing, since sweat doesn't evaporate well.

**Food is an open question worth thinking about in advance.** Nothing grows in Ossary. Everything plant-based has come up the cable-lifts from the lowlands, bought with fertilizer and nest-glue. Grain, vegetables, and fruit are probably expensive and possibly status foods. What's cheap and local is likely:
- Midge flour and chitin products
- Things made from rat, gull, or perhaps rendered bat
- Possibly eggs or other animal products from the roosts
- Possibly swift-nest preparations, though the text says nests are boiled into glue, so edible nest soup may or may not be a thing

**Be adventurous but careful.** Your gut flora is not Ossaran. Bring anti-diarrheal medication and oral rehydration salts. Eat cooked food, peel anything you can peel, and avoid anything raw that's been sitting out in the open air, because something is always falling onto open food in Ossary. Watch how locals cover their dishes. You'll probably see lids, cloths, and bowls with inward-curving rims everywhere.

Pack a supply of your own familiar food: compact, sealed, and high-calorie. You'll want it on the days when everything is too much.

## Your Ears

Visitors "go half-mad in the first week." Take that seriously.

There are three layers of sound:
1. **The screaming**, a constant thin shriek at the top edge of human hearing, which never stops during daylight.
2. **The wingbeat roar**, a surf-like, rail-like rumble underneath it.
3. **The machinery**: the Fanworks' 40-beat-per-minute pulse felt through the stone, plus kilns, rail carts, cable-lifts, and steam whistles.

Then twice a day, at the **Turning**, the volume rises until shouting is impossible.

**Your age matters here.** Hearing at the high end of the range fades with age. A young traveler may experience the shriek as constant torture. An older one may barely hear it and experience Ossary mainly as a place of rumbling and roar. Know which you are before you go.

**Bring:**
- High-fidelity musician's earplugs, which cut volume evenly without muffling speech entirely. Wear these most of the day.
- Foam earplugs for sleeping and for the Turning.
- Possibly a good pair of noise-cancelling headphones for rest periods. They're good at low constant rumbles like the fans and wingbeat roar, less good at high-pitched sounds like the screaming. Combine them with plugs.
- Something to listen to that isn't Ossary. A few hours a day of your own chosen sound will help your mind stay intact.

**Sleep:** The fans run constantly. Their pulse runs through the stone at roughly a resting heart rate. Some people will find this oddly soothing after a few nights. Others will lie awake feeling it in their ribs. A folded pad or foam layer under your bedding helps damp the vibration. Also note that the night isn't quiet: the bats take over at dusk, with their chittering and leathery rushing. Expect your first several nights to be poor. Don't schedule anything demanding for the first three days.

**Communication:** Learn some basic hand signals, or arrange signals with a companion before you arrive. Carry a small writing slate. Ossarans have almost certainly developed gestural communication for the Turning and the loud industrial wards. Learning a little of it would help you practically and win you some goodwill.

## The Turning: Rules for Survival

At dawn and dusk the flocks lift or settle, and **the density of birds is enough to knock a person off a stairway.** This is not a figure of speech. Hundreds of millions of fast-moving birds are all going to the same places at the same time, and you are in the way.

**Know when it's coming.** Every day, find out the times of the Turning for your district. They'll shift with the seasons. Carry a watch.

**Where to be:**
- Off the stairs
- Against a wall, ideally in a recessed doorway or under an overhang
- *Not* near the mouth of a major roost chimney or a Throat
- Face the wall or cover your face with your arms, since a small bird at speed can hurt an eye badly

**What to do:**
- Stop talking before it starts. Children are taught to finish their sentences before the Turning. You should be too.
- Stand still and don't flail. If a bird hits you, don't swat it. (See below.)
- Keep your hat on and your eyes protected.
- Wait until locals move before you move.

At some point, when you're settled and it feels safe, go and watch a Turning properly from a good vantage point: a western-rim terrace at dusk, looking out over the glowing towers as the black spirals funnel down into the chimneys. The first time will probably be one of the most extraordinary things you ever see. Just make sure you're somewhere secure when it happens.

## Law and Etiquette

### No one kills a swift.

This is the most important social rule, and the one most likely to trip up a visitor, because the danger is **reflex.** At some point a swift will clip your head, blunder into your face, or tangle briefly in your clothing. Your body will want to swat, grab, or throw it off. If you kill it, even by accident, even in panic, you may be exiled down the escarpment. At best, you will be deeply distrusted.

**Practice this before you go.** Seriously. Train yourself so that when something small and fast hits your face, your response is to go still, close your eyes, and turn your head, not to lash out. Keep your hands low and open.

If you find a swift on the ground or trapped somewhere, don't handle it clumsily. Swifts can't take off from flat ground and can only cling to vertical surfaces. Ask a local, or look for the designated collectors. There may be a correct way to return a grounded bird to a wall, and a wrong way. Don't guess.

Remember too that the gulls prey on young swifts. If you see a gull take one, don't interfere. That is the city's business.

### Nothing is flat.

- Tables tilt. Set your cup in the carved rings.
- Don't set your bag down on a flat surface expecting it to stay dry or clean. If you find a flat surface at all, there's a reason it's dirty.
- Bring a hanging or wall-hook organizer for your lodgings rather than expecting shelves.

### Respect the slowness.

Ossarans think in strata and generations. Don't expect urgency. Don't praise new buildings too enthusiastically, since they aren't really part of the city yet. Admire the bruise-purple of old walls, the rounded softness of snail-grazed corners, the deep drip-grooves. Those are the things Ossarans are proud of.

## Wildlife Precautions

**Bats:** On Earth, bats can carry rabies and related lyssaviruses. Get pre-exposure rabies vaccination before you go. It's a series of shots over several weeks, so plan ahead. If you're bitten or scratched by a bat, wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water for fifteen minutes and seek treatment. Don't handle bats, dead or alive.

**Rats:** They're "fat and slick and unafraid." Keep food sealed. Keep your lodgings closed. Rat urine in standing water can carry leptospirosis, and Ossary is all standing water. That is another reason to keep your skin intact and cover cuts, and a reason to consider asking a travel doctor about preventive antibiotics.

**Gulls:** Huge and vicious, holding the harbor-ward rooftops. Don't eat outdoors in the harbor wards. Don't carry exposed food. Keep your hat on. Gulls go for the head.

**Snails:** Harmless, and everywhere in the wet lower districts. You will step on them. You'll hear the crunch and the frying crackle of their grazing. Accept it. Try not to step on them *deliberately*, in case there's a local feeling about it you haven't learned yet.

**Beetles and spiders:** Mostly harmless, probably. The colonial spider sheets spanning courtyards are breathtaking. Don't walk into them, don't tear them, and watch where locals duck.

**Dead things:** Thousands of birds and bats fall daily. You will see bodies. Some will be fresh and some won't. Don't touch them. That's the collectors' job, and it's a ceremonial one.

## The Psychological Load

This is what most travelers underestimate.

**Nothing is ever still.** Every surface you look at has something moving on it: snails, films, beetles, midges, the flicker of passing flock-shadow. The sky moves. The light strobes ("sieving"). The ground vibrates. For people with any sensitivity to crawling, clustering, or constant motion, this can become genuinely distressing after a few days. It never stops, and there's nowhere to look that's calm.

**There is no green.** Not one leaf, not one blade of grass, not even lichen. This sounds like a minor aesthetic point until you've gone a week without seeing a plant. Many people will find it quietly, strangely depressing. Your eyes will start hunting for green and not find it. Some ideas:
- Bring photographs of green places.
- Bring a small object in a green you love.
- A sealed glass terrarium with a moss or small plant might survive if it's truly sealed from the air and water. Don't open it. Ask first whether bringing living plants is permitted or considered offensive.

**The light under chitin windows is the color of weak tea.** Combined with the dim, wet interiors of the old wards, this can wear on your mood. Get up to the high districts and the western rim regularly for clean blue sky and hard sun.
