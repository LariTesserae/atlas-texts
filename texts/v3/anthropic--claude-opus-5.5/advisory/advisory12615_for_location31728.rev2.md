# Preparing for the Stump at Hollow Coin

## A Traveler's Briefing

The first thing to understand is that you are not visiting a place. You are visiting a *body*: a single living organism sixty kilometres across. You will walk on it, sleep on it, and breathe what it sheds. It will vibrate your skeleton for the whole of your stay. Almost every piece of advice below follows from that fact, so hold onto it.

The second thing to understand is that the Stump is not hostile. Very little here wants to hurt you. The danger comes from the place being fundamentally *indifferent to wet, soft, rhythmic, noisy creatures*. You are all four of those things.

---

## 1. Should You Go At All?

Honest self-screening matters more here than in most places.

**Reconsider the trip if you have:**
- **Respiratory conditions** such as asthma, COPD, or severe pollen allergies. The air is thin, and it permanently carries a fine organic dust.
- **Vestibular disorders, migraine, or seizure history.** Constant infrasound is a known trigger for nausea, vertigo, and headache. Some sensitive people also experience anxiety or visual disturbances from it.
- **Cardiac issues.** You face altitude, cold stress, and chronic low-frequency pressure on the chest.
- **Anxiety disorders, PTSD (especially combat-related), or severe misophonia.** Every night the plain sounds like a distant artillery barrage. That is not a figure of speech. It is a direct description of what you'll hear.
- **Pregnancy.** The effects of prolonged infrasound on a foetus are unknown, and the Tuners' own birthing practice is telling: they give birth in the one silent room.

**You will do well if you** are patient and physically quiet, can tolerate discomfort without complaint, have a decent sense of rhythm, and find vast empty spaces calming rather than frightening.

---

## 2. Timing Your Visit

Three cyclical events should shape when you go.

**Avoid ringing season at all costs.** Roughly once a century, the Stump lays down a new ring and changes key over several weeks. During that time:
- bridges fall;
- held rooms collapse;
- doors seal;
- the Tuners are working frantically to save their towers.

A visitor then is at best a burden and at worst a casualty, and nobody will have time for you. Before you commit, find out when the last one happened. The Tuners will know, because their histories are counted in keys.

**Plan around dust-storms.** They come every few weeks. Visibility drops to metres, and you must navigate by sound alone. Arrive just after one if you can, so you have the longest possible clear window.

**Sawyer swarms cannot be predicted.** Have a plan for where you'll be when one happens (see Section 7).

**Length of stay:** Keep a first visit short, no more than about a week. Sound fatigue builds up, and so does dehydration from the dry air. Most people cope with the first three days better than the fifth.

## 3. Getting Up There

The climb is three kilometres of fluted, tendon-like wood, from a grey dust basin to the summit. It is a serious technical ascent. It is also an ascent up the side of a living thing.

**Don't drive pitons or bolts into the Stump.** This isn't only about manners. The Tuners' entire civilisation rests on *never cutting the wood*: they sang their towers up rather than carving them. Arriving with drill holes behind you is like walking into someone's house through a hole you punched in the wall. It may also have consequences you can't foresee. The grain carries the Stump's circulation, and you would be wounding it.

**What to do instead:**
- Use removable protection that jams into the natural seams between the braided flutes. Cams and nuts are ideal.
- Better still, find out in advance whether the Tuners keep a route, or whether they know one. There may be a grown stair, or an old ramp from when the city was full.

**Friction and footing.** The wood is "tight as horn." Expect it to be hard, smooth, and in places polished. Soft, sticky rubber soles will serve you better than stiff crampon-style boots.

**Temperature during the climb.** Rounded, eroded stone surfaces can be surprisingly warm by day and brutally cold after dusk. Don't be on the face at dusk. When the wood contracts, the surface cracks with cannon-loud reports. Imagine one of those going off under your hands, three hundred metres up. Time the climb so you top out by mid-afternoon.

**Pace yourself for altitude.** The basin floor may already be high. Add three kilometres of cliff on top, and the summit air is described as *thin*. Treat this as a high-altitude expedition:
- consider acclimatising in the basin first;
- carry acetazolamide if it's appropriate for you;
- know the signs of altitude sickness.

## 4. Air, Cold, and the Absence of Water

### Water

This is the single biggest logistical fact of the trip. **There is no water on this world, and there has never been any.** No stream, no ice, no dew, no humid air to wring moisture from. Every drop you drink, you carry. Every drop you use for hygiene, you carry. Plan on roughly 4–5 litres a day, because the dry air will pull moisture out of you with every breath. Carry at least 20% more than you calculate, and keep it in rigid, sealed, spill-proof containers.

### Spills

Water here is not just a supply. It is a potential contaminant, and it needs its own rule.

The Stump is an organism whose whole chemistry evolved without water. Nobody knows what liquid water does to its grain:
- it might swell the wood locally and throw off the pitch;
- it might be harmless;
- it might be, to this chemistry, something like acid.

It could also carry microbes that finally have the one thing they were missing. **Do not spill. Do not pour. Do not urinate on the wood.** Pack out all liquid waste in sealed containers. The Tuners have probably never seen water outside a record. Consider keeping your supply discreet, and think carefully before you show it to anyone.

### Dryness

Expect split lips within the first hour. After that come cracked fingertips, bleeding nostrils, and gritty eyes.

**Bring:**
- **Heavy lip balm**, applied constantly. Choose a wax-based one; an oil-based one will collect dust.
- **Heat-and-moisture-exchange masks.** These are the kind used in cold-weather mountaineering. They recapture moisture from your exhaled breath and will dramatically reduce water loss.
- **Saline nasal gel**, which is better than spray here because it doesn't waste water.
- **Preservative-free lubricating eye drops.**
- **Thick barrier cream** for your hands.
- **Gloves**, even when it's warm.

### Cold

Temperatures fall fast at dusk, and the air holds little heat. Dress in layers, with wool next to the skin. The reason for wool is covered in Section 5.

## 5. The Dust, and the Absolute Rule About Fire

### The dust

The Stump constantly sheds a bronze pollen-dust. It is fine, faintly sweet, and hangs in a permanent veil overhead. During storms it comes down to ground level. There are several reasons to take it seriously:

- **It's biologically active.** It is literally the Stump's substance: food for the chaff swifts, spread on the wind. You don't know what it does inside human lungs over days.
- **It's fine enough to get everywhere.** It will reach your eyes, your food, and your gear seals.
- **The air during storms is described as "faintly electric."** That means static.

**Bring:**
- A well-fitted respirator with P100 filters, and plenty of spare filters.
- Sealed goggles, not sunglasses.
- A balaclava or buff to seal the gap between the two.

Some visitors will be tempted by the sweet smell. **Don't taste it.** Don't let children taste it. Don't assume it's inert because it smells pleasant.

### Fire

Consider what the environment adds up to:
- a world made entirely of *wood*;
- air that has never held moisture;
- an atmosphere suspended with fine organic dust;
- static electricity in the storms.

Those are the textbook conditions for a **dust explosion**, in the open air, above a sixty-kilometre fuel source.

Near the centre of the Stump there is a black, blistered ring called the Burn. Nobody knows what burned. Its playback is described as a roar, and those who have heard it through won't describe it.

**So bring no fire at all:**
- **No open flames.** No matches, lighters, candles, or cigarettes.
- **No combustion stoves.** Use flameless chemical heating packs for food, or eat cold.
- **Check all electronics for sparking risk.** Pay particular attention to battery safety: lithium cells that can vent and ignite are a real concern.
- **Choose antistatic clothing.** Wool and cotton blends are better than synthetics, which build a charge as you walk. This is why the base layer should be wool.
- **Carry a grounding strap** and touch it to the wood before you handle electronics.

You should also assume that fire is, culturally, the deepest horror these people have. Don't mention it casually. Never make a joke involving it.

## 6. The Sound

This is what will define your trip. Nothing can fully prepare you for it, but some things help.

### What you'll be dealing with

**Infrasound (the drone).** It has a period of several seconds, felt more than heard. It comes up through your feet and bones. Earplugs do almost nothing against it, because it travels through your skeleton, not your ear canal. Expect:
- a sense of pressure in the chest;
- mild nausea for the first day or two;
- difficulty judging your own heartbeat;
- a vague, unplaceable unease, sometimes a feeling that you're being watched;
- possibly visual "swimming" at the edges of your sight.

Most people adapt over two or three days. Some never quite do.

**Beating chords and interference zones.** Places where the wind seems to stall and the air shimmers are standing-wave nodes. Walk around them. They may be harmless, but a body sitting in a strong pressure node could feel real effects: ear pain, disorientation, perhaps worse. The Tuners will know which ones matter.

**The dusk cracks.** These are impulsive noise events, cannon-loud and coming in volleys. They are a genuine **hearing-damage risk**. Impulse noise at that level can cause permanent loss.

**The shoot-wail.** A high, sustained, glassy note, loud enough to be heard three kilometres away. It comes every evening when the wind rises.

**The constant background.** The rasp herds' grinding, the swifts' wing-cracks, the humming grainmoss.

### Hearing protection

**Electronic level-dependent protection** is the right tool. It's the kind shooters use: it passes normal ambient sound through and instantly clamps down on sharp impulses. You need this specific kind, not simple earplugs.

The reason is that **you need to hear quiet changes in pitch in order to stay safe** (see sawyers, Section 7), while also being protected from the cracks. Plain foam plugs will save your hearing and cost you your situational awareness.

Also carry foam plugs for sleeping, and wear them *under* the muffs during the worst of the dusk volleys.

### Sleep

This will be your hardest problem. The night is the loudest time: the pitch climbs, the cracks run in cascades, and the shoots sing.

- Bring a sleeping pad with real vibration damping, meaning dense closed-cell foam at least a few centimetres thick. It won't stop the drone, but it takes the edge off.
- Earplugs, an eye mask, and a clear plan.
- Consider a mild sleep aid for the first nights, if it's safe for you, *and* only if someone else is awake on watch.

Better still, ask the Tuners whether you can sleep in a tower.

### Practise before you go

- **Rhythm.** You may need to cross a bridge-song, and to do that you must walk in time (see Section 9). Practise walking to a metronome at a range of tempos. Practise holding a beat while distracted, tired, and frightened.
- **Pitch discrimination.** Practise noticing small pitch changes in a low, sustained tone. A tone generator and ten minutes a day for a few weeks is enough. That skill is what lets you notice a "sour note" underfoot.
- **Quiet movement.** Learn to walk softly and to pack your gear so it doesn't rattle. Clinking carabiners, jingling zips, and crinkling packaging all add small tones to a world where tone is structure.

## 7. The Ground and What Lives In It

### Walking

- **The combed ridges** are a hand high and run for kilometres, and they're hard to see in dim, shadowless light. They are an ankle-rolling hazard. Walk *along* them when you can, rather than across them.
- **Polished surfaces** may be slick, especially with fine dust on them, which acts like ball bearings. Keep your stride short.
- **Shoot-ghosts** are long pale stripes where fallen shoots have been absorbed. They're usually smooth and make good walking paths.
- **Standing shoots are masts up to 400 metres tall.** They fall after a few centuries, and you won't know how old any given one is. Don't camp at the base of one. Don't linger under one in high wind.

### Where to camp, and when

The dusk contraction cracks run *along the rings*.
- **Don't** camp on the narrow, dark "starvation" rings; they already look like cracks for a reason.
- **Don't** camp right on a boundary between rings.
- **Do** pick the middle of a broad, pale ring of plenty.
- **Do** keep away from grainmoss patches. The moss grows where the surface is already cracked.

Have camp set up and be settled *before* dusk. The cracking starts as the light copper-shifts. You don't want to be pitching a tent while the ground goes off like artillery.

You won't be able to stake a tent into the wood, and shouldn't try. Bring a freestanding tent with weighted anchor bags, and fill them with basin dust before the climb. Choose a low-profile shelter that doesn't flap: a drumming tent fabric is one more noise, and possibly one more tone.

### Sawyers

These are the one true predator. Take them seriously.

They're long, armoured, and segmented. They swim *through* the wood by vibrating at the local bell frequencies, and they pull prey down through the floor, which then closes smoothly over it. Their usual prey, rasps, are dog-sized. A crouching or sleeping human is not so different.

**What you can do:**
- **Stay away from rasp herds.** The herds are what sawyers hunt. Watch them from a distance; they're remarkable. Don't walk among them, and don't camp near one. If a herd suddenly scatters, "screaming like gears stripping," move *away* from where they were at once.
- **Listen for the sour note.** This is the warning: a change in pitch in the ground, a wrongness in the chord. This is exactly why you want electronic ear protection rather than plugs, and why you've practised pitch discrimination.
- **The vibration-damping tradeoff.** Heavily damped boots make your legs more comfortable, but they may also keep you from *feeling* a sawyer approach. Consider moderately damped soles, and learn to pay attention through your feet.
- **Glowing tracks at night** show where a sawyer has passed within the last hour or two: a forking ember-line under the surface. That means one was here recently. It doesn't mean it's gone. Don't stand on a fresh track.
- **Never sleep alone on the open plain** if you can avoid it. Keep watches. The watcher should be awake, standing, and *listening*.

**Swarms.** Swarms surface by the thousands in a single night and rework kilometres of plateau. If you get any hint of one, you need to be inside a Tuner-maintained tower, or as far from the front as you can get. Before your trip, ask the Tuners what they do during swarms. Their towers are wood too, but they are *tuned* wood, and that may matter.

### Other fauna

- **Rasps** are grazers and not known to be aggressive, but they are shell-backed and file-jawed. Don't get between a herd and a swelling bell, because they turn toward the drone together.
- **Chaff swifts** are harmless, but a flock turning nearby produces a single sharp crack. Don't flinch into a ridge.
- **Knellbirds** may be doing something essential to the whole ecosystem. **Don't disturb them, don't imitate their calls, and don't approach their perches.**

## 8. Light and Navigation

### Light

- The day is dim and tea-coloured, and shadows are soft and brown.
- The sun, Hollow Coin, is "bright enough to look at directly." Even so, don't assume the veil filters UV the way it filters visible light. Use sunscreen, and don't stare.
- Depth perception suffers in shadowless light, which makes the ridges harder to see.
- Dawn and dusk last for hours. That's beautiful, but it also stretches out the most hazardous transition of the day.
- **Your circadian rhythm will struggle.** Bring a small light-therapy device if you're sensitive, and keep to strict sleep and wake times.

### Night

There are no stars. The only lights are the veil's faint glow, the violet grainmoss, and sawyer tracks.

Bring a headlamp with a **dim red mode.** Bright white light will wreck your night vision, which you need in order to read the glowing tracks. It may also be rude in ways you can't guess.

### Navigation

This is genuinely hard. The plain is featureless, there are no stars, and any compass is unproven on this world.

- **The rings are your map.** They're concentric. By watching the curvature of the arcs you're walking across, you can always tell which way the centre lies. Crossing rings moves you toward or away from the centre; following a ring moves you around it.
- **The towers are landmarks,** though only on clear days and only a few at a time.
- **During dust-storms, direction can only be told by pitch.** Since you can't learn that in time, *don't travel in storms.* Stay put, sheltered, until the veil lifts.
- **The edge has no railing.** It is a rounded shoulder that slopes away into haze, and in poor visibility you won't see it until you're on it. If you're anywhere near the rim, stop moving whenever visibility drops. The drone thins near the edge, and you'll hear the wind more clearly. Treat that as a warning.

**GPS** almost certainly doesn't exist here. Carry an inertial or pedometer-based tracker as a backup, and log your ring crossings by hand.

## 9. The City and the Tuners

### First contact

The Tuners are quiet, few in number, and live mostly in near-empty towers. They communicate by touch and by small tones pitched just above the drone. Silence is the thing they value most, because their world never offers it.

- **Arrive quietly.** Don't shout a greeting. Approach slowly and visibly, with open hands, and wait.
- **Keep your voice low and even.** Whisper if you can manage it. A whisper is breath, which carries less tone than voiced speech.
- **Expect touch.** Contact on the arm or hand may be *language*, not intimacy. Let them lead, and ask (by gesture) before you touch them.
- **Learn what you can in advance.** If any other travelers have made contact, find out what the basic touch-signals and tone-signs are.

### Don't make tones

This matters more than any cultural rule. In the towers, **sound is architecture.**
- Walls on the upper floors are sustained chords.
- Bridges are single held notes.
- Doors open only at specific pitches.

Humming, whistling, singing, tapping a rhythm, or letting a metal cup ring could in principle interfere with the structure around you. Worst case, you'd be doing it while someone is leaning against a held wall, or crossing a bridge.

**So:**
- Don't hum. Don't whistle, not even absent-mindedly. Practise catching yourself doing it for weeks before you go. Most people don't realise how often they do it.
- Bring **no speakers, no music, no tuning forks, no bells, no singing bowls,** nothing designed to resonate.
- Pad metal gear. Don't bang things.
- If you must speak, keep it low, soft, and brief, and watch the Tuners' faces.

### Held rooms

You can lean on a held wall, but *only* when a Tuner indicates that it's safe. Tuners watch the chords the way sailors once watched weather. If one of them moves suddenly away from a wall, go with them at once and don't ask why.

On upper floors, stay near a Tuner and near grown wood. Grown wood won't vanish if a note falters.

### Bridge-songs

Bridge-songs are ribbons of solidified pressure. They are walkable if you step on the nodes; step on an antinode and you fall through. You can see one as a faint line where dust refuses to settle.

- **Don't cross one without a Tuner guiding you.** Ideally, cross roped to them, or ask whether that's possible.
- **Walk in their rhythm.** Watch their steps, not your own feet.
- **Expect the surface to bounce.** Don't fight it; move with it.
- **If you can't hold a beat under stress, decline.** There's no shame in it, and the view from the tower throat is just as good.
- **Never** cross in a wind, during pitch transitions at dusk and dawn, or in anything close to ringing season.

### Doors of pitch

Tuners carry resonant heartwood tokens that ring at personal pitches. These are their *keys*, and in a sense their identities. **Never touch someone's token uninvited.** Don't admire one too closely either, and never offer to buy one.

If someone opens a door for you, the wood will flow around you like cold honey. Expect it to feel intensely strange, to be cold, and possibly to feel frightening. Close your eyes if that helps, keep walking steadily, and **don't stop halfway.** Ask beforehand exactly how to move through.

### The null rooms

Each tower has one perfectly silent chamber at its core, used for birth, marriage, and death. These are sacred.

- **Never enter one without an invitation.**
- **If you are invited,** understand that it's a significant gesture, and prepare yourself. Your body has spent days braced against sound, and when the sound stops you may feel as if you're falling. You may weep. You may lose your balance. **Sit down before the silence begins.** Let it happen. Don't be embarrassed, because everyone does it.
- **Don't speak** in the null unless your hosts do.

### The rings and the records

The Tuners spend their lives listening to the rings for the voices of the builders, using listening-spikes, in silence.

- Don't interrupt someone who is listening.
- Don't ask to hear the Burn. If it's offered, think hard before you accept. The people who have heard it through won't describe it, and you shouldn't assume you're sturdier than they are.
- Don't ask about the distant drone at the edge. They don't talk about it.

### Gifts

Think carefully about what's appropriate.
- **Avoid water, anything that burns, anything that rings, and anything made of wood from another world,** which could carry pests or fungal spores.
- **Silence-making things could be deeply meaningful.** Good sound-dampening material, a finely made pair of earmuffs, or dense felt might be treasured.
- So might **information**, but only if you have it: any record of other singing stumps, of forests, of what happened to them. Treat that carefully too. It may be the one subject they most want to hear about and least want to discuss.

## 10. Biosecurity: What You Bring In

You are travelling onto a single living organism that has never known water, fungus, termites, or beetles from any other world.

- **Clean all gear thoroughly before you arrive.** That means boots, packs, tent, and clothing, and it especially means anything wooden. Better still, bring nothing wooden.
- **Don't bring organic material** that could harbour spores or insect eggs.
- **Food:** bring sealed, dry, low-crumb rations. Bag every wrapper and pack out everything.
- **Human waste:** use a sealed pack-out system. Nothing leaves your body onto the Stump.
- **Don't take samples.** No chips, no moss, no dust samples taken without asking. Heartwood is keys, identity, and possibly library. The grainmoss is the Stump itself.

---

## 11. Food

Nothing here is food for you. Everything that grows *is* the Stump, and its chemistry is alien. The rasps eat wood, and you can't. The pollen smells sweet, and you still shouldn't eat it.

Bring all your food. Choose calorie-dense, dry rations that don't need cooking, since you'll have no flame and should use your water for drinking. Expect your appetite to drop, because nausea from infrasound suppresses it. Eat anyway, by the clock. Cold and altitude burn calories even when you're not hungry.

---

## 12. The Psychological Side

**The noise never stops.** That is the whole of it. There is no quiet corner, no calm hour, no rest from it except in a room you aren't allowed into. People underestimate this. On the third or fourth day, many visitors hit a wall of irritability and exhaustion, sometimes close to despair. Expect that to happen, and know it passes, or at least levels off.

**The ground is alive.** It hums under your hands. It flows when predators swim through it. It cracks and reports at night. It changes key. Some people find this magical. Others quietly start to feel as if they're being digested. Both are normal reactions.

**The emptiness is huge.** Sixty kilometres of combed floor lie under a coin-dim sky, with a handful of towers kilometres apart. The population is a few thousand in a city built for vastly more. You are walking through a place in decline. That has a weight.

**The edge.** If you go to the rim, you may hear the other drone: lower, slower, in no key the Stump knows. It is worth hearing. It may also stay with you for years.

**Coping tools:**
- a journal, written, since it makes no sound;
- a travel partner you truly trust;
- structured routines;
- simple breathing exercises that you *don't* need to hum through.

---

## 13. Leaving, and Coming Home

The descent carries all the dangers of the ascent, plus fatigue. Don't be on the cliff at dusk.

**After you return:**
- **Expect "drone sickness."** It is something like sea legs. You may feel a phantom pulse in your bones for days, and you may catch yourself waiting for the next swell.
- **Ordinary silence will feel wrong.** A quiet room at home may feel like the null did: like falling. Some returning visitors can't sleep without a low-frequency sound for weeks.
- **Get a hearing test.** Tinnitus is common.
- **Watch your respiratory health for a month,** in case the dust has an effect.
- **Tell someone about it.** Few people will understand. The ones who do may be the only ones who have been.

## 14. Packing List Summary

**Water and body:**
- all water, plus 20% extra, in rigid sealed containers;
- heat-moisture-exchange masks;
- wax-based lip balm;
- saline nasal gel;
- preservative-free eye drops;
- barrier cream;
- altitude medication if appropriate;
- anti-nausea medication;
- mild sleep aid.

**Air and eyes:**
- P100 respirator and many spare filters;
- sealed goggles;
- balaclava.

**Hearing:**
- electronic level-dependent muffs;
- foam plugs;
- spare batteries in a fire-safe case.

**Clothing:**
- wool base layers;
- antistatic outer layers;
- windproof shell;
- insulated gloves plus liner gloves;
- soft sticky-soled boots with moderate damping.

**Shelter:**
- low-profile freestanding tent that doesn't flap;
- weighted anchor bags;
- thick closed-cell foam pad;
- warm sleeping bag.

**No-fire kit:**
- flameless heat packs;
- no lighters or stoves;
- battery-safety pouches;
- grounding strap.

**Navigation:**
- inertial or pedometer tracker;
- hand log for ring crossings;
- red-mode headlamp.

**Climbing:**
- removable protection only, with no bolts or pitons;
- rope and harness, which can also serve for roping in on bridge crossings.

**Waste:**
- sealed pack-out containers for all solid and liquid waste.

**Silence:**
- padding for all metal gear;
- no speakers, no instruments, nothing that rings.

**Gifts:**
- sound-dampening materials;
- your quietest good manners.

## A Last Word

Visitors describe the same arc. At first the place is simply too much: too loud, too big, too dry, too strange, the ground alive in a way the body refuses to accept. Somewhere around the third copper dusk, standing still while a crack-volley runs out along a ring like a lit fuse and the shoots begin to wail, you stop bracing. You start to hear it as the Stump hears itself: a metabolism, a heartbeat kilometres long, a thing that has sung itself into existence one micron at a time since before your species had a word for tree.

Go quietly. Carry your own water. Never make a flame. Don't hum. Listen through your feet. When they offer you the silence, sit down first.
