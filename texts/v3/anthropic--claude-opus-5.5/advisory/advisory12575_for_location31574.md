# Preparing for Hesk

## First, an Honest Impression

Hesk will not try to kill you. It isn't hostile, and nothing there hates you. Mostly it will not notice you. The dangers come from indifference: thin air, no water, and a place whose geometry and time were not made with you in mind. Most people who come to harm here will do it slowly, through dehydration, altitude, and a gradual loss of their sense of direction and duration, until they are three days down an avenue that should have taken an afternoon and their water is gone.

The main preparation is a mindset. **You are a visitor to a process, not a place.** Plan around your supplies and your body, never around distances or the map in your head, because the city will not keep either one steady for you.

## Part One: Your Body

### The Air

This is the most serious problem, and people underestimate it.

The description gives several clues. The sky is nearly black at noon. Birds are too heavy for the air to hold. The air feels like being "pressed between two sheets of glass." That is not ordinary high-mountain thinness. At the least you should treat Hesk as equivalent to the Himalayan death zone. It may well be worse, because a daytime sky that dark suggests an atmosphere thinner than anywhere humans survive unassisted on Earth.

**Before committing to a full expedition, do a short scouting visit with a barometer or altimeter.** Measure the actual pressure at the plateau's edge. This single number decides your whole gear list:

- **If it's comparable to 7,000–8,500 m (roughly 23,000–28,000 ft):** you need weeks of staged acclimatization and supplemental oxygen, and you are operating at the edge of human tolerance.
- **If it's beyond that:** you need a pressure-demand oxygen system, and possibly a partial-pressure suit. The creatures living here tell you nothing about what *you* can survive. Masons are made of the same stuff as the walls.

Whatever the number turns out to be:

- **Acclimatize on the ramp.** The approach is days long, which is useful. Climb slowly, sleep lower than you climbed where you can, and spend rest days at the last lichen line. Treat the ramp as an acclimatization tool, not just a commute.
- **Carry a pulse oximeter** and check it every morning and evening. Write the readings down, because you won't remember them accurately. Hypoxia damages judgment first, and it hides that damage from you.
- **Learn the signs of HAPE and HACE** (fluid on the lungs and fluid on the brain): a wet cough, breathlessness at rest, confusion, clumsiness, a headache that won't lift. Your companions should know them too, because you will not notice your own confusion. In Hesk this is especially dangerous. The city's own disorientations are designed to feel like madness, so you need an objective check, the oximeter or a simple coordination test, to tell "the city is strange" apart from "my brain is swelling."
- **Bring acetazolamide and dexamethasone** if a physician approves them for you, and know the doses.
- **The only real cure for severe altitude sickness is descent.** Descent from Hesk means days of broken slate. So **cache oxygen and water on the ramp** as you go up, at intervals of about a day's walk. If someone gets sick in the city, they must be able to stumble down to a cache without finishing the whole ramp.

### The Light

The light comes from below. This will ruin your sun protection if you don't think it through.

The sun is a hard white disc in a black sky, and the atmosphere above you is very thin, so ultraviolet exposure will be severe. But most of that light is reflected up off the cloud sea, like snow glare but constant and total. Consider what this means for you:

- **A hat brim does almost nothing.** It shades the top of your head, which was already in shadow.
- **You will burn in unusual places:** under your chin, inside your nostrils, under your jaw, beneath your eyebrows, on the undersides of your wrists and forearms, and on your upper lip from below. Put high-SPF mineral sunscreen *everywhere*, especially the places you've never needed it.
- **Snow blindness is the real threat.** You need glacier glasses with shielding on the sides *and underneath*. Ordinary sunglasses, even wraparounds, leave a gap at the bottom where the brightest light comes in. Consider goggles, or have side and bottom shields fitted. Snow blindness can take hours to appear, so the damage is done before you feel it. Bring a spare pair.
- **A buff or neck gaiter** pulled up under the eyes shields the underside of your face and also helps with dust.

The light has a psychological cost as well. Your visual system assumes light comes from above. In Hesk, shapes will look inside-out: domes may read as bowls and steps as overhangs. Expect headaches and eye strain, and possibly mild nausea like motion sickness.

**A useful trick: carry a headlamp and use it even in the day.** A small, dim, warm light shining down onto the ground in front of you restores normal lighting cues just around your feet. It gives your brain a patch of the world that makes sense. You will trip less, and you will feel less like you're dissolving.

### Water

There is no water in Hesk. Plan as if the dawn rime does not exist, because for your purposes it doesn't (see *The Masons*, below).

Thin, dry, cold air takes water out of you with every breath. You will lose far more through respiration than you'd expect, and you won't feel thirsty in proportion. Dehydration also makes altitude sickness worse.

- **Carry all your water, plus a large margin.** Estimate four to five liters per person per day, then add more.
- **Weight becomes your limiting factor.** This, more than anything, sets how long you can stay. Plan the expedition backward from the water.
- **Use insulated containers.** It is a cellar chill, not a freezing one, but at night and in the blackouts when the cloud sea parts, temperatures may drop. Sleep with a bottle inside your bag.
- **Bring electrolytes,** saline nasal spray (your sinuses will crack and bleed), lubricating eye drops, and heavy lip balm.
- **Check your urine color** every time. This is your best simple measure of dehydration.

### Cold

Here, at least, Hesk is kind. "The steady, dry chill of a cellar" means you'll want warm layers, not extreme-cold gear. Plan for:

- A merino or synthetic base layer, a fleece or light down midlayer, and a **windproof shell**. The wind is slow but constant, and constant wind drains heat over hours.
- Light gloves plus warmer overmitts.
- A sleeping bag rated comfortably below what you expect. You'll be sleeping in the open, and the blackouts can bring sudden drops.
- **Do not sit or lean on the black threshold stone.** It stays cold even at midday and will pull heat out of you steadily. This matters most when you're tired and want to rest on a doorstep.

## Part Two: The Approach

The ramp is days of broken slate, frost-cracked into plates, tilting "past the point where anything grows." It is miserable and dangerous in its own way, and it is also your lifeline home.

- **Boots:** stiff-soled, ankle-supporting mountaineering or heavy trekking boots. Slate plates shift, slide, and have sharp edges. Break your boots in long before you go.
- **Gaiters,** to keep grit out.
- **Trekking poles.** Your knees will thank you on the descent, which is when most injuries happen.
- **Gloves for scrambling,** because slate cuts.
- **Mark your route and your caches.** Use something you'll recognize from both directions, since the ramp will look different going down. Luckily the ramp seems to be ordinary space. The strangeness appears to start at the city.
- **Establish a base camp at the plateau's edge,** where the ramp flattens. This is your anchor. Keep your largest cache here. Every excursion into the city should be planned as a round trip back to this point.

## Part Three: Moving Through the City

### Navigation Does Not Work Normally

This is the core difficulty of Hesk, and it needs its own discipline.

Towers take an hour to reach, or no time at all. A straight avenue can go on for three days. Doorways repeat, aged or un-aged. The city is "larger on the inside of its streets than on the outside of its rim."

So:

**1. Turn around by supplies and time, never by distance.**
This is the most important rule in this guide. Before each excursion, set a hard turnaround point: *when I have used a third of my water, or when X hours have passed, I turn back.* Not half, a third, because the return may take longer than the outbound leg, and Hesk makes no promise that the way back will be as long as the way in. Once you hit that limit, you turn around, whatever is ahead of you. The tower you were going to reach may not be reachable anyway.

**2. Carry a reliable way to find the rim.**
You have one cue that seems to stay honest: **the light comes from below, from the cloud sea, and the cloud sea lies beyond the rim.** When you can see the edge of the plateau, you can find the way out. From deep in the city you may not be able to see it. Climbing a staircase for a vantage point *might* help, but stairs are built for feet of a different length than yours, so they'll be tiring and awkward, and the upper terraces carry their own risk (see *The Unseen Hunter*).

**3. Consider a physical line.**
This is an unusual suggestion, and I don't know that it will work. But a long, thin, strong cord unspooled from base camp might be the most honest navigation tool you can bring into a city that folds. Maps and compasses depend on the space around you behaving. A line depends only on its own continuity. If you follow it back and it leads home, good. If it does something strange, if it goes taut when it shouldn't, runs off at an impossible angle, or arrives looking frayed and aged, you have learned something important, and you should get out.

A few hundred meters of cord weighs little. Use it at least for your first excursions and for any trip toward the seam.

**4. Bring multiple timekeepers.**
Carry a mechanical watch *and* a digital one, and ideally a third on a companion. Compare them regularly and write the readings in a logbook. If they drift apart, that is data. Keep a paper journal with a timestamp on every entry. Your internal sense of time will fail in the silence and the dim, directionless light. You need external records.

**5. Photograph and sketch repeating features.**
When you pass a distinctive doorway, a particular crack or spill of dust, photograph it. If you pass it again, photograph it again and compare. This isn't only curiosity. It tells you whether you're in one of the city's loops. If the second sighting is *newer* than the first, you have moved backward in the city's time in some way, and you should seriously consider heading for the rim.

**6. Mark your path without marking the city.**
Don't chalk or scratch the walls. That is defacing the masons' work, and in a place like this it may matter more than it seems. Instead, leave small, removable markers of your own: a colored pebble brought from home, a numbered tag, a strip of bright ribbon weighted on the ground. Collect them on the way back. If you ever find one of your markers where you didn't place it, or looking weathered when you set it down yesterday, treat that as a serious warning.

### The Dust-Rivers

The dust moves along the streets "like slow water," wearing troughs down the centers.

- **Walk to the sides of streets, not in the troughs.** You'll disturb less, and you won't be wading through the fine material the masons collect.
- **Wear a mask or pull up your buff.** Fine dust in dry, thin air irritates the lungs, and you already have a breathing problem. A lightweight particulate mask is sensible, but it must not restrict your breathing or interfere with your oxygen system.
- **Protect your gear.** Fine dust gets into zippers, cameras, watch crowns, and oxygen regulators. Bag your electronics. Check regulator fittings every day.
- **In the low season,** when the cloud sea drops, "the dust-rivers run fast." Dust exposure will be worse, and the city darker.

### Buildings That Exist Only From Certain Angles

**Do not enter a building whose existence depends on your angle of view.**

It's tempting. It is also a question you don't want answered: what happens to you if you are inside the dome when you, or it, step out of alignment?

To test a structure you're unsure of, stand still and shift your position a step to each side. If it wavers, leave it alone. Enter only buildings that stay solid from every direction you can check. Even then, going inside is optional, and I'm not convinced it's wise. The doorways and stairs are proportioned for other kinds of bodies, and those bodies may not be absent in all times.

### The Seam

The seam is a band about a quarter mile wide, running north to south, where the ancient eastern city and the self-healing western city meet. Buildings there flicker between old and new, and the dust hangs in the air, "unable to decide which way to fall."

My gut says: **look at it from the edge. Do not cross it casually, and do not linger in it.**

The masons gather there because it is where the most work is needed. It is also where time is least settled. If you insist on approaching:

- Go tethered, with your line back to a point well outside the band.
- Go briefly, with a strict time limit.
- Don't enter any structure within the seam.
- Watch your timekeepers and your own hands. Look at your skin, your fingernails, your gear. If anything looks older or newer than it should, leave at once.
- Never cross the seam simply to reach the other side of the city. Plan separate excursions to east and west, each starting from the rim.

The eastern districts carry a more ordinary danger: leaning towers and collapsed arcades. Rubble can shift, and old structures can fail. Don't shelter beneath leaning stone. The western districts are gentler, but be cautious there too. A wall that is reassembling from rubble is, by definition, in motion.

### When the Sky Has a Floor

On certain evenings the upper sky seems to turn solid. The shadows pool against it like smoke on a ceiling, and "you feel the pull to fall toward the light."

This is the most immediately dangerous effect in Hesk, because the light is at the rim, over the edge, down in the cloud sea.

- **Learn the warning sign:** shadows rising from the towers that stop dissipating and begin to flatten and spread.
- **Before dusk, get well away from terrace edges, balconies, and the rim.** A large open plaza, far from any drop, is the safest place.
- **When it happens, sit down.** Get your center of gravity low. Lie flat if you have to, and hold on to the ground.
- **Close your eyes.** The feeling is built on visual cues. Without them, your inner ear and your contact with the ground should agree about which way is down.
- **Consider clipping into an anchor.** A short strap to a heavy piece of your own gear or a solid block of stone, if nothing else is available, and if it can be done without damaging the masons' work.
- **Hold a companion's hand.** It sounds sentimental, but it gives you a physical reference that doesn't depend on the sky.

### When the Cloud Sea Parts

Occasionally the clouds below open, the city's light source vanishes, and "everything freezes for minutes until the white closes again."

- **Keep your headlamp on your head or in your hand at all times.** Never pack it away.
- **Stop moving** during a blackout, especially near stairs or edges.
- **Expect a sudden chill.** Keep a warm layer where you can reach it.
- Use a dim or red light if you can, so you don't lose your night vision. The blackout is short, and the light returning from below will be dazzling after it.

## Part Four: The Living Things

### The Masons

You will see very few, maybe one a day, maybe none. Across the whole city there may be forty or fifty. They are building, or stitching, something far larger than themselves, and they are doing it one fingernail's width at a time.

**Etiquette, which here is also safety:**

- **Never take the dawn rime.** This is the one firm rule. It is the only moisture in Hesk. The masons need it to live, and they need it to make the paste that holds the city together. The rime lasts twenty minutes and is "licked or blown away." It will be tempting, especially if your water is running low, but it would not meaningfully rehydrate you and it could matter a great deal to them. If you ever need to scrape rime off a wall to survive, your planning has already failed badly and you should be heading down the ramp.
- **Never step on or touch a fresh bead of paste.** Watch where you put your feet near walls.
- **Don't block a mason's path.** They move slowly and test every step. Give them wide berth, and move slowly yourself when they're near.
- **Don't collect dust from the troughs or the lees of walls.** That is their material.
- **When the stone rings at dusk and the masons stop to stand with necks lifted, stop too.** Stand still, be quiet, and wait for the chord to fade. You won't know why this matters, and I don't either. But when the only builders of a city go still, a visitor should follow their lead.
- **Be aware that they vanish against walls.** You may be closer to one than you realize. Look carefully before leaning on anything.
- **Don't try to feed, touch, or communicate with them.** They have shown no interest in the people the city seems built for. They will show none in you.
