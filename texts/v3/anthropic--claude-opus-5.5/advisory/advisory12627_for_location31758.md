# Preparing to Visit the Held Sea of the Uplands

## The Short Version

This is a dive site, not a hike. It is also a dive site whose basic physics you cannot assume. It sits at mountain altitude, at bath temperature, with a floor you can fall out of, over rock that cuts like broken glass. The place itself is gentle, perhaps the gentlest you will ever visit. The dangers come almost entirely from bringing lowland habits and ordinary-world assumptions into it.

Your intuition should tell you to go **slowly, warm-bodied but cool-headed, and with an instrument in your hand before your body follows it in.**

---

## 1. The Questions You Must Answer Before You Dive

Most of the serious risks come from a few unknowns. Treat your first day or two at the waterline as a survey, not a visit.

### What is the pressure inside?

This is the central question, and the description does not answer it.

In an ordinary sea, pressure increases with depth. If the Held Sea behaved that way, its underside would be the *deepest* point. It would sit beneath a column of water perhaps a kilometer or more tall, at a hundred atmospheres or more, and pushing your hand into it would be impossible or catastrophic. Yet the account says your hand passes in easily, meeting only mild resistance. So whatever holds the water also seems to cancel or reshape the pressure, at least at the boundary.

On the other hand, rocks tumble downward inside it and oxygen bubbles rise upward. Gravity and buoyancy work normally within the body of the water. That suggests pressure may still change with height once you are inside, in some way that differs from ordinary hydrostatics. Possible regimes include:

- **Near-ambient throughout.** The water is held at roughly the air pressure of the waterline everywhere. In that case decompression is not a concern and diving is easy.
- **Increasing downward from the top surface, but capped or zeroed at the underside.** A strange discontinuity at the boundary. The lowest valleys could be at high pressure a few meters above a boundary that feels like nothing.
- **Something else entirely.** For example, pressure increasing *upward*, or following the terrain.

**The protocol:** Before any part of your body goes past your wrist, push a depth/pressure gauge up through the surface on a pole or line and read it. Then send a logging gauge upward on a float line in stages: 1 m, 5 m, 20 m, 50 m, and as far as you can reel it. Better still, anchor a line and walk the gauge up a slope. Log temperature and dissolved oxygen at the same time. Only when you have a pressure profile should you plan dive depths, gas mixes, and ascent schedules.

If pressure behaves normally inside, then:
- Treat any excursion upward toward the top surface as an **ascent** requiring decompression planning.
- Treat any descent back toward the underside as a **descent**.
- Remember that the long swim to the upper surface ("several hundred meters") would be a serious technical dive with a heavy decompression obligation. It is not a casual paddle.

### Is it fresh or salt?

It is described as a remnant of an ancient sea, so assume salt until tested. Salinity changes your weighting and eye comfort. Salt also affects wound care.

### What's dissolved in it?

The rock is young and "leaching fresh" minerals, and the waterline wears a mineral crust. Test for:

- pH
- Dissolved metals
- Silica
- Sulfur compounds
- Anything caustic

The description says the water feels "heavier than it should be." That points to high density, which may mean a high mineral load, or it may mean something stranger. Test a patch of skin for irritation over a few hours before full immersion. Do not drink it, even if it tests "fresh."

### What's its temperature profile?

"Skin temperature, maybe slightly more" is the most underrated hazard in the whole description. See Section 2.

## 2. The Things Most Likely to Hurt You

In rough order of likelihood.

### Overheating

Water at 37°C or above means **your body cannot shed heat.** In ordinary diving, cold is the enemy. Here the enemy is the opposite, and it is sneakier: warm water feels wonderful, and the feeling of drowsy comfort *is* the early symptom.

- Swimming exertion, especially fighting through ribbon-plant sheaves or ascending, generates heat you cannot dump.
- A rebreather's scrubber produces warm, humid breathing gas. That is lovely in cold water and a burden here.
- You will sweat underwater without noticing, and dehydrate.

**Prepare by:**
- Doing heat acclimatization before the trip: several weeks of hot-weather exercise or sauna protocols.
- Wearing the thinnest possible exposure suit. Its job is protection from cuts and minerals, not warmth. Consider a light, pale, perforated skin or a thin lycra suit rather than neoprene.
- Swimming at half your normal effort. The place rewards slowness anyway.
- Setting hard time limits and hydrating heavily between dives with water you brought.
- Having your partner watch for confusion, lethargy, or headache. These can easily be mistaken for the dreamy mood of the place.
- Taking cooling seriously on the dry side. The lowlands are hot and dusty too, so your base camp needs shade.

If you plan to visit for the **golden seed-release in the warmest weeks**, the water will be at its hottest. Go fitter, stay shorter, and rest more.

### Falling out of the sea

The underside of the sea is a floor you can drop through into open air.

Where the waterline meets rock, that is simply the exit. But the description says the underside also extends as a ceiling over the foothills, and possibly past slopes and gaps in the terrain, with open air beneath it. Rocks that drift down and exit fall "the rest of the way at ordinary speed."

**Anything that sinks out of the sea over empty air falls. That includes you.**

- **Never be negatively buoyant near the underside unless you are directly over rock you can safely land on.** Treat the underside like the edge of a cliff.
- Stay well above it when crossing any area that is not ground at the line.
- Don't overweight. The dense water may tempt you to add ballast. Add it carefully, and consider a quick-release system that sheds weight *and* one that doesn't release accidentally.
- If you lose buoyancy control near the underside, **inflate immediately.** This is the reverse of most diving instincts about runaway ascent. Here, a runaway *descent* over open air is the fatal case.
- Clip in. On any dive near the boundary, a line anchored to rock is not overcautious.

### The rock

The basalt is described as glassy, fresh, faulted into knife-edges, "cutting boots and palms." Assume it behaves like obsidian: edges sharper than steel blades.

**Bring:**
- Cut-resistant gloves (Dyneema or similar) rated for both wet and dry use.
- Stiff, tough-soled boots for the approach and the waterline climb, and hard-soled dive boots inside. Standard fin-booties will be sliced.
- Knee and elbow protection.
- A helmet. See rockfall below.
- Tape and abrasion sleeves for lines and hoses. Anything soft will be cut on those edges.

Inside, the velvet fronds and paddle-leaves pad everything. That will lull you. The raw rock is still underneath, and a fern-soft handhold may hide a blade.

### Rockfall, in both directions

The range is young and still fracturing. You'll hear distant cracks and slow rumbles.

- **On the approach:** The foothills are covered in fresh scree and recent rockfall. Stones dropping out of the underside fall at full speed, "trailing a brief rain." A little patter of falling droplets overhead may be a warning. Wear a helmet the whole way up. Don't linger directly beneath the underside in areas of recent debris. Learn where slabs have been landing.
- **Inside:** Loosened rock moves slowly in water. You'll have time to see it coming, but it is still massive and will not stop for you. Watch upslope. A tumbling slab could pin or crush you against rock.
- **Seismic instability:** The ground is still cooling and settling. Ledges may give way. Don't trust a slab because it looks solid, and don't set up anything important below overhangs.

### Entanglement

Two sources, and both deserve respect.

1. **The canopy tethers:** hundreds of hair-thin lines per canopy, strung vertically through every valley and glinting only when the light catches them. This is monofilament fishing line on a landscape scale. In the dusk under a canopy you will not see them.
2. **The ribbon-plant sheaves:** dense, streaming several times your height. They behave like kelp forest.

**Prepare by:**
- Using streamlined gear with nothing dangling. No loose octopus hoses or clips.
- Carrying **two cutting tools**, one on each side, reachable with either hand: line cutters or trauma shears.
- Practicing entanglement drills in advance.

Move through the tether zones slowly, with a light held out ahead to catch the glint.

**An important ethical note:** Cutting a tether harms a colony that grows "a bladder or two a season," meaning it may be centuries old. Cut only to save yourself. The better strategy is not to get caught: go around the rigging, not through it.

### Getting lost

Navigation here is odd.

- **Footprints vanish.** The velvet closes over any track within a day. You leave no trail to follow back.
- Everything is green, layered, and similar. Canopies drift, not much, but they are not fixed landmarks.
- Light shifts slowly. The shafts only move "an arm's width in an hour," so light direction isn't a quick cue.

**What helps:**
- **The underside is level.** It is a perfect horizontal reference across the whole range. Knowing your height above it gives you a contour line to follow home, *if* you understand the pressure profile well enough to use your gauge as an altimeter.
- **Bubbles rise.** "Up" is always toward the upper surface, which gives you at least one axis.
- A compass, if it works. Fresh basalt can be strongly magnetic and may deflect it locally. Test it at the line.
- Reels and guidelines for any serious exploration, left anchored and retrieved on the way back, like cave diving. Use a biodegradable or retrievable line. Don't leave rope in a forest this slow.
- Your own slate map, sketched as you go.

### Oxygen and gas issues

The forest breathes oxygen into the water in quantity, and the beads sometimes release "all at once." Dissolved oxygen may be well above normal, and the water may be supersaturated. That won't hurt you through the skin, but:

- If you use a rebreather, calibrate your sensors and monitor oxygen partial pressure carefully.
- If pressure increases inside, standard gas-toxicity limits apply at depth. Plan your mixes accordingly.
- Dissolved gas may matter for decompression models. Be conservative.

### Altitude

The waterline is about a third of the way up a mountain range. Depending on the range, that could be 1,000 to 3,000 meters above the lowlands' sea level. That means:

- Lower air pressure at your entry point. Any decompression you calculate must use **altitude-adjusted** tables or computer settings, if pressure inside behaves hydrostatically.
- Acclimatize to the altitude of the line for a few days before diving.
- **Don't fly, or step into a lower-pressure multiverse location, soon after deep dives.** Build in off-gassing time before you travel onward.
- **At the upper surface**, several hundred meters above the highest peaks, the air you breathe may be very thin, perhaps at serious high-altitude levels. Surfacing there to "take a breath of fresh air" could hit you with hypoxia on top of decompression stress. Bring your breathing gas to the surface with you. The sun there will also be intense, at altitude with no shade. Protect your skin and eyes.

### Infection

Warm, mineral-rich, biologically rich water is a good environment for microbes. Combine that with glass-sharp rock that produces cuts, and infection is a real risk.

- Clean every cut thoroughly with your own sterile water.
- Bring broad-spectrum antibiotics and a wound-care kit.
- Seal cuts before re-entering the water.
- Treat ear infection as likely on a multi-day trip: carry drying drops and use them after each dive.

## 3. Crossing the Boundary

Entering and leaving are the most technically awkward moments. They are unlike any ordinary dive entry.

### Entering

You enter **upward**: by standing up into the water, or climbing a ledge into it, the way you'd put your head up into an attic.

- Get fully kitted and breathing from your regulator *before* your head crosses. Once inside you are supported, but until then you are standing on sharp rock under the full dry-weight of your gear.
- Expect some resistance: "heavier than it should be." It may take a deliberate push, like breaking a surface film. Test with a hand, then an arm, then your head.
- Choose an entry where the rock slopes gently up through the line, so you can walk in rather than climb.

### Leaving

To leave, you descend out the bottom.

- **You regain full weight instantly.** One moment you're weightless; the next, you and 20 to 40 kg of gear are standing, or falling, on raw basalt. Exit feet-first, slowly, onto a surface you've already inspected. Ideally lay a pad or tarp at your exit point.
- Expect to be dripping in warm mineral water and suddenly exposed to dry, possibly cold, altitude air. Evaporation chill can be rapid despite the heat inside. Have a towel and a windproof layer at the exit.
- **Mark your exit clearly** from the inside (a float, a light, a line), so you can find the right spot to come down, and avoid exiting over air.

### The tide

The waterline rises and falls over roughly a decade. Your visit will catch it somewhere in that cycle. If it has recently fallen, there will be a band of limp, browning forest hanging in the air above the line, kept alive by the upward drips. Try not to disturb it: it is waiting. It is also slippery, and it hides the rock beneath.

## 4. Equipment

### For the approach (dry)
- Helmet
- Rigid-soled mountaineering boots and gaiters
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Sun protection, and plenty of water. The lowlands are dry and the scrub is thorny.
- Trekking poles with rubber or hard tips, because glassy rock will be slick in places.
- Climbing rope and harness for the steeper shelves near the line.
- Shade shelter and a cooling kit for base camp.

### For the water
- **Breathing system.** A closed-circuit rebreather suits the quiet and allows long dives, but watch the heat load. Open
