# Preparing for Kessel Col

## The Short Version

Kessel Col is quiet, clean, and beautiful, and it will kill a careless visitor in several independent ways. The summit shelf has almost no air. The chamber is full of heavy, flammable, narcotic vapor. The room is loud enough to cut you off from your companions. And the place has one law, *nothing changes*, which means anything you break stays broken, and anything you leave behind may be kept.

Go as a guest into a sealed museum that also happens to be a fuel tank. Bring everything you need, touch almost nothing, and leave nothing.

---

## 1. The Summit: Altitude and Exposure

### The air
A sky that is nearly black at noon, with stars showing, means you are far above any altitude where humans live, possibly above the height of the highest peaks on Earth. The description says your heartbeat becomes the loudest thing in your body. Take that literally: your heart is working hard to deliver too little oxygen.

- **Assume you need supplemental oxygen from the moment you arrive.** Don't count on acclimatizing. If your crossing lets you stage the trip, do real altitude training beforehand. Even then, carry bottled oxygen with a well-fitted mask.
- **Carry a pulse oximeter** and check it often. Hypoxia wrecks judgment before you notice it, and a visitor who feels fine may already be making poor decisions.
- **Know the signs of altitude illness**: headache, confusion, clumsiness, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough. If any appear, the treatment is to leave. This place has no "down" nearby unless your exit gives you one.
- If there is any chance the shelf is extremely high, consult someone who understands pressure suits before you go. A sky with stars in it at noon is not something to be casual about.

### The cold and the dryness
Nothing freezes here because there is no water. The cold is "clean" and "mineral," which sounds gentle but isn't. The dry air pulls moisture from your lungs and skin with every breath.

- **Bring every drop of water you will need, plus a reserve.** You will find none at all: no snow to melt, no ice, no condensation. Insulate your bottles so they don't freeze, since your water will be the only water on the mountain.
- **Wear layered cold-weather clothing**, but choose the fabrics carefully (see Section 3).
- Carry **lip balm and moisturizer**, and consider a heat-and-moisture-exchanging face covering while you're on the shelf.
- Expect nosebleeds and cracked lips.

### The sun
Thin air filters little sunlight. Even with no snow to reflect it, ultraviolet exposure up here will be severe.

- Wear **glacier glasses or goggles with side shields**. Photokeratitis, a sunburn of the eye, is a real risk.
- Cover every patch of skin, or use high-SPF sunscreen. Be aware that some sunscreens contain oils, and you'll want to keep oils away from your oxygen equipment.

### The ground
The granite slabs are door-sized, fractured, and rounded at their edges. They are stable, but the gaps between them can snap an ankle. Wear stiff boots and watch your footing. A twisted ankle is an emergency where there is no rescue and no water.

## 2. The Chamber Atmosphere: The Real Danger

The draft from the adit smells pleasantly of pine and turpentine. Don't let that reassure you. The vent releases **hydrocarbon vapor**: volatile oils rising steadily from a deep reservoir. That brings three hazards at once.

### Asphyxiation
The vapor is described as heavy. Heavy vapor pushes out breathable air, and it will pool in low places. Your air is already thin at this altitude, so the chamber may hold even less oxygen than the shelf outside.

- **Do not breathe the chamber air.** A charcoal filter mask is not enough. Cartridge masks remove contaminants, but they don't add oxygen, and a filter mask in an oxygen-poor room just helps you pass out comfortably.
- Use **supplied air**: your bottled oxygen through a sealed full-face mask, or better still a closed-circuit rebreather.
- **Never lean over the vent.** One lungful from the column itself could knock you down, and the bell-mouth is polished smooth by ages of abrasion. It is a hole a stride across, and falling in would be permanent.

### Narcosis and toxicity
Turpentine-like vapors cause headache, dizziness, nausea, euphoria, and confusion. Combined with hypoxia, the result is a mind that feels calm and clear while making terrible choices. That euphoria may be part of why the place feels so profound.

- A full-face mask also protects your **eyes**, which the vapor will sting.
- Accept that you will **smell very little** through the mask. The turpentine-and-green perfume is the one sensation you'll have to mostly give up. A brief, controlled sniff near the adit mouth, where the draft has mixed with outside air, is the safest compromise. Do it only with a partner watching you.

### Fire and explosion
This is the hazard that matters most, because it threatens you and the whole chamber at once.

The room is packed with vapor. The plants are made of resin, which is essentially solid fuel. There is a dynamo in the room and a hot filament overhead. This has never ignited in all its long history, probably because the mix sits outside the range where it can burn: too rich near the vent, too starved of oxygen elsewhere. **You are the thing that could upset that balance.**

- **Bring no flame of any kind**: no lighters, matches, stoves, or cigarettes.
- **Bring no ordinary electronics.** Phones, cameras, and headlamps can produce small sparks in switches and batteries. If you must carry a light or camera, use gear certified for explosive atmospheres ("intrinsically safe"), or leave it outside the frame.
- **Watch for static.** Dry air and synthetic fabrics make sparks. Wear natural fibers (wool, cotton canvas, leather) or anti-static workwear. Touch the iron frame on your way in to discharge yourself.
- **Avoid hobnails, crampons, or steel-shod boots.** Steel striking granite or the iron rails can throw sparks. Wear rubber-soled boots.
- **Treat your oxygen system with great care. This point is critical.** Oil and resin in contact with pressurized oxygen can ignite spontaneously. The chamber is full of airborne oil, and the plants are tacky with resin.
  - Keep all fittings and regulators capped and sealed.
  - Never connect or disconnect oxygen equipment inside the chamber or passage.
  - Swap bottles only on the shelf, after wiping every fitting clean of resin with oil-free materials.
  - Never let a leaking oxygen line hiss into that room. You would be enriching a fuel-filled space with exactly what it lacks.
- **Afterward, remember your clothes are soaked in fuel.** They will hold turpentine vapor for days. Don't approach a campfire, stove, or spark until you have changed and aired your gear.

## 3. The Noise

The three-part chord of vent, turbine, and dynamo presses on the chest and drowns out anything short of a shout. It never varies. After a while it stops sounding like sound and starts feeling like pressure.

- **Wear hearing protection**: earplugs plus earmuffs if they fit over your mask straps. Hours in steady, loud noise can permanently damage hearing, however soft and blended it seems.
- **Agree on hand signals before entering**: *OK*, *problem*, *low oxygen*, *leave now*, *stop moving*, *look here*. Agree that any squeeze on the arm means *turn around and follow me out, no discussion.*
- **Rope up.** Use a light tether between partners, or a line back to someone stationed at the frame. In the amber gloom, among knee-deep growth, with no speech and sluggish judgment, losing each other is easy.
- **Expect aftereffects.** When you step onto the silent shelf, your body will keep bracing for the chord. You may feel unsteady, hear a phantom hum, or have ringing ears. Sit down and give it time. Don't try to navigate difficult rock right away.

## 4. The Growth: Handling a Living Fabric

### Your safety
- The plants are dry, cool, and tacky, and they bleed amber oil when broken. Turpentine and resins irritate skin and can cause **lasting allergies** (resin allergy is well documented). Wear **gloves and full sleeves**, and assume anything that touches the growth will come out sticky.
- The growth is knee-deep and hides the floor. The **rails** vanish into it a few steps in, and the tipped **ore cart** sits half-buried near the entrance. Slide your feet rather than stepping high, and probe ahead if you can.
- The adit's mouth is a frame about chest height. **Expect to stoop**, and be careful with your mask and oxygen hoses on the iron edges.

### Its safety
This is what makes Kessel Col different from an ordinary hazardous cave. **Nothing regrows.** A broken frond seals itself with a bead of amber, and the gap stays exactly that size forever. Every step you take through the chamber leaves a permanent mark in something that has been unchanged for ages.

- **Decide beforehand how far in you will go.** The honest recommendation is to stop at the edge of the chamber, where the passage opens. From there you can see the machine, the bulb, and the tiers of growth. The center, the vent, and the dynamo's inscription are all dangerous for you and costly for the room.
- If you do go further, **follow a single path in and out** and keep it narrow. Don't wander. Let the growth close behind you, and go back the same way.
- Don't pick fronds as souvenirs. Don't test the amber bleed "just once." Somewhere a record exists of everyone who did.

## 5. The Light

The chamber's only light is the orange carbon bulb at the apex of the ceiling. Every upper leaf is angled toward it with "dreadful precision." It may be this ecology's sun.

- **Your own light is a second sun.** A bright white beam sweeping across angled leaves is an event the place has never experienced. Nobody knows what it would do, and the principle is to find out by not doing it.
- **Let your eyes adapt.** Wait several minutes inside the passage before entering the chamber. The amber dimness will slowly open up, and you'll see far more than you expect.
- If you truly need light, use a **dim red lamp** that is certified safe for explosive atmospheres. Keep it pointed at your feet and switch it off whenever you stop.
- **Never try to reach, touch, or climb toward the bulb.** A cracked envelope would expose a white-hot filament to the vapor.

---

## 6. The Machine

The turbine, dynamo, and cable are about as old as anything humans make, and they are still running.

- **The turbine blades are behind a grille for a reason.** Keep your fingers, sleeves, straps, and hoses well clear.
- **The dynamo is live.** Its insulation is old cloth, and the cable is stapled along rock that is furred with oily growth. Don't touch the cable. If you want to read the inscription on the housing, *It keeps itself.*, which can be read by touch, do it only with a gloved hand, on the enamel above the nameplate, and only after you've confirmed where the terminals and wiring are. Better still, let your eyes read it in the dark.
- **Don't slow, stop, or adjust anything.** You'd be the first change in a system whose whole identity is that it doesn't change, and you have no idea what depends on that constancy, including possibly the fact that the room hasn't exploded.

## 7. The Traces, and a Quiet Warning

Look closely at what the miners left. An ore cart has been "planted," its interior filled with rosettes. A coat has been colonized, with green threads coming out of its buttonholes. A ledger records the same number shift after shift. Hooks, rails, cart, and coat are all still here, and the people are not.

Nothing in the description says the chamber is dangerous to the living beyond its air. But my intuition says this is a place that **absorbs what stays in it.** Objects set down are slowly taken into the fabric. A person who lingers, dulled by vapor, hypoxic, lulled by the unchanging chord and the unmoving light, might find it very easy to sit down among the soft bronze tiers "just for a minute." The ledger reads like someone keeping watch, then understanding, then perhaps accepting.

- **Set a hard time limit before you go in**, and enforce it with a mechanical vibrating timer (no spark-prone electronics). Twenty to thirty minutes in the chamber is plenty. You will lose track of time; everyone does in a room with no change to mark it.
- **Put nothing down.** Don't lean your pack against the wall. Don't rest your gloves on the cart. Anything you set down may be kept.
- **Don't take the ledger page.** Read it if you must, in place, and fold it back into the pocket exactly as you found it. It belongs to the room now, and in a sense so does whoever wrote it.
- **Have a partner who stays outside**, on oxygen and on the shelf, with the authority to haul on the rope if you overstay your time or stop answering signals.

---

## 8. Being a Guest

You are the only source of water, carbon dioxide, dust, microbes, heat, and motion that this room has known in ages. It is cleaner than any air on the surface precisely because its leaves have caught every mote that ever drifted in.

- **Clean your gear** before you come: brush your boots and clothing free of soil, seeds, spores, and dust.
- Your mask's **exhalation** releases warm, humid air. With a rebreather you give off almost nothing, which is the gentlest choice. With an open-circuit mask, keep your visit brief.
- **Carry out everything**, including any wrappers, tape, or scraps. If you're unsure whether something has fallen, check before you leave the chamber, not after.

---

## 9. Packing List

**Life support**
- Bottled oxygen or closed-circuit rebreather, oil-free and oxygen-clean, with spares
- Sealed full-face mask (also protects eyes from vapor)
- Pulse oximeter
- All the water you need plus a reserve, in insulated bottles
- High-calorie food that needs no cooking

**Protection**
- Layered natural-fiber or anti-static clothing; no synthetic outer layers
- Rubber-soled, non-sparking boots
- Nitrile gloves under leather or canvas work gloves
- Glacier goggles or glasses for the shelf
- Earplugs plus earmuffs
- A sunscreen and lip balm that won't contaminate oxygen fittings (and no application near them)

**Navigation and safety**
- Light climbing rope or tether, with a partner staying outside
- Mechanical vibrating timer
- Pre-agreed hand signals
- An optional dim red light, intrinsically safe
- Oil-free wipes for cleaning fittings before any oxygen changeover

**Leave behind**
- Lighters, matches, stoves
- Ordinary phones, cameras, headlamps, and anything with a battery that isn't rated for explosive atmospheres
- Crampons, hobnails, steel tips
- Anything you'd be tempted to leave as a token

---

## 10. After You Leave

- **Change clothes on the shelf** or at your first safe stop, and bag the chamber clothes. They are fuel-soaked and will smell of turpentine for days. Keep them away from heat and flame.
- **Wash exposed skin** and watch for rashes over the next few days, which can signal a developing resin allergy.
- Expect a **headache, lingering phantom hum, and dryness** in your nose and eyes. Rehydrate aggressively.
- Expect something harder to name. Visitors to places that don't change often report a strange grief afterward, or a sense that the ordinary world is too noisy, too shifting, too wet. The body keeps expecting the chord. Give yourself time.

The room will not notice you've gone. It didn't need you, and it keeps itself. The best visit is one that leaves it exactly as full, exactly as quiet in its roaring, and exactly as unchanged as you found it.
