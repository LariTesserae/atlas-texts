# Preparing for the Held Summit

## The One Thing to Understand First

The Held Summit is two lethal environments stacked on top of each other, joined by a doorway a hand's breadth wide.

The outside is the death zone: air at perhaps a third of sea-level pressure, cold that freezes exposed skin in minutes, ultraviolet light that burns eyes and skin, and a silence you will find disturbing. The inside is a sealed, windless, saturated tropical greenhouse: survivable and even gentle, but relentless in its own way. The doorway between them is the most dangerous place on the mountain. You will pass through it twice, and **the second crossing, going out, is the one that kills people.** Arrival is dramatic but mostly benign. Departure is where you will be tired, soaked, heat-softened, stripped of your altitude acclimatization, and full of dissolved nitrogen, stepping into air that can knock you unconscious in minutes.

Plan the whole trip around the exit.

---

## If Your Mode of Travel Allows It, Arrive and Leave From Inside

If your multiverse transit lets you choose your arrival and departure points, set both inside the Hold, well away from the rim. Doing this removes most of the lethal risk in this guide. You skip the high-altitude climb, both pressure transitions, the freeze-thaw clothing problem, and decompression sickness. The rest of this guide assumes you can't do that, or that you want to see the Approach and the Curtains with your own eyes, which is understandable.

Even if you can portal in and out, read the section on living inside. The jungle has its own ways of hurting you.

---

## Part One: Preparing Your Body

You need two incompatible kinds of fitness: tolerance of extreme altitude and tolerance of saturated heat. Train for both.

**Altitude.** Treat the approach as a serious Himalayan expedition, because it is one. The description (indigo sky at noon, air too thin to carry sound, water that boils as it leaves the boundary) suggests an altitude at or beyond the highest peaks on Earth. You need:
- Weeks of staged acclimatization on the lower slopes, climbing high and sleeping low.
- Supplemental oxygen for the final ascent and, more importantly, for the exit.
- A medical screening that rules out heart problems, poorly controlled asthma, and sickle-cell trait.
- Experience, or a guide with experience, in high-altitude mountaineering. This is not a trek.

**Heat.** Do ten to fourteen days of heat acclimatization before departure. Daily sessions of exercise in a hot, humid room or sauna will expand your plasma volume, make you sweat earlier and more efficiently, and lower the salt content of your sweat. This helps a great deal inside the Hold. Heat acclimatization also offers some cross-protection at altitude.

**Ears and sinuses.** You will go through a pressure change of roughly two-thirds of an atmosphere in a single step, in each direction. That is more abrupt than an aircraft decompression. Do not attempt the crossing with a cold, sinus infection, recent dental work (trapped air under fillings or crowns can cause severe pain), or a history of ear-drum problems. Practice equalizing (the Valsalva and Toynbee maneuvers) until they are reflexive.

**Feet.** Toughen your feet and learn proper wet-foot care before you go. Inside, nothing ever dries, and trench foot and fungal infection are the most likely things to end your visit early.

## Part Two: The Ascent

The climb is ordinary in the sense that Everest is ordinary: known dangers, well documented. Some notes specific to this mountain:

- **Avoid routes beneath the Curtains.** The frozen outflow cascades grow continuously and must shed ice. Columns hundreds of meters tall, in harsh sun, will calve. Do not climb, camp, or rest in their fall zone.
- **Watch the outflow points.** Where streams leave the Hold, water boils and freezes at once. Expect spray, ice pellets, and sheet ice on the rock below.
- **Use the Whistles for navigation.** The ice-crystal banners streaming from the leak points can be seen from far off and mark the rim. You cannot hear them from outside, since sound does not cross, but you can see them.
- **Protect your eyes.** The violet-edged sun is brutal. Glacier glasses with side shields and high-SPF sunscreen on every exposed surface are mandatory. Snow blindness near the rim would leave you unable to find the boundary or your cache.

## Part Three: Crossing In

You will feel it before you see it: warmth on your face, then the smell of rot and flowers. Stop there, on the frozen side, and prepare.

**Establish your cache first. This is the most important step of the whole expedition.** Choose a spot a few meters outside the boundary, in the cold. Mark it with something visible from inside, such as a tall wand with a bright flag, since you will be looking out through the boundary when you return. Leave there, in a sealed and weighted bag:
- A complete dry set of your warm layers: base layer, insulation, down suit or parka, hat, gloves, and a second pair of boots or liners if you can manage it.
- A full oxygen cylinder, mask, and regulator.
- A dry, highly absorbent towel.
- Emergency food and a stove.

Outside, in the dry cold, these things will stay dry indefinitely. **Do not bring your cold-weather insulation inside unless it is sealed in waterproof bags.** Cold gear entering saturated hot air instantly collects condensation, the way a cold bottle sweats on a summer day. Your down will be soaked within minutes and will not dry.

**Record the location.** Take a GPS fix, compass bearings, and photographs. Note which Anchor and which Whistle are nearest. From inside, the rim can look much the same for kilometers, and your compass may not be reliable (see below).

**Strip before you cross.** Get down to lightweight, quick-drying clothes. You will go from deadly cold to a hot greenhouse in one stride. Arriving in a down suit invites heat stroke.

**Bag your electronics and optics before crossing.** Cameras, lenses, phones, and glasses are cold and will fog inside and out. Keep them sealed in dry bags with desiccant for twenty to thirty minutes after entering, until they reach the inside temperature.

**The crossing itself:** equalize your ears as you step. Expect a hard pop, a flood of thick air into your chest, and a sudden wall of noise. Many people feel euphoric; after days of oxygen starvation, sea-level air is intoxicating. Some feel dizzy or nauseous, or get a sudden headache as blood vessels that were dilated for altitude react to rich oxygen. Sit down, drink, and wait ten minutes before moving. Do not let the euphoria make you careless.

## Part Four: Living Inside

### Heat and Humidity: The Main Danger

The Hold is hot, completely saturated, and completely still. Those three facts together are a serious threat to a human body.

You cool yourself mainly by evaporating sweat. At 100% humidity, sweat does not evaporate. It pours off you uselessly and you keep heating up. With no wind, there is no convective cooling either. And the rock beneath you radiates heat from below.

- **Carry a wet-bulb thermometer, or a thermometer and hygrometer from which you can calculate wet-bulb temperature.** In saturated air, wet-bulb and air temperature are nearly the same. If it reads above about 31°C, a healthy acclimatized person can only rest, not work. Near 35°C, even resting in shade becomes fatal over hours. "Greenhouse in August" may well fall in this range, especially low in the basin near the steaming pools.
- **Move slowly.** Plan for a quarter of your normal daily distance. Twenty kilometers across sounds small. In this place it is a long way.
- **Don't lie on bare rock.** It is warmer than your blood and will push heat into you. Sleep in a hammock.
- **Know where the cool places are:**
  - Under the drip-threads and the Lid's rain. The drops condense on the ceiling, which means the ceiling is cooler than the air, so the rain may be noticeably cooler than everything around it. Standing under a steady drip-line may be your best way to cool off.
  - **At the boundary itself.** Extending a hand or forearm through into the outside cold is a powerful emergency cooling method. Do it in short intervals of seconds, not minutes, because frostbite is real on the other side. A partner should time you.
  - A phase-change or ice-pack cooling vest can be recharged by hanging it outside the boundary on a line until it freezes. That trick alone may save your trip.
- **Watch for heat illness** in yourself and your partners: confusion, stumbling, a stopped sweat response, irritability, headache. Confusion is the dangerous sign, because a person with heat stroke usually cannot tell they have it.
- A small battery fan offers some relief even in saturated air, since any moving air helps a little.

### Salt and Water

- **The rain from the Lid is condensate, essentially distilled water.** Catching it directly, from a drip-thread before it touches leaves, may give you the cleanest water in the basin. Filter it anyway.
- **Do not drink the mineral water from the terraces untreated.** Geothermal water often carries arsenic, heavy metals, and very high dissolved mineral loads that ordinary filters don't remove. The steaming pools may also be scalding in places. Test the temperature before touching.
- **Do not drink from the bromeliad cups.** Each one is its own small ecosystem.
- **Take electrolytes.** This is critical. You will sweat liters a day, and if you replace that with nearly distilled water you risk dangerous low blood sodium, which can look like heat illness. Bring plenty of oral rehydration salts and salty food.

### The Air Itself

The air stays in the Hold. It has been sealed for a very long time over a volcanically warm mountain, with no wind to stir it.

- **Carry a four-gas monitor** (oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide). Volcanic gases, especially carbon dioxide, are heavier than air and pool in low places when nothing mixes them. Be especially wary of the deep drip-wells, gorges, hollows, and the enclosed spaces inside the fig-cathedrals. If the alarm sounds, climb upward immediately.
- **Pollen season:** if the lower basin has turned gold with sedge pollen, allergic people should consider staying higher or turning back. Masks become soaked quickly, so bring several and rotate them. Antihistamines and an inhaler belong in the kit regardless.

### Wet: Skin and Feet

Assume nothing you own will ever be dry again while you are inside.

- **Feet:** wear quick-draining, non-waterproof shoes. Waterproof boots only trap sweat. Bring many pairs of thin synthetic socks. Every evening, strip your feet, dry them as well as you can, powder them with antifungal powder, and sleep with them bare and elevated. Check them twice a day for whitening, wrinkling, pain, or numbness.
- **Skin:** use antifungal cream in the groin, armpits, and between the toes from the first day, not after problems start. Wear loose clothing, and use anti-chafe balm.
- **Drying trick:** freeze-dry your gear at the rim. Pass wet socks and clothes through the boundary on a line or pole and leave them in the cold, thin, sunny air. The water will freeze and then sublimate away, as it does on high-altitude expeditions. Retrieve them, let them warm (they will get damp again from condensation), and wear them while they are at their driest. Do the same for any gear you need truly dry.
- **Electronics:** keep them in sealed dry bags with desiccant. Expect some failures, and bring paper backups of maps and notes.

### Noise

Under the canopy, the chorus is often too loud to talk at a normal volume, and it never stops.

- **Bring hearing protection,** foam earplugs or better. Continuous exposure at those levels can damage hearing over days, and it will ruin your sleep.
- **Plan how you will communicate.** Agree on hand signals with your partners before entering. Radios with earpieces worn under earplugs work well.
- **Low-frequency sound:** the hum of the Anchors and the deepest Whistle are felt as pressure in the chest. Infrasound is known to cause unease, nausea, a sense of being watched, and dread. If you feel inexplicably uneasy near the Anchors, that is probably the cause. It doesn't make you less uneasy, but it may help to know.

### Navigation

- **Your compass may be wrong.** Old basalt is often magnetized, and whatever the Anchors are, they may add distortions. Test your compass against a known bearing when you enter.
- **GPS may fail** beneath the Lid. Don't depend on it.
- **The sun won't help.** Light is diffused through the pearled ceiling and gives no clear direction.
- **Navigate by landmarks instead:** the black towers, the great eastern pools, the drip-threads (which never move, making them excellent permanent landmarks), the Whistles (each at its own pitch, audible from far inside the basin, like a sound compass once you learn them), the gray scar of dead forest in the northwest, and the Anchors along the rim.
- Keep a written log of every landmark and bearing. Walking downhill will always lead you toward the pools. Walking uphill will always lead you to the rim, but not necessarily to your cache. Once at the rim, you can walk along it until you find your flagged wand.

### Terrain

The softness of this landscape is deceptive.

- **The drip-wells** are perfectly round holes, some deeper than a house, often screened by vegetation and slick-edged. Probe ahead with a trekking pole where the ground is overgrown, especially near a drip-thread.
- **Travertine is slick** where algae grows and sharp where freshly built. **Do not walk on terrace rims.** Every rim took centuries or millennia to grow, a fraction of a millimeter at a time, and a single boot can break it. It will not be repaired within the lifetime of anyone now alive. Walk on established soil and rock.
- **The towers are soaked, moss-covered honeycombs.** Don't climb them. The moss peels away, and the rock underneath is running with water.
- **Near the northwest scar,** where the boundary sagged after an Anchor fell, the forest is dead and falling. Expect deadfall, unstable ground, and possibly abrupt cold pockets. Cross it quickly, or not at all.

### Shelter

- A hammock with a built-in insect net and a large tarp is the best setup.
- The fig-cathedrals are beautiful but full of roosting flightless birds (droppings, noise, possible parasites) and may hold stagnant, gas-laden air. Check your gas monitor.
- The ruined huts are appealing. Treat them with respect (see below), and check for loose stones and for snakes, if the Hold has any.
- Fire is nearly impossible and unnecessary for warmth. Bring a stove for cooking, and keep in mind that a stove tuned for altitude will burn differently at inside pressure.

### Fauna and Flora

- **Insects are large and numerous.** Bring strong repellent, a head net, and permethrin-treated clothing. Assume there are leeches. Don't handle the big moths, beetles, or anything else you can't identify.
- **Frogs:** look, don't touch. Many rainforest frogs have toxic skin, and your hands carry things that could harm them.
- **Don't eat anything you find,** however ripe the fallen fruit smells. This is an ecosystem that has evolved in isolation. Assume every unfamiliar plant is toxic. Bring all your food, plus extra.
- **Be careful around the aquatic hunter** in the pools at dawn. It is probably shy, but it may bite if cornered.
- **Watch the glide-birds.** They fly in spirals over the thermals; the biggest are almost invisible against the ceiling. They are probably harmless, but you should notice them because they will likely be the most memorable thing you see.

### Night

It gets very dark under canopy and cloud and Lid. Carry a headlamp with a red mode. After twenty minutes with the white light off, your eyes will begin to see the faint ember-orange glow of the succulent on the towers, mapping the heat of the mountain across its skin, and the glowing snails carrying it about. It is worth keeping your eyes dark-adapted for this.

## Part Five: What You Must Not Do

### Biosecurity: You Are the Most Dangerous Animal Here

This is a sealed island in the sky, isolated for an unknown and very long time. It has no immunity to anything you bring.

- **Chytrid fungus (*Batrachochytrium*) has wiped out frog species across entire continents** on Earth. The Hold's chorus, the foundation of the whole place, could be silenced by spores on a boot. Before entering, scrub and disinfect your boots, trekking poles, and anything that has touched soil or water elsewhere. Freezing and drying help, and your gear will be very cold and dry on the approach, but don't rely on that alone.
- **Carry no seeds, soil, or plant material.** Inspect your velcro and pockets.
- **Don't carry animals, or food that could sprout.**
- **Don't bathe with soap or sunscreen in the pools.** Wash well away from the water.
- **Carry out all waste,** including human waste if you can manage it. Bring more waste bags than you think you need.

### Things Not to Touch

- **The Anchors.** Don't press them, cut them, climb them, sample them, or lean on them. They are what keeps the Hold in existence. When one fell, the jungle nearby died over fifty years. The surface gives slightly under pressure, and whether it responds to damage is unknown. Stand near one if you like and watch the standing-wave patterns in the water on its surface. Don't touch.
- **The black platform.** Nothing grows on it, and birds are uneasy on it. Take their unease as a warning. If you step onto it, do so briefly and for a reason, and don't sleep, camp, or leave anything there.
- **The instrument in the fig roots.** It has been ticking for centuries. Don't pry it free, open it, or take it. Record it, listen to it, and leave it.
- **The rope bridge.** Someone maintains it, recently. That means someone may be nearby, or comes and goes. Don't cut or modify the bridge. Don't trust it with your weight until you've tested it carefully, since crude repairs can fail.
- **The cairns and offerings.** Don't disturb them. If you want to leave something, consider a piece of glass or a stone brought from elsewhere and cleaned. Don't leave dried flowers or organic material that might carry seeds or spores.
- **The huts.** Don't move stones, and don't remove anything from the hearth.

### The People Who May Be Here

The fresh bridge, the recent offerings, and the child's shoe mean the Hold is not abandoned. Someone who reaches this place has survived the same deadly approach you did, or has a way around it that you don't know about, and has done so repeatedly over long spans of time. If you meet them, be quiet, patient, and respectful, and don't assume you understand the place better than they do. They have been doing what the traces suggest: paying attention without changing much. Follow their lead.

## Part Six: The Edge

The rim will probably be the most memorable place of your visit. Some notes:

- **The silence outside is not just an experience; it's a hazard.** Stepping out cuts the chorus off as if by a knife. People are disoriented by it. Before you step out for any reason, look for your landmark and your cache wand, and know exactly what you'll do.
- **Don't stand in a Whistle's jet.** Air leaking at high speed may be strong enough to carry ice crystals or debris, and the pressure difference at the leak points is not something to put your face or ears into. Watch from the side.
- **The deepest Whistle** may make your chest feel strange. If you feel nauseous or anxious, step away from it.
- **The outflow streams** are where water boils and freezes at the same time. Stay well back.
- **The inward-leaning stunted trees** at the edge are the plants that have been flinching from the cold for longer than your civilization has existed. Leave them be.

## Part Seven: Leaving (Read This Twice)

By the time you leave, several things have changed in your body:

1. **You've lost acclimatization.** Days at sea-level pressure undo your breathing adaptations quickly, even though extra red blood cells persist for a while.
2. **Your tissues are saturated with nitrogen** at a full atmosphere. Going from one atmosphere to about a third in a single step is exactly how aviators and astronauts get decompression sickness, the bends. It can cause joint pain, skin mottling, a choking cough, and in severe cases neurological damage.
3. **You are soaked.** The instant you step into the thin, cold, dry air, water on your skin and clothes will evaporate violently and then freeze. That produces massive, sudden heat loss.
4. **You have minutes of useful consciousness** if you breathe unsupplemented air at this altitude without acclimatization.

### Exit Protocol

**One or two days before exit:**
- Stop heavy exertion. Rest, rehydrate, eat well, and treat any foot or skin problems.
- Walk to the rim near your cache, and find and confirm your flag wand from the inside.
- Freeze-dry your innermost layers and socks through the boundary.
- Retrieve your oxygen cylinder from the cache and bring it inside with its mask and regulator, sealed. Or, if you packed a second one inside, check it now.

**Exit day:**
- **Pre-breathe 100% oxygen for 30 to 60 minutes** while resting inside, just behind the boundary. This flushes nitrogen from your tissues, which is how astronauts and high-altitude pilots prevent the bends. Keep the mask on and don't exert yourself.
- Keep breathing oxygen through the crossing, and stay on it for the whole descent until well below the death zone.
- Strip to your thinnest, driest layer. Tie up or bag everything else you're carrying.
- Towel off as thoroughly as you can at the last moment.
- Have your partner ready. Equalize.
- **Step out and go straight to the cache.** Strip off the wet layer immediately; it will be stiffening with ice. Towel again. Pull on the dry cache layers: base layer, insulation, down, hat, gloves, boots. You should have rehearsed this changeover, in order, several times on the approach so you can do it in under three minutes with numb hands.
- **Get your eyes protected immediately.** The outside sun is intense, and your eyes will have adapted to soft light.
- **Move down.** Every hundred meters lower helps. Watch each other for confusion, excessive clumsiness, joint pain, cough, or skin mottling. Any of these could be the bends, cold injury, or altitude illness, and all of them require descending and breathing oxygen.

Budget time in your itinerary for re-acclimatizing lower down if your descent is long.

### Set Your Exit Date Before You Enter, and Keep It

The Hold invites people to stay. It is warm, soft, loud, and alive after the brutal climb, and everything outside seems unreal. But each day inside, you lose altitude acclimatization, your feet get worse, and your fitness slowly declines in the heat. The exit gets harder the longer you remain. Decide your turnaround in advance and hold to it.

## Part Eight: Preparing Your Mind

What the Hold will probably feel like, so it doesn't catch you unprepared:

**The first minutes** will be overwhelming: breath flooding back, sweat everywhere, a wall of sound, the smell of rot and flowers. Many people laugh or cry. That's normal.

**The first days** will bring constant low-level discomfort—never dry, never cool, never quiet—alongside intense beauty. The frog chorus rolling across the basin like wind through grass. The glide-birds vanishing into the ceiling. The silver threads of rain that fall in the same places forever.

**Your sense of time will shift.** There is no weather, no season, no directional sun. The ticking instrument, the Anchors thickening by a fingernail's width in a lifetime, the tree that flowers once every hundred and nine years—the Hold operates on timescales where your visit barely counts. People describe a peculiar stillness settling over them: a gentle, not entirely comfortable sense of being very small and very brief. Keep a watch, keep a log, and keep to your schedule; the timelessness is part of what makes lingering dangerous.

**The traces will affect you.** The hearth in a ruined hut. The cairn with a child's shoe. You will wonder who came here, how, why, and whether they left. It is fine not to know. Nobody else does either.

**The exit will feel like a small death.** The chorus cut off at a line. The cold, the silence, the black sky. Many visitors say the hardest part is looking back through the boundary at a place that is still steaming, singing, and weeping exactly as it was, and exactly as it will be long after they're gone.

## Packing Summary

**For the approach and outside:**
- Full high-altitude mountaineering kit, including a down suit or parka system
- Supplemental oxygen: cylinders, masks, and regulators, with enough for the ascent, the exit pre-breathe, and the descent
- Glacier glasses and high-SPF sunscreen
- Altitude medications, as advised by a physician (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine)
- Cache bag: waterproof, weighted, and flagged with a tall wand
- Complete duplicate dry layers for the cache
- Disinfectant for boots and gear

**For inside:**
- Light, quick-drying, loose clothing
- Quick-draining shoes and many pairs of thin synthetic socks
- Hammock with insect net, and a tarp
- Wet-bulb thermometer or thermometer plus hygrometer
- Four-gas monitor
- Water filter and purification tablets
- Large supply of oral rehydration salts, and salty food
- All food, plus extra
- Earplugs and radios with earpieces
- Antifungal powder and cream, anti-chafe balm
- Strong insect repellent, head net, permethrin-treated clothing
- Antihistamines, inhaler, and several masks
- Headlamp with red mode
- Cooling vest (recharge by freezing outside the boundary)
- Small battery fan
- A line or pole for passing gear through the boundary to freeze-dry
- Dry bags and desiccant for all electronics and optics
- Compass (verify it on entry), and paper maps and notebook
- Trekking poles for probing hidden drip-wells
- Waste bags, more than you think you need

**For the mind:**
- An exit date, written down, and a partner who will hold you to it

---

Go slowly. Touch little. Leave nothing living behind. And remember: the jungle is the part of the Hold that will let you live. The doorway is the part that decides whether you do.
