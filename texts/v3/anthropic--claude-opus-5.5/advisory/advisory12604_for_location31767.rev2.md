# Preparing to Visit the Settling Basin

## A Traveler's Briefing

### First, the Feeling

Before the checklists, here is what my intuition says this place will be like.

The Basin is not hostile the way a volcano or a vacuum is hostile. It is *indifferent*, in the specific way of industrial infrastructure. Nobody built it for you. Nobody built it for the fish either. It exists to carry heat away from somewhere you'll never see, and everything living in it, the crews included, is a tolerated side effect.

The experience will be dominated by three things. The first is **sound you cannot escape**. The second is **space you cannot comprehend**: kilometers of empty blue with a lit column fading into haze somewhere in the distance. The third is **newness**. Sharp edges, bright paint, uncured tar, raw concrete. It's like visiting a cathedral the week the scaffolding came down, except the cathedral is flooded, it hums, and there are pike in it.

You will feel very small, very cold, and slightly nauseous most of the time. If you prepare well, you may also feel something like awe. A cathedral is mostly air, and this place is mostly water, lit from every direction by lamps that are keeping time for a sun that never existed.

Now, how not to die.

## Part One: The Question That Decides Everything

### What pressure is it, really?

Get this answer before you go, and don't trust the description to have given it to you.

The Basin is a sealed, water-filled chamber 400 meters tall. Under ordinary hydrostatics, the floor sits at least **~40 atmospheres higher in pressure than the ceiling**, however the ceiling is pressurized. The ceiling itself lies below "whatever surface this place is below," fed by intake shafts coming down from above. So the true floor pressure is 40 atmospheres *plus* an unknown amount. You should treat it as the equivalent of a **400 to 600+ meter ocean dive**.

That has consequences:

- **No ordinary human can breathe ambient-pressure gas at the floor and walk away.** Air becomes narcotic, then toxic, long before 400 m. Even professional saturation divers breathing helium-oxygen mixes hit High Pressure Nervous Syndrome at these depths: tremors, nausea, confusion, microsleeps. The deepest open-water human dives on record are in this range, and they involved weeks of compression and decompression under medical supervision.
- **The brass-and-canvas divers are a warning sign, not a reassurance.** In our physics, a traditional hard-hat suit is good to perhaps 60 meters. If the locals genuinely work at the pillar bases in such gear, then one of three things is true. The local physics differs, or the local people differ, or the description is wrong about where they work. You do not know which. Do not assume their exemption extends to your body.
- **Ask whether the "pressurized" workshops are at one atmosphere or at ambient.** The text says they are the only places where you can take off the helmet. That strongly suggests they're **saturation habitats at ambient pressure**, meaning the crews may effectively *live* at depth. If you enter one from a one-atmosphere crawler cab, you will be compressed. Leaving afterward would mean **roughly a week or more of decompression** in a chamber. That is not a side trip. It's a commitment.

### Your realistic options, in order of safety

1. **One-atmosphere crawler cab.** The crawler-trains have pressurized cabs with thick portholes. If "pressurized" means held at surface pressure, this is your best seat in the house: warm (ish), dry, breathable, and moving along the grid past pillars, bollards, and fish. You see nearly everything you'd want to see, and you get off without decompression.
2. **Bathysphere or diving bell at one atmosphere.** The description mentions people coming down in bathyspheres to check gauges. Hanging mid-column in a sphere, lamps on, is the way to experience the Basin's defining emptiness safely.
3. **Atmospheric diving suit (a "hardsuit").** This is a rigid, articulated armor suit that keeps you at one atmosphere inside. Modern ones are rated to 300 to 600 m. It is the *only* sane way to be "outside" at floor depth. It's heavy, awkward, and demands training, but it's survivable. Get one rated with margin above your best estimate of floor pressure, and confirm your universe's version works in theirs.
4. **Ambient-pressure saturation diving.** Only for travelers with professional saturation training, a medical team, a compression and decompression plan, and weeks to spare. Not recommended for an ordinary visitor. I mention it only so you know what you'd be signing up for if a friendly crew says, "Come on into the workshop, we'll get you out of that suit."

**If you remember one thing:** treat every pressure boundary (a hatch, a lock, a "just step through here") as a decision you must understand before you make it.

## Part Two: Arrival

Multiverse travel has one failure mode that matters above all others here: **where you materialize.**

- **Never arrive in open water.** Arriving unprotected at the floor is instant, catastrophic injury. Arriving unprotected mid-column is only slightly slower death in 8°C water, far from any structure.
- **Ideal arrival point:** a dry, one-atmosphere space. The crawler cab is best. A ceiling-side maintenance space, if one exists at lower pressure, is second best. Even arriving inside a sealed hardsuit or bathysphere you've pre-positioned is better than improvising.
- **Arrange contact first if you possibly can.** The Basin is run from above by people with gauges, telegraph lines, and schedules. The crews ride out for days at a time. An unexplained stranger in a crawler cab is a safety problem, a supply problem (air, food, CO₂ scrubbing), and possibly an administrative crisis. Be expected.
- **Know your exit before you enter.** Where is the nearest intake station? The nearest workshop? How long by crawler? A week to cross the Basin means that if something goes wrong in the middle, help is *days* away.

## Part Three: Cold

The water is about 8°C in open reaches and colder near the floor. Fresh water is densest near 4°C, and that is probably what's pooling in the "quieter layer" at the bottom.

- **In a cab or hardsuit:** the hull conducts cold relentlessly. Expect cold surfaces, condensation on portholes, and cold feet. Pack thermal base layers, wool, insulated boots, and a hat. Sit away from the hull when you sleep.
- **Condensation matters.** Damp cabs mean damp clothes, damp bedding, and fogged portholes. Bring moisture-wicking fabrics, spare socks, and anti-fog wipes for any viewports and lenses you control.
- **If you ever end up wet at ambient conditions,** hypothermia is quick at 8°C. Functional loss comes within tens of minutes. That's another reason not to be outside without a dry, insulated, one-atmosphere barrier.
- **Warm zones aren't safe zones.** Near the outflow manifolds, the radiator fins could be dangerously hot to the touch, and the water beside them could be far warmer than the open reaches. Temperature gradients near them may be sharp. Don't rest a suit limb on a radiator bank.

## Part Four: Sound

This is the hazard most visitors will underestimate, because it doesn't look like anything.

### What you're dealing with

- **The hum** is a constant, low, whole-body tone. Low-frequency noise and infrasound are known to cause nausea, anxiety, fatigue, a sense of pressure in the head and chest, and a vague feeling of dread.
- **The intake stations** are loud enough that visitors report eyeballs vibrating in their sockets and vision blurring. That suggests intense low-frequency energy in the range where body cavities and organs resonate.
- **Transients** include rivets snapping "like pistol shots," ceiling plates shrieking for minutes, and brakes squealing.
- **You can't block it the normal way.** In water, and in any hull or hardsuit coupled to water, sound reaches you through **bone conduction**. Earplugs help with the airborne portion inside a cab, but the hum will come up through your skull, your teeth, and the soles of your feet.

### How to prepare

- **Get a baseline hearing test before you go** so you can tell afterward whether you've been damaged.
- **Bring layered hearing protection:** foam plugs *plus* over-ear muffs for cab time. Wear them *always* near intakes and walls. They won't fix bone conduction, but they cut what can be cut.
- **Anti-nausea medication.** Ask a physician about options for motion sickness and vestibular disturbance. You'll have the eleven-second surge plus infrasound plus a moving train. Test the medication at home first so you know its side effects.
- **Padding.** Anything that decouples your body from the hull helps: foam sleeping pads, cushioned seating, soft-soled shoes. Sleep in a hammock if the crew permits it.
- **Stay away from the walls** unless you have a very good reason. Sound concentrates there. Standing waves in the corners are strong enough to shake loose settling debris, and they'll do the same to you and your equipment. Even the shoals avoid the walls. Take the hint from the fish.
- **Avoid the intake stations** beyond what's necessary to reach a workshop. If you do go, limit your time, stay inside the thickest structure available, and keep your mouth slightly open. It can reduce the "rattling" sensation in the sinuses and ears.
- **Learn the sounds.** Half of the psychological strain here comes from not knowing what a noise means. Ask the crew to teach you, and learn to tell these apart:
  - the eleven-second pump stroke (normal, reassuring)
  - a ceiling plate moaning (normal, unnerving)
  - a rivet snap (normal-ish)
  - a crawler brake (normal)
  - a pressure valve venting forty kilometers away (normal, whale-like, beautiful)
  - **anything the crew reacts to** (not normal: watch their faces)

## Part Five: The Water Itself

### Fresh water, not sea

Fresh water is about 2.5% less dense than seawater. Any ballast, buoyancy, or trim calibrated for ocean use will be **off**. You will sink more readily than expected. If you bring your own hardsuit, bathysphere, or instruments, recalibrate for fresh water.

### New concrete is chemically aggressive

This detail won't show up in a romantic description, but it matters. **Freshly poured concrete leaches lime.** It can push nearby water toward strong alkalinity, especially in the newest, only-recently-flooded sections. Near the newest slabs, and in the sediment storms of "fine concrete dust," expect:

- water that may irritate skin and eyes badly, or burn them
- fine abrasive grit that scours seals, joints, and viewports
- dust that clogs filters, valves, and pumps

If any of that water gets on you, rinse with plenty of fresh, clean water. Protect your eyes above all. Treat the dust like wet cement, because chemically, that's close to what it is.

### The tar

The seam compound "weeps thin threads upward like smoke." It is uncured, it is coming out of the joints, and you don't know what it is. Assume it is:

- sticky: it will foul suit joints, gloves, and moving parts
- possibly toxic: industrial sealants often contain solvents and bituminous compounds
- impossible to clean off in the field

Keep your gear away from the seams, especially in warm zones where more of it is weeping. Carry an appropriate solvent and rags *for your equipment*, and use them inside, not on skin.

### Sharp edges

"A hand run along them would come away cut." The slab edges, bollards, fresh welds, and rivet heads are unweathered and sharp. Everything here is. If you're in a suit, protect hoses and fabric sections. If you're handling anything, wear cut-resistant gloves. A cut glove at depth isn't a scrape. It's a suit breach.

## Part Six: Weather

Yes, weather. It's slow, huge, and it can kill you.

### The eleven-second pulse

Every eleven seconds, the entire water mass shifts. In a cab, you'll feel it as a gentle rock. In a suit or a sphere, it's a surge that pushes you, then relaxes. It's regular, which makes it predictable:

- **Time your movements to it.** Divers everywhere learn to work with surge: brace on the push, move on the release. Count it. Eleven seconds. You'll be counting it in your sleep, possibly for weeks after you leave.
- **Anything loose will rock.** Secure your gear. Don't leave anything unsecured in the cab.
- **Tethers and hoses cycle-load** with every pulse. Check your attachment points for fatigue.

### Thermal plumes and currents

Near the manifolds, warm water rises in columns kilometers wide. Where plumes meet the cold floor layer, you get turbulence. In the open, you get currents that can run in one direction for weeks.

- A current of even a fraction of a knot, sustained, will carry a free-floating sphere or suit a very long way in a place where the nearest landmark may be three kilometers off and invisible.
- **Never be un-tethered in the open water.**
- Plumes shimmer. Learn to recognize that shimmer and treat it as terrain.

### Sediment storms

These are brown walls of concrete dust that drift for days and can roll a hundred kilometers when released.

- Visibility drops toward zero. Lamps become dim smudges.
- The grit is abrasive and alkaline (see above).
- **The eels come out** during storms, hundreds of them, writhing up from the seams to feed.
- **In a crawler:** you may have to stop and wait it out. Pack for delay: extra food, water, medication, and entertainment that doesn't depend on light or quiet.
- **In a suit:** don't be outside when a storm arrives. If you are, get to structure, anchor, and wait. Don't try to navigate blind.

### Pump shift changes: the big one

When a turbine goes down or demand spikes above, **the entire circulation reorganizes**. Currents reverse. Warm zones migrate. Storms that have been building for weeks break loose.

- **Watch the fish.** They know hours in advance, from changes in the hum too fine for human ears. When the shoals break up and scatter for no visible reason, or eels begin rising without a storm, **something is about to change**. This is the most useful early-warning system in the Basin. Learn it.
- **Ask the crew to relay schedules from above.** The telegraph lines may carry advance notice of planned maintenance. Unplanned failures, though, arrive without warning except through the fish.
- **Build slack into your plans.** A day's outing may become three days of waiting.

## Part Seven: Navigation

### The core problem

The pillars are the only landmarks, and **from one pillar you cannot see the next**. Between them lies kilometers of featureless blue twilight, lit faintly from every direction. There is no shadow to orient by, no up-sun, no horizon.

### The psychological trap

Divers in open water with no visible reference experience something often called **"blue orb" disorientation**, or the break-off phenomenon. Up, down, and distance lose meaning, and panic or dissociation can follow. The Basin is this effect scaled up to an entire world. Mid-column, the floor is a pale grid dissolving into blue, and the nearest lit column is three kilometers off. For some people, that view will be transcendent. For others, it will be the worst moment of their lives. You won't know which you are until you're there.

**Prepare:**

- Practice in a controlled low-visibility or open-water environment beforehand if you can.
- In a sphere or suit, **keep a physical reference in view at all times**: a tether line, a depth gauge, an attitude indicator.
- Have a simple ritual for panic. Count the eleven-second pulses. Name the lamp colors: sodium yellow, mercury blue, the green where they overlap.

### Practical navigation

- **Follow the rails.** The crawler rail grid connects the pillars. It is your road. If you are ever outside and lost near the floor, finding a rail is finding a way home.
- **Pillar sound signatures.** Each pillar groans at its own pitch, and the pike use this. Crews may know them by ear. Ask. Even learning two or three is valuable.
- **Stenciled numbers.** The bollards are painted orange with white numbers. Get a map or list of the numbering scheme before you go. It's the closest thing the floor has to street addresses.
- **Inertial or dead-reckoning instruments** from your own universe may help, but magnetic compasses will be useless near all this steel and electrical plant. Don't rely on one.
- **Chalk marks.** Divers leave inscriptions on the concrete. Some may be navigational. Ask what they mean before you trust them.
- **Radio won't work.** Radio barely penetrates water. Communication here is by wire (telegraph, telephone along tethers) or by sound. Expect to be out of touch the moment you leave a wired connection.

## Part Eight: Light and Time

### The lamps

- Sodium and mercury-vapor lamps in thick glass housings. The light is industrial, factory-yard-at-night light, and colors will look strange. Reds and browns go murky under sodium. Faces look ill. That's normal.
- **Glass housings under pressure** are a latent hazard. A housing that fails at depth will **implode violently**, sending a shock pulse through the water. Don't linger right next to lamp strings in a suit. If a lamp string shorts out (one of the stated failure modes), keep clear of the repair until the crew says otherwise.
- **Mercury-vapor lamps** contain mercury. A broken one is a small but real contamination source. Don't handle debris.
- **Electrical cables** run beside the rails, feeding the trains. Treat every cable as live. Never touch, cut, or anchor to them.

### The schedule is the sun

Sixteen hours full light, then down to a third. Every living thing here keeps this time.

- **Shift your circadian rhythm before you arrive.** Start adapting several days ahead: bright light at the start of the Basin "day," dimness at its "night."
- **Bring a sleep mask.** A third-strength lamp-night is still bright.
- Expect poor sleep regardless. "Nobody sleeps well here." The hum, the pulse, the cold, and the plate-moans all see to that. Plan for cumulative fatigue. Build rest days into your schedule. Don't make important decisions, especially about pressure boundaries, while exhausted.

## Part Nine: The Living Things

In the Basin you are a large, noisy, lit, slow object. Most fish will avoid you. Some won't.

### Shoalers (small silver fish, huge schools)

Harmless. Spectacular. Riding a plume up in a sphere during lamp-day while a tornado of gold foil spirals around you may be the finest thing this place offers. The only hazards are visual, since they can obscure everything, and mechanical: dense schools near intake screens.

### Grazers (flat sucker-mouthed fish on the pillars)

Harmless to you. Their territorial drumming sounds like rain on a tin roof, and on a pillar they'll be the loudest nearby sound. They're a good sign: a healthy pillar has thousands. If you're in a suit near a pillar, they may investigate you or try to graze your hull. Let them.

### Pike (man-length, pale, heavy-jawed, ambush predators)

The real wildlife hazard.

- They hang motionless in the twilight between pillars. You won't see one until it moves.
- They strike at fast, flashing movement. **Avoid shiny, reflective equipment** outside a cab. Matte finishes on suits, tools, and tethers. No dangling bright objects.
- They're territorial. A pike may treat a slow-moving suit as an intruder. A man-length fish with a heavy jaw striking a hardsuit likely won't breach it, but it could damage hoses, lights, or joints. A strike on a fabric suit or an exposed tether could be serious.
- They navigate by pillar sound signatures. They're attuned to acoustics, so unusual noises from your equipment may attract or agitate them.
- If one shadows you, move steadily, don't flail, and head toward a pillar or crawler.

### Eels (thick grey floor-seam dwellers)

Sluggish most of the time. During sediment storms they emerge by the hundreds. They're probably not predatory toward something your size, but in near-zero visibility, hundreds of thick bodies rising around you could entangle equipment and cause real panic. **Another reason not to be outside in a storm.**

### Crustaceans (pale shrimp clouds)

Harmless, but dense clouds can **dim lamplight and clog intakes, filters, and pumps** on your equipment. Near the warm ceiling pools, they may be thick enough to blind you.

### The larger visitors

- A four-meter sturgeon-like fish has been seen feeding on eels at the floor. It's probably not interested in you, but it is heavy enough to knock a suit over by accident.
- **The long, slow shape patrolling the outer pillars.** Nobody has a photograph. I would not go looking for it. The description is written carefully: rarely seen, far reaches, never documented. In a place where help is days away, an unknown large animal is not a sightseeing opportunity. **Stay out of the outer dim zones.** If you must go there, go in a crawler, with the crew, and with lights.

### Plants

- **Pondweed near warm manifolds:** long streaming strands, an entanglement hazard for suit limbs and hoses, in an area that's also hot.
- **Kelp on bollards and ceiling chains:** leathery and tough, also an entanglement risk. It is **important young-fish habitat**. Don't cut it or disturb it without reason. The grazers fight over it. Let them have it.

## Part Ten: The People

The Basin wasn't built to be visited. It was built to be run. The people who come down are working. Be useful, be low-maintenance, or stay home.

### What to understand about the crews

- They ride out for **days at a time** in small pressurized cabs, cold and loud and cramped. They share air, food, and scrubber capacity. You'll be consuming all three.
- Their technology is roughly early-to-mid-twentieth-century: diesel-electric, brass, canvas, telegraph, hydraulic linkages. Your gear may look like sorcery, or like a liability. **Do not assume anything of yours can be repaired with their tools, or anything of theirs with yours.**
- They leave chalk inscriptions, welds, and lost tools. These are part of their culture of the place. Some may be humor, some memorial, some warning. Ask.
- **Their instincts are your best safety equipment.** They know what normal sounds like. When they get quiet, you get quiet.

### Etiquette and useful contributions

- **Bring your own consumables.** Food, water, CO₂ absorbent compatible with their systems if you can arrange it, medication, and anything else you'd otherwise draw from their limited supply.
- **Bring gifts appropriate to their life:** good coffee or tea, preserved food that isn't the usual rations, warm socks, gloves, and really good earplugs, which may be an enormous luxury here. Paper, pencils, chalk.
- **Offer help with menial work.** Keeping watch, logging gauge readings, and cleaning are all useful. Don't touch anything mechanical unless asked.
- **Don't ask about the walls, or what's beyond them,** more than once. "Nobody who works here talks about it much." That's either superstition, policy, or experience. In each case, respect it.
- **Learn basic signals:** telegraph codes, knock codes on hulls, rope and tether pulls for divers. Communication is scarce here, and simple codes carry lives.
- **Don't wander in the workshop.** It may be at ambient pressure, and its locks and hatches are pressure boundaries. Ask before you touch any hatch, valve, or lever. Every time.

### Cab life

- Diesel-electric systems in sealed spaces can mean fumes. Bring a **personal CO and CO₂ monitor**. If it alarms, tell the crew immediately.
- Bring things to do that work under low light and constant noise: a paper book, a notebook, cards. Noise-canceling headphones may give you a little respite inside the cab, though not from bone-conducted hum.
- Motion: crawlers are slow, heavy, and rough on junctions. Brakes squeal. Secure yourself.

## Part Eleven: Psychological Preparation

The Basin will work on your mind in ways that are hard to anticipate:

- **Constant low-frequency noise** produces unease and irritability, sometimes without any conscious awareness of the cause.
- **The eleven-second pulse** will enter your body. Some people find it soothing, like breathing or a heartbeat. Others find it unbearable after a few days: a clock that never stops.
- **Emptiness at scale** brings three-dimensional agoraphobia and blue-orb disorientation.
- **Structural sounds** like minutes-long ceiling shrieks will make you feel, viscerally, that the roof is about to fail. It almost certainly isn't. It will feel that way anyway.
- **Isolation.** Help is days away. Radio doesn't reach you. You are under a ceiling holding up a world you can't see.
- **Newness.** It feels unsettling to be somewhere this vast that nothing has softened yet. It isn't a ruin. It isn't a wilderness. It's a machine that has only recently started to grow life, like moss on a car.

**What helps:**

- Short visits. Days, not weeks, until you know your tolerance.
- A companion, if possible.
- Routine: eat, sleep, and work to the lamp schedule.
- Attention to the living things. The grazers drumming, the gold flash of a shoal in a plume, the hair-like pondweed streaming in the warm current. The Basin is overwhelming as a whole. It's beautiful in its details.
- A clear exit plan you can recite from memory.

## Part Twelve: Packing List

**Life support and protection (priority)**
- Confirmed seat in a one-atmosphere crawler cab or bathysphere, *or*
- Atmospheric diving suit rated with margin beyond estimated floor pressure, calibrated for fresh water, matte finish
- Personal CO and CO₂ monitor, spare batteries
- Your own emergency breathing supply compatible with whatever vessel you're in
- Written confirmation of the pressure status of every space you'll enter

**Cold**
- Thermal base layers (wool or synthetic, not cotton)
- Insulated mid-layers, hat, gloves, warm socks (many)
- Insulated boots or overshoes for cab floors
- Foam sleeping pad; sleeping bag rated well below 8°C

**Sound and sleep**
- Foam earplugs (lots) plus over-ear muffs
- Noise-canceling headphones for cab downtime
- Sleep mask
- Anti-nausea medication, tested at home first
- Baseline audiogram record

**Hazard handling**
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Eye protection; eyewash bottles with clean fresh water
- Solvent and rags for tar on equipment
- Spare seals and gaskets for your own gear (grit and alkalinity will chew them)
- Anti-fog wipes for any viewport or lens

**Navigation**
- Map of the pillar grid and rail network, if obtainable
- The bollard and pillar numbering scheme
- Notes on known pillar pitch signatures
- Non-magnetic dead-reckoning instrument or inertial navigator
- Matte tether line if doing any external work

**Consumables and contributions**
- All your own food and water, plus several days' surplus for storm or current delays
- Personal medication with extra supply
- Gifts for crews: coffee or tea, treats, socks, earplugs, chalk, paper
- Notebook and pencils (pens may fail in the cold)

**Comfort**
- Paper book, cards, a small familiar object
- Something with a warm color in it. Under sodium and mercury light, you'll miss red.

## Part Thirteen: A Suggested First Visit

**Before:** Establish contact through whoever runs the Basin from above. Confirm the pressure status of all spaces. Arrange a one-atmosphere crawler seat. Shift your sleep schedule to the lamp cycle. Get your hearing tested.

**Day 1 (lamp-dawn):** Arrive into the crawler cab at an intake station. Stay inside. Watch the wave of lamps step up pillar by pillar across the grid. Learn the cab and the crew's names. Listen to the hum and let the pulse settle into you. Write down every sound and ask what it is.

**Day 2:** Travel the rails toward a manifold pillar. Watch shoals spiral up the warm plume, gold in the sodium light. Watch the grazers on the steel. Listen for their drumming.

**Day 3:** If the vessel allows it and the crew agrees, a short tethered bathysphere drop into mid-column between pillars. Experience the emptiness with a gauge in front of you and a line above you. Keep it brief.

**Day 4:** Rest day in the cab. You'll need it. Watch the fish for signs of a shift change. Help log readings.

**Day 5:** Return toward the intake station. If a storm or a current reversal intervenes, wait. You packed for this.

**Exit:** Leave from the one-atmosphere space you arrived in, with no pressure transitions in between, if at all possible.

**Afterward:** Hearing test. Rest. Expect to count to eleven in the quiet for a while. Expect some part of you to miss it.

---

## Final Words

The Basin is a machine that has, by accident, started to become a world. The pumps and radiators don't know the fish exist. The fish have organized their whole lives around the pumps and radiators. The kelp is finding the bollards. The eels are finding the seams. In a few decades the sharp edges will soften and the paint will fade, and the Basin will be something else again.

You're visiting in its raw first years, when everything is still bright and cold and loud and new. Go in a steel shell at one atmosphere. Listen to the fish. Count the pulses. Don't touch the walls, don't chase the long shape, and never step through a hatch you don't understand.

It won't care that you came. But if you prepare properly, you'll get to see it breathing.
