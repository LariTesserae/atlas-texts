# Preparing to Visit the Comb

## First, understand what kind of place this is

The Comb looks peaceful, and in a sense it is. Nothing in it hunts, stings, rots, or grows. But the reason nothing lives here is the same reason you have to prepare carefully. It is a place built entirely from cold, thin air, hard light, and patient mechanical force, and it treats your body the way it treats everything else: as another surface for fog to freeze onto, frost to pry at, and sun to scour.

The good news is that the Comb is predictable. It runs on a daily cycle that barely varies:

- **Dawn:** Clear, brutally cold, still. Rime is glassy and hard.
- **Morning:** Sun arrives, glare builds, rime sublimates and sheds. The falling ice rings. This is the most beautiful time and the best time to be inside the field.
- **Midday:** Total, shadowless glare from above and below.
- **Early afternoon:** The cloud rises and pours through the notch from the west. Visibility collapses. Rime grows on everything, including you.
- **Evening:** Cloud recedes, light goes gold, pink, violet. Long parallel shadows for a few minutes.
- **Night:** Clear sky and a cold far deeper than the day suggested.

Plan around this cycle and the Comb becomes manageable. Ignore it and you'll get caught in freezing fog in a stone maze at 5,000 meters, which is how people die.

## 1. Altitude

The description gives several clues: a "hard, colorless blue" sky, a sun with "little air to pass through," a cloud sea far below, and a place so extreme that even lichen, one of Earth's hardiest organisms, doesn't grow. Assume you'll be somewhere between **4,500 and 5,500 meters**, and treat it as the upper end until you know better.

**Before you go:**
- **Acclimatize properly.** If you arrive from sea level by any instantaneous means, including portal, jump, or transition, you risk acute mountain sickness within hours and potentially high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema (HAPE/HACE). Both can kill. Spend at least a week or two staying progressively higher, whether in your home world or in the lower valleys of this one, before sleeping at the notch.
- **If your multiverse transit drops you directly at the Comb, do not stay overnight on the first visit.** Make a short daytime visit and leave. Sleeping high without acclimatization is where the real danger lies.
- Talk to a physician about **acetazolamide** for acclimatization, and carry **dexamethasone** and **nifedipine** for emergencies, with instruction on their use.
- Carry a **pulse oximeter**. Know your baseline.

**Your exit plan must be a descent plan.** The treatment for altitude illness is going down. Know how you get down from the notch, which side, and how far you'd need to drop to get relief. If your only exit is a return transit, make sure it can be triggered quickly, while confused and impaired, because that's what altitude sickness does to you.

At this height, everything will be harder than it looks. The hollow is "a hundred paces across," but you'll be stopping to breathe, and squeezing between slabs will leave you gasping.

## 2. Eyes and Skin: The Light

This is the hazard people most underestimate.

The Comb is lit from above by thin-atmosphere sun and **from below** by the cloud floor reflecting it back up. There are almost no shadows. In the mornings the rime glitters "too sharply to look at." Ordinary sunglasses will not do.

**Eyes:**
- **Glacier glasses, category 4**, with side shields that block light coming from the sides and from below. The light from below is the unusual part: it will come up under your brim and around the bottom of your lenses. Wraparound frames that sit close to the face are essential.
- A **backup pair**, since losing or breaking your glasses here is a genuine emergency. **Photokeratitis (snow blindness)** can develop within a few hours and will leave you unable to open your eyes, in a maze, at altitude.
- **Goggles** for the afternoon fog, both for wind and because your glasses will ice over (see below).
- Don't take your eye protection off to "see better" in flat light. Flat light is still intense UV.

**Skin:**
- **High-SPF, broad-spectrum sunscreen**, reapplied often, including **under your chin, under your nose, inside your nostrils' rims, under your earlobes, and around your eyes.** Reflected light burns the places sunburn normally misses.
- **Lip balm with SPF.**
- A **buff or balaclava** that covers the lower face, and a **brimmed hat or hood**. Cover as much skin as possible. The total absence of life on these stones is worth taking seriously as a signal about how punishing the radiation and cold are.

## 3. Cold: Two Kinds

You'll face two very different cold problems.

### The afternoon: supercooled fog

This is the Comb's signature, and it's genuinely unusual. The fog is **below freezing but still liquid.** It doesn't freeze until it touches something, and **you are something.**

When the cloud pours through the notch, rime will grow on your windward side the same way it grows on the stones, **into the wind**, in feathers, and fast. "A feather the width of a finger in the time it takes to eat a meal." Expect:
- Your **glasses and goggles to ice over** within minutes. Carry a way to clear them, or accept that you'll be half-blind.
- Your **jacket, pack, hood, eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, and hair** to grow a white fringe on the west-facing side.
- **Zippers, buckles, and toggles** to freeze shut or stiffen.
- **Ropes, straps, and poles** to accumulate heavy ice.
- Any **exposed skin** to take on a film of ice, which means frostbite risk, especially cheeks, nose, and fingers.
- **Wetting.** Fog droplets that hit you and don't immediately freeze will soak into fabrics that aren't waterproof, then freeze there.

**Prepare with:**
- A **hard-shell outer layer**, waterproof and windproof, with a hood that fits over a helmet. Smooth-faced fabrics shed rime better than fuzzy ones. Fleece on the outside will fur up spectacularly.
- **Insulation that works when damp**, such as synthetic fill or treated down under a shell.
- **Mitts over gloves.** You'll want dexterity for scrambling, but also coverage.
- A **full face covering** for fog hours.
- Glove-friendly zipper pulls, large buckles, and anything you can operate with numb hands.

### The night: radiative cold

After the fog drains back into the valleys, the sky clears and heat pours straight out into space. "The cold deepens past anything the day suggested." Plan for **−20°C to −30°C (−4°F to −22°F)**, possibly colder, and prepare for the worst case. You'll likely go into the night already damp from afternoon fog, which is dangerous.

**Prepare with:**
- A **sleeping bag rated well below your expected minimum**, since there's always wind, and altitude makes you colder.
- **Two sleeping pads**, one closed-cell foam and one insulated inflatable. You'll be on frozen gravel, and the ground pulls heat out of you relentlessly.
- **Dry clothes reserved only for sleeping**, kept in a waterproof bag. Change out of fog-dampened layers before the night cold arrives.
- A **bivy sack or small mountaineering tent** that can handle steady wind.
- **Insulated bottles** and a **pee bottle**, clearly labeled. Nobody wants to go out in that cold.
- Keep **batteries, phone, fuel canister, and water** inside your sleeping bag.

## 4. Water and Food

There is no water here in any usable quantity. "There are no pools. There is no stream." The ancient visitors worked for hours to gather a cupful. **Bring everything you need, or bring the means to make it.**

- **Carry a stove that works at altitude and in extreme cold.** Liquid-fuel stoves are more reliable than canister stoves at these temperatures, or use inverted canisters with cold-weather fuel blends. Remember water boils at a lower temperature up here, so cooking takes longer.
- **Bring extra fuel.** You'll melt snow or ice if there is any. Rime is the obvious source, since it's everywhere by evening, but you'll need a lot of it, because it's fluffy and airy.
- **Dehydration is severe at altitude.** Dry air and hard breathing strip water out of you. Aim for 4 or more liters a day.
- **Rime water is nearly distilled.** It's pure and safe, but it contains almost no minerals. Add electrolytes if it's your main supply.
- **Calorie-dense food that doesn't freeze solid**, such as nut butters, chocolate, fatty cheeses, and anything you can eat with gloves on. Appetite drops at altitude, so bring food you actually want to eat.

**Do not take water from the ancient basins.** More on this below.

## 5. Moving Through the Stones

The Comb is a hundred paces across, but you "cannot walk straight." It's a maze of standing slabs, packed close, sometimes touching, sometimes leaving just enough room to slide sideways.

### Physical prep and equipment

- **Pack small.** A large expedition pack will not fit through the gaps. You may need to leave your main pack at the western lip and carry only a small daypack or even just a waist pack into the field. Plan which gear lives where.
- **A helmet.** The slabs rise up to three times your height, their tops are sharp, and every morning the rime sheds and falls. Most feathers are small, but ice falling from 5 meters onto an unprotected head is not a trivial thing. Slabs can also crack and tip.
- **Durable gloves.** Schist splits into sharp edges, and the rough leeward faces are flaky and full of mica. Your hands will be on the rock constantly.
- **Abrasion-resistant outer layers**, since you'll be scraping along stone in tight squeezes. Your shell will take damage, so bring repair tape.
- **Stiff boots with good ankle support** and good grip. See below about the ground.
- **Microspikes or light crampons** for iced mornings. Rime and meltwater refrozen overnight make every surface glassy.

### Rules for moving through the field

- **Never lean on, push, or pull the slabs.** They are ancient, they lean a little further every year, and some of them are very close to giving up. A slab "cracks at the base with a sound like a single hand clap" and tips. You do not want to be the thing that tips it, or the thing it tips onto.
- **Know which way they'll fall.** Everything leans **east**, away from the wind. If a slab goes, it goes east. **Avoid lingering, sitting, or especially sleeping on the eastern (leeward) side of tall slabs.** That's exactly the sheltered side you'll instinctively want, and it's also directly underneath the lean.
- **Be especially wary of pairs that have already tipped together.** Two slabs leaning on each other are a stressed arch. Don't squeeze through under them.
- **Don't wedge yourself.** When sliding sideways between slabs, keep your chest and pack oriented so you can always back out. Getting stuck between converging stone in freezing fog at altitude is a real and terrible way to get into trouble. Don't enter gaps that narrow toward the far end.
- **Walk on the coarse stone of the frost stripes, not the fine grit in their centers.** The patterned ground is sorted by frost: coarse rims, fine centers. The fine grit is likely softer, more frost-heaved, and damp from meltwater. It's poorer footing and more easily damaged. The coarse stripes are firmer underfoot and less fragile.
- **Watch the ground.** Shattered chips are uneven and loose, and ankles roll easily.

### Navigation

The Comb gives you a built-in compass, which is useful since your electronics may fail in the cold:
- **The stones lean east.**
- **The polished faces point west**, into the wind.
- **Rime grows on the west side** of every stone.
- **Fog flows west to east.**
- **Long evening shadows point east.**

Even so, **do not wander into the field in the afternoon.** When the cloud arrives, visibility drops, everything turns white, the slabs look alike, and your glasses ice over. A hundred paces becomes a disorienting labyrinth.

- Carry a **compass**, but check it against the stone lean. Schist can contain magnetic minerals that deflect a needle locally.
- Carry a **GPS** with a track recorder, kept warm.
- Consider a **thin line** anchored at your entry point, which you unreel as you go and follow back, especially on your first visit. Don't anchor it to a slab; weight it with stones you bring, or anchor it outside the field.
- **Know your retreat route before the fog comes.** Mark your camp's location clearly, ideally just outside the western lip, near but not in the old shelter.

## 6. Where to Camp

The best evidence is what the people who came before did: a **low wall just inside the western lip**, sheltering "a hollow about the size of a sleeping body." They slept at the edge, not deep in the field, and not under tall slabs.

**Do this:**
- **Camp at the edge of the field or just outside it**, on flat ground, away from any slab tall enough to reach you if it fell.
- If you must be near stones, stay **west** of them, on the windward side, which means more wind and more rime but no risk of a leaning slab coming down on you. Or stay far enough away that nothing could reach.
- Build shelter from **your own gear**, not by rearranging the slabs.

**Don't:**
- Sleep in the ancient shelter. It's an artifact. Leave it as you found it.
- Build new walls or move stones. The Comb's patterned ground and standing field are the product of immense time and have never been disturbed by roots or animals. You'd be the first disruption in a very long while.

## 7. Conduct: What People Left

The Comb holds human traces: the low wall, the pecked water basins with their guiding grooves, channels scored and recut down the faces of certain slabs, a flint scraper, a hammerstone, worked bone, a bar of iron driven between two slabs, and the cairn at the center, the only thing that doesn't lean.

These were left by people who came here for reasons nobody wrote down, maybe need, maybe practice, maybe something like devotion. The recut grooves suggest people came back, generation after generation.

**Treat all of it as a site worth protecting:**
- **Don't touch, move, or take the tools.** Not the flint, not the bone, not the hammerstone.
- **Don't pull the iron bar.** Apart from its value as an artifact, the gap has closed around it and it may be holding stress between two slabs.
- **Don't drink from or disturb the basins.** They catch a cupful on a good day. Leave that water to the stone.
- **Don't add to or remove from the cairn.** Its slow twist into the wind is one of the most remarkable things here, a record of the rime cycle written in stone. Adding a stone, as visitors to cairns often instinctively do, would disturb it.
- **Photograph, sketch, observe.** That's plenty.

**If you want to understand the practice:** bring your own small cup and your own scraper. In the morning, find a slab with no basin at its foot, scrape a little rime from its western face, and let it melt in your cup in the sun. Drink it. Water that has never touched the ground, taken from a cloud by a stone. You'll likely understand something the description can't tell you. Do it on stones the ancient visitors didn't mark, so you leave theirs untouched.

### Leave no trace, and here that means more than usual

The Comb is sterile. "Nothing grows on them." "The rock is clean and has been clean for as long as it has stood." In a place without decomposers, **nothing you leave will break down.** Food crumbs, a drop of cooking oil, and especially human waste will just sit there, freeze, thaw, and persist.

- **Pack out all human waste.** Bring waste bags (WAG bags or equivalent) and use them for everything.
- **Pack out all food waste**, including water used for cooking.
- **Don't spill fuel.**
- **Be aware that you're carrying microbes, seeds, and spores** on your boots, clothes, and gear, especially as a multiverse traveler moving between worlds. Clean your boots and outer gear thoroughly before arriving. Brush out velcro, cuffs, and boot treads. The Comb has gone uncolonized for ages, and you don't want to be what changes that. This is also just good multiverse hygiene in general.
- **Don't touch the stones more than necessary.** The oils from your skin are organic matter on stone that has never had any.

## 8. Sound, Silence, and the Mind

The Comb is quiet. The wind is steady and nearly soundless, sometimes producing a faint breathy tone "like someone blowing across the mouth of a jar." Evening rime ticks as it cools. Morning ice falls with a hiss on grit or a clear glassy ring against stone. Rarely, a slab cracks at the base with a sharp clap.

This matters for preparation in a few ways:

- **The clap of a failing slab is the one sound worth reacting to.** If you hear it close by, step away from nearby tall slabs, toward the west if you can.
- **Altitude, silence, strong light, and the disorientation of fog** can have strong psychological effects. Some people find extreme quiet deeply peaceful; others find it unsettling, especially under hypoxia. The "crowd that has turned its back on you" feeling may be stronger than you'd expect. Know how you respond to isolation and bring someone with you if you can.
- **Don't fill the silence.** The ringing ice is best heard when you're still. Plan time on a clear morning to just stand in the middle of the field, near the cairn, and listen. It's the reason to come.
- **The thin tone of the wind may seem to come from nowhere.** That's normal. It's a slab at the right angle.

## 9. Gear and Electronics

- **Batteries die fast in extreme cold.** Keep them inside your clothing and sleeping bag. Bring spares, and prefer lithium over alkaline.
- **Cameras**: lens icing in fog; extreme glare in the morning; condensation when you bring a cold camera into a warm sleeping bag. Seal it in a bag before warming it. Bring lens cloths. Use an ND or polarizing filter for the glare. The evening "comb" shadows are the shot, and they last only minutes.
- **Headlamp** with fresh cold-rated batteries. Nights are long.
- **Repair kit**: tape for torn shells, spare buckles, stove maintenance kit.
- **Emergency shelter**: a lightweight bivy or emergency blanket in your daypack, in case you get caught in the field.
