# Preparing for the Cold Rise of Asker

## The Short Version

Asker punishes people who prepare to *see* it. Everything about it is built for bodies that hear, touch, and endure cold wet. The ideal visitor arrives already comfortable in the dark, dressed like a winter canyoneer, protected against noise without being deafened, carrying all their own food, and humble enough to be led around by someone with a bone clicker.

The main dangers aren't monsters. They are **wet cold, sharp rock, deep fast freezing water, disorientation, noise, and whatever lives in a million bats' worth of droppings.** Prepare for those and the place becomes wonder instead of ordeal.

---

## Part One: Training Before You Leave

Most of the preparation happens at home, weeks or months ahead.

### Learn to move in darkness

This matters more than any piece of gear. You will spend nearly your entire visit in lightless conditions, and a lamp is something to use sparingly, not a way to live.

- **Blindfold practice.** Walk your home, then a park, then uneven ground while blindfolded. Get used to shuffling your feet, sweeping with a hand, and trusting your soles. Aim for hours at a time, not minutes.
- **Learn basic human echolocation.** It is a real, learnable skill. Practice tongue clicks and listen to how they come back off walls, open doorways, and corners. Many blind practitioners teach this. The keepers navigate this way, and even a crude version will help you notice when a wall ends or a ledge opens out. Buy or make a small clicker and practice with it too.
- **Practice tactile reading.** The keepers carve wall-textures at shoulder height to mark junctions, stairs, and drop-offs. You can't learn their vocabulary in advance, but you can train your fingertips. Practice braille or textured-tile identification while wearing thin gloves, since that is how you will actually be touching things.

### Train your nervous system for overload

Asker combines total visual deprivation with massive auditory and tactile overload. That combination is disorienting in a way few people have experienced.

- Spend time in very loud environments, such as machine rooms or near large waterfalls, while blindfolded. Notice when you start to panic and practice slowing your breathing.
- **If you are squeamish about crawling sensations, desensitize.** Within seconds of touching almost any wall, fingernail-sized many-legged creatures will walk over your hand. Wings will flick your face constantly. If you know you react badly, work on it beforehand, or accept that you will need a guide to hold your elbow through the first days.
- **Trypophobia** (discomfort with clustered holes) is worth a moment's thought. The walls are honeycombed cliffs of fist-sized cells, each full of living bodies. You will mostly not see this, but touching it, or seeing it in lamplight or the grey season, may hit harder than you expect.
- **Claustrophobia and vertigo.** Streets are ledges barely wide enough for two. Some end without warning over canals. The walls tower out of sight. You will feel both hemmed in and exposed at once.

### Physical conditioning

- **Altitude.** The approach climbs through the cloud layer onto a plateau. Assume real altitude, perhaps several thousand meters, and acclimatize on the way up. Headache, nausea, and breathlessness in the dark and noise are miserable to sort out.
- **Endurance in cold wet.** Your body will burn calories just staying warm. Arrive fit and slightly heavier rather than lighter.
- **Leg and ankle strength.** You'll be on wet, uneven, freshly cleaved stone at all times. Strong ankles prevent many injuries.

## Part Two: Clothing

The environment is a degree or two above freezing, fully saturated, with water running over every surface and a few millimeters underfoot at all times. It never quite freezes, so nothing ever dries. This is the most dangerous kind of cold, because it soaks you slowly and steals heat without ever announcing itself.

**Dress like a cold-water canyoneer or a winter caver, not a mountaineer.**

### Layering

- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic, never cotton. Cotton in Asker is a hypothermia invitation.
- **Insulating layer:** synthetic insulation or fleece that keeps warmth when wet. Down is nearly useless here.
- **Outer shell:** fully waterproof and robust. Fresh rock is glassy and sharp, and brushing a wall will shred light fabric. The keepers wear close-fitting waxed layers, which is a strong hint. Loose clothing catches on edges and gets in the way of flying skerries. Aim for close and smooth.
- **Consider a semi-dry suit or neoprene layer** if you plan to be anywhere near the canals, or for long stays in the dense districts.
- **A hood that doesn't block your ears.** Your hearing is your eyes here. A close hood of thin material, or a balaclava with ear openings under a waterproof cap, works well.

### Footwear

Footwear is the hardest problem. You need boots that will:

- Resist cutting from sharp, unweathered basalt and glass. Choose thick, stiff, well-protected soles.
- Grip on stone coated in **microbial slime.** The mineral-eating films are the only "vegetation" on Asker and will likely be slick, as biofilms usually are. Felt soles grip well on slime but would be torn apart by glassy edges, so they are probably not worth it. Sticky rubber with deep lugs is a reasonable compromise, and removable microspikes or studs may help on the worst ledges. Ask the keepers what they use.
- Keep your feet warm while wet. Accept that your feet *will* be wet. Neoprene socks under wool socks, inside sturdy boots, are a good approach.

### Gloves

Your hands need to touch walls: to read carvings, to steady yourself, to feel for edges. Your hands also need protection from sharp stone and cold.

- Bring **thin, tough, cut-resistant gloves** that keep enough sensitivity to read textures, plus **insulated waterproof overgloves** for moving between places.
- Some visitors may prefer fingerless liners so fingertips can read carvings. Your fingertips will get cold and nicked. Decide which you'd rather lose: sensitivity or skin.

### Spares

- Carry at least **two complete sets of dry base layers and socks** sealed in waterproof bags. These are for sleeping and emergencies. Protect them fiercely. A dry sleep layer can be the difference between recovery and slow collapse.

## Part Three: Health and Hazards

### Hypothermia

This is your most likely serious problem. It comes on quietly: shivering, then clumsiness, then confusion. In darkness and noise, confusion is very hard to notice in yourself or others. Watch for:

- Fumbling hands and slurred speech in companions.
- Your own sudden apathy or irritability.
- Stopped shivering while still cold. This is a very bad sign.

Eat often, keep moving, rest in the quieter older districts, and never sit still for long on wet stone without insulation beneath you. Carry an insulated sit-pad and an emergency bivvy bag.

### Trench foot and skin maceration

Constant wet feet in near-freezing conditions will cause immersion foot within days if you're careless. Every rest period:

- Remove boots and socks, dry your feet as well as you can, apply a barrier balm, and change into dry socks.
- The same applies to your hands.

### Hearing damage

A dense district like Tallow is "loud enough to feel in the teeth." That is likely well into the range that damages hearing with prolonged exposure. But you *need* your hearing to navigate.

- Use **flat-attenuation musician's earplugs** that reduce volume without muffling the detail you need for echoes and water sounds. Bring several strengths.
- Wear full protection in the densest districts and during the evening outflow, when you should stand still anyway.
- Limit your time in the loudest places. Use the quieter keeper districts to let your ears rest.
- Expect ringing in your ears. If it persists after rest, reduce your exposure.

### Guano and disease

The description stays tactfully silent on this, but intuition shouldn't. Millions upon millions of small warm-blooded flying mammals, roosting constantly in stone cells, produce vast amounts of droppings. Much is probably washed away by the running water, and the drip-lice and microbes likely eat some of it. Even so:

- **Bring a good respirator** (N95 or better, rated for damp conditions) for the dense districts. Guano environments on Earth carry fungal spores such as histoplasmosis and ammonia fumes. Asker's version is unknown, which calls for more caution, not less.
- **Assume the skerries may carry diseases.** Don't handle them, even fallen ones. If you're bitten or scratched, treat it as a potential rabies-type exposure. Consult a physician before travel about pre-exposure vaccination.
- The canals collect skerry carcasses, which the keepers clear. **Canal water is not for drinking.**

### Drinking water

The water running over the glass seams is described as the best to drink, tasting of iron and struck flint. It is leaching minerals out of the stone, including iron and sulfur and compounds "that exist nowhere else on the surface," while also running near roosts full of skerries.

- **Always filter and treat water.** Use a good microfilter plus chemical or UV treatment.
- **Consider a heavy-metal test kit.** Fresh volcanic rock can leach arsenic and similar metals. A taste or two from a glass seam for the experience is probably fine. Living on it for weeks untested is a gamble.
- Better still, drink what the keepers drink and ask how they treat it.

### Sharp rock

Fresh-cleaved basalt and volcanic glass cut like broken crockery. Expect minor lacerations on hands, shins, and knees.

- Carry a proper wound-care kit: antiseptic, closure strips, waterproof dressings, and something to treat embedded glass slivers. Cuts heal badly in constant wet.
- Knee and shin pads, worn under or over trousers, are worth it if you'll be scrambling.

### Canals and falls

The canals are straight, black, fast, deep, and cold enough to numb a hand in seconds. They run toward the plateau's edge and fall. **Falling into a canal is the likeliest way to die on Asker.** Cold-water shock will take your breath and your coordination within a minute, and then the current carries you toward the edge.

- Stay away from canal edges unless guided.
- **Move roped to a guide or companion** on unfamiliar ledges, especially near watercourses.
- Learn the keepers' carved mark for "ledge ends over canal" first, before anything else.
- Wear a buoyancy aid near canals if keepers permit it. It won't save you from the cold, but it may buy seconds for someone to reach you.

### Wildlife

- **Skerries** will not collide with you. Let them stream past. Don't swat, flinch wildly, or wave your arms. You're a rock in their river.
- **Drip-lice** are harmless and investigative. Let them walk. Brushing them off at every wall touch will exhaust you.
- **Longcombs** are blind, slow, heavy, and in the canals. You shouldn't be in the canals.
- **Gleaners** are dog-sized or larger, stilt-legged, and feel ahead with antennae. They eat skerries, not people, as far as anyone says, and nobody is sure whether they are animals or something else. If you hear the low wet rattle nearby, stand still and let it pass. Don't block a ledge a gleaner is using. Give them space the way you'd give it to a large, uncertain animal in the dark.

### Light, rhythm, and mood

Night lasts most of the year. You'll have no sunlight at all.

- Bring a watch with a clear tactile or audible readout, and keep a sleep schedule. The colony's rhythm, with its evening outflow and midnight swelling, can serve as a daily clock once you learn it.
- Take vitamin D for longer stays.
- Expect low mood, dreaminess, or strange emotional swings. Constant noise with no light is disorienting. Rest often in the quiet districts.

## Part Four: What to Carry

**Navigation and communication**
- A clicker, bone or glass if you can get one, for echolocation.
- A red or very dim headlamp with spare waterproofed batteries. Use it sparingly, both out of courtesy and to keep your dark-adaptation. A lamp reveals the rock's colors (black basalt, green glass) but blinds you to everything else afterward.
- A brighter lamp for emergencies only.
- A thermal imager, possibly. It would show warm skerry-streams, the faintly warm new rock, gleaners, and your companions. It may feel like cheating, but it is a real safety tool.
- A short rope or tether and a harness for ledges.
- A whistle, though in a loud district it may be inaudible. Agree on tactile signals with companions beforehand: squeezes on the arm, tugs on the tether.

**Food and fuel**
- **All your food.** Nothing on Asker is edible in any practical sense. There are no plants, and the only animals are bats, lice, blind eels, and gleaners. The keepers may eat longcombs or skerries. Don't count on sharing what is surely scarce.
- Calorie-dense food, and more than you think you need.
- A way to heat water. A hot drink in that wet cold is medicine. Stoves may be hard to light in saturated air, so test yours in similar conditions.

**Shelter**
- The keepers live in older, quieter districts where the rock has gone inert. Ask to stay with them, or near them.
- Bring a waterproof bivvy or a small tent that can be pitched on stone, and an insulated sleeping mat. Stone at a few degrees above freezing will leach heat from your body all night.
- A synthetic sleeping bag rated well below freezing, sealed in a dry bag.

**Protection**
- All electronics fully waterproofed. Condensation gets into everything.
- Dry bags, many of them.

**Gifts for the keepers**
There are only a few hundred people here, and they live a demanding life. Think about what would actually help: fine tools, good wax for waterproofing, durable cord, preserved food that keeps, perhaps well-made instruments. Ask what they want rather than assuming. Some might value news or stories, told in voices they've never heard.

## Part Five: Conduct

### You are a small vote in the city

This is the strangest and most beautiful fact about Asker: **the city grows toward crowding and noise.** The engines read density and sound as instructions. The keepers influence the city's growth by gathering, clapping stone on stone, and whistling, night after night for years.

A visitor's influence is negligible. You are one body among millions. Still, it's worth knowing that your presence is literally a very small civic act. Don't join the keepers' noise-making unless invited. They may be working toward a particular wall or channel, and your noise in the wrong place is, in principle, a vote against them. If they do invite you, it is an honor. Take part.

### Respect the keepers' ways

- **Don't flood places with light** without asking. The keepers mostly don't use lamps. Light may disturb the colony, and it certainly marks you as a foreigner who hasn't learned to listen.
- **Never damage or cover the wall-carvings.** They are the city's only map, and people's lives depend on them.
- **Never block canals.** Keepers spend their lives clearing skerry bodies from the watercourses. Don't drop things in them, and never leave waste near them.
- **Ask about waste.** Human waste and garbage may need to go somewhere specific, or you may need to carry them out. Anything that dams a channel or fouls a drinking seam is a serious offense against a small community in a fragile place.
- Don't handle skerries, collect drip-lice, or poke at gleaners.
- Be patient with the keepers' way of talking. People who navigate by sound may speak, listen, or pause differently from what you're used to.

### Be a good rock in the stream

During the evening outflow, a single large wall can pour out skerries for an hour. Don't stand in the mouth of the stream. If you're caught in it, stand still, cover your face, and let it pass. It will feel like standing in a river of wings. They won't hit you.

## Part Six: When to Go

### The long night (most of the year)

This is Asker as it truly is: dark, roaring, wet. The roar swells toward the middle of the night, when young are born deep in the walls. It's the most intense and most "honest" time to visit, and the hardest.

**For a first visit, go early or late in the night**, when the roar is building or easing, rather than at its peak.

### The grey season (a few weeks)

A thin light creeps over the eastern rim and the fog lifts almost to the tops of the tallest districts. For a few weeks the city can be *glimpsed*: a black honeycombed expanse streaming with silver water under a low lid of cloud, swarming with shadow.

This is the easiest time to arrive and the only time you'll actually see what you've been touching. It is also brief and probably crowded, by Asker's standards, with keepers who want to look too. If your goal is to understand the place, go during the grey season first, when your eyes can help you build a mental map. Then return in the night and experience it properly.

## Part Seven: A Suggested Arc for a First Visit

1. **Acclimatize below the cloud line.** Rest, adjust to the altitude, check your gear.
2. **Climb the approach slowly.** As the grey thickens and the light fails, you will feel the drowning-dusk the description speaks of. Put away your lamp early and let your senses shift. Listen as the roar grows before the path levels out.
3. **Go straight to the keepers' quiet districts.** Find a guide. Learn the carvings for "stairs," "junction," and above all "drop to canal." Practice with a clicker alongside someone who really uses one.
4. **Spend a few sleeps just listening.** Learn the drip-tempos. The keepers can tell where they are by the rhythm of water on a ledge, and you may start to hear it too.
5. **Visit a low, new district.** These are waist-high walls, sharp and wet, smelling of deep earth. The noise is lower here, and you can touch fresh stone and feel the microbial film and the lice.
6. **Walk a canal-side, roped.** Hear the ticking of disturbed drip-lice in the water, the longcombs' bow-waves, and the falls thundering at the edge.
7. **Stand below a great wall like Tallow,** with earplugs, respirator, and guide. Feel the roar, the wings, the swarm on your palm, the taste of iron in the air.
8. **Stand very still and try to feel the rising.** Through your soles, at the edge of perception, there may be a patient, immense pressure from far below. You may not feel it. You may convince yourself you do. Either way, you are standing on a city still being built by the voices around you.

---

## Part Eight: Leaving

- **Decontaminate thoroughly.** Scrub and dry your boots, gloves, and outer shells. The microbes here eat fresh rock, and the chances they survive elsewhere are unknown. Don't carry Asker's life to other worlds by accident. Do the same with anything that touched skerries or canal water.
- **Watch your health for several weeks.** Report any fever, cough, or lingering illness to a doctor, and mention the bat-dense environment specifically.
- **Rest your ears.** Silence may feel strange afterward. Some visitors will feel that the ordinary world has gone deaf and flat, that it is too bright, too dry, and far too still.

---

## A Final Note

Most places ask you to look. Asker asks you to *listen*, and to be touched, soaked, and outnumbered. It is a city shaped entirely by who is loudest and most numerous, and you will never be either. The best preparation is to accept that you are a guest in a place built to someone else's specifications. The drip on the ledge, the tick of the canals, the brush of wings on your cheek are all instructions, just not addressed to you.

Go prepared to be cold and humble, and you'll come home with something almost nobody else has: the memory of a city you knew with your whole body except your eyes.
