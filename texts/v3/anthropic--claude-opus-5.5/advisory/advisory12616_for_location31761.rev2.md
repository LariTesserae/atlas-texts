# Preparing for the Shelf

## A Traveler's Briefing

The Shelf is not violent the way a jungle or a war zone is violent. Nothing there wants to hurt you. What kills people on the Shelf is subtler: the air, the silence, the sameness, and the fact that there is nowhere lower to go. Most of your preparation comes down to those four facts.

---

## 1. The Most Important Thing: There Is No "Down"

On any high mountain on Earth, the universal cure for altitude sickness is descent. You feel your brain swelling or your lungs filling, and you walk downhill a thousand meters and live.

**On the Shelf, descent is not available.** The tableland is the size of a continent. The lowlands are somewhere impossibly far below and beyond, across thousands of kilometers of identical plain. If you develop high-altitude cerebral or pulmonary edema, the only descent you have is **the way you came in**.

This changes everything:

- **Your crossing point is your hospital.** Know exactly where it is, how to get back to it, and how quickly it can be opened. If your method of multiverse travel can't be triggered in an emergency, don't go.
- **Never travel farther from your exit than you could return from while impaired.** Hypoxia degrades judgment before it degrades your legs. Plan your range as if you'll be walking back stupid.
- **Bring a portable hyperbaric chamber** (a Gamow bag or equivalent). It's heavy and awkward, and it's the only "descent" you can carry.
- **Carry the standard pharmacology:** acetazolamide for acclimatization, dexamethasone for cerebral edema, nifedipine for pulmonary edema. Know the doses before you arrive. You won't be reading instructions well once you're there.

---

## 2. The Air

The clues in the landscape are alarming. The zenith is nearly black at noon, the stars don't twinkle, and sound barely carries. That puts the Shelf at or beyond the altitudes mountaineers call the death zone, the height where the human body cannot acclimatize and is simply dying slowly the whole time it stays.

Assume the following:

- **You need supplemental oxygen, always.** Not just for exertion or sleep. Bring a closed or semi-closed rebreather if you can get one, because open-circuit bottles for a multi-day trip are an enormous weight. Carry redundancy: two regulators, spare masks, a backup supply that you never touch unless the primary fails.
- **Pre-acclimatize anyway.** Spend two or three weeks at high altitude somewhere survivable before your crossing. It won't make the Shelf safe, but it gives you a margin if your oxygen system hiccups.
- **Consider a partial-pressure suit** if the air turns out thinner than expected. At extreme altitudes, fluids in the body begin to behave badly. "Blood slow" in the description is not poetry. Check the actual pressure with an instrument within the first minutes of arrival, before you commit to going anywhere.
- **Expect to move slowly.** Every step costs. A walk that would take a day at sea level might take three here. Plan distances accordingly, and don't let the flatness fool you into thinking travel is easy.
- **Watch each other for hypoxia:** blue lips, slurred speech, stumbling, sudden irritability, or a strange contentment. The last one is the most dangerous. People dying of oxygen deprivation often feel fine.

---

## 3. The Silence

This is the part people underprepare for, because it sounds like a curiosity rather than a hazard.

**You will not hear the storms coming.** A wall of dust a hundred kilometers long can approach with no sound whatsoever. You will also not hear your companions shout, a warning whistle, a rock falling, or anything behind you.

Prepare for this deliberately:

- **Radios for everyone,** with voice-activated or push-to-talk headsets built into your mask. Test them before you arrive. Carry spare batteries in an inner pocket, because the cold kills batteries.
- **Visual signals.** Carry bright handheld lights and agree on a simple flash code: *storm*, *stop*, *come to me*, *emergency*. In the brown dusk of a storm, a strobe is the only thing anyone will see.
- **A barometer or altimeter watch with a storm alarm.** The description says you'll feel the pressure change in your ears. Let an instrument feel it first. Set an alert for any rapid pressure drop.
- **Make a habit of turning around.** Every few minutes, do a full slow scan of the horizon. Don't rely on "I'd notice." You won't. You'd be looking down at your footing while the sky behind you turned amber.
- **Tether in storms.** When a sheet or front arrives, rope together. Visibility drops to arm's length in seconds, and without sound you could lose someone two meters away and never find them.

Be psychologically ready for the silence, too. Many people find it beautiful for about an hour and then deeply unsettling. Your own breathing, loud in your mask, will be the only constant sound for days. Some travelers bring a little recorded music for camp. Others find that feels like a violation of the place. Decide which you are before you go.

---

## 4. The Dust

The Shelf is constantly grinding itself into powder, and you will be living inside that powder.

**Protect your body:**
- **A sealed full-face mask or tightly fitted goggles plus respirator.** The fine powder in the pans is "soft as flour." That grain size is what causes silicosis and other lung damage, and it will scour your eyes. Your oxygen mask should seal well enough to keep it out.
- **No exposed skin during storms.** Wind-driven grit at the speeds described will sandblast your face raw.
- **Eye drops, and plenty of them.** The air will be desperately dry even without dust.

**Protect your gear:**
- Every zipper, hinge, valve, and connector will fill with grit. Bring gear with minimal moving parts, sealed bags for everything, and brushes and compressed-air cans for cleaning. **Your oxygen regulator is the most critical thing to protect.** Keep its connections capped whenever they aren't in use.
- Assume anything left out overnight will be buried or gone by morning.

**Respect the pans:**
The description says the dust "behaves more like water than it should." Take that seriously. A pan might be ankle-deep in one place and drop away beneath you in another, especially in the bowls around dead Risers where dust pools deepest. Probe ahead with a trekking pole, avoid the pans when you can, and never cross a large one alone. Something lives down there, too. The sifters are almost certainly harmless, but you don't want to learn otherwise while waist-deep.

---

## 5. The Storms, Type by Type

**Sheets** (common): Low, fast, one or two meters deep. These are the ones you'll actually experience. If you're caught in one:
- Crouch or sit, face turned away from the wind, and let it pour over you. Standing puts your head above it in clear air, which sounds nice, but a sheet can knock your legs out from under you.
- Keep your tether on and your light ready.
- Watch for skates. A dinner-plate-sized animal hurtling past at storm speed could injure you, and a face strike could crack goggles. They steer well and probably avoid you, but keep your face protected.

**Towers** (rotating columns lasting days): **Do not approach.** They lift the finest powder "straight up into the thin air." A tower can lift a skate; it can likely lift a pack, a tent, or a person. Give them kilometers of berth. Note their positions and drift direction when you make camp. The pale shape seen inside them is another reason to stay far away. You don't need to find out what it is.

**Fronts** (once or twice a season): This is when the jet stream itself touches the ground and everything loose goes airborne and travels hundreds of kilometers. **A front is the storm that kills expeditions.** Before going, try to learn whether a front is due. If you can time your visit between fronts, do. If one comes:
- Get into shelter if at all possible: a cracked-open dome, the lee of a Riser, or the deepest hollow you can find.
- Otherwise, anchor everything, including yourself, lie flat, and wait it out.
- Afterward, **expect your surroundings to be unrecognizable.** Pans will have moved, reg-fields will have shifted, and caches and trail markers will be gone.

---

## 6. Shelter and Camping

**Tents:** The pavement is made of interlocking plates that don't shift. **You cannot drive stakes into it.** Plan for this:
- Use a very low, aerodynamic tent, like a dome or tunnel, pitched with its narrow end into the wind.
- Anchor it with weight. Bring stuff sacks you can fill with reg stones, which are conveniently uniform in size and already sorted for you by the wind.
- Try wedge anchors or cams that can lock into the seams between plates, like rock-climbing protection. Test them the first day.
- Pitch in the lee of something if possible. Here, "something" usually means only a Riser or a waystone.

**The Risers as shelter:** The dust flows around each Riser's base "in clean, curving wakes" and never touches it. That suggests a pocket of relatively calm air in its lee, which might be the best campsite on the plain. It might also be dangerous in ways no one understands. The ground there is being pushed upward, and something is pulsing. Camp a respectful distance downwind rather than against the column, and watch how the dust behaves around it before deciding.

**The shelters (domes):** The few that are cracked open are warm, enclosed, and out of the wind. That makes them lifesaving in a front and tempting at night. But:
- Dust "has somehow gotten in," so the air inside may be thick with fine particles. Keep your mask on.
- The warmth is coming from an unknown source that has run for ages. Don't sleep pressed against the floor for days on end. Treat it like camping near an unknown heat source, because that's exactly what it is.
- Don't try to open the sealed ones. Whatever keeps them sealed has held for longer than your civilization has existed.

**Cold:** Nights are "the deep, dry cold of high places." Bring an expedition-grade sleeping bag rated well below what you expect, an insulated pad (the pavement will pull heat out of you), and a way to keep your oxygen equipment and batteries warm. Many sleep with them inside the bag.

---

## 7. Water, Food, and Fuel

**There is no water on the Shelf.** None. Carry everything.

- The air is extremely dry, and breathing at altitude dumps enormous amounts of water from your lungs. You may need four or five liters a day, even without much exertion.
- Water is heavy, and your ability to carry weight is severely limited by the thin air. **This is probably the hardest constraint on how long you can stay.** Do the math before you go. It may come out to two or three days.
- Water boils at a very low temperature at this altitude, so cooking by boiling barely works. Bring food that doesn't need real cooking: dense, high-calorie, easy to eat through a mask gap.
- Stoves burn poorly in thin air. Bring one designed for extreme altitude, or skip hot food and just melt or warm water.
- Your appetite will vanish. Force yourself to eat anyway, because your body is burning fuel just to stay alive up there.

---

## 8. Light and Sun

The sun is "ordinary and sharp," and above you there's hardly any atmosphere to filter it.

- **UV exposure will be brutal.** Snow blindness and sunburn can happen in well under an hour. Wear glacier glasses or UV-rated goggles at all times in daylight, and cover every square centimeter of skin or use high-SPF sunscreen reapplied constantly.
- The pale pans and ochre pavement will reflect light upward, so burns under the chin and nose are common. Cover those, too.
- On the positive side, the hard shadows make excellent sundials. You can orient yourself by them with a little practice.

---

## 9. Navigation

The Shelf is flat, featureless, and periodically rearranged. It's among the easiest places in any world to get lost.

**Fixed points you can trust:**
- **The Risers.** They don't move, don't weather, and can be seen from a long way off. They're your lighthouses. Record your exit point's bearing and distance from the nearest one.
- **The waystones.** They run "arrow-straight for hundreds of kilometers" and were clearly built for exactly this purpose: finding your way through storms. If you find a line of them, follow it. Their faint glow during sheets may even help you stay on course when visibility is gone. Somebody long ago built this navigation system for travelers like you. Use it.
- **The stars.** Barely any atmosphere means they "burn steady and hard." Celestial navigation should be superb here, *if* you know this world's sky. Take star sightings on your first clear night near your exit, so you have reference data.

**Things you can't trust:**
- **Pans and reg-fields.** They move with every front.
- **Footprints.** They're gone within the hour.
- **Magnetic compasses,** at least until you've tested them. You don't know this world's magnetic field, and the Risers may disturb it.
- **Your own sense of distance.** The low horizon and lack of objects destroy depth perception. Something that looks an hour away could be a day away.

**A natural wind-vane:** The keel-moss grows on the downwind side of stones. Look back the way you came and the plain looks green; look forward and it looks bare. That tells you the prevailing wind direction wherever you stand. It's useful for predicting storm approach, orienting your tent, and maintaining a heading.

**Mark your exit redundantly:** a GPS or inertial waypoint if your devices work there, a bearing from a Riser, a star fix, and a physical beacon. A radio or light beacon is ideal, anchored to the pavement's seams or weighted down.

## 10. Life on the Shelf and How to Treat It

- **Keel-moss:** Don't walk on it deliberately, and don't move stones you don't need to. It regrows after fronts, but it is slow.
- **Puffballs:** A very old one might be a meter-long stalk underground that's been growing for centuries. Never pull one up.
- **Skates:** Harmless, as far as anyone knows. If you find one pressed flat on the pavement, you might step on it by accident. They're the same color as the ground. Watch your feet.
- **Sifters:** Leave them be. You won't see them anyway.
- **Lanterns:** These are the most beautiful things on the Shelf, and they grow nowhere else in the universe. **Do not pick them. Do not take cuttings.** There's no telling what they are or what they're connected to. Photograph them, sit with them at night, and leave them exactly as they are.
- **The pale shape in the towers:** Don't seek it out. If you see it, note the time and position and keep your distance. The people who know the Shelf speak about it "only carefully." Take that as advice.

---

## 11. The Built Things

**The Risers:** It's probably safe to lay your palm on one and feel the pulse. People have done it. But don't chip at them, climb them, or try to take samples. You couldn't scratch them anyway. More importantly, they're holding up a continent, and you're standing on it.

**Dead Riser shards:** These are unweathered volcanic-glass-like fragments with presumably razor edges. They're a real puncture hazard for boots, gloves, and suits. Walk carefully in the bowls around dead Risers. Don't collect shards. Something that doesn't erode in a place where mountains erode is not something to put in your pocket and carry home.

**The bowls:** They're the only low places on the Shelf, sheltered and a little warmer, with deep dust and glowing plants. That makes them lovely to visit and treacherous to cross. The dust there is deepest of all. Probe every step.

**The lines:** You'll almost never see them from the ground. If you notice faint straight scorings in low raking light, you're looking at something enormously old. Take a photograph and move on.

---

## 12. Your Mind

The physical preparation is hard, but the mental part may matter more.

- **Scale and emptiness:** You may walk for a week without seeing a Riser. Many people find total featurelessness disorienting in a way that's hard to describe until you've felt it. The eye keeps searching for something to land on. Expect some anxiety, and expect your first sight of a Riser to feel almost overwhelming.
- **Hypoxia and emotion:** Oxygen deprivation makes people euphoric, irritable, weepy, or strangely detached. Tell your companions ahead of time that you may behave oddly, and agree that any of you can call the trip off at any time without argument.
- **The silence and the storms:** Watching a hundred-kilometer wall of dust roll toward you in perfect silence is one of the most awe-inspiring and frightening things a human can witness. Some people freeze. Rehearse your storm routine until it's automatic: *signal, tether, crouch, face away, wait.*
- **Time:** The Shelf is a place where "ten thousand ages" is the unit of measure. Some travelers come back from it changed, with a different sense of how small and brief they are. Some find that comforting. Some find it hard to shake. Both are normal.

---

## 13. Suggested Kit, Summarized

**Life support**
- Oxygen system (rebreather preferred), with full redundancy
- Sealed full-face mask or goggles plus respirator
- Portable hyperbaric bag
- Altitude medications (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine)
- Pulse oximeter
- Barometer/altimeter with storm alarm

**Protection**
- Layered expedition clothing, windproof outer shell
- Expedition sleeping bag and insulated pad
- Glacier goggles, full-coverage sun protection
- Heavy gloves and puncture-resistant boots

**Shelter**
- Low aerodynamic tent
- Weighted stuff sacks and seam-wedge anchors
- Tethers and harnesses for each traveler

**Communication and navigation**
- Radios with mask-integrated headsets
- Strobes and headlamps with an agreed signal code
- Exit-point beacon
- GPS/inertial unit, sextant or star-finding app, notebook
- Trekking poles for probing dust

**Supplies**
- Four to five liters of water per person per day, plus reserve
- High-calorie food requiring no real cooking
- Extreme-altitude stove (optional)
- Sealed bags, brushes, and cleaning supplies for dust

---

## 14. A Short Visit Is a Good Visit

The honest recommendation is to make your first trip brief: a day or two, never more than a few hours' walk from your crossing point.

Arrive in the morning. Check the air pressure. Watch the sky turn from blue at the horizon to near-black overhead. Walk out toward the nearest Riser, if one is in sight, and put your hand on it. Wait several minutes for the pulse. Lie down on the cold pavement with your ear to the stone and feel the continent being pushed up beneath you.

If a sheet comes, crouch, let it pour over you, and watch the skates flicker past in the brown light. At night, if you can reach a bowl, sit with the lanterns. Look up at the steady stars. Listen to nothing.

Then go home before your body tells you to.

The Shelf has been exactly this way for longer than your species has existed, and it will be this way long after you leave. There is no hurry, and there is nothing you need to take back except the memory of standing near the top of the sky.
