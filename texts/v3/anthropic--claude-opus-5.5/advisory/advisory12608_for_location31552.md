# Preparing for The Level

## What You're Actually Walking Into

The Level is not trying to kill you. There is no predator, no toxic atmosphere, no hostile inhabitant. The temperature won't burn you and the water won't drown you. It may be one of the gentlest-seeming places in the multiverse.

That gentleness is the danger. The Level is a place where nothing is wrong, forever, and your body and mind will not handle that as well as you expect. The physical hazards are real but slow: immersion foot, heat strain, infected cuts, electrical faults, getting lost across a continent with no landmarks. The deepest hazard is that you will stop wanting to leave, or stop noticing that you haven't.

Prepare for three things: **permanent wetness and warmth, total directionlessness, and time dissolving.**

---

## Part One: Before You Go

### Heat and humidity acclimatization

Start at least two weeks before departure. The Level's air is saturated, which means sweat can't evaporate. Your main cooling mechanism simply stops working. The air isn't dangerously hot in absolute terms, but saturated warm air produces heat strain far faster than dry heat.

- Spend 60–90 minutes a day for 10–14 days exercising lightly in a humid environment: a steam room, a hot bathroom with a running shower, or a tropical climate if one is available.
- Your body will adapt: sweat onset gets earlier, plasma volume rises, and your heart rate under heat stress falls. You need all of that.
- Learn your own early warning signs of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, irritability, a sense that your heartbeat is too present.

### Medical checks

- **Heart conditions, pacemakers, implanted electronics:** consult seriously before going. The Level carries live current in dense cabling, and the main lines produce fields strong enough that visitors feel them in their dental fillings. If you can feel a field in your teeth, it can affect a pacemaker.
- **Metal fillings, piercings, implants:** expect a faint buzzing or pressure near the large conduits. This is uncomfortable rather than harmful, but know it's coming so it doesn't frighten you.
- **Skin conditions:** eczema, fungal susceptibility, and diabetes (because of foot wounds and slow healing) all deserve a doctor's input. Your skin will be wet the entire time.
- **Psychological history:** if you've experienced depersonalization, dissociation, or depression that shows up as a sense that nothing matters, think carefully. The Level produces a strong, pleasant version of exactly that state.

### Trip length

**First visit: a few hours. Second visit: one day. Do not plan a multi-day stay until you have been there and come back at least twice.** You need to learn how the place affects you specifically before you give it time to work.

## Part Two: The Body in the Film

### Your feet are the front line

You will stand in blood-warm water from the moment you arrive until the moment you leave. It is only a fingernail deep, but it is everywhere and it never dries. Warm-water immersion foot, sometimes called tropical immersion foot, sets in within one to three days of continuous wetness. The skin wrinkles, turns white, softens, cracks, and becomes painful and prone to infection. Fungal infections follow quickly after that.

**Footwear:**
- Wear boots or shoes that drain completely rather than ones that try to stay waterproof. The film is shallow, so waterproof boots might keep water out for a while, but your own sweat will fill them in the saturated air. Draining footwear dries your feet faster whenever you do get off the floor.
- Use thick, rigid, rubber or rubber-compound soles. You need them for three reasons: the sharp rivet heads and knurled treads, **electrical insulation** (see below), and grip on iron plates that are slick with water and crusted in places with mineral scale.
- Choose closed toes. The tinkers are harmless but numberless, and you don't want a few dozen of them investigating the gaps in a sandal.

**Foot protocol:**
- At every rest, get your feet out of the water, dry them, and air them.
- Use foot powder or a zinc-oxide or silicone barrier balm before walking.
- Pack at least three pairs of socks per day of travel, or go sockless if your boots allow it. Wet socks are worse than no socks.
- Check your feet morning and evening, or what you designate as morning and evening. If the skin is white and deeply wrinkled, stop walking until it recovers.

### Finding dry places

Nearly every surface on the Level is wet. Anything cooler than the air collects condensation: pipes, columns, railings, girders. The exceptions are surfaces **warmer** than the air, meaning the flanks of boiler houses, lagged steam lines, and the upper parts of furnace buildings. These stay above the dew point and can be dry.

That makes the boiler districts your refuges, with a caveat: warm enough to be dry can also mean hot enough to burn. Test every surface with the back of your hand before sitting or leaning, and never grab a pipe you haven't tested.

Bring a way to make your own dry space:
- A lightweight **hammock** with a waterproof underquilt or a sealed bivy. Gantries, railings, and truss members are everywhere and will take a hammock. Getting your whole body out of the film for sleep matters enormously.
- A roll-top dry bag big enough for sleeping clothes you never wear while walking.
- Desiccant packs for electronics and medical supplies.

### Heat strain

The "body stops trying to hold itself apart from the air" feeling described of the Level is literally true, and it is a warning sign.

- **Move slowly.** Walk at half your normal pace. Exertion in saturated air builds core heat you can't dump.
- **Rest before you're tired.** A good pattern for a first visit is 20 minutes of walking, then 10 minutes of rest off the floor.
- **Hydrate steadily.** You will sweat heavily even though the sweat doesn't cool you, and it will be hard to notice because your skin is wet anyway. Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.
- **Electrolytes are mandatory.** Bring oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets. Plain water in these conditions can dilute your blood sodium dangerously over several days.
- A small body thermometer, or a wearable that tracks core temperature estimates, is worth carrying.
- There is no cold anywhere on the Level. The pillar-rain is warm, the film is warm, the air is warm. Your only cooling is rest, shade (of which there is little, since the light is sourceless), and slowing down.

### Skin

Expect chafing wherever skin touches skin or cloth, and heat rash (small, itchy, prickly bumps from blocked sweat ducts). Bring:
- Anti-chafe balm for thighs, underarms, and under straps
- Loose clothing
- Antifungal cream and powder
- Talc-free drying powder

## Part Three: Water and Food

### Water

There is water everywhere on the Level, and most of it is questionable to drink.

- **The film** is the worst choice. It is continuously evaporating and being replenished, which means it carries dissolved salts and lime. The mineral ferns on the plates are proof of that. It is also where the tinkers live, where swift droppings fall, and where dead swifts are stripped to bone.
- **Pipe weep** is condensation, so it starts close to distilled, but then it runs over iron and through three-deep crusts of grazing snails.
- **Pillar-rain** is your best source. It condenses a mile up and falls in fixed, predictable places. Collect it directly as it falls, using a tarp or a wide-mouthed container held out in the curtain, before it touches anything.

Filter everything and then treat it, either by boiling or with chemical or UV treatment. The ecology may be sterile and perfect, but you don't know that, and your gut has no experience of this world. Carry enough treated water for at least a full day in case you can't reach a pillar.

### Food

Bring all of it. Nothing on the Level is food for you. Don't eat the snails, the crabs, or the lampreys, and certainly don't eat the herds. Even setting aside safety and ethics, removing anything from a system this exactly balanced is something you shouldn't do casually.

The saturated warmth will turn anything perishable fast. **Your own** microbes, carried in on your body and in your food, will thrive here even if the Level has none of its own.
- Use retort pouches, sealed packets, and canned goods.
- Avoid anything that goes soft or molds: bread, cheese, open packets.
- Once a package is opened, finish it.
- Salty foods help with electrolyte balance.

## Part Four: Clothing and Gear

### Clothing

- **Loose, light, long-sleeved, long-legged.** Not for sun, since there is none, but for protection. Every edge on the Level is factory-sharp. Knurled stair treads, crisp rivets, clean-cut gear teeth, and plate edges that have never been worn will slice skin that brushes them. In this environment, a small cut turns into an infected wound quickly.
- Choose fabrics that don't absorb heavily. Thin synthetics or merino blends work. Nothing will ever fully dry, so the aim is fabric that feels least terrible wet.
- **Gloves**: thin, grippy, and ideally electrically insulating. Use them for every railing and ladder.
- **A fine-mesh head net or a breathable face covering.** The midges don't bite, but they settle on lips and eyelashes. You'll walk through clouds the size of sheds, and without cover you'll inhale some. Most visitors find this harmless, but it's unpleasant, and anyone with airway sensitivities should take it seriously.
- **Wraparound eye protection**, clear-lensed. Use it in midge clouds and around machinery.
- Nothing dangling. No loose straps, cords, scarves, or long unbound hair. See the machinery section.

### Gear principles

- **Everything must be waterproof, and more than that, condensation-proof.** Any sealed item that is cooler than the air when you arrive will fog inside. Let cameras and devices warm up in a sealed bag before opening them.
- **Metals:** your steel will rust even though the Level's doesn't. The Level's self-maintenance does not extend to you. Bring stainless steel, titanium, or coated tools.
- **Paper dissolves.** Use a waterproof notebook and a pencil. Ink pens smear.
- **Electronics may misbehave** near the main lines. Expect GPS to be meaningless, since it's a different world, and compass readings to be useless (more on that below).

## Part Five: Electricity

This is the most immediate lethal hazard on the Level, so read it twice.

The Level is wet everywhere, the water is mineral-laden and therefore conductive, and it is threaded with live, high-capacity cabling hung from porcelain insulators, which are themselves wet.

- **Never touch a cable, an insulator, or any junction box or switchgear.** Even if a line looks insulated, the tarred braiding and the wet surfaces may leak current.
- **Never touch a pipe and a cable-carrying structure at the same time.** Contact with two points at different potentials is how you complete a circuit through your chest.
- **Keep your rubber soles intact.** Check them for cuts from rivet heads. A sliced sole is a path to the film.
- **Treat your forearm hair and your fillings as an alarm.** If the hair on your arms lifts or your teeth begin to buzz, you are near a heavy line. Step back and go around.
- If you ever feel a tingle through your feet while standing in the film, some fault is energizing the water nearby. **Do not stride away.** Shuffle out with small steps, feet close together, or hop with feet together. A long stride across an energized film can drive a lethal voltage up one leg and down the other.
- Pacemaker wearers should keep well clear of the main conduits, or better, not go at all.

The Level is so well maintained that faults may be vanishingly rare. But "the equipment has never failed" is exactly what people believe right up until it does, and you won't get a second chance.

## Part Six: The Works — Machinery and Structures

### The belts

The belts are wide, slow, constantly moving, and carry dozing herd-animals in rows. They will tempt you. Riding one looks restful and seems like free transport.

**Don't, at least not on your first visits.**
- Conveyors have pinch points where the belt meets the rollers. Rollers "the width of a street" can pull in a limb, a strap, or a hem and not notice.
- The belts go through junctions and transfers where animals slide off. You have no idea how those junctions behave with something your shape.
- **The loops may be continental.** You could doze off on a belt, as the herds do, and wake a thousand miles from your exit with no way to know which direction you came from.
- Doze on a belt and you become one of the herd. This is the Level's quiet trap in its purest form.

If you ever do ride one, stay awake, set an alarm, don't lie down, get off within minutes, and record exactly where and when you got on and off.

### Gears, pistons, open machinery

The Level's machinery is precise and quiet, and there's no sign it was designed with guards for human bodies. Silent machinery is more dangerous than loud machinery, because you don't instinctively flinch from it.
- Keep at least an arm's length from any moving part.
- Never reach into or across machinery.
- Tie back hair, tuck in clothing, and remove anything dangling.
- **Never touch valves, levers, switches, or controls.** Beyond the danger to you, you would be introducing a disturbance into a system that corrects every disturbance, and you don't know what that correction looks like.

### Surfaces

- **Snail-crusted pipes and columns** are slick, fragile (glass shells crunch and cut), and possibly hot or electrically bonded. Don't grab them for balance.
- **Gantries and stairs**: the knurling is sharp and the treads are wet. Climb with three points of contact and gloves on. Railings are painted green or oxblood depending on district. Note the colour; it's a navigation clue.
- **Mineral scale** is felt-soft in some places and slick in others. Test your footing.
