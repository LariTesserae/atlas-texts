# Preparing for the Settling Basin: A Traveler's Guide

## The Short Version

You are not going swimming.

Everything about the Basin invites you to picture yourself floating in that clear blue volume, watching a shoal spiral up a warm plume like a tornado of gold foil. Hold onto that image as the reason you're going, and then put it away, because an unprotected human in that water is dead within minutes. The cause wouldn't be the pike or the cold. It would be the pressure, which the description never mentions and which governs everything about how you can visit.

The best way to see the Basin is through a thick round porthole, from inside a crawler-train cab, with your ears plugged, a thermos of something hot, and a week to spare. Almost everything below explains why, and how to make that trip go well.

---

## 1. The Pressure Problem

The Basin is 400 meters tall. Every 10 meters of water adds about one atmosphere of pressure. Even if the ceiling sits at surface pressure, the floor is at roughly **41 atmospheres absolute**. The ceiling probably isn't at surface pressure, either. The water is fed from above through intake shafts, so the whole Basin may be pressurized by whatever head of water stands in those shafts. The floor could plausibly be at 50, 80, or 100+ atmospheres. You don't know, and you need to find out before you go.

What this means in practice:

- **Ambient-pressure diving is out.** Breathing ordinary air at 41 atmospheres gives you an oxygen partial pressure high enough to cause convulsions within minutes, and nitrogen narcosis severe enough that you wouldn't notice you were convulsing. Even with exotic gas mixes, reaching that depth at ambient pressure is saturation-diving territory. That means days of compression, weeks of living under pressure, and days of decompression afterward.
- **The local divers are a warning sign.** The description has divers in "heavy suits of brass and canvas" working at the pillar bases. On Earth, that equipment is good to perhaps 60 meters. If people here really work 400 meters down in canvas and brass, then one of three things is true. They're breathing something you don't know about, their bodies differ from yours, or the physics of this universe differs from yours. **Do not assume you can do what they do.** Before committing your body, send a probe through first, or ask to see their decompression tables and gas logs. If they have no such tables, be very worried.
- **Your arrival point matters enormously.** If your method of crossing between worlds drops you into open water, you're dead. If it drops you into a dry workshop that is itself held at ambient pressure, you are instantly saturated. You'd breathe normally and feel fine, and then when you go home to one atmosphere you'd suffer catastrophic decompression sickness. **Confirm the internal pressure of wherever you're arriving and wherever you'll depart from.** The ideal is a space held at one atmosphere by its hull, such as a crawler cab or a bathysphere. The thick portholes suggest the cabs are built this way, but confirm it.
- **Check your traveling mechanism's behavior under pressure differentials.** If you're departing from a sealed one-atmosphere space surrounded by 40+ atmospheres of water, make sure whatever opens your way out doesn't also open a hole in the hull.

## 2. Ways to Visit, Ranked

**1. Crawler-train passenger (strongly recommended).** Pressurized cab, floodlights, portholes, a crew who knows the place. You ride between pillars along the floor grid for days. You'll see the grazers on the pillar bases, the silt ripples, the eels during storms, the fish regathering behind the train, the bollards, and the lamp strings climbing into haze. This is the Basin as its workers know it.

**2. Workshop stay near an intake station.** Dry, lit, warm, with portholes looking up the intake shafts at the silver shoals. It's excellent for watching, but it's also the loudest part of the Basin, and you must confirm its internal pressure (see above). Plan for short stays.

**3. Bathysphere or suspended observation vessel.** This is the only realistic way to experience the mid-column: hanging in the blue with the floor dissolving below you and the nearest pillar a dim string of lights kilometers away. It's spectacular and it's also the most psychologically demanding option. If the locals operate one, beg for a ride. It will need a tether or reliable propulsion, because the currents shift without warning.

**4. Atmospheric diving suit.** This is a hard-shelled, articulated suit that keeps you at one atmosphere inside, of the Newtsuit or JIM type. It's the only sane way to actually go outside. You'd need to bring one or find a local equivalent, and you'd need training beforehand. The joints are stiff, you're clumsy, and you're tethered. Limit yourself to short excursions near a train or pillar base.

**5. Saturation diving.** Only if you are already a professional, have weeks to give, and have confirmed that local physics and gas behavior match your own. Otherwise, no.

**6. Ambient-pressure scuba or free-diving.** Absolutely not.

## 3. Sound: The Thing You'll Underestimate

People prepare for the pressure and the cold because those are obviously dangerous. The noise will be what actually wears you down.

The Basin is a sealed resonant box full of heavy machinery, in a medium that carries sound far better than air. Even inside a cab, the hull transmits it, and low-frequency sound comes through the steel, into the floor, and into your bones.

- **The hum** is constant, and you feel it in your sternum. Within hours you'll stop consciously hearing it. You won't stop feeling it, and by day three you may notice a low-grade fatigue, irritability, or anxiety you can't account for. That's the infrasound. Expect it and don't take it personally.
- **Near the intake stations,** the description mentions eyeballs vibrating and vision blurring. That's real low-frequency resonance of body tissue, and it can bring nausea, pressure in the chest, headaches, and a sense of dread. Limit your time there, and don't try precise work like photography or writing near the intakes on your first visit.
- **Near the walls,** standing waves concentrate the sound enough to drive away even the fish. The fish are telling you something. Visit briefly if at all.
- **The sudden sounds** will startle you over and over: rivets snapping like pistol shots, ceiling plates moaning for minutes at a time, brakes shrieking. For the first few days you'll flinch constantly, and then gradually you'll stop.

**Bring:**
- High-rated foam earplugs, plenty of them, *plus* over-ear protective muffs. Doubling up helps with midrange noise even if the low end still gets through.
- Anti-nausea medication, both for infrasound and for the rocking.
- A small, dense pillow or pad to put between your head and any hull surface when sleeping. Resting your skull against the steel pipes the hum straight into your inner ear.
- A baseline hearing test before you leave and one after you return.
- Something to listen to that you like, such as music or voices, for the moments when the hum starts to feel like it's inside your thoughts.

## 4. The Eleven-Second Pulse

This deserves its own section, because you will not escape it.

Every eleven seconds, the whole water mass shifts. In the cab you'll feel it as a faint surge or a soft shove through the floor. Loose objects tick. It's small, but it never stops, and your body will begin to count it.

Some people find this deeply soothing, like sleeping beside the sea. Others find it maddening, like a dripping tap they can't turn off. You won't know which kind of person you are until you're there. Before you go:

- Practice sleeping with a slow, regular external rhythm, such as a metronome set to eleven seconds or a recording of distant surf.
- If it starts to bother you, try syncing your breathing to it. Breathing in over five and a half seconds and out over five and a half is a calm, slow rhythm, and it turns the pulse from an intrusion into a pacemaker. The locals may already do this without thinking.
- Motion-sensitive travelers should take the anti-nausea medication preemptively for the first day or two.

After you leave, you may feel phantom pulses for a while. This is normal.

## 5. Cold

The open water is 8°C, and the floor layer is colder, probably near 4°C, where fresh water is densest. This matters only if something goes wrong. It would matter a great deal then.

- Inside cabs and workshops, it's presumably heated, but hull surfaces will be cold and damp with condensation. Bring warm layers, wool socks, and fingerless gloves for writing or operating a camera by a porthole.
- If you ever do go outside in a suit, know that immersion in 8°C water incapacitates your hands within about ten to fifteen minutes and leads to unconsciousness within an hour or two. If you're in a suit with any chance of flooding, you need a thermal undergarment rated for it.
- Near the outflow manifolds it's the opposite. The radiators may be hot enough to scald. Keep your distance from the fins.

## 6. Light and Time

The lamp schedule is the only day there is. Sixteen hours at full, then the banks step down to a third for the night.

- **Match your circadian rhythm before you go.** Shift your sleep over the preceding week so that "lamp-dawn" is your morning. Find out the total cycle length. If it isn't 24 hours, you'll need more preparation, or you'll be permanently jet-lagged.
- Night is never dark, only dim. Bring a good sleep mask.
- The light is sodium yellow and mercury blue, with green overlaps. It flattens colors oddly. Skin looks sallow, and reds go brown or black. Warn yourself ahead of time so you're not alarmed when your companions look ill. If you're photographing, shoot in formats that let you correct white balance heavily, and know that the Basin's "true" colors are the lamp colors.
- **Don't miss lamp-dawn.** The light rolls out bank by bank, pillar by pillar, a wave spreading across the grid. If you're positioned well, perhaps mid-column in a bathysphere or at a porthole with a long view, it may be the single most beautiful thing you see.

## 7. Navigation and Getting Lost

The Basin is mostly empty volume. The pillars are kilometers apart, and between them there are broad twilight reaches where you can see nothing in any direction but dim, even glow.

If you're riding the rails, this doesn't matter much, because the train knows where it's going. If you ever leave the train or the tether:

- **Never go untethered.** Currents can reverse within hours, and distances are enormous. A lost diver in the twilight reaches is a dead diver.
- **Learn the pillar numbering system** before any excursion. The bollards have white stenciled numbers, and the pillars presumably do too.
- **The fish navigate by each pillar's pitch.** Every pillar groans at its own tone. Crews may know these by ear. Ask them. It's the kind of knowledge that won't be written down anywhere.
- If you're in a bathysphere or suit, carry an independent way of finding your way back: inertial navigation, a sonar transponder, or a homing beacon on the train. Don't rely only on lights. A sediment storm can remove them entirely.

## 8. Specific Hazards

**Sediment storms.** Brown walls of concrete dust that drift for days. Visibility drops to near zero. Concrete dust is alkaline and abrasive. If it gets into eyes or open cuts, it burns, and it clogs filters, intakes, and mechanisms. Inside a train you're fine, and in fact a storm is the best time to watch the eels rise from the seams in their hundreds, lit by floodlights. Outside, get back to shelter when a storm approaches. Watch the fish. When the shoals scatter hours ahead of a current shift, trust them over the gauges.

**Current reversals.** When a turbine goes down or demand spikes above, the whole Basin reorganizes. Crews may get a telegraph warning. The fish sense it before anyone. Plan any exterior activity around the maintenance and demand schedule if the locals will share it.

**The tar seams.** The black sealing compound is still uncured and still weeping. Assume it is toxic, sticky, and very hard to get off. If it ever reaches your skin, through a suit fault or when handling a returned tool, clean it with a solvent the locals approve, not whatever you have on hand. Some tar solvents are worse than the tar.

**Sharp edges.** The slab edges will cut a hand, which means they'll cut canvas, soft suit joints, and hoses. Anything with a soft skin should stay away from the vertices and seams.

**The pike.** Man-length, heavy-jawed, and very fast. They hunt fish, not people, but a bright, clumsy, noisy object hanging in the twilight could confuse one. A hard suit is probably safe. A soft suit or a hose might not be. Don't hang motionless in the twilight zones, which is exactly where they wait.

**The eels.** Sluggish and probably harmless, but they swarm during storms. Don't put hands into floor seams.

**The unknown large thing.** Something long and slow patrols the outer reaches, and nobody has photographed it. If you glimpse it, *do not follow it* into the twilight. Note the pillar numbers, the time, and the heading. Take your photograph if you can, from where you are. Whether it's dangerous is unknowable. What's certain is that the outer reaches are far from rescue.

**Hull failure.** At these pressures, a cab or workshop breach is not something you survive by swimming out. Accept that honestly and learn the crew's emergency procedures anyway: which compartments seal, where the diving bells are, and what the alarm sounds like. Keep your world-exit mechanism, if you have one, within reach and ready to use from inside the cab at all times.

## 9. Your Body Before You Go

- **Get a medical screen** with emphasis on heart health, ears, and sinuses. Even at one atmosphere, cabs may undergo small pressure fluctuations, and congested sinuses and ears will make you miserable.
- **Have dental work checked.** Old fillings with air pockets can cause pain under pressure changes.
- **Don't go with a cold.**
- **Build some fitness.** Cabs are cramped, ladders are steep, and hatches are heavy. If you'll wear a hard suit, the joints take real strength to move.
- **Assess yourself honestly for claustrophobia and thalassophobia.** You'll be sealed in steel for days, surrounded by more water than you can imagine, with hundreds of meters of it overhead. Beyond the portholes is a blue emptiness that goes on for days in every direction. If you're unsure how you'll react, try a long stay in a small submarine, a cave tour, or a multi-day stay somewhere enclosed first.
- **Waterborne illness is not much of a concern** as long as you stay dry. The water is fresh and cold. If you do get exposed, treat any cuts seriously, since freshwater organisms can infect wounds.

## 10. The People

The Basin wasn't built to be visited. It was built to be run. The people you meet are maintenance crew, doing hard, loud, cold, dangerous work far from anywhere. You are, at best, a curiosity and, at worst, a liability.

- **Arrange your visit through proper channels** above: whoever runs the foundries and generating halls, or whoever schedules the trains. Turning up unannounced in a pressurized workshop will not go over well.
- **Be useful, or at least be no trouble.** Bring skills if you have any, such as electrical, mechanical, or medical work, or even cooking. Offer to haul things. Stay out of the way during repairs.
- **Follow every crew instruction immediately,** especially about hatches, valves, and alarms.
- **Bring gifts** that matter in a sealed life: good coffee, tobacco if they use it, fresh fruit, news from above, and letters.
- **Learn their marks.** Chalk inscriptions on the concrete and welds on the pillars are their record of the place. Ask what they mean. Don't add your own unless invited.
- **Don't ask too insistently what's beyond the walls.** The description notes that nobody here talks about it much. That kind of silence among working people usually has a reason, whether superstition, secrecy, or simply something they'd rather not think about down there. Let
