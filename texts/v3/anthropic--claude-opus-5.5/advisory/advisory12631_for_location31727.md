# Preparing to Visit Anhel

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Most dangerous places are dangerous because of something they do: storms, heat, predators, sudden changes. Anhel is difficult because of what it doesn't do. It never dries out. It never warms up. It never gets louder. Nothing in it wears smooth, and nothing in it changes.

For a visitor, this means the hardships are slow and cumulative. You will not face a single dramatic crisis. You will face a hundred small ones: a sock that has been damp for six days, a nick on your finger that won't close, a night when the dripping gets under your skin, a moment when you realize you have lost count of the days. Prepare for endurance, not for emergencies.

It also means that you are, by arriving, a change. In a city whose deepest fear is that something might move, you are the thing that moved. Everything about how you pack and behave should aim at being as small a change as possible.

---

## Getting There

If your crossing puts you directly on the Rim Road, you're lucky. If it puts you anywhere above the city, on the ridges or upper slopes, your first day will be the hardest of the trip.

Above the damp band the mountains are bare, glassy basalt: knife-edged crests, shard talus, and vertical scarps. There is **no water up there**. The moisture stays in the forty-pace band just above the cloud. Carry at least two litres for the descent, even though you're walking toward the wettest place you've ever been.

- **Descend slowly and look for the green.** The belt of moss and fern marks the damp band. Below it lies the cloud. Above it lies dry rock.
- **Do not glissade, scree-run, or slide on the talus.** The shards are fresh-fractured volcanic glass, and a fall on them is a fall onto knives.
- **Watch for the cairns.** Four tall basalt-column cairns stand around the rim. They are the easiest landmarks to aim for, and each one sits on the Rim Road.
- **Never descend through or into the cloud to "shortcut" to the rim.** If you've misjudged and find yourself on a slope where the white is rising around your knees, go up.

Once you reach the Rim Road, you're in the city, even if the nearest house is a quarter-hour's walk away.

## Hands and Feet: The Stone Will Cut You

This is the most immediate physical fact of Anhel. The rock has never weathered, and every edge is as sharp as the moment it broke.

**Hands**
- Bring **two pairs of cut-resistant gloves**: thin, grippy ones for daily use and heavier leather-palmed ones for scrambling. Leather is what the locals use, and it grips wet stone better than most synthetics.
- Better still, ask to have **Anheli palm-guards** made or traded for you. It's a good first act of exchange, and they're designed for exactly this rock.
- Learn the local habit on your first day: **touch rock with the heel of your hand, never with your fingertips.** Don't grab edges. Press on surfaces.

**Feet**
- Wear **sturdy boots with thick, puncture-resistant soles.** Glass-edged talus will slice thin trail runners.
- Choose soles that grip **wet rock**, since everything is always wet. Soft, sticky rubber outsoles beat hard lugs. Deep aggressive lugs also tear moss, which you should avoid.
- Learn to walk **flat-footed**, as Anheli children do. Put your whole sole down and don't toe off the edges of stones.
- Consider **lightweight gaiters** to keep wet moss and shards out of your boots.

**Knees and shins**: Soft knee pads or padded trousers help if you'll be scrambling. A stumble onto columnar basalt ends in a laceration, not a bruise.

---

## Clothing: You Will Never Be Dry Again (Plan Accordingly)

The air near the city is saturated all the time. Condensation forms on every cool surface, including you. Your clothes will not dry on a line, on a rock, or on your body. Only a hearth dries anything, and hearths burn scarce fuel.

**The core principle: dress to be warm while wet.**

- **Wool is essential.** Merino base layers and thicker wool mid-layers keep insulating when damp. The Anheli live in felted goat wool, and they have tested this for generations.
- **No cotton at all.** Cotton that gets wet here stays wet, and wet cotton in constant cool air slowly drains your body heat.
- **Synthetics are only a partial answer.** Fleece and synthetic insulation handle damp well. Their "quick-dry" advantage is useless where nothing dries, but they still insulate when wet.
- **Down is a liability** unless it is fully sealed in a waterproof shell and never exposed. Leave down jackets at home. A down sleeping bag is only acceptable in a waterproof bivy and dry bag.
- **Bring a light waterproof shell,** mainly for working near the nets and drip-walls, where water runs in sheets. Heavy "storm" gear is unnecessary because there's no rain or wind. What you need is breathable protection against constant fine wetness.
- **Hat and neck gaiter in wool.** Beads of water form in your hair.

**The Sacred Dry Set.** Pack one complete set of sleep clothes in a sealed waterproof bag: base layers, socks, and a hat. **Never wear them outside your sleeping place.** Change into them at night and back into your damp day clothes in the morning. It will feel awful. Do it anyway. Having something dry to sleep in is what keeps you well over weeks.

**Colours**: Muted greys, greens, and browns fit in and don't startle the moss-hares or the watchful ravens. Nobody will punish bright colours, but a vivid red jacket moving along a grey-green slope is visible from across the valley, and in Anhel, being conspicuous is a kind of noise.

## Body Care in Perpetual Damp

**Your feet are your greatest vulnerability.**
Constantly wet feet in cool conditions lead to maceration and eventually immersion foot (trench foot). It creeps up on you.
- Bring **more wool socks than seems sensible**, at least five or six pairs.
- Every night, **take off your boots and socks, dry your feet thoroughly** (by the hearth if your host allows), and apply **foot powder or a barrier balm.**
- Rotate socks daily. Keep a damp pair warming in an inner layer near your body. It won't dry, but it will be less cold.
- If your feet go white, wrinkled, numb, or tingling for long periods, **stop and warm them.** Don't dismiss it.

**Skin**
- Chafing gets worse in moisture. Bring anti-chafe balm.
- Skin folds (armpits, groin, between toes) are prone to fungal infection. Bring **antifungal cream or powder.**

**Chronic chill**
The air is never cold enough to be dramatic. That's the danger. You'll feel cool every waking hour and may never notice your core temperature slowly dropping. Eat well and often, since calories are heat. Warm up at hearths when invited. Watch for the early signs of mild hypothermia: clumsiness, irritability, and apathy. In Anhel's quiet, apathy is easy to mistake for calm.

## Wounds: Treat Every Cut Seriously

The basalt cuts cleanly and deeply, and in a world where nothing dries, cuts heal slowly and infect easily.

Bring a **well-stocked wound kit**:
- Antiseptic (iodine or chlorhexidine)
- Wound closure strips and a skin adhesive
- **Waterproof adhesive dressings** (ordinary plasters won't stay on here)
- Waterproof tape
- Fine tweezers and a magnifier, because glassy basalt and obsidian can leave slivers in wounds
- A course of broad-spectrum antibiotics, if you can obtain them, plus the knowledge of when to use them
- Irrigation syringe for cleaning cuts

**Clean every cut, however small, the moment it happens.** Cover it and check it daily. Redness spreading outward, heat, swelling, or red streaks all mean it's time to act.

## Water: The Only Currency That Matters

This is the most important cultural and practical section.

**For your health**: The water is condensed from fog, so it starts out nearly pure, like distilled water. Then it runs over stone and moss, sits in cisterns with frogs, and is wrung from wool nets handled by people and grazed around by goats. **Treat all water before drinking it**, with a filter, tablets, or boiling if fuel allows. A compact filter is ideal. The locals may be adapted to what's in it. You are not.

**For your welcome**: In Anhel, water is the basis of all social order. The distance between houses exists because each home needs its share of condensing surface and air. **Your presence consumes water** that someone collected, one bead at a time.

- **Never take water from a cistern, basin, gutter, or channel without being given it.**
- **Never help yourself to a full net or a clay jar.**
- **Offer to walk the nets and wring them.** It's the most obviously useful thing you can do, and doing it well, without dropping jars or tearing felt, earns respect.
- **Don't stand in front of drip-walls or lean against them.** Your body blocks the ribs, and your warm back steals condensation.
- **Don't hang your wet clothing on net frames or drip-ribs.** It's both rude and pointless.
- **Mind the channels.** Don't step in the chiseled troughs, dam them, or divert them. They're a unified system, and a blockage at one house starves the next.

**An idea**: Bring a lightweight **fine-mesh fog net or a tarp** you can rig as a condensing surface. Ask your host where it may be placed without stealing air from anyone else. Give whatever it collects to the household. You arrive as a drain on the water economy, and this lets you become a small contributor to it instead.

## Food

The Anheli diet is modest and hard-won: tubers, climbing beans, bitter greens, a drooping-headed grain, watercress-like herb from the flooded beds, goat milk and meat, and snail broth.

- **Bring most of your own food.** Choose calorie-dense, compact food that won't spoil fast in damp: nut butters, hard cheese, dried meat, oats, chocolate, and fats. Pack everything in sealed containers, because anything with paper packaging turns to paste.
- **Accept offered food graciously**, but eat modestly. Terrace soil was carried up in baskets over generations, and the granaries are communal.
- **Two specific cautions**:
  - **Snails**: Cook them thoroughly. Snails are a classic carrier of parasites in many worlds. The broth, well boiled, is probably fine. Never eat them raw or undercooked.
  - **Flooded-bed cress**: Plants grown in standing water where grazing animals roam are a known route for flukes. Eat it cooked, or not at all.
- **Expect your gut to protest** for a few days. Bring oral rehydration salts and antidiarrheal medication.

**Cooking**: Hearth fuel is shrub-wood and dried dung, both limited. If you bring a stove, bring a **quiet one**. A roaring pressurized burner would be the loudest thing in the valley. A small alcohol stove is nearly silent. Cook only where your host indicates, because in still air, smoke and cooking smells hang for a long time.

## Shelter and Where You Belong

**Don't pitch a tent on the Rim Road or between houses without permission.** The spacing between homes is law, marked in carved stones and never broken. A camp between two houses sits in someone's air. It may look like a claim, or like a new house placed too close, which is exactly what the city forbids.

Instead:
- **Find a warden.** Each spur has one. Present yourself, explain yourself in whatever way you can, and ask where a guest may sleep. The larger communal structures, such as granary-houses and open meeting shelters, are probably where a traveller would be placed.
- If you're given a place by a hearth, **you've been given something precious.** Keep your gear tidy and compact. Don't spread out wet things to dry without asking.
- **Bring a bivy sack and a closed-cell foam pad** as a fallback. Foam doesn't absorb water. Air mattresses work too but can be punctured on basalt, so bring a patch kit.
- **Stone sleeping benches are cold.** Put your pad under you even inside.

## Gear: What Survives and What Doesn't

**Tether everything.** The city sits on a steep slope above a cloud. **Anything you drop will roll, and anything that rolls goes into the cloud.** Nothing that goes into the cloud comes back. Use lanyards on your knife, water bottle, camera, and anything else you'd grieve. Clip your pack when you set it down.

**Electronics suffer badly.** Constant saturation means condensation inside devices, fogged lenses, corroded contacts, and dying batteries.
- Keep electronics sealed in dry bags **with silica gel packs**, and bring spare gel.
- Expect cameras to fog whenever you bring them from your warm body into the cool air.
- Solar charging will be weak in the pale, even light.
- Honestly consider leaving most electronics behind. Their beeps and screen-glow will be conspicuous anyway.

**Paper disintegrates.** Keep a journal in **waterproof paper** with pencil, not ink.

**Metal**: Metal is scarce in Anhel and reserved for a few bronze chisels, needle-points, and net fittings. A steel knife, a titanium pot, or a handful of carabiners is **extraordinary wealth** here.
- Keep metal gear discreet.
- **Don't trade or give away metal items casually.** A gift of steel could shift the balance between households along the rim, and this city is built on balance. If metal changes hands, let it go through the wardens or the granary-houses, where bronze is already handled communally.

## Gifts and Exchange

What can a visitor give without disrupting a place defined by stillness?

- **Labour** is the best gift. Wring nets, carry soil baskets, mend felt, clear moss from gutters (only when shown how), and haul water jars. The Anheli value keeping, and helping to keep things is the gift that fits their world.
- **Salt**, perhaps, in small amounts. A sealed valley with no sea and no rivers may lack it. Offer a little and watch how it's received before offering more.
- **Wool, thread, or strong cord** could be welcome for mending nets. It's a familiar material, just more of it.
- **Stories**, told quietly by a hearth. Be careful which ones you tell (see below).

**Do not bring**: seeds, plants, or animals of any kind. An invasive species could unbalance this ecosystem, which "stays balanced as if counted." Clean your boots, gaiters, and pack of soil and seeds before you cross over.

---

## Sound: Learn to Be Quiet

The quiet of Anhel will be the first thing you notice after the sharp stone, and possibly the thing you remember longest.

- **Speak low. Always.** The Anheli speak softly by habit, and near the cloud's edge sound seems to fall away and vanish. Matching their volume is basic courtesy.
- **Never shout.** Children are told the cloud is listening. Whatever you believe, respect that they believe it.
- **Leave behind** whistles (keep an emergency whistle only for true emergencies), bells, rattling cookware, loud zippers if you can, crinkly packaging, and any device that beeps.
- **Pad your gear.** Wrap loose items so your pack doesn't clink. Put a rubber tip on your trekking poles, since metal tips on stone are loud and also scratch the rock, which never heals.
- **Walk softly on stairs.** The goats pass with a sound like a brushed sleeve. Try to come close to that.

**The dripping.** You will hear it constantly, an irregular soft ticking from everywhere. On the first night it may be soothing. By the fourth night, some people find it unbearable, especially those prone to insomnia or who dislike irregular rhythms. **Bring earplugs**, soft foam ones, even though you'll feel a little guilty using them. People with tinnitus should know that the quiet will make it much more noticeable.

## The Cloud: Absolute Rules

1. **Do not enter the cloud.** Not one step, not to test it, not on a dare, not to retrieve something you dropped. People who went a few steps in and came back would not talk about it. Take that seriously.
2. **Do not follow the waders.** If one walks past you toward the white and slips in, let it go. Don't film it, call after it, or trail it to the edge.
3. **Do not throw anything into the cloud.** Not a stone, not a scrap. You'd be making a sound in the place where sound goes and doesn't return. You'd also be putting something into the one thing this city is organized around not disturbing.
4. **Do not ask aloud what is at the bottom.** It is "not a question the city asks aloud." If you're curious, and you will be, keep it to yourself. If someone offers to talk about it, listen quietly and let them decide how much to say.
5. **Do not touch "floor-silt"** if you see it matted in a wader's fur. Don't collect it, sample it, or take it home. Nobody knows what it is. Leave it that way.
6. **Stay away from the lowest terraces** unless invited. The morning ritual of checking the cloud against the carved mark belongs to each household. If you're allowed to watch it, stand back, stay silent, and don't comment on the level. Never say anything like "it looks higher today," even as a joke. It would be the cruellest thing you could say in Anhel.

## The Landscape Remembers Everything

Nothing here weathers. The oldest carved lintel still has crisp corners.

**Any mark you make is effectively permanent.** A scratch from your boot on a stair, a chip knocked off a column, initials cut into rock, or a scuff from a trekking pole will be there, unchanged, long after you're gone. Walk with care. Never carve or chip anything. Don't pry up stones or rearrange them, especially not the **carved distance-markers along the Rim Road**, which are law made physical.
