# Preparing for the Shelf

## The Short Version

The Shelf is not dangerous the way a jungle or a war zone is dangerous. Nothing here wants to hurt you. It is dangerous the way the upper atmosphere is dangerous: the air, the light, the cold, and the dust all work quietly against a human body, and there is nowhere lower to retreat to. Most of your preparation will be about breathing, water, and not getting lost. The rest is about learning to watch a landscape that gives almost no warnings, and learning to be alone with a great deal of silence.

Plan your first visit as a short stay near a single Riser or shelter. Do not attempt a crossing. The people who "spend long enough here to name the storms" earned that knowledge slowly, and you should too.

---

## 1. Understand the Altitude Problem First

Everything else depends on this.

The clues in the description are severe. The sky is nearly black at the zenith at noon. Sound barely carries. The jet stream touches the ground. On Earth, those conditions would put you somewhere between the summit of Everest and the cruising altitude of an airliner. At the upper end of that range, an unprotected person loses useful consciousness in a minute or two. Yet the description also says the air makes "lungs ache and blood slow," which implies people have breathed it and lived. This world's atmosphere may be deeper, denser, or richer in oxygen than ours at the same apparent height.

You cannot know which is true until you arrive, so prepare for the worst version and hope for the gentler one.

**Before you step through:**
- Carry a **pulse oximeter** and a **device that measures ambient pressure and oxygen partial pressure**. Check the readings in the first ten seconds, before you trust how you feel. Hypoxia feels like mild euphoria or nothing at all until it suddenly doesn't.
- Arrive **already wearing a supplemental oxygen mask**, flowing, with at least several hours of supply on your body. Remove it only after your instruments confirm the air is breathable for you, and even then keep it within arm's reach.
- If the readings are extreme (roughly equivalent to above 9,000 m on Earth), you will need a **partial-pressure suit or a sealed suit**, not just a mask. Treat the Shelf as an EVA environment in that case. The rest of this guide assumes the gentler case, where supplemental oxygen and acclimatization are enough.

**Acclimatize before the trip, not during it.** Spend weeks at real altitude somewhere on your own world, or sleep in a hypoxic tent on a staged schedule. Talk to a physician experienced in high-altitude medicine about acetazolamide for prevention. Carry dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies (cerebral and pulmonary edema, respectively), and know exactly when to use them.

**The critical difference from any mountain on Earth:** on a mountain, the cure for altitude sickness is going down. On the Shelf there is no down. It is a continent-sized tableland, and the only low places are shallow bowls a few kilometers wide. Your only real descent is the way you came in. So:
- **Your exit must be reliable and fast.** Know exactly how to reach your transit point, or how to open a return, from anywhere you go. If your method of crossing between worlds takes time or conditions to work, never travel farther from it than you could reach while sick, confused, and half-blind in a dust storm.
- A **portable hyperbaric bag** (a Gamow bag or equivalent) is worth its weight here. It is the closest thing to "descent" you can carry.
- Watch for headache, loss of coordination, confusion, a wet cough, or breathlessness at rest. On the Shelf, any of these means you leave. Do not wait until morning.

**"Blood slow."** Thin air, dehydration, and cold all thicken the blood and raise the risk of clots. Drink constantly. Move your legs even while resting. If you have any clotting history, get medical clearance before considering this trip at all.

## 2. Light and Sky

The sun is "ordinary and sharp," and there is very little atmosphere to filter it. Ultraviolet exposure will be brutal, probably several times what you would get at sea level, with the pale ground reflecting it back up at you.

- **Glacier glasses or goggles with full side coverage**, rated for the highest UV category. Carry a spare pair. Photokeratitis (snow blindness) is agonizing and will blind you for a day or more, and blindness here is fatal if a storm comes.
- **Cover all skin.** Wear long sleeves, gloves, a neck gaiter, and a wide brim or hood. Use high-SPF sunscreen on anything exposed, including the underside of your nose and chin, which catch reflected light.
- **Lip protection**, reapplied constantly.

The dark sky will also play tricks on your sense of scale. The horizon sits low and there are no verticals. You will badly misjudge distances. Something that looks an hour away may be a day away. Use a rangefinder or triangulate. Don't trust your eyes for distance.

## 3. Cold

The days are sharp and bright. The nights are "the deep, dry cold of high places." The description says this cold is not deadly, but that assumes you are dressed for it. Given how thin the air is, the air temperature may be colder than the sunlight makes it feel even at noon. In thin air, the sun warms whatever it touches while the surrounding air stays cold, so your sunlit side can be hot while your shadowed side is freezing.

- Pack a **layering system** built for high mountains: base layer, insulating layer, windproof shell, plus a heavy down parka for camp.
- Bring a **sleeping bag rated well below the temperatures you expect**, and an insulated pad. The pavement pulls heat out of you all night.
- **Windproofing matters more than insulation.** The wind never stops. A good shell with sealed cuffs and hood is essential, and it also doubles as dust protection.
- Carry **vapor-barrier liners** for your hands and feet, since the air is so dry.
- **Warm the batteries.** Keep electronics and spare batteries inside your clothing at night.

If you find one of the warm shelters, it is the best place on the Shelf to spend a night. See Section 9.

## 4. Water: The Hard Limit on Everything

There is no water on the Shelf. None. The only "liquid" is dust.

At altitude, with dry wind, strong sun, and hard breathing, you will lose water fast, likely four to six liters a day. Every drop must come with you. This, more than anything, decides how long you can stay and how far you can go.

- A three-day stay near your arrival point means carrying roughly 15 to 20 liters for one person, plus a reserve. Water weighs a kilogram per liter. Plan accordingly.
- **Insulate your water.** At night it will freeze. Sleep with a bottle inside your bag. Carry bottles upside down so ice forms at the bottom rather than in the cap.
- **Don't count on melting anything.** There is no snow or ice here.
- If your multiverse method allows it, consider **pre-positioning caches** near your arrival point: sealed, anchored, and marked. Remember that fronts rearrange loose things across hundreds of kilometers, and anything not bolted down may simply be gone. Anchor caches to the pavement, or leave them in the lee of a Riser (see below).

**Cooking:** water boils at a much lower temperature at this altitude. Pasta and rice won't cook properly. Bring a **pressure cooker** or rely on foods that only need rehydrating. Combustion may be weak in the thin air, so test your stove on your own world at a similar altitude. Canister stoves with pressure regulators do better than simple burners.

## 5. Dust

The Shelf's dust is not like ordinary sand. It is ground past the point of being any particular stone. The finest of it, in the pans, is "soft as flour" and faintly luminous. It flows like water, and it gets into shelters that are sealed.

**For your lungs:** extremely fine mineral dust is a serious health risk (silicosis and similar diseases), and you will be breathing it constantly while also breathing hard.
- Wear a **respirator with P100 filters, or better**, whenever there is visible dust. Better still, use a system that combines oxygen delivery with dust filtration.
- Bring **plenty of spare filters**. They will clog faster than you expect.

**For your eyes:** sealed goggles, not just sunglasses. Bring eyewash.

**For your gear:**
- Fine dust will destroy zippers, bearings, camera lenses, and anything with moving parts. Choose **sealed, dust-rated equipment**, with covers on connectors and cameras in housings.
- Bring a brush, compressed air, and zip-top bags for everything.

**The luminosity:** "faintly luminous" powder is worth taking seriously. It could be harmless, or it could be mildly radioactive, or it could be something the builders left. Carry a **dosimeter or Geiger counter**. Check the pans before you walk them, and check your clothing after.

**Static electricity:** dry air, fast wind, and moving mineral dust are a perfect recipe for static charge. Storms may build up large charges on your body and equipment. This is a real hazard around supplemental oxygen, which makes fires burn fiercely. Keep oxygen equipment away from sparks, ground yourself before handling it, avoid synthetic fabrics that spark easily near your oxygen, and never smoke or use open flame near it.

## 6. Storms: The Central Skill

This is what makes the Shelf different from any other high desert. **The storms come without warning and without sound.** Your ears will tell you nothing. You must learn to watch.

### Early Warning

- **A barometer with an alarm** is essential. The description says you "feel the pressure change in your ears" before a storm arrives. An instrument will notice it sooner and more reliably, and it can wake you.
- **Keep a visual watch.** When traveling with others, one person always faces upwind. Look for a thin ochre or bone-white line on the horizon, a change in the quality of light, or distant columns.
- **Watch the skates.** If they peel off the ground and take flight, a storm is coming. They know before you do.
- **Watch the light.** Amber, then brown, then grainy dusk. By the time it is brown, you are in it.

### Sheets (Common)

Low, fast rivers of dust a meter or two deep. Your head stays in clear air while your body is submerged.

This leads to a counterintuitive rule: **during a sheet, stay upright.** Every instinct says to crouch down out of the wind, but lying down puts your face in the dust. Standing or sitting tall keeps your head above it. Tie down gear so it isn't carried off, turn your back to the flow, and wait. Sheets polish; they don't carve. You will be sandblasted and dusty, but fine.

Mark your companions with **tall flagged poles** so you can see each other over the top of the sheet.

### Towers (Rotating Columns)

Towers rise from the pans and last for days, and they migrate slowly. **Stay away from pans when towers are forming.** Watch their direction of drift, and don't camp in their path. Their slowness gives you time to move, which makes them the most avoidable storm, as long as you're paying attention.

Keep your distance from towers for another reason too (see "The Pale Shape," below).

### Fronts (Rare, Once or Twice a Season)

A front is the jet stream touching the ground. **Everything loose goes airborne and travels hundreds of kilometers.** That includes you, your tent, and your supplies, unless they are anchored to the bedrock itself.

A front is the single most dangerous event on the Shelf. Prepare for one even if you plan a short stay:
- **Know the season.** If you can learn when fronts tend to come, schedule your visit away from them.
- **Anchor into the pavement.** The pavement plates "don't shift." They are interlocked bedrock. Bring a small cordless rock drill and expansion bolts, the kind climbers use. Clip yourself and your essential gear to them. Ordinary tent stakes will not hold.
- **Get into a shelter or a Riser's lee if you possibly can.** See below.
- **Wear a harness** so you can clip in quickly.
- **If you cannot shelter:** anchor, lie flat in whatever small hollow you have, face down, respirator and goggles sealed, and hold on. This is the one storm where lying flat may be right, because the danger is being lifted, not being buried. Keep your oxygen line secured. Expect it to last a long time.
- After a front, **the landscape will be rearranged.** Your caches, markers, and trail will likely be gone. Make sure you can navigate without them (Section 7).

### The Riser's Wake

The description holds a vital clue: dust "flows around their bases in clean, curving wakes... and never touches them." Something about a Riser repels the dust. The area immediately around its base, and especially its downwind lee, may be the calmest, clearest place during a storm.

This is untested, and you should not assume it will protect you from a front. But if you are near a Riser when a sheet comes, move close to its base and see. Don't lean your full weight against it; just stand near. Test this in a small storm before you trust it in a large one.

## 7. Navigation: A Flat, Featureless, Shifting World

The Shelf offers almost no landmarks. The horizon is empty. Your footprints disappear within the hour. Fronts rearrange the ground. You can walk for a week between Risers. Getting lost here is easy and possibly fatal.

- **Don't assume satellite navigation exists.** This is another world. Bring tools that work without infrastructure.
- **Celestial navigation.** The stars are extraordinary: steady, bright, and barely twinkling in the thin air. This may be one of the best places anywhere for sextant work, provided you have first learned this world's sky. The sun at noon gives you latitude and direction. Spend your first night or two mapping the stars before relying on them.
- **Compass, with caution.** Test whether this world has a magnetic field, and whether the Risers or waystones disturb it.
- **Inertial or dead reckoning.** Bring a pedometer or step counter, a good watch, and a heading. Log them constantly.
- **The waystone lines.** These run "arrow-straight for hundreds of kilometers" between Risers. They were built for exactly this purpose: finding your way through storms. If you find one, follow it. It is a road, and the builders meant it for travelers. During a sheet, they even glow faintly.
- **Keel-moss as a wind compass.** Moss grows on the lee side of stones, so it points downwind. Turning around and seeing the plain go faintly green tells you the prevailing wind direction. This works only between fronts, since the moss regrows slowly after one.
- **Tethers.** When traveling with a companion in low visibility, rope yourselves together. Voices don't carry. You could lose each other at five meters.
- **Beacons.** Bring a strobe or radio beacon to mark your camp, and carry radios. Line-of-sight radio will work very well on terrain this flat.

## 8. Moving Across the Ground

**Pavement** is the best terrain you'll ever walk on: flat, firm, and unshifting. It is also relentless and monotonous. It may be hard on the joints over long distances, so bring cushioned boots.

**Reg** (fields of small rounded stones) is harder going. The stones may roll underfoot. Watch your ankles.

**Pans** are the unknown. The powder is "soft as flour," deep enough to swallow a meter-long puffball stalk, and home to the sifters, which "swim" through it. Treat pans like thin ice or quicksand:
- **Probe ahead with a long pole.**
- **Consider snowshoes** or something similar to spread your weight.
- **Avoid them when you don't need to cross them**, and especially when towers are forming.
- Don't walk into the ring of a surfacing sifter. They are probably harmless, but you don't know that.

**Traveling far:** The Shelf's flat, hard pavement and constant wind make it close to ideal for **wind-powered land sailing or kite-sledding**. A well-built land yacht or a kite-pulled sled could cover distances in a day that would take a week on foot, carrying the water that makes long travel possible. This is advanced travel for a later trip, not your first, and you would need to know how to stop, anchor, and collapse your rig fast when a storm appears. On pavement, a lightweight wheeled cart is also a good way to haul water.

**Solar power** will work beautifully here: strong sun, no shade, no clouds except dust. Bring folding panels, and a brush to clean them.

## 9. The Built Things: Etiquette and Caution

Nobody knows what the Risers do, only that they are holding up a continent. Treat everything built here as both a treasure and a live machine.

**Risers**
- You may lay your palm on one. The description says you can feel its slow pulse, and there is no sign of harm. But do this gently, briefly, and without tools.
- **Don't strike, scratch, sample, drill, or climb them.** Aside from the danger, you would be interfering with the one mechanism keeping this world standing.
- Lying with your ear to the pavement near one to feel the upward pressure is fine. It may be one of the most profound experiences the Shelf offers.
- Their iridescence makes them visible at great distance in daylight and starlight. They are your best landmarks.
