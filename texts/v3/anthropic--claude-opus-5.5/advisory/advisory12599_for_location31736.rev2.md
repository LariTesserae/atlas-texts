# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Terraces of the Upper Tarn

## First, Understand What Kind of Trip This Is

The description makes the tarn sound quiet and gentle: clear water, green light, frogs. It is gentle in spirit, but physically it combines three demanding things:

1. **High-altitude wilderness travel.** You are above treeline and must come in over a pass. Nothing indicates roads, shelter, or help.
2. **Cold-water diving.** Below the top few meters the water sits a few degrees above freezing all year.
3. **Diving at altitude, with the exit route going up.** This is the detail most likely to hurt an unprepared visitor.

The town itself will not threaten you. The environment around it can. Everything below follows from those three facts, plus a fourth: the place is fragile in a way that is easy to damage and impossible to repair.

You can have a profound visit without diving. Much of the preparation below is about choosing which visit you actually want.

## Decide How You Want to Meet the Town

There are several ways in, from easiest to hardest. Pick deliberately.

### Option 1: From the Rim and the Shore (Accessible to Most Fit Hikers)

On a clear day the shapes resolve from above. To see them well:

- **Polarized sunglasses are the single most valuable item for a non-diver.** They cut surface glare and turn the lake from a dark mirror into a window. Bring a spare pair.
- **Go around midday.** When the sun is high, light reaches the deepest terrace and reflection off the surface is lowest. Early and late light will show you mostly sky.
- **Climb for angle.** The steeper your view down, the more you see. The scree slopes above the waterline, carefully chosen, give better angles than the shore.
- **Wait for calm.** The steady wind ripples the surface. There are often still windows in early morning, but the light is poorer then. Be patient and let the lake settle.

### Option 2: On the Surface (Moderate)

- **A packraft or small inflatable with a viewing bucket** (a bucket with a clear bottom) lets you drift over the lanes and look straight down with no glare. This is the lowest-impact way to see detail. You touch nothing, stir nothing, and can take hours.
- Keep oars or paddles out of the shallows. Anchoring is a bad idea: an anchor dropped into the marl will leave a scar that lasts centuries. Drift, or let the calm hold you.

### Option 3: Snorkeling the Upper Terraces (Moderate to Demanding)

In late summer the top few meters warm enough to swim briefly. Snorkeling puts you inside the green curtains of pondweed and milfoil, face to face with newts and char.

- You still need a **thick wetsuit (7mm with hood, gloves, and boots) or a drysuit**. "Warm enough to swim briefly" in a high tarn means painful but survivable for minutes, not comfortable for an hour.
- Stay in the upper water and **do not freedive down to the deep terraces** unless you are trained. Cold water shortens breath-holds, provokes involuntary gasping, and dulls judgment.

### Option 4: Scuba Diving the Town (Advanced; Requires Real Training)

This is the full experience: the stonewort meadows, the marl-choked lanes, the standing stone in the square, and in spring the frog organ. It is also a serious technical undertaking. An ordinary human should **not** attempt it without, at minimum:

- **Drysuit certification and real experience in near-freezing water.** Not one warm-water course; many cold dives.
- **Altitude diving training.** Explained below.
- **Excellent buoyancy control.** Explained below; this matters for the town as much as for you.
- **A buddy who is equally qualified.** Solo diving here is inadvisable.

### Option 5: The Winter Ice Window (Rare and Weather-Dependent)

There is a brief period after freeze-up and before snowfall when the ice is clear enough to lie on and look straight down, as if through a window. It may be the most extraordinary view the place offers. It is also the most dangerous and unpredictable time to be there. See the section on seasons.

**Ice diving under the lid is not an "ordinary traveler" activity.** It requires specialized training, tethers, surface tenders, and cutting gear. Leave it to experts.

## The Altitude Problem (Read This Even If You Don't Dive)

### For Everyone

- **Acclimatize.** Spend a few days at intermediate altitude before climbing to the cirque. Thin air means headaches, nausea, poor sleep, and breathlessness. Hauling gear over a pass while unacclimatized is miserable and can become dangerous.
- **Know the signs of altitude illness.** A persistent headache that painkillers don't touch, confusion, loss of coordination, or breathlessness at rest are signs to descend, not to push on.
- **UV is fierce.** There is less atmosphere above you, and you are surrounded by reflective water, rock, and snow. Bring high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm with SPF, a wide-brimmed hat, and glacier-grade sunglasses. Snow blindness is possible even in late summer near the north-facing gullies.
- **Water boils at a lower temperature**, so cooking takes longer. Bring more stove fuel than you think you need.

### For Divers

This is the critical part.

- **Decompression works differently at altitude.** You are surfacing to lower atmospheric pressure than at sea level, so standard dive tables and default computer settings are wrong. You need altitude-adjusted tables or a computer set for the tarn's elevation. You won't know the exact elevation until you arrive, so **bring a barometric altimeter** and set your computer after you get there and have rested.
- **Your exit goes uphill.** The path leaves over the pass, higher than the lake. Climbing after diving is like flying after diving: it lowers the ambient pressure on a body still carrying dissolved nitrogen and raises the risk of decompression sickness. **Plan to camp at the tarn** and allow a long surface interval (a full day or more, per your training's guidelines) after your last dive before crossing the pass.
- **Dive conservatively.** Stay shallower and shorter than you could, and add long safety stops. There is no recompression chamber in this world that you know of, and possibly none in reach of it at all. A bent diver here is in grave trouble.

## The Cold Problem

The deep water stays a few degrees above freezing all year, and the air on the rim is thin and windy. Cold is your constant companion.

### In the Water

- **Drysuit with a heavy undersuit**, dry gloves or thick wet gloves, and a thick hood.
- **Cold-water-rated, environmentally sealed regulators**, plus a fully independent second first stage. Regulators free-flow in water this cold, and a free-flowing regulator drains a tank in minutes.
- **Keep dives short.** Your hands will stop working before your mind notices. Set a time limit before you get in and obey it.
- The surface warms in summer, but the **thermocline** (the sharp boundary between warm upper water and cold deep water) will hit like a slap as you descend to the lower terraces.

### Out of the Water

The dangerous time is often after the dive. You surface wet and chilled into steady wind at altitude.

- **Have a warm-up routine ready before you get in.** A windproof shelter or tent at the shore, dry insulating layers laid out, a stove and hot drinks prepared.
- **A four-season tent and a sleeping bag rated well below freezing.** Nights above treeline can freeze even in late summer.
- **Learn to recognize hypothermia:** shivering that stops, clumsiness, slurred speech, apathy. Treat a companion who "seems fine but quiet" after a dive as hypothermic until proven otherwise.

## Getting There and Being There

### The Approach

- The old path comes over the pass, marked by the cairn at the rim. Nothing suggests maintained trails beyond it. Bring **map-making or navigation tools**: compass, GPS if your devices work in this world, and a way to record your route. You need to be able to find the pass again in cloud.
- **Weight is your main logistical enemy for diving.** Tanks, weights, drysuit, undersuits, and camping gear add up to a brutal carry at altitude. Options include multiple carry trips, pack animals if available, a compact rebreather (only if you are trained on it), or smaller tanks and fewer dives. **There will be no fill station.** Every breath you take underwater is one you carried in, unless you bring a portable compressor, which adds even more weight.
- Freshwater is less buoyant than seawater, and a drysuit adds buoyancy. **Do a weight check in the shallows** before committing to depth, and bring a few spare weights.

### Camp

- **Don't camp beneath the cirque walls.** The description notes that stones occasionally let go and plunge in. Choose a site out of the fall line.
- **Be wary of the eastern rockfall.** That rockfall is the dam holding the lake. It has held for centuries, but don't climb carelessly on it, dislodge stones, or camp directly in the outflow path. Leave the outflow boulders alone entirely.
- **Treat drinking water** even though it looks perfectly clean. Filter it or boil it. The water is clear, but it is full of life, some of which you can't see.
- **Weather in the mountains turns fast.** Afternoon storms, sudden cloud, and wind shifts are normal. Get off exposed ridges if you hear thunder, and don't dive in a storm, since lightning and a surface swimmer are a bad combination.
- **Pack out everything.** Nothing decays quickly at this altitude and temperature. A dropped wrapper will outlast you.

## Protecting the Town (This Is the Heart of It)

The place is at a delicate balance point, and it is remarkably easy to damage. The same slow processes that make it wonderful make every mark you leave nearly permanent. The water moves so little that disturbed silt will not clear quickly, and marl grows so slowly that a gouge might take centuries to fill.

### Biosecurity: The Most Important Preparation

You are an ordinary human arriving from somewhere else, possibly another world entirely, carrying microbes, spores, eggs, and seeds you can't see. This tarn is isolated, cold, and clean, exactly the kind of ecosystem that invasive organisms and diseases devastate.

- **The frogs and newts are especially vulnerable.** Amphibian diseases such as chytrid fungus have wiped out populations across our own world, spread largely on wet gear and boots. Arriving with contaminated equipment could silence the stone organ for good.
- **Clean, check, dry.** Before travel, scrub all gear that will touch the water (suits, fins, boots, the raft, nets, the viewing bucket) and remove all plant material and mud. Disinfect per current amphibian biosecurity guidance, then **dry everything completely for several days.** Ideally, bring gear that has never been in another body of water.
- **Clean your boots, too.** You will walk the shore where frogs come down from the scree.
- Bring no bait, no live food, and nothing that could escape into the lake.

### Underwater Conduct

- **Perfect buoyancy and trim, horizontal in the water.** Hovering head-up and feet-down, or sculling with your hands, will put your fins into the stonewort meadows and the marl.
- **Use the frog kick or a modified flutter kick**, gentle and directed back rather than down. A single careless kick into soft marl can raise a cloud that won't settle for hours in water this still, blinding you and burying the delicate life of that spot.
- **Do not touch the walls.** The moss, the diatom film, and the stonewort crust are the living skin of the town. Your hand can strip in a second what took decades to grow.
- **Do not enter the houses.** The doorways are low, the rooms small and filled with marl, and the doorways are slowly closing. Entry means brushing moss, stirring silt, and risking entanglement in weed in a confined space, in near-freezing water, at altitude. Look through the doorways; don't swim through them.
- **Take nothing.** No caddis cases, no charcoal fragments, no worked stones, no mussels. The caddis larvae are literally building from the town. It is theirs now.
- **Leave the mussels alone.** Some are decades old. Don't dislodge them from the marl or even brush against them.
- **Avoid the upper terrace gravel in autumn.** The char spawn there. Disturbing spawning gravel destroys eggs.
- **Avoid the shallows during spring frog breeding.** The frogs gather in the upper terraces and call from inside the houses. Swimming among them disrupts breeding, and you'd be crushing the event you came to hear.
- **Leeches:** you may pick some up. They are harmless. Remove them gently and move on.

### The Cairn

The cairn at the rim may be the last gesture of the people who left. **Don't add stones, remove stones, or rebuild it.** Whatever it meant to them, it should stay as they left it. Photograph it, sit by it, but leave it untouched.

You don't know whether the descendants of the townspeople live somewhere beyond the pass. If they do, the tarn may matter to them in ways you can't guess. Behave as you would at someone else's grave or ancestral home.

## Choosing Your Season

Each season offers a different town. Decide which one you want.

### Spring (Ice-Out)
**For:** the sound. The frog chorus, the ice cracking like thunder as it breaks up, and the lake's spring turnover.
**Against:** The water is at its coldest, the path may still be snowbound, the weather is volatile, and the turnover stirs up haze that reduces visibility. The frogs are breeding and must not be disturbed.
**How to do it well:** **Bring a hydrophone** (an underwater microphone) and a recorder with good headphones. You can drop it into the water from shore or from a drifting raft and listen to the whole stone organ without ever entering the water or disturbing a single frog. This may be the best way for an ordinary traveler to experience the tarn's most remarkable feature. Record long sessions. Different houses have different pitches, so try moving the hydrophone slowly along the shore and listening to how the chord shifts.

### Summer
**For:** the clearest water, the warmest surface layer, the easiest access, and the longest days. Oxygen bubbles silver the stonewort meadows on bright afternoons. It is the best season for snorkeling, surface viewing, and diving.
**Against:** The frogs are quieter. Water boatmen still chirr in the shallows, however, and the hydrophone is worth bringing anyway: the faint crackle of bubbles popping off the stoneworts, the rasp of diving beetles, a mussel closing across a street.
**This is the recommended season for a first visit.**

### Autumn
**For:** char in red spawning colors, the second turnover (weed leaning, a slow shiver through the green), and the first ice, which booms, groans, and sings in descending whistles.
**Against:** Visibility drops during turnover, it is colder, the weather worsens, and snow may close the pass unexpectedly. **Watch the weather obsessively.** Being trapped on the wrong side of a snowed-in pass is a real risk.

### Winter
**For:** the ice window. Lie on clear black ice and look straight down into the town, as if through glass.
**Against:** Everything. It means extreme cold, avalanche risk on the approach and on the cirque walls, deep snow, and short days. The window before snow covers the ice may last only days, and ice safety is a serious concern.
**If you attempt it:** You need winter mountaineering skills, avalanche training and gear (beacon, probe, shovel), and ice safety knowledge. **Check ice thickness with an auger** before going out, carry ice picks around your neck, use a rope, and have a partner on shore. New ice over a lake fed by inflow can be thin near the inlet and outflow. Stay off the ice at night, and be aware that the booming is a normal sound of ice movement, not necessarily a sign of breaking.

## A Practical Checklist

**Navigation and safety:** map-making tools, compass, GPS, barometric altimeter, first aid kit with hypothermia and altitude sections, emergency shelter, communication device if any works, headlamps, a way to signal.

**Camping:** four-season tent, subzero sleeping bag, insulated sleeping pad, stove with extra fuel, water filter, windproof and waterproof layers, down or synthetic insulation, warm hat and gloves, sturdy boots, gaiters, trekking poles.

**Sun:** glacier glasses, polarized sunglasses (and spares), high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm, wide-brimmed hat.

**Viewing and listening:** polarized glasses, viewing bucket, packraft or small inflatable, **hydrophone with recorder and headphones**, binoculars, camera with polarizing filter, an underwater housing if you dive.

**Diving (if trained):** drysuit and heavy undersuit, dry gloves, thick hood, sealed cold-water regulators with a redundant first stage, altitude-capable dive computer, tanks (and a plan for getting them there), spare weights, dive light (the edges of the deep terraces go blue), line cutter, safety sausage, spare O-rings and seals.

**Biosecurity:** scrub brush, disinfectant, and time. Gear should be dried completely before arrival.

## What It Will Probably Feel Like

After all the logistics, here is what to expect in yourself.

**The approach will be hard and plain.** Grey rock, scree, thin wind, snow in the gullies, and a small, dark lake. You may be briefly disappointed at the rim, because it looks like any mountain tarn.

Then the light shifts, or you put on your polarized glasses, or the wind drops for a minute, and the walls appear. Expect a strong jolt at that moment, the feeling of the ground changing meaning under you. Give yourself time there. Sit by the cairn. Many travelers will want to rush down; resist that.

**Underwater, or looking through the bucket, the dominant feeling will likely be stillness.** The weeds seem motionless until you watch for a full minute. The char hold station in lanes where the water barely moves. Everything is green, soft, and furred with life. There is almost no bare stone. The effect is less like a ruin than a garden that happens to have a town's shape. The quiet, orderly abandonment (swept hearths, no bones) keeps it from feeling tragic. It feels more like a home that was deliberately handed over.

**If you listen in spring, prepare to be startled.** The description is emphatic that people pull their heads out of the water in surprise. A hundred frogs in thirty stone rooms, each room tuned to its own note, all ringing off the walls of the cirque, is uncanny and a little overwhelming. It may be unsettling before it is beautiful. Let it be both.

**Expect a melancholy sense of time.** You will be looking at something on a clock set by algae: a town slowly entombed, its voice rising in pitch across centuries. Knowing that the doorways are closing, and that no one will ever again hear exactly the chord you are hearing, tends to make visitors quiet and careful. That is appropriate. Let it make you gentle with the place.

**When you leave, you'll likely look back from the pass**, as the townspeople may have when they built the cairn. That is the moment to take stock of what you've kept: recordings, photographs, memory. What you leave behind should be nothing at all, not a footprint in the marl, not a spore on a frog, not a stone moved from the cairn.

The town gave itself to the lake once. Your job is to visit without taking anything back.
