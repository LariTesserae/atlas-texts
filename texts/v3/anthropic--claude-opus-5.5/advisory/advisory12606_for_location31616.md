# Preparing for Kessel Flat

## The Short Version

Kessel Flat will not try to kill you, and that is exactly why it can. Nothing there is hostile. The birds ignore you, the flies ignore you, and the few hundred residents will probably ignore you too. The danger is that the place slowly and patiently wears things down to equilibrium. It does this to concrete, steel, and rails, and it will do it to your skin, your feet, your gear, and your judgment if you stay long enough without respecting it.

The three things that matter most:

1. **Your body cannot cool itself here.** The description says sweat doesn't evaporate. That is the single most important fact in it.
2. **The water and ground are caustic.** This is a soda lake, not a salt lake. The brine is alkaline, not just salty, and alkali burns are insidious.
3. **Everything metal is rotten.** Railings, stairs, roofs, ladders. Assume nothing will hold you until proven otherwise.

Everything else is detail.

---

## 1. Heat: The Real Threat

### Why this heat is different

In a dry desert, you sweat, the sweat evaporates, and you cool, as long as you drink enough. In Kessel Flat the sweat runs off you uselessly. Your main cooling mechanism is mostly offline. This is a **wet-bulb problem**. When heat and humidity together push past a certain threshold, even a healthy, rested, well-hydrated person sitting in shade cannot shed heat fast enough. Core temperature rises no matter what you drink.

I don't know the exact numbers in the basin. But "every metal surface carries condensation by mid-morning," "the skin never dries," and "a permanent haze" together suggest a place that may flirt with that threshold at midday in the hot season. Treat it as potentially lethal until you've measured it yourself.

### What to bring

- **A wet-bulb thermometer or combined temperature/humidity meter**, and learn to read it. Know your danger lines before you arrive. Check it several times a day. Sealed or marine-rated models will last longer (see the corrosion section).
- **Active cooling that doesn't depend on evaporation.** Evaporative cooling towels and misting fans will do almost nothing here. Better options:
  - Phase-change cooling vests (the kind that refreeze at around 15°C rather than 0°C). You'll need access to a freezer or cold water to recharge them; ask whether the plant or the inhabited blocks have refrigeration.
  - A battery-powered fan still helps a little through convection, but don't rely on it.
  - The best cooling of all is **immersion in cool fresh water**: forearms, feet, or ideally the whole body. Find out on arrival whether this is possible anywhere.
- **Electrolytes in quantity.** You will lose salt and water constantly, even at rest. Pre-measured oral rehydration salts are better than sports drinks.
- **Far more fresh water than you think.** Everything around you is brine. The town must bring water in by rail, desalinate, or pipe it down from the escarpment. Either way it may be rationed, company-controlled, or precious. Don't assume you can buy it. Bring a filter that can handle a questionable freshwater source, but know that **no ordinary filter makes the lake or pan brine drinkable**. You'd need a reverse-osmosis desalinator, and even then high-pH soda brine may damage it.

### How to behave

- **Acclimatize before you go** if at all possible. Ten to fourteen days of daily exposure to hot, humid conditions, in a sauna, a hot climate, or a heat chamber, meaningfully improves your body's response. Arriving cold from a temperate world and walking out onto the berms at noon is how people die.
- **Invert your day.** Do your moving around in early morning (wet, gray, drippy, but cooler) and in the long dim evening. Spend midday in the deepest shade and stillest posture you can find. The thick-walled concrete barracks probably hold cooler interiors than the air outside, especially ground floors and north-facing rooms.
- **Learn the early signs of heat illness** and have your companion learn them too. Headache, nausea, cramps, dizziness, irritability, and clumsiness are the early ones. Confusion, stopping sweating, or collapse is an emergency. The confusion part matters most. Heat stroke impairs the judgment you'd need to notice you have heat stroke.
- **Never go alone onto the pans or berms at midday.** The causeways run kilometers out into an unshaded, featureless landscape where the horizon disappears. Collapsing out there alone is the most plausible way to die in Kessel Flat.
- Nights won't give much relief. The description says the air cools only "slightly." Plan for poor sleep (see below).

## 2. The Ground and the Water: Caustic, Not Just Salty

The lake yields **soda ash and trona**, which is sodium carbonate and bicarbonate. That means the brine is strongly alkaline, possibly around pH 10 or higher in the concentrated pans. Alkali behaves differently from acid on tissue. It doesn't sting sharply at first. It saponifies fat, turning your skin oils into soap, and it keeps penetrating. You may not notice real damage until it's done.

### Skin

- **Avoid brine contact on bare skin.** Wet feet in brine-soaked boots for hours could cause chemical irritation on top of ordinary maceration.
- The "gray, salty paste" the handrails leave on your palm is exactly the thing to wash off promptly. Carry a small bottle of fresh water, or better, a squeeze bottle with a weak vinegar solution (diluted acetic acid). Rinse exposed skin, *then* rinse with fresh water. (For burns and eyes, use fresh water only. See below.)
- **Lightweight gloves** for handling anything: railings, doors, sluice wheels, salt crust. Nitrile under thin work gloves works if you're touching lots of surfaces.

### Eyes

This is the most serious chemical risk. **Alkali in the eye is a genuine emergency.** It can cause permanent corneal damage within minutes.

- Wear **sealed goggles** whenever there's airborne dust (afternoon wind), whenever you're near the kilns or crusher hall, and whenever you're working near splashing brine.
- Carry **a large eyewash bottle** (at least 500 mL of sterile saline or clean water) on your body at all times. If brine or ash dust gets in your eye, irrigate continuously for at least fifteen to twenty minutes. That is much longer than feels necessary. Then seek help.
- Don't wear contact lenses. Dust plus alkali trapped under a lens is a disaster. Bring glasses, or prescription goggles.

### Stepping off the pavement

The description is precise: "Step off the paved streets and your boot breaks through a brittle skin into warm mud. The footprint fills with liquid within a minute."

- **Stay on the slag pavement, the berms, and the rail beds.** The crust is not ground. Off-pavement, you may sink ankle-deep or knee-deep into warm, caustic, sulfide-rich ooze. In the wrong spot on the old pans or lake margin, you could get stuck. This is real, and it has happened at soda lakes on Earth.
- The black layer smelling of eggs is **hydrogen sulfide**, produced by bacteria. In open air it's mostly just unpleasant. In confined low spaces, such as culverts, sumps, the basements of flooded buildings, pumphouse pits, and drainage tunnels, it can accumulate. **H₂S is deadly at high concentrations, and it kills your sense of smell before it kills you.** If the egg smell suddenly vanishes while you're somewhere enclosed, get out immediately. A personal H₂S gas monitor, the kind oil and sewer workers wear, is a very good idea if you plan to explore underground spaces. Better still, don't explore them.
- Don't wade into the pans. Beyond the chemistry, the berms were built to hold liquid, and pan floors may be crust over mud.

### The colored pans

The pink and red water is halophilic archaea, which are generally harmless to humans. You won't catch anything from them. The chemistry is still the chemistry, so don't touch it.

## 3. Feet, Skin, and the Slow Stuff

Humidity that never lets skin dry creates predictable problems that sound minor and can end a trip:

- **Trench foot / immersion foot.** Feet that stay wet and warm for days break down. Bring **many pairs of synthetic or merino socks** and change them at least twice a day. Let your feet air out fully every night. Rotate two or three pairs of boots so each can dry, though here they may never fully dry.
- **Footwear strategy:**
  - For the pavement and town: well-ventilated boots with good soles. The slag is "polished," and polished pavement with morning condensation and salt paste will be slick. Consider soles with good wet-grip rubber.
  - For berms and anywhere near brine: tall waterproof boots (rubber or similar) that seal above the ankle. Wear them for as short a time as possible, because your feet will cook inside them.
  - Wash your boots in fresh water when you can. Salt and alkali rot leather, stitching, and glue.
- **Fungal infections, heat rash, and chafing.** Expect them in skin folds, between toes, in the groin, and under straps. Bring antifungal cream and a barrier balm (zinc oxide or a similar ointment). Powders will just cake into paste in this humidity, so creams and balms are better.
- **Clothing:** loose, light-colored, quick-drying synthetic or light linen. It will never actually be dry, but it shouldn't cling. Long sleeves and trousers protect against brine splash, salt dust, and sun, even through haze. Bring enough changes to rotate, and expect every garment to stiffen with salt. A wide-brimmed hat keeps drips off your face in the mornings.
- **Sun:** the haze makes the sun a pale disk, but haze scatters UV rather than blocking all of it. Wear sunscreen, but choose one that doesn't sting when it runs into your eyes, because it will run.
- **Lips and nose:** salt dust dries and cracks mucous membranes even in humid air. A saline nasal rinse at night helps. Use fresh-water saline, not the local stuff.

## 4. Breathing

The air carries several different things:

- **Salt and soda-ash dust** in the afternoon wind. Soda ash dust irritates airways and eyes. A good-fitting particulate respirator (N95/FFP2 or better) plus goggles for the afternoon.
- **Kiln and calcining dust** near the plant. Calcining trona produces fine sodium carbonate dust and releases carbon dioxide. Combustion in the kilns adds more. Don't linger in the kiln hall, and never go into enclosed areas around the kilns.
- **Diesel exhaust** from generators and locomotives.
- **Hydrogen sulfide** near the mud (see above).
- **Bird and bat material** (see below).

The respirator will be miserable in wet heat, because breathing through it makes you hotter. Wear it when the dust is up and take it off when it isn't.

## 5. Living Things: Mostly Harmless, With Exceptions

### Flies

They don't bite, but at midday there will be clouds of them. They'll land on your face, crawl at the corners of your eyes and mouth, and get into food and drink. The sheer quantity will bother most people more than they expect. On the first day it's disturbing. By the third you'll brush them off without thinking.

- A **head net** will be very welcome at peak emergence, especially for eating outdoors.
- Cover food and drinks. Drink through a straw or from a bottle with a closing lid.
- The decaying fly drifts along the berms will smell. Don't walk through them.

### Birds

- **Stay away from the flamingo nesting colony.** Disturbance can cause mass abandonment of nests. The colony is on hard crust over old pans that may or may not hold your weight. Watch from the salt silo, and see the structural warnings below before climbing it.
- **Bird droppings are everywhere.** Dried droppings can carry pathogens, including fungal spores and psittacosis. Don't disturb heavy accumulations or sleep in rooms coated in them. Wear your respirator if you have to go through heavily fouled spaces. Wash your hands before eating.
- **Don't handle dead or sick birds.** Large dense colonies are exactly where avian influenza outbreaks happen.
- **Marabou storks** are large, powerful birds with heavy beaks. They're not normally aggressive to humans, but don't approach them or corner them.

### Bats

Thousands pour out of the culverts at dusk. It's a spectacular sight. Watch from a distance.

- **Get rabies pre-exposure vaccination before you go.** Don't handle bats, including ones on the ground, which are often sick.
- Don't enter the culverts. Bat roosts can accumulate guano that carries histoplasmosis, and the culverts may also hold hydrogen sulfide. That's two separate reasons.

### Lizards, insects, other

The lizards are harmless and charming. The description doesn't mention mosquitoes, and the alkaline brine is hostile to most of them. Wherever the town keeps fresh water in tanks or cisterns, though, there may be mosquitoes. Bring repellent and consider whether the local rift has mosquito-borne diseases.

### Vaccinations and medicine

- Rabies pre-exposure series
- Tetanus booster, since everything is rusted and you'll scrape yourself
- Hepatitis A, and typhoid if water or food hygiene is uncertain
- A good first aid kit with wound cleaning supplies, antibiotic ointment, antifungal cream, an oral rehydration supply, eyewash, anti-diarrheal medication, and a broad-spectrum antibiotic if you can get one prescribed for emergencies. **Small cuts will not heal well** in constant wet heat with salt and droppings everywhere. Clean every scrape seriously.

## 6. Structures: Nothing Is What It Looks Like

Ninety years of salt in rebar means the concrete may look solid and be structurally hollow. Corrugated roofs are "lace-thin." Rust runs from every bolt.

- **Never lean on a handrail, especially at height.** Test railings gently and never trust your weight to them.
- **Stairs**, especially steel ones, may be corroded through underneath. Stay near the wall edge, where they're usually strongest, and test each step.
- **Don't walk on roofs.** You'll go straight through.
- **The salt silo on H Avenue** is where the description places you for the view of the colony. Ask a resident whether it's safe and how people get up. Someone local will know which ladders and stairs are still used.
- **Sunken pumphouses and flooded basements** combine structural risk, H₂S, and caustic water. Stay out.
- **Salt heave** has lifted pavement joints into low ridges. Watch your footing, especially in the dim evenings and at night.

## 7. Industrial Hazards

The plant is still running, and it's running on old equipment with limited maintenance.

- **Railways:** the narrow-gauge locomotives move at walking pace, but they share narrow berms with you. There may be no room to step aside without stepping off into mud or brine. Know where the trains run and when. If one comes, get well clear early. The standard-gauge freight is heavier and faster, so treat its track with full respect.
- **Pumps and intake canals:** big impeller pumps drawing brine means strong suction at the intakes. Never go near the intake mouths or into the canals.
- **Sluice gates:** they move with force, and water surges through them.
- **Power line:** the pylons are salt-crusted, and the wires crackle in humid air. That crackle is corona discharge. Salt-coated insulators in wet air can arc. Stay well away from the pylon bases, especially in fog or after rain, and never touch a pylon.
- **Plant interior:** only go in with a guide, if you're allowed in at all. Crushers, kilns, conveyors, hot surfaces, and dust.
- **The generators and electrical infrastructure** are exposed to the same corrosion as everything else. Don't touch old electrical fittings in buildings, even in "dead" blocks.

## 8. Your Gear Will Die

Salt, humidity, and alkali are about the worst possible combination for equipment.

- **Electronics:** sealed, waterproof, marine-rated devices only. Keep everything in dry bags with desiccant packs, and bring many spare packs, since they'll saturate quickly. Condensation will form inside anything you carry from a cooler room into the outside air. Assume ordinary phones and cameras will fail within days to weeks.
- **Lenses:** they'll fog constantly as temperature shifts. Let equipment acclimate slowly inside sealed bags.
- **Metal:** zippers, buckles, knife blades, and tools will corrode. Plastic zippers, stainless or titanium, and a small bottle of corrosion inhibitor or light oil will help. Rinse and wipe everything in fresh water every evening.
- **Batteries:** they don't love heat. Store spares in the coolest place you can find.
- **Paper:** will go limp and moldy. Waterproof notebooks. Laminated maps.
- **Navigation:** in the haze, landmarks disappear and the horizon dissolves. The grid helps in town, where the numbered streets run east toward the lake and the lettered avenues run north–south. Out on the pans, there are hundreds of identical rectangles. Carry a compass and a GPS. **The plant's orange glow at night is a reliable beacon** back to the inhabited blocks.

## 9. Sleep

This may be the hardest part of the stay.

- Nights barely cool, the air is saturated, and the pumps thud through the ground every few seconds. At first they'll feel like a pressure in your chest. Many people find that subliminal thudding oddly soothing after a night or two; others find it maddening.
- Look for lodging in the thick-walled lower rooms of the inhabited blocks near Second and C. If there's any refrigeration or air conditioning in the works, even one room, it's the most valuable thing in town. Ask about it.
- Bring a light **mosquito net anyway**, to keep flies, swallows, and whatever else off you.
- A fan, earplugs, and a sleeping mat that doesn't hold moisture (closed-cell foam, not cotton).
- Sleep will come in pieces. Nap in the midday heat, when you shouldn't be doing anything else anyway.

## 10. Getting In, and Getting Out

- The standard-gauge freight leaves **twice a week**. If you arrive by rail and plan to leave by rail, you have narrow windows and could be stuck up to four days if you miss one. Know the schedule before you arrive and plan buffer days.
- If your multiverse transit is independent of the local railway, you're less trapped. Still, make sure your exit point is somewhere you can reach in all conditions. "Out on the pans" is not a good exit point.
- Tell someone in town when you're heading onto the berms and when you expect to return. There are six hundred people in a grid built for eleven thousand. If you disappear in the empty blocks, nobody will notice for a long time.

## 11. The People

Kessel Flat is a dying company town, and its residents call it *the works*. That tells you a lot. Six hundred people have stayed where ten thousand left. They're probably plant workers, pump tenders, rail crews, and their families. There may be a few older residents who never left, and possibly company management.

- **You'll be unusual.** Visitors are probably rare. Expect curiosity, suspicion, or indifference, possibly all three from the same person on different days.
- **Ask permission** before entering the plant grounds, riding a salt train, climbing the silo, or photographing people. The company may technically own everything, including the empty blocks.
- **Bring things of value.** Fresh produce, fresh water, medicine, batteries, tools, news, and anything that doesn't grow in a place where nothing grows. Just as important, bring the patience to listen. People in shrinking towns often have a lot to say about what the place used to be.
