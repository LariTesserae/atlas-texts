# Preparing for the Warm Throat

## The Short Version

The Warm Throat is a small, dark, very hot room at the top of a waterless mountain. It is packed wall to wall with tens of millions of soft, living animals. The main dangers are not dramatic. They are **heat, bad air, dust, and your own body's water**. The main obligations are **to move slowly, take nothing, and touch as little as possible**. Most of what can go wrong here comes from staying too long, breathing too deeply, or treating the place as empty when it is completely full.

Go with a partner. Go during the mountain's exhale, not its inhale. Keep your visits short. Leave the hands alone.

---

## Part One: Before You Leave Home

### Honest self-assessment

This is not a place for everyone, and it's worth deciding that before you're standing in the cleft.

- **Heat tolerance.** Deeper in, the chamber is probably somewhere between 45 and 60°C, and hotter near the vents, with almost no humidity. Dry heat is more survivable than wet heat, but it's deceptive: you won't feel soaked, so you won't realize how fast you're losing water. If you have heart problems, blood pressure issues, take medications that affect sweating or heat regulation (many antihistamines, some antidepressants, beta-blockers, diuretics), or have had heat illness before, speak with someone qualified. You may decide to view the Throat only from the cleft.
- **Respiratory health.** The air is full of shed scales. Scales from moths and butterflies are a known allergen and irritant on many worlds, and you'll be breathing a concentrated drift of them. Asthma is a serious concern here. So is any history of insect allergy.
- **Entomophobia, claustrophobia, and darkness.** Be truthful with yourself. The cleft is narrow enough to touch both walls at once. The chamber is lightless. Everything you touch is alive and moving. People who handle crowds, caves, and insects without trouble have still found the combination overwhelming. Visitors describe the silence as either the most frightening or the most peaceful thing they've experienced, and you won't know which it will be for you until you're there. Plan as though it might be the frightening one.
- **Altitude.** The Throat sits just below a summit. Depending on the mountain's height you may be dealing with thin air on top of everything else. Heat and altitude together make you tired and foolish faster than either alone.

### Acclimatize

If you can, spend a week or two before the trip getting used to heat: saunas, hot-weather exercise, gradual exposure. Heat acclimatization is real and trainable. It improves how early and efficiently you sweat, and how well your body holds its salts. It won't make you immune, but it widens your margin.

### Medical preparation

- Get a check-up that covers heart and lungs.
- If you have *any* allergy history, carry antihistamines and an epinephrine auto-injector, and make sure your partner knows how to use it.
- Carry eye drops, preferably preservative-free lubricating drops. Your eyes will dry out within minutes.
- Carry oral rehydration salts, not just water.
- Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke: confusion, stopping sweating, headache, nausea, a strange calm. Agree with your partner in advance that either of you can end a visit for either of you without argument.

## Part Two: The Approach

### The mountain itself is dry and warm

There is nothing on the upper mountain: no water, no plants, no shade that means anything. The ground is scoria, sharp and crumbling and rust-and-charcoal colored, and it holds heat even at midnight. The shimmer in the air never fully goes away.

- **Carry all your water, for the whole round trip, plus a large reserve.** Nothing within a day's walk will resupply you. A reasonable rule is to plan for twice what you think you'll need, then add the water you'll lose inside the Throat on top of that. Inside, you may be losing a liter an hour or more.
- **Footwear matters more than usual.** You want thick-soled, heat-resistant boots with stiff soles for the sharp scoria. Avoid soles that soften or become sticky in heat; some rubbers and foams do. Gaiters help keep grit out.
- **Walk at night if you can.** The ground doesn't cool, but at least the sun isn't adding to it.
- **Pace yourself.** Arrive at the cleft rested, fed, and fully hydrated, not wrung out from the climb. Make camp at a distance from the opening, on the windward side if possible, where the air is freshest.

### Scout the mouth before you enter

Spend time outside before going in. Watch, feel, and listen.

- Hold a hand in the draft coming from the cleft. Note how hot it is. Then come back later and compare. This is how you start learning the mountain's breath.
- Look at the line of upright stones at the threshold. You don't need to know what they meant. **Step over them, not on them, and don't move them.** Whatever they were, a boundary or a warning or a welcome, they're still doing it.

## Part Three: Learning the Breath

This is the most important part of planning, and the part people most often skip.

The mountain breathes on a cycle of about five hours. Over roughly an hour, the vents grow stronger and hotter, the red seam brightens toward a smolder, and the whole living carpet climbs upward, away from the heat. Then it eases, and the carpet flows back down.

**Do not be inside during the peak.** The ash-moths live within a few degrees of their limit, and the peak kills the slow ones. It's calibrated to the edge of *their* survival, not yours. More heat means more gas, and nobody knows exactly what's in that gas.

The practical plan:

1. **Spend at least one full cycle, better two, watching from the mouth.** Time the rise and fall of heat at the cleft with a thermometer and a watch. Hang a thermometer a few paces inside on a pole if you can.
2. **Enter just after the peak has passed**, when the heat is clearly falling. You'll then have three to four hours before the next rise, which is far more time than you should spend inside anyway.
3. **Set a hard exit time**, well before the next rise begins. Use an alarm, not your judgment. Your judgment will be worse than you think by then.

A useful side effect: when the tide is going *down*, the carpet is flowing toward the floor and the vents, which means the upper walls are becoming bare. This is also when the hands on the eastern wall are fading back into the rock. If you want to *see* the hands outlined in living bodies, you'll need to be present as the tide rises past them, which means being inside closer to the dangerous part of the cycle. Decide in advance whether that's worth it to you. Many visitors conclude it's better to hear that it happens than to risk staying to watch.

## Part Four: What to Bring Inside

### Air

This is the hazard people underestimate most. The chamber is a dome with a single narrow entrance, filled with hot gas coming up from volcanic fissures. That gas smells of sulfur and flint. It could contain sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and other things. Carbon dioxide is heavy and pools low. Hydrogen sulfide deadens your sense of smell at dangerous concentrations, so the rotten-egg smell *disappearing* is a warning, not a relief.

- **Carry a multi-gas monitor** covering at least oxygen, CO₂, H₂S, and SO₂, and clip it at chest height, *not* at your belt. Turn back at the first alarm. Don't argue with it.
- **Wear a respirator.** A well-fitted half- or full-face mask with particulate filters (for the scale dust) and acid-gas cartridges is ideal. A full-face mask also protects your eyes. It will be miserably hot, and you'll want to pull it off. Don't. Keep visits short enough that you can bear it.
- **Don't crouch or lie down** near the floor or the vents. Heavy gases collect there. Keep your head high.

### Heat

- **Clothing:** long sleeves, long legs, loose-fitting, light-colored, and made of natural fibers such as wool or cotton. Synthetics can soften or melt if you brush hot rock near a vent. Covering your skin sounds backwards in heat, but in dry heat above body temperature, exposed skin *absorbs* heat from the air. Cover it.
- **Seal your openings:** tuck trousers into boots, and wear cuffs that close at the wrist and a collar that closes at the neck. The reason is in Part Five.
- **Gloves:** thin, heat-resistant, and not bulky. You'll want to feel the wall, and gloves still let you do that gently.
- **A cooling vest** with phase-change packs is worth its weight. It won't last long in this heat, so treat its lifespan as another timer.
- **Water inside:** a hydration bladder with a tube you can drink from through or under the respirator, or planned exits to drink. Sip steadily. Don't wait for thirst.

### Light

The Throat is dark, and it's supposed to be dark. Nothing in it evolved with light.

- **Bring a red headlamp on its dimmest setting**, plus a backup. Red is least disruptive and preserves your night vision.
- **Consider turning it off** once you're positioned somewhere safe. The dim red seam at the back and the blue-white ripples of the moths only become visible after a long wait in true darkness. That's most of what there is to see, and a flashlight erases it.
- **Never shine bright white light across the walls.** You don't know what it does to them, and you'd be flooding a room that hasn't known light in ten thousand years.

### Navigation and safety line

- **Tie a line at the cleft entrance and pay it out as you go.** The route is only thirty paces and one room, but that's plenty to get disoriented in the heat, the dark, and the fog of dust. The line gets you out with your eyes closed.
- **Your partner stays outside**, or at the very least one of you stays at the mouth while the other goes in. They hold the line, keep the clock, and can pull you out.
- **Agree on signals** you can give by tugging the line.

## Part Five: The Thing Nobody Warns You About

You are a source of water.

In this chamber, a single drop falling every hour or two sustains a living mound of animals straining toward it. Then you walk in: sweating, breathing out moist air, your eyes and lips and skin damp. You are probably the wettest thing that room has encountered in a very long time.

You may also be *cooler* than your surroundings. If the chamber is hotter than your body, you're a relatively cool, vertical, climbable surface. That's exactly what the ash-moths seek when the heat rises.

**Expect them to come to you.** They may climb your boots, your legs, and your back. They may gather at your wrists, collar, face, and anywhere else sweat collects. They're soft, velvety, and harmless as far as anyone knows, but tens of thousands of them pressing toward your eyes and mouth is a different experience than reading about it.

This is why you seal your cuffs and collar, and why a full-face respirator is better than a half mask. If they gather on you:

- **Don't swat or brush.** It won't work, and it will kill many of them.
- **Move slowly toward the exit.** As you move into the cooler, less crowded cleft, most will drop off and return toward the chamber.
- **Keep your mouth closed** and breathe through the respirator.

Also consider this: **if the moths are on you, the threaders may follow them.** The threaders are pale, forearm-length, many-legged hunters that move through the moth carpet like needles through wool. They hunt by feel, not sight. No one has recorded a threader biting a person, but no one has recorded much of anything. Treat them as potentially able to bite and potentially venomous. If you see the faint blue-white ring of light blooming around you, or anywhere on your body, a threader is close. Stay still and let it pass, or move slowly away. Don't grab at it. The moths' light seems to slow the threaders. Let it do its work.

## Part Six: Conduct Inside

### Walking

The floor isn't really a floor. The living layer lies on a drift of dead moths many meters thick in places. It's felt-like near the top and stone-like deeper down, and it's cut by old fissures, some still open and venting.

- **Move slowly.** The moths part around your feet like thick fur if you give them time. Fast steps crush them.
- **Test each step.** You're walking on compacted bodies over hidden cracks. Put weight down gradually.
- **Stay away from the back wall and the red seam.** That's glowing rock, hot enough to burn instantly and hot enough to ignite clothing. The floor ends there. It's the only light in the room, and it exists to tell you exactly that.
- **Don't stand on the vents** or on the lips of open fissures. The crust around them is also the moths' only food source, and your boots would scrape away what they graze.

### Places to leave alone

- **The mound under the drip.** This is the most crowded spot in the room, directly below the chimney where water condenses. Don't walk on it, don't stand over it, and don't reach into it. Don't block the drop. Don't try to catch it or look at it closely with a light. You'll be carrying more water on your body than that room receives in months. Keep that water to yourself and give them their drop.
- **The ring of stones near the drip.** Their tops are blackened and slick. You don't know what was done there, and you don't need to. Don't sit on them or move them.
- **The bowl in the niche.** It has been fired harder than any kiln could manage and is partly fused to the rock. It holds only scale-dust. Don't touch it and don't put anything in it. Resist the urge to leave an offering, however kind the impulse. Whatever you'd put in it would change the room.
- **The hands.**

### The hands

On the eastern wall, at standing height, are twenty or thirty faded handprints. When the tide rises, the moths flow over the wall but part around each print, leaving the hands bare. The threaders won't cross them. The warning-light won't cross them.

Nobody knows why. It may be something in the old pigment, or something else.

You'll probably want to put your own hand against one. **Don't.** You don't know whether whatever protects them can be rubbed away by a human palm, sweat, or a glove fiber. You don't know whether it's chemical, or something else entirely. You don't know what you might carry away on your skin, or what you might leave. There are only twenty or thirty. The room has kept them for longer than anyone remembers. Look at them in the dim light if you're there at the right moment. That's enough.

Don't scrape pigment for study. Don't try to add a print of your own. If you find yourself wondering whether the people who made them were protecting themselves, whether the prints were a kind of permission or a kind of warding, notice that the question is sufficient. You don't need to test it.

### Timing your stay

- **First visit: ten to fifteen minutes**, even if you feel fine. Especially if you feel fine.
- Come out, cool down, drink, rest for at least as long as you were inside, and check each other for heat signs.
- Later visits can be longer if you tolerated the first well, but rarely beyond thirty minutes in one stint.
- **Watch the clock and the thermometer, not your comfort.** The heat in the chamber is designed, in a sense, to be tolerable for the organisms that live within a few degrees of death. You're not one of them.

### Sitting in the dark

If you want to see what the Throat actually is, the dim red seam and the rain-on-a-black-pond ripples of blue light across the walls, you'll need to stop, stand still near the cleft end of the chamber where the air is least bad, turn off your lamp, and wait. Your eyes will take many minutes to adjust. Keep your line in your hand the whole time. Let the moths settle around your feet and don't mind if they climb a little. Listen to the silk-on-silk hush that turns into pressure in your ears.

This is where visitors report either terror or peace. Either response is ordinary. If it's terror, turn your red light back on, hold the line, and walk out slowly. There's no shame in it. The room will not be offended.

## Part Seven: Leaving

### The cleft

Walk out slowly. Moths clinging to you will drop off as the air cools toward the mouth. **Before you step over the threshold stones, stop and check yourself thoroughly**, or have your partner check you: cuffs, collar, hood, bootlaces, pack seams, folds of clothing. Gently shake out anything that holds a moth, back toward the cleft. Don't carry one out. They can't survive the cold outside, and they don't belong anywhere else.

Look for threaders especially. A forearm-long animal hiding in a pack is easy to miss.

### The cold shock

Stepping out into the night air, which is warm by any ordinary standard, will feel shockingly cold. Visitors describe the stars as seeming impossibly loud. This is real: you've been in heat and total darkness and near-silence, and now your senses are recalibrating all at once.

- **Sit down before you do anything else.** Blood pressure can drop sharply when you leave heat. Fainting on scoria is unpleasant.
- **Put on a layer.** Wet clothes and sudden cool air can chill you faster than you'd expect.
- **Drink, with salts.**
- **Remove your respirator only once you're well away from the mouth,** upwind if you can manage it.

### Decontamination

You'll be coated in scale dust. It tastes of chalk and pepper, and it will smell faintly sweet on your skin for days. There may also be the gray-green film from the vents, and possibly spores from the white filaments that grow on the dead.

For a multiverse traveler this matters more than for a local. You don't know what those organisms do elsewhere.

- **Change out of your chamber clothes before leaving camp** and bag them, sealed.
- **Wash skin, hair, and especially your face** as thoroughly as your water allows. Rinse your eyes, nose, and mouth.
- **Clean your boots.** The soles will carry the most film and filament material.
- **Don't carry out souvenirs**: no scales in a vial, no dead moth from the drift, no chip of crust, no fragment of stone. The deep drifts are a record, thousands of bands, one for every season of dying. Leave the record whole.
- Treat your bagged clothing and gear according to whatever biosecurity practice you'd use for any unknown ecosystem before crossing to another world.

### Afterward

- Watch for delayed respiratory symptoms: coughing, wheezing, tightness in the chest, or fever in the days after. Dust exposure and possible gas exposure can both show up late. Seek care if they appear, and tell whoever treats you exactly what you breathed.
- Watch for skin rashes where the dust sat longest.
- The sweet smell will linger. That's normal, and many people find they miss it once it fades.

## Part Eight: The Part That Isn't Gear

It's worth arriving with the right frame of mind, because the Throat is unusual in a way that's easy to feel and hard to describe.

It's a complete world, one room wide. Rock breathes out heat and chemistry. A film grows on the chemistry. Moths eat the film. Hunters eat the moths. Threads feed on the dead. A single drop of water falls once an hour and is just enough. The floor rises with the bodies of the dead, very slowly, forever. Everything in it is tuned to everything else within a few degrees and a few drops. It has been this way for a very long time, and it asks nothing of you.

That means the right way to visit is to be as little as possible: little light, little noise, little movement, little time, little taken, little left behind. You'll inevitably crush some moths. You'll breathe their dust and they'll drink your sweat. That's the cost of being there at all, and it's why a short visit is kinder than a long one.

The people who came before left a threshold of stones, a bowl, a ring near the water, and their hands on the wall. Whatever they understood about this place, they understood it well enough that the room still respects what they left. You don't need to understand it that well. You only need to step over the threshold carefully, leave everything where it is, stay out of the heat's peak, and walk back out into the loud, cold starlight before the mountain breathes in again.
