# Preparing to Visit the Chorus Hollow

## A Practical and Sensory Guide for the Ordinary Traveler

---

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

Most dangerous places threaten you in one obvious way. The Chorus Hollow threatens you in four at once, and each makes the others worse.

1. **The approach is a desert crossing** with no water for days.
2. **The destination is an industrial-grade noise environment** that damages hearing and, at low frequencies, reaches straight through your body.
3. **The light is a snowfield's glare turned inward**, coming from above, below, and every side.
4. **The place changes you.** The last line of the description matters: *most of them come back.* That reads less like a recommendation than a symptom.

The glare makes you rely on sound. The sound makes it hard to think. The dryness makes you tired. Tiredness makes you careless on balconies no wider than a sandal. And you can't hear anyone shout a warning.

So prepare for the combination, not for each hazard separately. Plan to arrive already rested, already hydrated, already fluent in a few hand signals, with your gear strapped down and your exit already decided.

---

## Part One: Before You Leave Home

### Get a Hearing Baseline

Have your hearing tested before you go, with a full audiogram, and again after you return. The keepers lose "the top of their hearing within a season." That means high-frequency loss, the kind that takes the consonants out of speech and leaves you nodding at conversations you can't follow. You won't notice it happening. A baseline is the only way to know what the Hollow took.

If you already have tinnitus or hearing damage, think hard about whether this trip is for you. The Hollow will not be gentle with ears that are already hurt.

### Learn to Talk with Your Hands

The keepers speak with their hands. You won't learn their sign system in advance, and it's probably local and old. What you can do is arrive with enough non-verbal fluency to be polite and safe. Practice until these are automatic:

- **Yes / No / Stop / Wait**
- **Water?** (you will ask or be asked this constantly)
- **Help** and **I'm hurt**
- **May I?** Pointing at yourself, then at the thing, with an open palm and a questioning face.
- **Thank you.** Choose one gesture and use it consistently so the keepers learn it is yours.
- **Which way out?** / **Up** / **Down**

Practice with a travel partner in a loud room, such as a bar, a machine shop, or beside a highway. You want the habit of *looking* at people when you need to communicate, because in the Hollow, turning your head toward a voice gets you nothing.

Bring a **wax tablet or small notebook with a pencil**. Not chalk: never mark the walls.

### Condition Your Body

You'll descend forty or fifty body-lengths of switchbacked stairs, some worn into dips, and climb back out in heat. Train on stairs with a loaded pack. Work on balance: single-leg stands and walking narrow beams. You want sure feet that don't need your eyes or ears to tell you where the edge is.

Fitness also buys you margin against dehydration. A fit body wastes less water doing the same work.

### Consider Your Mind

Be honest with yourself about three things:

- **How you handle sensory overload.** People prone to panic, migraines, or sensory processing difficulties may find the midday roar unbearable. It isn't merely loud. It stacks into phantom voices and underground crowds.
- **How you handle emptiness.** A maintained city of thousands of rooms with almost no one in it produces a specific eerie loneliness. Some people find it peaceful. Some find it devastating.
- **How susceptible you are to longing.** If you have ever struggled to leave something that felt good, whether a substance, a person, or a place, take the "thirst" seriously. More on this below.

## Part Two: The Crossing

### Water Is the Whole Problem

There is no well or seep for days, and **there is no water in the Hollow either.** The gardens don't need it, and the keepers don't have spare. You must carry enough for:

- the crossing in,
- your entire stay,
- the crossing out,
- plus a reserve of at least a day or two, in case you're delayed or arrive during a still season and end up pumping bellows.

The air "cracks lips in an hour." That is very low humidity, and you'll lose water through breath and skin faster than you'd guess. Budget generously: 5 to 8 liters per person per day in heat with exertion, more when climbing terraces at midday. This is heavy. Seriously consider:

- **pack animals**, if they'll tolerate the noise. They may not. Plan to leave them at a camp some distance out on the plain, with a handler or a secure picket, shade, and their own water.
- **caching water** along the route on a preliminary trip.
- **finding out how the keepers get their water.** They must drink. Someone supplies them, or they have a source far out on the plain. If there's a supply caravan, travel with it. That is the single best way to arrive.

Bring electrolytes. Pure water in this heat, without salt, can make you sick in a different way.

### Travel at Night, Arrive at Dawn

Cross the plain in the cool hours. Rest shaded through midday. Time your final approach so you **reach the rim at dawn**, the quietest hour, when the city is only a deep breathy drone from the lowest chambers.

This matters. Arriving at midday means stepping from a silent plain into full roar with no adjustment, in blinding glare, exhausted from walking in heat. Arriving at dawn lets your body and ears acclimate as the city wakes "one register at a time."

### Navigating In

You'll find it by ear before you find it by eye. That pressure in your ribs is your beacon. At night the sound will be lower and weaker but should still carry across the flat. Walk toward it. A compass or star bearing is useful backup, but the Hollow announces itself.

Note: **the hum in your ribs is the start of your exposure.** You're already absorbing low-frequency energy before you can see the rim.

## Part Three: Gear

### Hearing Protection, in Layers

This is the most important gear you carry.

- **Foam or molded earplugs**, plenty of them, since you'll lose some and grit will foul others.
- **Over-ear earmuffs** worn *over* the plugs. At midday you want both.
- **Musician's filtered plugs** for dawn and evening, when you want to actually *hear* the city's shape without being damaged.
- **A noise dosimeter** if your world has them. If not, a rule of thumb: if you have to shout to be heard at arm's length, you're taking damage without protection. In the Hollow at midday, you probably couldn't be heard shouting at all.

Here is the part no gear fully solves. **The low notes come through your body, not just your ears.** Earplugs block the ear canal. They don't stop your ribs, skull, and teeth from vibrating. Some of the Hollow's sound will reach you through bone conduction and your chest cavity no matter what. You can reduce this by standing away from the walls, not leaning against resonant stone, and not sitting on floors during the roar. Mostly you have to accept it and limit your time.

### Eye Protection

Treat this like glacier travel.

- **Category 4 glacier goggles or dark wraparound sunglasses with side shields.** Light comes from every angle, including up from the floor. Ordinary sunglasses leave gaps at the sides and bottom where reflected glare pours in.
- **Traditional slit goggles** of bone or wood are a good backup, and they work if lenses scratch in blowing grit.
- **A wide-brimmed hat helps less than you'd think**, since so much light comes from below and the sides. Still wear one.

Photokeratitis (snow blindness) is the real risk. It's a sunburn of the cornea, and it often doesn't hurt until hours later, when it feels like sand ground into your eyes. You'll lose your sight for a day or two. In a place where you're already half-deaf, being temporarily blind as well is genuinely dangerous. Don't take your goggles off at midday "just to see it properly." You can't see it properly anyway. The far side is a white blaze.

### Skin Protection

You'll burn in places that never burn. The bowl reflects light upward, so shield the **underside of your chin, your nostrils, your earlobes, under your eyes, the backs of your hands, and your palms** if you're touching stone.

- High-SPF sunscreen, reapplied often.
- Lightweight, long-sleeved, light-colored clothing.
- A neck gaiter or scarf you can pull over your lower face.
- **Heavy-duty lip balm** and **nasal saline or ointment.** Cracked lips and nosebleeds are near-certain otherwise.
- Gloves, thin ones, for handholds on sun-heated stone.

### Clothing That Stays Quiet and Still

Think about how your gear behaves in wind and vibration:

- **Nothing that flaps.** Loose fabric whips in the afternoon dust-gusts and snags on narrow balconies.
- **Nothing that whistles.** An open bottle neck, a hollow tube, or a buckle with a gap may start to sing. That's harmless, but it's disrespectful to add your own voice to an instrument someone spends their life tuning.
- **Secure everything.** Grit "walks across flat stones in slow patterns." Your loose items will walk too. Set a cup down and it may wander off a ledge. Tie things to your pack. Clip your water. Put small items in closed pouches.

### Footwear

Soft-soled, grippy shoes. Worn stone stairs, polished interior floors, and velvety groundcover on steps are all slippery in different ways. Avoid hard or hobnailed soles. They'll scar the stonework and slide on polish. Close-fitting boots are better than sandals for ankle support on the switchbacks.

### Rope

Bring a short length of cord for tying gear, and consider a light harness and a longer line if you plan to walk the narrowest balconies. Better still, just don't walk those.

### Shade

The bowl is literally described as a kiln: "sound pours up out of it like heat from a kiln," and "the stone gives back its heat" in the evening. The walls will be hot to the touch by afternoon. Bring a light shade tarp, but pitch it only up on the plain or where a keeper indicates. Don't drape fabric across doorways. You'd be muting the city.

### Things to Leave Behind, or Expect to Fail

- **Delicate electronics.** Constant vibration shakes connections loose and grinds grit into seals. Bring nothing irreplaceable.
- **Anything you'd be sad to lose off a ledge.**
- **Musical instruments.** Tempting, but don't. You'd be whispering into a cathedral organ, and you might put a keeper's careful work out of tune.
- **Any tool that cuts stone.** Don't even give the impression you might carve.

### Gifts

Arriving with something useful is the most natural introduction to people who don't talk. Consider:

- **Water.** This is almost certainly the most valuable thing you can offer.
- **Fine abrasive sand, good clay, or a well-made hard-stone chisel.** These are their working materials.
- **Leather in good condition** for bellows repair.
- **Durable food**, dried and dense.
- **Fiber cord.**

Offer by gesture: place it down, step back, open your palms. Don't be hurt if a keeper accepts without ceremony. Their hands may be too busy for thanks.

## Part Four: Arriving and Descending

### The Rim

Stop at the rim before you go down. This is your chance to acclimate. Sit a few paces back from the edge, since the full volume rolls up and over it, and let the dawn drone settle into you. Notice where the sound pressure sits in your body.

Watch for a hawk circling and veering "as though struck." Something near the rim, maybe a standing wave or a focused infrasonic blast, disturbs creatures in the air above it. Don't stand right at the lip during the roar. You might feel dizzy, nauseated, or struck by a sudden dread with no source. That's the sound, not a premonition.

### Finding a Keeper

Try to meet a keeper before going deep. You won't find them by calling out. Look for movement, a figure with a bag of sand or a bellows cord, or feel along a wall for the rhythm of someone grinding. Approach slowly and visibly from the front. They can't hear you coming, so don't startle someone standing on a narrow ledge.

Use your gestures. Show your gift. Then let them decide where you're allowed to go. They may not care at all. They may assign you a sleeping place. They may put you to work.

### Descending

Go down slowly. Each terrace is louder than the last, and the bottom chambers carry the deepest notes. On your first day, **don't go all the way to the lowest terrace at midday.** Build up to it over days if at all. Treat depth like altitude: go down, come back up, sleep higher.

## Part Five: Living in the Hollow's Day

Plan your hours around the city's weather.

### Dawn: Your Best Hours

The quietest time. Use it for moving between terraces, eating, drinking, communicating, and looking around with the lightest ear protection. The light is gentler at low sun angles. If you want to explore the deep rooms, the honeycomb going back farther than anyone has mapped, do it now, with a keeper's permission and a way to mark your path that doesn't mark the stone. Tie a cord at the entrance, or count turns and write them down.

**Never go deep into the unmapped honeycomb alone.** If you get lost in there as the roar builds, you'll be in a disorienting maze of sound, unable to hear anyone searching for you and unable to be heard. Light will be bright but directionless, and you won't find your way by shadows because there aren't any.

### Mid-Morning: Settle In

As the terraces wake, find your position for the day. Put full protection in before the middle registers are all singing. Hydrate heavily now, ahead of the heat.

### Midday: Endure, Don't Explore

This is the roar. Your goals are simple: stay protected, stay still, stay hydrated, stay away from edges.

- Sit somewhere stable, ideally a place a keeper has shown you, away from the loudest apertures.
- Don't lean your back or head against the walls.
- **Expect auditory phantoms.** Stacked harmonics will make you hear crowds cheering, voices, maybe your name. These are not real. Don't follow them.
- **Expect your thoughts to go quiet.** Visitors say this is part of the experience, and it can be profound. But it also means your judgment is impaired. Make no decisions about routes, depth, or climbing during the roar.
- You can't look across the bowl. You'll be tempted to navigate by sound. Don't navigate at all. Wait.

If you feel nausea, pressure in your head, chest tightness, vertigo, or panic, go **up**, not down, and toward the rim where the sound thins. If it's severe, get onto the plain.

Midday is when the horn-shaped flowers open and the moths feed. If you're somewhere safe, this is worth witnessing, even if you mostly feel rather than hear it.

### Afternoon: Watch the Gusts

Dust-gusts arrive suddenly and swirl. They'll shriek up individual chimneys and hurl grit at your face. This is the most physically dangerous time on narrow ledges: a sudden push of wind, a burst of grit in your goggles, a shriek right beside your head. **Stay off balconies and bridges in the afternoon.** Keep your face covered.

### Evening: Listen

The long fall. The heat bleeds out of the stone and the air reverses. Visitors hear mourning. Keepers hear eating. This is the second-best time to experience the city without damage. Switch to filtered plugs and actually listen. You'll start to hear its moods.

The stone will be radiating heat, so don't sit on it immediately.

### Night

Sleep where the keepers suggest. Don't pick an empty singing room for yourself. Your body inside a chamber changes its note, and you might damp a room the gardens depend on. Some keepers probably sleep in quieter or deliberately dead chambers. Follow their lead, or sleep up on the rim or plain if permitted.

Wear earplugs to sleep even though night is quieter. The drainage drone from the deep chambers continues, and continuous low sound disturbs sleep in ways you may not notice until you're exhausted by day three.

## Part Six: Conduct

The Hollow is not a ruin. It is a working instrument, maintained by people who have given their bodies to it. Behave as you would inside someone else's precision workshop, or a temple.

### Do Not

- **Touch, pick, lean on, or prune the plants.** Pruning is tuning, and only the keepers know the tuning. A vine you move could flatten a doorway. A bulb you bruise may lose the sound it's storing.
- **Block apertures.** No gear in doorways, no fabric over windows, no sitting in narrow window slits.
- **Mark the stone.** No chalk, no scratches, no initials. Even a pack's metal frame scraping a wall is damage.
- **Pack, plug, or "fix" anything**, even if something seems cracked and flat. That is their work, and silence here is catastrophe. Your good intentions could cause drought on a terrace.
- **Harm the lizards or catch the moths.** The moths change the flowers' notes. They're part of the song.
- **Make deliberate noise.** Shouting, whistling, and drumming are probably inaudible and certainly pointless, but they may be considered rude, or they may interfere with a keeper who is listening through their jaw for a flaw in a wall.

### Do

- **Watch how the keepers touch the walls.** Palms flat, jaws near the stone. If one invites you to feel which note a wall carries, accept. This is how you begin to understand the place.
- **Walk softly and stay on the swept paths.**
- **Offer labor** if it seems welcome: carrying sand, holding cords, fetching.
- **Learn their signs.** Mirror them. Show you're trying.
- **Respect their silence** about the carvings and the city's purpose. They don't say, or don't know. Don't pester.

## Part Seven: If You Arrive During a Still Season

You might arrive to find the wind has failed. The city will be eerily quiet, the gardens gray and wilting, bulbs wrinkling, and every keeper working bellows in the lowest chambers in shifts.

**This is an emergency, and you should help if you're able.** This is the one real catastrophe the Hollow knows, and every pair of arms at the bellows matters. It's heavy, repetitive, exhausting work in hot, dry air. Pace yourself and drink constantly. Keepers work until they collapse. You shouldn't. A visitor who faints on a bellows is one more problem, not one more worker.

Your water reserve becomes critical here. Stillness can last days, and you may be pumping far longer than you planned to stay. This is another reason to carry more than you think you need.

If you can't help, **don't stay just to watch.** You'll be drinking water that might be needed. Leave something useful behind and go.

When you see the keepers dropping asleep where they stand, the wind is coming back. That is a beautiful thing to witness. You will feel the city wake up in your chest.

## Part Eight: Health Watch

Keep a mental checklist, or better, a written one, since the roar steals thought.

| Sign | What it may mean | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Ringing in ears that doesn't fade by morning | Hearing damage accumulating | Reduce exposure, add protection, consider leaving |
| Speech sounding muffled after the roar | Temporary threshold shift | Rest in quiet, and don't go back down the next day |
| Nausea, vertigo, chest pressure | Low-frequency exposure | Go up and out; rest on the plain |
| Sudden unease, dread, sense of presence | Infrasound, and entirely normal | Name it to yourself; don't act on it |
| Gritty, burning eyes hours later | Photokeratitis | Darkness, cold compress, and no light for a day |
| Headache, dark urine, confusion | Dehydration | Drink with electrolytes, and stop exertion |
| Nosebleeds, cracked lips | Dry air | Saline, ointment, cover face |
| Losing track of time, reluctance to leave | The "thirst" beginning | See below |
