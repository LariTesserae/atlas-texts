# Preparing to Visit the Throat of Seven Landings

## The Short Version

You are going to a small, very hot, very loud, very old place. People work there, it is full of animals, and it is held together by family obligation and habit. It is not a monument. It is closer to someone's kitchen, workshop, church and town hall stacked seven stories deep into the ground. The main dangers are ordinary ones: heat, slippery stone, bad water, animal bites, and offending people. The main reward is spending a day inside something that has been lived in so long it has started to breathe.

Prepare your body for the stairs, your ears for the noise, your gut for the water, and yourself for being a guest in a place where every brick belongs to somebody.

---

## Part One: Before You Leave

### Physical conditioning

Two hundred steps down is easy. Two hundred steps up, in heat, after a day of noise and smoke, with dehydration creeping in, is not. The treads are also worn into bowls, so you will not be stepping onto flat surfaces. You will be stepping into shallow dishes, and that works the ankles and knees in unfamiliar ways.

- For a few weeks beforehand, climb stairs, ideally uneven ones: stadium steps, trail steps, anything irregular.
- Strengthen your ankles. Balance exercises help a lot.
- If you have bad knees, bring a brace. Trekking poles are not advised. They clatter, and the noise will be amplified, which is rude (see *Sound*). They also take up space the pillared galleries don't have.

### Medical preparation

Consult whatever passes for a travel physician in your home reality, and take the following seriously:

- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** This is the most important item. The Throat holds thousands of bats, a troop of thieving monkeys, and town dogs. A monkey bite or a bat scratch in a place like this is a real risk, and post-exposure treatment may not exist there at all.
- **Hepatitis A, typhoid, tetanus.** Standard for anywhere with shared water and old stone that can cut you.
- **Respiratory caution.** Accumulated pigeon and bat droppings can carry fungal spores (histoplasmosis and similar). The deepest, stillest gallery where the bats roost is the worst place for this. If you are immunocompromised, or have asthma or lung trouble, bring a light cloth mask and avoid that gallery. The smoke column adds to the burden.
- **Stagnant-water parasites.** A shallow, warm, slow-fed pool with frogs, snails, algae and laundry in it may harbor parasites that enter through skin. Do not wade in unless locals clearly do so routinely and without harm, and even then think twice. Do not put open cuts in the water.
- **Ear protection.** See below. This is not optional.

### Learning before you go

If you can find anyone who has been there, ask them to teach you the hand signals. You already know two:

- **Flat palm:** *wait*
- **Two fingers tapped to the collarbone:** *water's coming*

The second one matters. It likely means a full bucket or a loaded carrier is coming through, and you need to flatten yourself out of the way. Learn to recognize it instantly. Ask for any others: *yes*, *no*, *thank you*, *how much*, *sorry*, *where*. In the Throat, a stranger who can sign a polite *thank you* will be treated very differently from one who shouts it.

Also learn the local words for *water*, *thank you*, *sorry*, *jar*, and *whose turn*. Practice saying them quietly.

## Part Two: What to Pack

### Clothing

- **Loose, light, long-sleeved clothing** in muted or earthy colors. It protects you from the sun on the rim and the upper stairs, it is modest (which is always a safe default among working people), and it shows droppings, dye and soot less. **Do not wear anything you love.** The indigo on the fourth landing is described as *permanent*, and it runs in fans down the steps. You will get some on you.
- **A head covering** for the rim and the open stair. At midday the stair empties because the heat is punishing. Believe the locals.
- **A second set of clothes** in a bag, for the walk back through town.

### Footwear

This is the most important clothing decision you will make.

- **Soft-soled, flexible, grippy shoes or sandals,** ideally with rubber or a similar natural sole. You want to feel the bowl-shaped treads and grip wet plaster, algae and polished stone.
- **Not hard boots.** They are loud on stone, and they don't grip well on worn, curved surfaces.
- **Something you can take off easily.** You may find people are barefoot on certain landings, especially the bottom one and the slab. Watch what others do and match them.
- The lowest landings are damp in the morning, with moisture beading and running down the walls, and the pool's edge is lined with algae. Expect slipperiness.

### Hearing protection

- **Earplugs, several pairs,** preferably the kind that lower volume evenly without muffling everything (musicians' earplugs, if your world has them).
- Wear them by default and take one out when you need to listen. The description says a newcomer's ears ring after an hour. A full day could leave you with lasting damage, or at least a severe headache and exhaustion.

### Water and food

- **Bring your own drinking water, plenty of it,** or a reliable purification method: boiling, filtration and chemical treatment together if you can. The well water has been drunk for centuries, but local people have gut flora and immunities you don't.
- Drawing water from the well yourself is a social act as well as a practical one (see *Water*). Don't do it casually.
- **Electrolyte salts.** You will sweat heavily on the climbs.
- **Simple, wrapped, quiet food.** Crinkly packaging makes the kind of noise the Throat amplifies. Cloth-wrapped flatbread, dried fruit and nuts are better.
- **Keep food out of sight.** The monkeys steal from the potters, who presumably guard their things carefully. A tourist with a visible snack is a gift to them.

### Other gear

- A **small cloth bag with a closure,** worn across the body. No open baskets or dangling items.
- A **light cloth mask or scarf** for the smoke, dust and droppings.
- A **small light source** for the deep galleries. Keep it dim and point it downward. Don't sweep it across roosting bats.
- **Hand-washing supplies.** You will touch droppings-streaked brick without meaning to.
- A **basic first-aid kit:** antiseptic, bandages for scraped shins (the pillars are close together, and broad people turn sideways), and something for headache.
- **Small trade goods or local currency.** Jars, rope and cloth change hands in the afternoon. You will likely want a jar. Clay water jars thrown from the Throat's own clay are probably the most meaningful souvenir you could take home, and buying one supports the people whose space you're walking through.
- **A small offering, if you choose to leave one.** See *The Niches* below before you decide.

## Part Three: Timing Your Visit

The Throat runs on a daily cycle. Plan around it.

**Best time to arrive: just before dawn.**
You'll see the well "breathe in," the hand's-breadth rise of water. You'll hear the frogs fall silent, and then the swifts pour out in screaming spirals. This is probably the most extraordinary sight the place offers, and it is before the heat. Morning mist may be sitting on the lowest landing like wool. Be at the rim or on the upper flights for this. Don't crowd the pool, where people are anxiously working out whose turn it is for the first bucket.

**Morning:** This is the loudest time, with laundry, hauling and dyeing all going. Good for watching, bad for talking. Earplugs in. Keep to the edges of the stairs and out of the carriers' way.

**Midday:** Do what everyone else does and **retreat into a gallery.** Do not sit on the open stair in the sun because it's empty and looks peaceful. It's empty because it's dangerous. Ask permission before settling in a gallery (see below), and take the chance to rest your ears and drink water.

**Afternoon:** Trading on the landings. This is the best time to buy a jar, some rope or dyed cloth, and to talk to people.

**Dusk:** The swifts go in, and the bats rise in a "dark rope" up the shaft. **Start your climb before this, or wait it out in a gallery well away from the bottom roost.** Don't stand in the flight path. It's thousands of animals. Watch it from the rim if you can. It's said to be the only time the Throat's noise changes key, and you'll feel it more than hear it.

**Night:** Unless someone local invites you to stay, leave. It's the quietest time, but also dark, slippery, full of animals, and home to a rat snake the length of a man. The occasional person sleeping in a gallery there belongs there. You don't.

**Weather:** If it rains, which is rare, go. The treads fill with water, the children splash, and the pool rises. It will likely be a festive time. It will also be the slipperiest the stairs ever get.

**Special days:** If your visit falls on the **annual reading of the names of the dead,** consider whether you should come at all. If you do, stay at the rim, stay silent, and stand behind the townspeople, not among them. This is their day.

## Part Four: Moving Through the Throat

### The descent

- **Step into the center of each bowl.** The worn troughs are deepest in the middle, where your foot naturally lands. Stepping on the lip is unstable.
- **Keep to one side** so carriers can pass. Assume anyone carrying water has absolute right of way, always.
- **Watch for the signal:** two fingers to the collarbone means get out of the way *now*.
- **Don't touch the tufts of grass** in the treads. They grow where feet don't go, so they're a good guide to where people *don't* walk. Either follow the pattern or leave the grass alone.
- **Watch the indigo stains** around the fourth landing. Old ones are part of the stone, fresh runoff will stain your shoes, and the overflow channels hold frogs.

### The galleries

- **They are workspaces and near-homes.** Treat each one as you'd treat walking through someone's open-fronted workshop. Pause at the edge, make eye contact, and sign or softly ask permission.
- **The pillars are tight.** Turn sideways. Keep your bag in front of you. Don't brush carved pillars with your pack. Those carvings are ancestors' work.
- **Don't step over or across a potter's wheel, a dye vat, or rope being twisted.** Go around, or wait.
- **Don't touch finished jars,** which are wet and drying, unless you're invited to handle one you're buying.
- **Never pick up clay from the pool's edge.** It's the only fine clay for a day's walk. It is a resource, and almost certainly an owned or rationed one.

### The fig roots

The pale roots hanging down three landings look like the perfect handhold or climbing rope. **Do not use them.** They're the monkeys' highway, so you'd be putting yourself in the troop's path. They're also the subject of a generation-long dispute between families. Touching, pulling on or cutting them could look like taking a side. Admire them from a distance. If anyone asks your opinion about whether they should be cut, the answer is always some version of *"That isn't for a visitor to say."*

## Part Five: Sound Etiquette

This is where outsiders embarrass themselves most, so give it real attention.

- **Speak low and clipped.** Don't raise your voice to be heard over the noise, because that just makes more noise. Local people lower their pitch and shorten their words so they cut through. Imitate them.
- **Set things down gently.** Your bag, your water container, your shoes. A jar set down carelessly at the bottom rings out at the rim like a struck bell. Anything you drop will be heard by everyone.
- **Don't whistle, sing, clap or shout to test the echo.** Every tourist in every reality wants to do it. Don't. The people there are working in a building that is itself an instrument, and you'd be playing it without asking.
- **Use hand signals whenever you can.**
- **Earplugs in, one ear out** when you need to follow a conversation.
- If people seem terse or unsmiling, it probably isn't rudeness. They're conserving sound. A small nod does more work here than a loud greeting.

### The slab

The dark polished stone at the exact center of the bottom landing is **the most important place in the Throat.** It's the only spot where speech carries clearly to the rim. It is a courthouse, an altar and a town crier's platform all in one.

- **Do not stand on it.** Walk around it. Its worn dish shows how many people have stood "where it matters." You're not one of them yet.
- **Do not speak on it**, even as a test, even quietly. Anything you say is broadcast to the whole town. Imagine walking into a legislature and reading out your grocery list over the public address system.
- If a dispute, announcement or marriage is happening there, **stop what you're doing and be still** until it's finished. Remove your earplugs if you can do so discreetly. It's respectful to actually listen.
- The one exception: if a local elder explicitly invites you onto it, for instance to greet the town or to be introduced, accept humbly, say something short and grateful, and step off.

## Part Six: The Water

- **Don't drink the well water untreated.** Hosts may offer it. If they do, accept the cup graciously, then make a quiet decision based on your purification kit and your gut. If you have to drink to avoid giving offense, drink a little and treat your stomach afterward. It's better to politely explain in advance that your body is "from far away."
- **Never try to draw water yourself, and never try to draw the first bucket at high mark.** There is a "quiet, constant, argued-over system" for this. Stepping into it would be like jumping a line where every place has been negotiated for generations.
- **Don't wade in the pool** unless explicitly invited and you've considered the parasite risk.
- **Don't wash yourself or your things at the laundry stones** unless invited.
- **Don't fish the silver fish, don't catch them, don't touch them.** Nobody knows why, and nobody needs to. This is one of those rules where the fact that nobody remembers the reason is itself the reason to obey it.
- **Don't scrub off the algae** or pull the maidenhair ferns. The algae is scrubbed on a weekly rhythm by specific people, and the ferns grow on seep lines that should be left alone.

## Part Seven: The Niches and the Bricks

### The niches

Hundreds of loaf-sized niches line the upper flights, filled with offerings: beads, cloth knots, ash, thumbprinted mud coins. They are accumulating slowly, like coral.

- **Never remove anything from a niche.** Not a bead, not a fleck of cloth, not a souvenir. These are acts of devotion or memory, some of them centuries old.
- **Don't touch the eroded carvings.** They've been worn into "suggestions" by weather and time. Your hands would speed that along.
- **Leaving your own offering is a real question.** It may be a kind gesture, or it may be presumptuous. Ask someone. If they say yes, leave something small, humble and made of local materials: a knot of plain cloth, a clay bead bought in the Throat itself. **Do not leave anything from your own world** (plastic, metal, anything foreign). You would be adding a permanent piece of your reality to a structure that is slowly fusing its offerings into stone.

### The bricks

Every brick carries a family mark, and each family maintains the wall its ancestors built.

- **Never pry at a loose brick,** even to "help." It belongs to someone.
- **Don't lean heavily against the patched sections.**
- **Don't photograph or copy family marks** without asking. They may be something like a coat of arms, a signature or a sacred sign.
- If you notice a crumbling brick, you may quietly mention it to someone who works there. They'll know whose it is.

You may find yourself wishing you had a mark of your own. You don't, and that is how it should be. You're passing through a place built by families who never left.

## Part Eight: The Animals

### Monkeys

- Assume they will steal. **Keep bags closed and zipped or tied,** keep food hidden, and carry nothing shiny in your hands.
- **Don't make eye contact, don't bare your teeth** (smiling can read as a threat), and **don't feed them.**
- If one grabs something, **let it go.** Nothing in your bag is worth a bite in a place where rabies treatment may not exist.
- Stay away from the fig roots, which are their road.

### Bats

- **Don't enter the deepest roosting gallery.** It's still, dark, and full of bats and droppings.
- **Don't shine lights at them.**
- At dusk, **stay out of the shaft's central column** while they rise.
- **Never handle a bat,** even one that's grounded and looks helpless. That is precisely the bat most likely to be sick.

### Swifts and pigeons

- Harmless, but bring a covering for your head and shoulders on the upper flights. The pigeons' droppings streak everything.
- The dawn and dusk swift eruptions are overwhelmingly loud. Earplugs in.

### Kestrels

- Enjoy watching them stoop into the shaft. Don't interfere.

### Geckos and frogs

- Harmless. The geckos' clicking is pleasant. At night the frogs' calls may sound like cattle lowing in the stone. You'll have been told, so you won't panic.
- Don't handle the frogs. Some frogs anywhere may secrete irritants, and the dye channels are stained.

### The rat snake

- It's seen about twice a year, and a sighting is discussed for a month. If you see it, **keep still, give it space, and let it go on its way.** Rat snakes are usually harmless to adults, but a snake as long as a person can still bite defensively.
- If you see it, you will have just witnessed an event. People will want to hear about it. Tell them quietly, precisely and humbly. You may have just given the town a month's conversation.

### Dogs

- Town dogs are around and fight with the monkeys. Don't pet them, and don't provoke them.

## Part Nine: Social Conduct

- **Be a visitor, not a spectator.** People here are working. The quickest way to earn goodwill is to buy something, stay out of the way, and show you respect what they do.
- **Ask before you watch someone working closely.** Most artisans don't mind being watched once asked. Many will mind if you don't ask.
- **Pay fair prices.** Haggling may be the norm. Watch how locals do it. Don't haggle aggressively over a water jar that took skill and scarce clay to make.
- **Don't try to settle arguments, offer "solutions," or ask provocative questions** about the fig, the water turns, or which family owns what.
- **Accept hospitality simply.** A cup of water, a place in the shade of a gallery, a seat on a mat. Thank them with the hand gesture if you know it.
- **Children** will likely be curious. Keep your interactions warm and quiet, and don't hand out gifts, which can create a scramble and noise.
- **Photography and recording:** ask before photographing people, and never record voices on the slab. If audio recording is possible in your world, the Throat's soundscape is extraordinary, and you should still only record with permission and never private speech.

## Part Ten: What It Will Actually Feel Like

Prepare yourself emotionally too, because the Throat is overwhelming in ways that are hard to predict.

**The first moment:** You'll be walking across flat, blinding heat, and suddenly the ground stops at a low brick lip. You'll look down into a rectangle of shadow and noise and bronze-lit smoke. You may feel vertigo, not because it's deep but because it's so *full.* Give yourself a minute at the parapet before you start down.

**The sensory flood:** Clacking pots, creaking pulleys, the crack of laundry, grinding wheels, burbling pigeons. Everything echoes, and the echoes echo. After half an hour you may feel disoriented, even a little frantic. This is normal. Sit in a gallery, put in your earplugs, drink water and breathe. The locals have built their whole manner of speaking around this. You can't adapt in a day, but you can pace yourself.

**The coolness:** Going down, the temperature drops, the air grows damp, and the plaster turns soft and cool like fruit skin. It will feel like relief. Don't let the relief fool you into forgetting to drink.

**The smell:** Probably wood smoke, wet clay, lime, dye (often sharp or fermented), animal droppings, damp stone, and people working hard in heat. It may take some getting used to.

**The weight of it:** At some point, maybe when you spot a thumbprint in a brick that's been there for longer than your family's recorded history, or when a woman taps her collarbone and you step aside without thinking, the place will stop seeming like a site and start seeming like a *body.* Something that breathes each dawn, that sheds and regrows, that people tend like a relative. Many travelers find this moving. Some find it unsettling. Both are fair.

**The climb out:** It will be harder than you expect. Go slowly. Rest at the landings. When you finally reach the parapet and step onto flat ground, with the Throat's roar behind you and the ordinary silence of the heat-struck town in front of you, the quiet may sound strange for a while, almost as though something is missing. Your ears may ring for hours. That's a sign you were really there.

## A Short List of Absolutes

1. **Get vaccinated against rabies beforehand.**
2. **Bring and wear hearing protection.**
3. **Don't drink untreated water.**
4. **Water carriers always have right of way.**
5. **Don't stand on or speak from the slab.**
6. **Take nothing from the niches, the walls or the pool.**
7. **Don't touch the fig or take sides about it.**
8. **Don't fish the silver fish.**
9. **Don't make noise for its own sake.**
10. **Get out of the sun at midday, and leave before dark.**

Do these things, arrive before dawn, buy a jar in the afternoon, watch the bats rise from the rim, and you'll leave with your health, your welcome and your hearing intact, and with a memory of a place that sounds like nowhere else.
