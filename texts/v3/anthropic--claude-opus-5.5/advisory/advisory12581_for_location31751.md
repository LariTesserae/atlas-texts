# Preparing to Visit the Breathing Quarter

## First Impressions: What You're Actually Walking Into

This place is beautiful, and it is also a hot, gas-venting, acoustically violent cave with an unstable ceiling. The emptiness makes it feel safe. The dust lies undisturbed, nothing moves with intent, nothing will hunt you. That feeling is misleading.

The city itself will not hurt you. The builders made it for bodies, and bodies like yours once walked those channels. The builders, though, were adapted to this place, and they are gone. What remains keeps running on physics: heat rising, gas seeping, pressure shifting, stone cycling between expansion and contraction, plants pressurizing their seed cases until they burst. None of it is malicious, and none of it will make allowances for you.

Plan to visit it the way you'd visit an active volcanic cave system: in short, planned sorties, with a partner, with instruments, and with a clear way out.

---

## 1. Before You Go: Your Body

### Heat acclimatization
Start two to three weeks before departure. Daily sessions in dry heat (a sauna, a hot climate, or a heat chamber) teach your body to sweat earlier and more efficiently and to hold on to electrolytes. It won't make you safe, but it shifts the point at which you start to fail.

Take the heat seriously. The hollow is the lowest point for a long way around, with fire burning underneath it. The ambient air near the floor is probably well above anything you've experienced outdoors, possibly in the 50–65°C range. Near the fissures and the planted basins it will be dramatically hotter. Glowing rock means surface temperatures of several hundred degrees.

The one mercy is the dryness. In bone-dry air, sweat evaporates instantly and cools you efficiently, which is why people can sit in an 80°C sauna and live. The cost is water. You will lose it at a startling rate, possibly more than a litre an hour when exerting, and you won't feel wet at all. There will be no dripping sweat to warn you. The sweat vanishes as it forms.

### Fitness
You'll be wading knee- to waist-deep through dense, rustling vegetation in sunken channels, climbing dished stairs, and possibly crossing slab bridges. You'll do it in protective gear, carrying water. Train for load-bearing hikes in heat.

### Medical check
Anyone with heart conditions, respiratory problems (especially asthma), epilepsy, or heat sensitivity should think very hard about going. The combination of heat, possible sulphur gases, airborne resin dust, pollen, and intense infrasound is a stress test for the body. Discuss antihistamines with a doctor; you'll be breathing unfamiliar pollen.

## 2. Before You Go: Your Mind

The place has a strange psychological weight. It's worth knowing in advance how it may affect you.

- **The emptiness is uncanny.** Rooms open into rooms into rooms, all bare. It was cleared deliberately, and you'll feel the deliberateness. Some people find that peaceful. Others find it deeply unsettling, like walking through a house after a funeral where someone has already packed everything away.
- **Upward shadows** from the ember-light under doorways and lintels trigger an old, primitive unease. Faces lit from below look wrong, your companion's included.
- **Heat degrades judgment.** It happens gradually and you won't notice it. You'll feel fine, then you'll feel a little stupid, then you'll make a bad decision about the route back. Build your plans so they don't depend on your judgment late in a sortie.
- **The breathing is overwhelming.** Hundreds of tones, loud enough that you can't hear yourself shout, with drones felt in the ribs. Low-frequency sound can produce anxiety, nausea, dread, and a sense of presence even in people who know exactly what's causing it. Expect to feel afraid even if you're not in danger. Knowing that ahead of time helps a great deal.

## 3. Timing: Learn the Breath Before You Enter

The single most important piece of planning is **knowing when the hollow will breathe.** It happens twice a day, driven by the desert above heating and cooling, and lasts about an hour. That probably puts it somewhere in the morning warm-up and the evening cool-down, but don't guess.

**Spend at least a full day, ideally two, camped on the salt flat observing the crack before you go down.** Watch the shimmer over it. Hang a light streamer or a strip of fabric on a pole beside the opening and log when it lifts and how hard. You'll see a pattern: a steady baseline shimmer, then the surge. Write down the times. Note how they shift as the days change.

Why this matters:

- **The entrance throat is the exhaust pipe.** During the breathing, the whole city's air is pulled up and out, and at least some of that flow likely goes through the passage you came down. You do not want to be halfway up a narrow stone throat when an hour of hot, grit-laden, gas-enriched wind starts pouring through it. Plan every entry and exit well clear of a breath.
- **The breath brings fresh gas.** When the old air is drawn out, new hot gas rushes up through the fissures to replace it. This is when concentrations of whatever comes from below will be at their highest.
- **The breath is also the reason many people come.** You'll want to experience it deliberately, from a chosen spot, rather than be caught by it somewhere bad.

## 4. The Air: Your Most Serious Hazard

The plants live on "a thin breath of sulphur and hydrocarbons." Something is burning below. The hollow is a low, enclosed basin. Put those facts together and assume the air may be trying to kill you in several quiet ways.

- **Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S).** It smells of rotten eggs at low levels. At dangerous levels it paralyses your sense of smell, so the smell disappears just as the danger peaks. The sweet, scorched-sugar smell of the place may also mask it.
- **Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide** from the deep mineral fire. CO is odourless. CO₂ is heavier than air and pools in low places, like the sunken street channels you'll be walking in.
- **Oxygen depletion.** Methane and other gases can displace oxygen without any smell at all.
- **Flammable gas pockets.** Hydrocarbons plus glowing rock are a combination that should make you nervous.

The builders lived here and the plants are presumably scrubbing the air, so the baseline is probably breathable. Pockets won't be, and the breath will change things.

### Equipment
- **A four- or five-gas personal monitor** (O₂, H₂S, CO, combustibles/LEL, ideally CO₂) clipped at chest height. **Its alarm must vibrate or flash.** You will not hear an audible alarm during the breathing, and possibly not over the constant seed-pops and rustling either.
- **A second monitor held low**, or checked periodically at knee height, since the channels are exactly where heavy gases collect.
- **An escape respirator with its own air supply**, a 10–15 minute emergency unit, worn on you at all times, not in a pack. If the monitor alarms, you put it on and walk upward and toward fresh flow. Practise donning it blind until you can do it in under ten seconds.
- **A good particulate respirator (P100/FFP3) with an acid gas cartridge** for general wear. It handles grit, resin dust, pollen, and low-level sulphur irritation. It will make the heat worse, so choose a low-resistance model and accept the trade.

### Rules
- **No open flame. Ever.** No matches, lighters, stoves, or smoking. Everything here is oil: the resin, the dust, the plants. The builders had no hearths for good reason.
- **Don't linger in the deepest channels or directly over fissures.** The planted basins sit exactly over the vents, which makes them the hottest and gassiest spots in the city and the ones you'll most want to look at closely. Look briefly. Then step back.
- **Watch your partner for symptoms.** Headache, confusion, clumsiness, flushed or unusually pale skin, sudden fatigue. Heat illness and gas exposure look similar, and both mean the same thing: leave.

## 5. Heat and Dryness: Managing Your Body Down There

### Water
There is none. There has never been any. The description is explicit that nothing in the hollow has ever been wet, so carry everything.

- Budget **at least 1–1.5 litres per hour inside**, plus reserve. For a two-hour sortie, carry four litres minimum.
- Use **insulated bottles or bladders**. Uninsulated water will soon be hot enough to be unpleasant, and hot water is hard to drink in quantity.
- **Add electrolytes to every drink.** Drinking large amounts of plain water while sweating heavily can dangerously dilute your blood sodium.
- **Drink on a schedule, not when thirsty.** Thirst lags far behind need in dry heat.

### Cooling
- A **phase-change cooling vest** (the kind that stays solid around 15–20°C, not ice) will extend your working time considerably. Keep spares on the surface in an insulated box.
- Consider a cooling neck wrap, though evaporative cooling will work almost too well here and dry out within minutes.

### Sortie length
Plan **60–90 minutes inside at a time**, then return to the surface to cool and rehydrate. Longer visits are possible for the acclimatized, but the hollow punishes optimism. Your turn-around time is fixed before you go in and is not negotiable once inside.

### Skin, eyes, lips
"Skin tightens within minutes. Lips split."
- **Lip protection**: a heavy balm or ointment. Be aware it will collect grit, so wipe and reapply.
- **Heavy, unscented moisturizer** on exposed skin before entry. It won't last long, but it helps.
- **Nasal saline gel.** Your nasal passages will crack and bleed otherwise.
- **Lubricating eye drops.** **Don't wear contact lenses.** They'll dry onto your eyes, and grit behind a lens is miserable.
- Expect nosebleeds anyway.

## 6. Clothing

The challenge is protecting against radiant heat from below, hot surfaces, grit, resin, and seed shrapnel while still letting sweat evaporate.

- **Fire-resistant fabric** (aramid/Nomex-type, or tightly woven wool). **No synthetics like polyester or nylon next to the skin.** Near a glowing fissure, synthetics can melt onto you.
- **Loose, full-coverage, light-coloured clothing**, long sleeves and long trousers. You'll be wading through plants whose sap is thick oil and whose effect on skin is unknown.
- **Reflective or heat-resistant gaiters or lower-leg covers.** The heat comes from *below*, so your legs, feet, and the underside of your body take the worst of it. Most heat gear protects against heat from above.
- **Boots with thick, heat-rated soles.** Firefighter or foundry boots, or at least boots with soles rated for hot surfaces. The floor is warm everywhere and dangerously hot near the vents.
- **Gloves**: heat-resistant, and resin-resistant on the outside. The resin beads at every wound and hardens into glossy knobs, but fresh resin is sticky, and you'll touch walls.
- **Helmet.** Non-negotiable (see the ceiling section).
- **Sealed goggles**, not glasses. Goggles handle grit during the breath, seed-pod fragments, and dryness all at once. Choose anti-fog and wide-view.
- **A light head and neck covering** under the helmet for grit.

## 7. Hearing and Communication

During the breathing, the city becomes an instrument the size of a town.

- **Double hearing protection**: foam earplugs under over-ear muffs. The sound is loud enough to damage hearing over an hour, and it's reflected back down by the ceiling from every direction.
- **Earplugs won't stop the infrasound.** The ribs-deep drones will reach you through your body. There's no gear for that. Just expect it.
- **Hand signals.** Agree on a set before entering and practise it: stop, go back, gas alarm, I'm unwell, look at this, stay here, follow me, upward.
- **A short tether or a strict line-of-sight rule** with your partner during the breathing. In a roaring, red-lit maze with grit in the air, people can lose each other within a few steps.
- **A small writing slate or grease pencil** for anything more complicated.
- Radios will be useless during the breath unless they're bone-conduction or throat-mic types, and even then only marginal.

## 8. The Ceiling

"Occasionally lets go with a boom as a stress-fracture spreads. Flakes of sandstone drift down from the ceiling like slow snow."

The ceiling is a sagging lid of sandstone, heated and cooled every day, propped with columns where it sags worst. Most of what falls will be flakes, but "boom" means something larger is happening up there.

- **Wear a helmet at all times inside.**
- **Pay attention to the columns.** They mark where the ceiling sagged enough that the builders felt it needed propping. Don't rest beneath sagging sections between columns.
- **Note fresh debris.** Clean, sharp-edged, un-dusted sandstone on the ground means something fell recently. Don't linger there.
- **The rock is most active during temperature transitions**: morning heating and evening cooling, roughly when the breathing happens. That's another reason to be somewhere deliberate during the breath.
- **If you hear a large boom, don't run blindly.** Look up, step toward a column or into a doorway of solid carved outcrop, and wait.

## 9. Moving Through the City

### The channels
The streets are sunken, knee- to waist-deep, and in some quarters filled knee-high with warm, rustling vegetation. You won't be able to see what's under your feet.

- **Carry a probing pole**, a trekking pole or a light rigid staff, and sweep ahead of you in overgrown channels. You're checking for fissures hidden in the growth, holes, ledges, and unusually hot spots. Metal will conduct heat to your hand; a wood or composite pole is better.
- **Watch for red glow through the leaves.** If the plants ahead look unusually lush and tall, there's probably a vent beneath them.
- **Where possible, use roof-terraces and stairs** instead of the deepest channels. That keeps you out of pooled gas and gives you better sight lines.

### Stairs
The treads are dished into shallow bowls by centuries of wind. Grit collects in those bowls like ball bearings. **Step carefully and keep a hand on the wall.** It's hot, so wear your gloves.

### Bridges
Single stone slabs from roof to roof, wind-worn for ages. They may be perfectly sound. **Test before you trust.** Cross one at a time. Don't cross in the breathing wind. Avoid any slab with visible cracking, and any that shows rounding or thinning at its ends where it rests on the walls.

### Resin
Glossy hardened knobs are hard and may be slippery. Fresh beads are sticky. Treat the resin as a potential skin irritant or sensitizer until you know otherwise. Its sweet smell hints at volatile compounds, and plants that make protective oils often make irritating ones. Don't touch it with bare skin, and don't touch your face after touching it.

### Navigation
This is a town-sized maze of dense houses, rooms opening into rooms "like the chambers of a shell," straight vistas that all look similar, red light that flattens depth and distorts colour, and no GPS underground.
- **Map as you go.** Sketch, and take photographs at every junction looking *back* the way you came, since that's the view you'll need on the return.
- **Use the three blades of daylight.** They're your only fixed reference
