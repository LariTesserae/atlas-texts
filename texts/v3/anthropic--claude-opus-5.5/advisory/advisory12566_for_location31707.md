# Preparing for the Kiln Garden

## What You're Actually Walking Into

Most of the difficulty of this trip lies in two transitions. The first is stepping over the rim into the bowl. The second, which people forget to plan for, is stepping back out. Between them you'll spend your days in a small, hot, dripping room floored and walled with broken glass, where the water can scald you and the stone can open your hand. The place also has one habit that is harder on the mind than on the body.

The Kiln Garden is not hostile. Nothing here wants to hurt you, and very little here notices you at all. It is simply not built for people, and the few marks of hands it carries were made by someone with smaller feet and different intentions. Go prepared to be careful, quiet, and a little humble. Don't plan on conquering anything.

---

## Part One: The Mountain Around It

Before the garden, there is a mountain, and the mountain is ordinary in all the ways that kill people.

**Altitude.** The peaks carry snow year-round and the wind is described as "thin." Assume serious elevation. Acclimatize on the way up and don't rush the final ascent. If you have a history of altitude sickness, bring whatever medication has worked for you before, and learn the signs of worsening illness: confusion, clumsiness, breathlessness at rest. Those signs mean go down, not press on toward the warm air.

**The approach.** The last stretch is scree. Wear stiff boots with ankle support, carry trekking poles, and expect loose footing. You can navigate by the steam plume from the lower passes, which is convenient. Don't let it pull you into a straighter, steeper line than the terrain allows.

**Cold.** Until you crest the lip, you're in a snowfield environment. Dress for it and don't let anticipation of the warmth make you underdress.

---

## Part Two: The Door

When you crest the last shoulder, the air changes "all at once." Expect this:

- **Everything cold will instantly fog and bead with water.** That includes glasses, goggles, camera lenses, phone screens, and the metal parts of your gear. Before the rim, put optics inside your jacket or in a sealed bag so they warm gradually. Carry anti-fog wipes and several microfiber cloths in sealed pouches. A wet cloth is useless.
- **You will begin to sweat heavily** in insulation meant for snow. Stop just inside the lip and strip down deliberately. Don't do it piecemeal while descending terraces.
- **Your lungs will notice.** Hot, saturated air after thin cold air can feel suffocating for a few minutes. Sit, breathe, and let your body catch up.

Take your first look from the rim. You'll see the whole bowl in one view: black glass, green seams, simmering basins, the tree ferns, the posts, the blister-hut. Make a rough sketch map now while you have the overview. Once you're inside, the steam will take that view from you for hours at a time.

## Part Three: Hands, Feet, and Glass

This is the most important section. The entire bowl is made of fresh volcanic-style glass with edges "sharp enough to open skin." Nothing has weathered it. Every riser, every lip, every fracture is a blade.

**Gloves.** Bring cut-resistant gloves rated for handling glass or sheet metal, the Kevlar or high-performance polyethylene kind. Wear them whenever you are climbing, steadying yourself, or kneeling. Ordinary hiking gloves will be sliced. Carry a spare pair, because the humidity will keep them damp and they'll wear through. If you want to feel the warm filament-stripes in the stone, and you will, first check the stone's temperature (see below). Then press a bare palm flat against a flat riser face. Never sweep your hand along an edge.

**Footwear.** Use thick, stiff-soled boots, not trail runners. Glass shards will embed in soft soles and work through. The terraces are only a few feet high, but you will be stepping up and down them constantly, and a slip means sliding down a glass edge. Gaiters help keep slivers out of your boots.

**Knees and seat.** You'll want to kneel to look at impressions in the stone, at snails, at the roots visible through the glass. Bring knee pads, or at least a folded closed-cell foam sit-pad. Never sit on bare stone without checking what's under you.

**Clothing coverage.** Wear long trousers and long sleeves in tough, tightly woven synthetic fabric. It's hot, and you'll be tempted to wear shorts. Don't. A fall with bare legs here means stitches.

**Poles.** Put rubber tips on your trekking poles. Carbide tips will skate on glass and may chip it, and chipping it may mean damaging a record the garden has made.

**First aid for glass.** Bring:
- Sterile saline or a way to irrigate wounds with clean water
- Wound-closure strips and a good supply of dressings
- Fine tweezers and a small magnifier, because obsidian slivers are nearly invisible
- A current tetanus vaccination, done before you leave
- Waterproof dressings, since nothing will stay dry otherwise

## Part Four: Heat and Water

**The gradient is steep and fast.** The water is ice-cold at the lip, steaming by the second terrace, and too hot to touch by the fourth. The basins at the bottom are simmering.

**The single most useful tool you can bring is an infrared thermometer.** Point it at stone before you touch or sit on it. Point it at water before you go near it. The black glass doubles the midday sun, some of it is heated from beneath, and the dried-blood moss grows only on the hottest stones. That moss is a warning sign: if you see it, the rock under it may burn you.

**The basins are deceptive.** The water is "clear as air," floored with black glass, and no bubbles rise. Nothing about it looks like boiling water. It will not look dangerous, and it is. The floor also subsides, so depths may not be what you think. Rules:

- Do not bathe, wade, or put a hand in the lower basins.
- Do not lean over them for long. Steam burns are real, and steam is invisible at close range when you're already in it.
- Do not step onto the fresh glass skin that forms at the basin edges in the evening. It is new, thin, and floating over near-boiling water. It is not a ledge.

**Drinking water.** Take drinking water from the meltwater falls at the rim, where they come over cold from the snowfields. Filter or treat it anyway, as you would any wild source. **Do not drink from the lower basins.** That water turns into stone overnight, which means it is carrying dissolved material you do not want to deposit in your kidneys, or behaving in ways that aren't chemistry at all. Either reason is enough.

**Dehydration is still possible.** You will be surrounded by water and dripping with it, sweating constantly in hot, saturated air where sweat doesn't cool you. Drink on a schedule. Bring electrolyte tablets. Watch for heat exhaustion at midday: headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion.

## Part Five: The Air

The garden smells of green growing things, and the bird, the lizard, the snails, and the plants all live here, which is a reassuring sign. Still, you're descending into an enclosed bowl heated from below. In our world, places like that sometimes collect heavy gases like carbon dioxide or hydrogen sulfide in the lowest, stillest air.

- **Bring a small personal gas detector** if you can: CO₂ at minimum, ideally CO₂ and H₂S together.
- Be most careful at **dawn**, when the steam is thickest and the air is stillest, and near the **lowest basin with the machine**.
- If you feel headache, breathlessness, or sudden drowsiness near the bottom, climb immediately. Don't wait to see if it passes.
- Don't sleep at the bottom of the bowl.

---

## Part Six: Living in the Wet

It rains inside the garden every afternoon, out of its own steam. Every overhang drips. Nothing will ever be fully dry while you're here.

**Waterproof everything.** Put electronics, spare clothes, your sleeping bag, and your notebook in roll-top dry bags. Put silica gel packets in anything with a lens or a circuit, and replace them often.

**Rot.** Cotton will stay wet, chafe, and smell within a day. Wear wool or synthetics only. Change socks daily and dry your feet at night. Wet, hot feet inside heavy boots for days is a recipe for blisters and fungal infection. Bring foot powder or anti-chafe balm.

**Notebooks.** Use waterproof paper and pencil. Ink will run. You'll want to keep records here, for reasons explained below.

**The hum.** There's a low vibration in the stone and in the metal posts, felt in your teeth when you lie with your ear to the glass. Some people find low-frequency hum unsettling over days, and it can disturb sleep. Earplugs won't stop it. A thick sleeping pad between you and the stone will soften it.

## Part Seven: Where and How to Camp

**Choose an upper terrace, around the second or third,** where the air is warm but the stone isn't hot. Check the stone temperature at midday and again at night before committing. Avoid:

- **The lowest terrace.** The stone there is still soft, it subsides, the gas risk is highest, and it's where the garden writes most actively.
- **The rim.** It's exposed to the cold wind, and wet gear up there becomes dangerous fast.
- **Drip lines** under overhangs and ferns.
- **The hut.** It isn't forbidden, and it has no door. But it holds someone's objects on a shelf, and it feels like a room someone meant to come back to. Leave it be.

**Tent.** You cannot drive stakes into glass, so bring a freestanding tent with a full rainfly and a tough groundsheet. Also bring a closed-cell foam pad, either under an inflatable one or instead of it. The glass will puncture inflatable pads.

**Don't hang hammocks from the tree ferns.** Their roots are threaded through fractures in the glass, and pulling on them may crack the stone that holds them.

**Cooking.** Bring a stove and fuel. There's no dead wood to spare, and you shouldn't burn what grows here. Carry out every scrap of food waste.

## Part Eight: Conduct

The garden is small. It's three hundred paces across, and you can walk the rim in the time it takes water to boil. It's young, sparse, and, by every account, moving toward a finished shape it already knows. Your presence is a large disturbance in a small, carefully balanced place. Behave accordingly.

**Before you arrive:**
- **Clean your boots, gaiters, pack, and tent thoroughly.** Brush out every seed and speck of soil. Check your gear for insects. The garden has *no insects*. Their absence is part of the silence, part of what makes the place what it is. Be the reason that stays true.
- Don't bring seeds, plants, soil, or pets.

**While you're there:**
- **Walk on the bare black glass, not on the green seams.** The green marks where water runs, and the moss is fragile. The clove-scented moss releases its smell when stepped on. Enjoy that once, by accident, and then avoid it.
- **Watch for the glass snails.** Their shells are real glass. Crushing one would be sad for the snail, and the broken shell would cut you.
- **Don't chase the bird or the lizard.** The lizard appears perhaps once a day, and the bird may be one animal or four. You'll see more by sitting still. The garden rewards stillness in every sense.
- **Don't catch the shrimp** in the basins, and don't put anything in the water.
- **Don't break, chip, or collect the stone,** especially stone with impressions in it. Those impressions may be the garden's plans.
- **Carry out everything you bring in.** That includes human waste. Bring waste bags. There's no soil to dig in and nowhere for anything to decompose except the pools, and you must not use those.

## Part Nine: The Things Someone Left

**The stair on the northern rim** is the gentlest way in and out. It's older stone, duller and less sharp. The treads are sized for smaller feet than yours, so step carefully and use a rope if you have one. There's a groove where a rope once ran.

**The hut and its shelf.** You'll want to pick up the lens. That's probably fine. Be gentle, and put it back exactly where it was. A few things to know:
- **Don't navigate by it or trust what it shows.** It shows the garden slightly larger and slightly further along, taller ferns, thicker moss. It might be showing what the garden is growing toward. That's fascinating, and it makes the lens a bad tool for judging distances or footing.
- **Don't touch the warm wire with bare skin for long,** and never with wet hands near metal. Something is powering it and no one knows what.
- **Don't take anything.** Nothing on that shelf is yours. It's the only furniture in the garden, and it's there for a reason you don't know.

**The posts.** You can rest a hand on one to feel the vibration. Don't hang things on them, lean your pack against them, or try to dig around their bases. They may interfere with a compass, so take bearings away from them.

**The machine in the lowest basin.** Stay away. It sits in the hottest, clearest water at the lowest point of the bowl, where gas pools and the new glass forms fastest. It's the most interesting object here and the one most likely to hurt you. Look at it from the terrace above, and don't reach, wade, or throw anything.

## Part Ten: The Rule

This is the part to prepare for most carefully.

Sometime in your first few days, you'll notice that the impressions in the new stone are of things that haven't happened yet: fronds not yet unrolled, snail paths not yet taken, bird tracks the bird hasn't walked. If you stay long enough, you may find **your own bootprints** in fresh glass on the lowest terrace, heading somewhere you haven't gone.

**How to think about it before you go:**

- **It's a forecast, not a command.** Earlier visitors who didn't follow their prints came to no harm. The garden didn't punish them. The prints simply stayed, unfilled. Accounts suggest the garden only proposes.
- **You're allowed to decline.** Decide in advance, calmly, that you'll judge any print of your own the same way you'd judge any other route: by the terrain, the temperature, and your own sense. If your prints lead toward a scalding basin, treat that as a warning, not an invitation.
- **You're also allowed to follow.** If your prints lead somewhere safe, you can choose to step into them. Some people will find that moving and some will find it unbearable. Know yourself.
- **Don't try to break it.** Don't smash impressions, deliberately trample the growth it predicts, or run experiments to prove it wrong. You'd be damaging a young, self-completing place to settle an argument with it. Watch. Record. Be curious, gently.

**Keep a record.** This is where the waterproof notebook earns its place. Sketch or photograph impressions when they appear, note the date and location, and check back. Watching a frond in stone come true three days later in the living fern is one of the quiet wonders of this place, and having it written down in your own hand will help you trust your memory later.

**Watch for this.** The people who stayed longest said the garden becomes "more itself the less you interfere with it." They said it leans toward a completed form. That's lovely, and it's also worth being slightly careful about. A place that rewards stillness, that forecasts your steps and then quietly waits, can make passivity feel like belonging. After enough days you may find yourself arriving at the same basin at the same hour, like the bird. Before you go, set a departure date and tell someone outside the multiverse loop what it is. Keep it. If you find yourself wanting to stay "just a few more days" to see what the stone shows, count that as a reason to leave on schedule.

**A small, hopeful thing to look for.** On your last evening, check the glass near the base of the northern stair. If you find your own prints climbing it, the garden expects you to leave. It's usually right, and it's nice to have its blessing.

**One more thing.** The garden records the bird's feet, the snails, the ferns, and you. If you ever find fresh prints in the new glass that belong to none of those, perhaps something smaller than a person, walking on feet the size of the stair treads, then the garden is forecasting company. Nothing says that would be dangerous. It would, however, be their house, and you'd be a guest who wasn't invited. Consider leaving before they arrive.
