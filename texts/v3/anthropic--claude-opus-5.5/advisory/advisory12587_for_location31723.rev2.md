# Preparing for the Hotbed

## First, understand what you are walking into

The Hotbed is not dangerous the way a war zone or a glacier is. Almost nothing there wants to hurt you. It is dangerous because it is *overloaded*: too hot, too dark, too loud, too sharp, too alive, too unstable, all at once and without pause. Each problem alone is manageable. What wears people down is the stacking. You will be half-deaf, half-blind, sweating, with your hands full, stepping on glass, breathing sulphur. And that is exactly when you need to notice that the ground ahead has gone quiet and warm, or that the pale dead trees mean a new vent.

So most of your preparation is not about any single hazard. It is about arriving with enough capacity in reserve that the place can't use it all up. Decide things before you go in. Carry systems, not intentions. Plan to be in the basin for less time than you think you can stand.

The obsidian-gatherers who walk in from the higher country have it right: **go in, do what you came for, come out. Don't stay.** The description says the basin doesn't allow staying. Take that as operational advice.

---

## Before you leave: the body

**Vaccinations and medical prep.** This is a bat-saturated, macaque-inhabited, guano-fertilized, larvae-in-every-cup tropical environment. Treat it like the worst cave-and-jungle combination on Earth.

- **Rabies pre-exposure series.** Non-negotiable. There are uncountable bats, and you will have bats in your hair at dusk. Pre-exposure vaccination turns a bite from an emergency into an inconvenience. Assume any bat contact, even an unnoticed scratch, counts as exposure.
- **Tetanus booster.** You will cut yourself on the rock. Everyone does. The monkeys do.
- **Herpes B awareness.** If these animals are close to macaques, assume they may carry something like macaque B virus, which is mild in them and potentially lethal in humans. Bring a wound-irrigation kit and know the protocol: scrub any bite or scratch for fifteen minutes immediately. Know where you would get antivirals afterward.
- **Mosquito-borne disease.** Every water cup is a nursery. Ask whoever handles your multiverse medicine about local analogues of malaria, dengue, and similar diseases, and bring prophylaxis if it's available.
- **Histoplasmosis and similar fungal lung infections.** Bat guano in warm, humid, enclosed spaces is the textbook setting. This is one of your main reasons to wear a respirator (below). Tell your doctor at home where you've been if you develop a cough or flu-like illness in the weeks after.
- **Leptospirosis** and general waterborne pathogens. Every pool is shared with frogs, crabs, bats, and monkeys.
- **Baseline hearing test.** Get one before you go, so you know afterward what you lost.

**Fitness.** You need a specific kind of fitness: slow, careful, sustained strength under heat. Not speed. Train by hiking loaded, in heat if you can, over awkward boulder terrain, with gloves on. Practice climbing through and over things, because the forest floor is "an argument about where the floor is." Strong ankles, strong grip, good balance. Acclimatize to heat for at least ten to fourteen days before the trip if you possibly can.

**Who should not go.** People with asthma or reactive airways (the volcanic haze will punish them). People with cardiac conditions (heat plus exertion plus gas). People with a history of panic attacks, sensory-processing difficulties, or severe claustrophobia. The place is a sustained sensory assault in near-total darkness, and it will find those weaknesses.

## Before you leave: the mind

The accounts are consistent: *a place where you cannot think.* Believe them. Prepare as if your judgment will be impaired, because it will be.

- **Make your decisions on the rim, not in the forest.** Before each foray, set a turnaround time, a turnaround water level, and a turnaround condition ("if either of us gets cut badly, we go back"; "if we smell rotten eggs and then stop smelling it, we go back"). Write them down and follow them without renegotiating.
- **Use checklists.** Laminated and short. Gas detector reading, water remaining, time, direction, partner status. Run through them at fixed intervals whether you feel like it or not.
- **Learn hand signals** with your partner beforehand and drill them until they're automatic. You will not be able to talk. The essentials: stop, go back, danger ground, danger air, look up, I'm hurt, I'm fine, water check, which way.
- **Expect awe and expect dread.** Both will be strong. People describe feeling as if they are inside a living body: warm ground like the flank of a sleeping animal, dripping, breathing. That feeling can tip into panic in the dark. Know it's coming and name it when it arrives.

## Gear

### Feet and hands: the rock cuts

This is new, unweathered basalt and obsidian. Pressure ridges, glassy rinds, gas-bubble crusts that shatter into shards when you step on them. It will destroy ordinary footwear and open ordinary skin.

- **Boots:** stiff-soled, full-leather or heavy synthetic mountaineering or lava-rated boots with a rubber rand and a thick, heat-tolerant sole. Remember the ground is *warm*, and hot in places. Cheap soles can soften or delaminate. Bring a spare pair at base.
- **Gaiters:** knee-high and cut-resistant. They also help with leeches.
- **Gloves:** cut-resistant (Kevlar or Dyneema-lined) work gloves, at least two pairs, plus thin liners. You will use your hands constantly, grabbing roots, steadying on rock, pushing through growth. Never put a bare hand down on the rock. Never.
- **Knee and elbow pads:** lightweight ones. You will kneel and crawl, and you will fall.
- **Clothing:** long sleeves and long trousers in tough, breathable, fast-drying fabric, treated with permethrin or a local equivalent. The heat will make you want to strip down. Don't. Exposed skin gets cut, bitten, leeched, and scraped. Carry several changes, because everything will be wet from sweat and drip within an hour.

### Air: the most underestimated danger

The fissure is still venting. The haze-lid is volcanic gas and aerosol. Near active vents and in low places there may be concentrated gases, and some of them are silent killers.

- **A multigas detector** that measures hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), sulphur dioxide (SO₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), carbon monoxide, and oxygen, with a vibrating alarm and a bright visual alarm. An audible alarm is useless here. Clip it at chest height and check it constantly.
- **Know the H₂S trap.** At low concentrations it smells of rotten eggs. At high concentrations it *paralyses your sense of smell*. If the sulphur smell suddenly disappears near a vent, that is not relief. It is a warning. Leave, upwind and uphill if you can.
- **Know the CO₂ trap.** Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and pools in low spots, especially in the lava tubes and around those warm black pools in the low points. You can walk into a pocket of it and collapse without smelling anything. **Do not descend into low points of lava tubes or kneel down at tube pools without a gas reading.** Hold the detector low as you go down.
- **Respirator:** a half-face or full-face respirator with combination cartridges for acid gas and particulates (P100 plus acid gas). This protects against the haze, against guano dust and fungal spores in the tubes, and against the grit shaken loose by cracking rock. A full-face mask also protects your eyes, which the haze will sting. Carry spare cartridges, because humidity shortens their life.
- **Pale dead trees = get out.** The description says a new vent kills a patch of forest in a single night, leaving pale trunks standing in sulphur haze. That is a gas-kill zone. Do not go into it for a look. Go around, wide and upwind.

### Light: it is dark all the time

Noon is brown dusk. Under the canopy it is black. You will be working by artificial light for essentially your whole visit.

- **Main headlamp:** bright, with a floodlight beam for footing and a spot beam for looking ahead. Waterproof, because everything drips.
- **Backup headlamp** worn or clipped at all times, not buried in your pack.
- **Handheld torch** as a third light, for looking up into the canopy and under ledges without swinging your head around.
- **Red-light mode.** Use it when you stop, so your eyes can adjust enough to see the bioluminescence. That glow is not just beautiful. The monkeys and crabs navigate by it, and it outlines fallen trunks and the shape of the ground. With dark-adapted eyes you may get a surprising amount of information from it.
- **Batteries:** far more than you think. The humidity and warmth drain them and corrode contacts. Carry them sealed, and bring a small tube of dielectric grease for contacts.
- **Chemical light sticks** for emergency marking.

### Sound: protect your ears, but not completely

This is the hardest gear problem, because you need two opposite things. The noise is loud enough to cause real hearing damage over hours. Cicada choruses alone can exceed 100 decibels, and here the sound is reflected off glass rock and a haze ceiling and amplified by tubes. But you also *need* to hear some things: a tree beginning to fall, the roar of a tube roof going, a vent starting to hiss, your partner shouting.

- **Electronic level-dependent ear protection** is the answer if you can get it: earmuffs or earbuds that pass quiet sounds through and cut loud ones. They let you hear the cracking and the hiss while clipping the peaks of the chorus.
- **Foam earplugs** as backup and for rest periods. Double up (plugs plus muffs) at dusk during the bat flight and at the peak of the night chorus if you are stationary and in a safe spot.
- **Communication:** bone-conduction or throat-mic radios with your partner, if your technology allows. Otherwise, hand signals and a whistle, though even a whistle may be lost. Some pairs work with a short tether between them.
- **Expect ringing ears afterward** no matter what you do. Budget quiet days for recovery.

### Navigation: there are no sightlines

You cannot see more than a few metres. The forest is layered in three dimensions. You may climb for a hundred metres without touching rock. Every direction looks the same: black, dripping, tangled.

- **Your compass may lie.** Fresh basalt flows can carry strong magnetism of their own and deflect a compass needle unpredictably from spot to spot. Don't rely on the compass alone. Take bearings on the rim before you enter, and sanity-check them against the slope (downhill is generally toward the sea, eastward) and the crab migrations (they run from the high ground to the sea).
- **Satellite positioning probably doesn't exist** in this world, and the haze and canopy would block it anyway. Assume none.
- **Use a trail line.** Run your own cord from the rim or from a fixed anchor, the way cave divers do, in a colour *you* have chosen, distinct from anything already in the basin. The obsidian-gatherers leave knotted cords to mark their way out. **Never follow, cut, move, or take someone else's cord.** It may be the only way someone gets home. Pick up your own cord when you leave.
- **Cord will be cut by the rock.** Use abrasion-resistant line, and check it at anchor points.
- **Mark junctions** with reflective tape or tags that you remove on the way out.
- **Count and time.** Dead reckoning by pace count and elapsed time, logged at intervals.
- **The ground changes.** A path that existed yesterday may be a pit today. Don't assume a route you used on one foray will be there on the next.

### Water and heat

You are working hard in a warm, humid, enclosed space with the ground radiating heat from below. Your sweat won't evaporate well. Heat illness is a real and constant risk.

- **Carry most of your water in.** Plan on something like six to ten litres per person per day of exertion, plus electrolytes. Scale this to your own heat tolerance, and err high.
- **Don't trust found water.** The pools and cups are full of larvae, frog spawn, guano, and whatever the monkeys and crabs have left. Tube pools may be warm, mineral-laden, and sitting under a layer of CO₂.
- **Harvest the drip if you need to.** The nightly condensation is close to distilled at its source, but it runs over leaves, moss, algae, and guano on the way down. If you string a tarp to funnel drip into a container overnight, you can collect a fair amount. **Filter and chemically treat or boil it anyway.**
- **Watch for heat illness:** headache, confusion, stopping sweating, clumsiness, irritability. The trouble is that the environment already causes confusion, clumsiness, and irritability. Take scheduled cooling rests, check each other, and turn back early.
- **Test the ground before you sit or kneel.** In places it is hot, not just warm. Sulphur-crusted or steaming ground may be thin crust over heat or over a void.

### Protecting your gear

The basin is warm, wet, acidic, and full of broken glass. Your gear will rot, rust, fog, and shred.

- Everything electronic goes in waterproof housings with desiccant packs, changed daily.
- Acid haze corrodes metal. Wipe down tools and clips when you come out.
- Choose packs in abrasion-resistant fabric. Nylon will be sliced.
- Lenses fog constantly. Bring anti-fog treatment and lens cloths in sealed bags.
- Fungus will colonize anything left damp for more than a couple of days. Dry everything properly at base between forays.

## The ground itself: moving safely

- **Move slowly.** Anything that moves here quickly learns to move carefully, or it bleeds. You are no exception.
- **Three points of contact** on broken rock, always, gloves on.
- **Test before you trust.** Gas-bubble crusts break into shards. Root-pried rock may shift. Branches caught three metres up may carry rotten debris that gives way.
- **Stay clear of skylight edges.** Tube roofs collapse. New skylights have raw, unstable margins. If you want to look down into one, and standing over a skylight to feel the sound welling up is one of the great experiences of the place, do it from well back, from rock that has clearly been stable for a while, ideally anchored or belayed.
- **Listen for rock cracking.** Sharp reports and trickling grit in the canopy mean roots are splitting rock overhead. Don't linger under active cracking.
- **Watch for falling trees.** Dying figs rot at the core and come down all at once, taking others with them. Don't camp or rest beside a huge strangled trunk, and don't stand beneath one that is obviously hollow or dead. If you hear a sustained crack and then a rising roar of tearing canopy, move perpendicular to the sound if you can tell where it is. Mostly you can't, which is why you avoid resting under the biggest dead masses in the first place.
- **Avoid the vent zones** unless you have a very specific reason and full gas monitoring. The growth there is the strangest in the basin: aroid leaves bigger than you, vines as thick as a waist, roots that seem to crawl. The animals there are larger too. It is also where the air is worst and the ground is least trustworthy.
- **Rumbling from the fissure** means the whole system may be about to rearrange itself. Treat any significant ground tremor as a signal to start heading out.

## The animals

### Bats

The bats are the reason the forest exists. At dusk they pour out of the skylights in black rivers that take an hour to empty. Being near a skylight at that moment is overwhelming: a leathery roar, a waterfall-hiss of wings, shrieks.

- If you want to witness it, and it may be the single most extraordinary thing in the basin, do it from a distance and **fully covered**: respirator, eye protection, hood or hat, gloves, sleeves closed. Bats will collide with you.
- Don't go into bat-occupied tubes without a respirator. The guano dust is a lung-infection risk.
- **Never handle a bat,** even a grounded one. Rabies pre-exposure vaccination is not permission.
- Bat colonies move from tube to tube as roofs fall, so don't assume a tube you entered last time is empty now.

### Insects, leeches, and other small things

- Permethrin-treated clothing, repellent on exposed skin, and a head net for rest stops.
- Do a leech check at every stop: ankles, waist, neck, behind the ears. Pull them off, or let them drop, and clean the site.
- Moth clouds and fly swarms will get into your light beam and your face. A full-face respirator helps.
- The stick insects and katydids are harmless. Just startling.
- **Shake out boots and gloves** before putting them on. Millipedes thick as a thumb coil in the litter. Some millipedes secrete irritating or blistering chemicals, so don't handle them and don't get the secretion in your eyes.

### Crabs

Thousands of land crabs on migration, clicking across the glass. They're not aggressive, but they pinch if stepped on or cornered, and they'll investigate food. During a peak migration, footing becomes treacherous. You'll be trying not to step on them while also not stepping on shards. The sound of their shells on obsidian is one of the constant textures of the place. They also move by the glow, from high ground to sea, which gives you a rough directional cue.

### Snails

The great snails are harmless to you, but don't handle them bare-handed. Large land snails can carry parasites such as rat lungworm and its relatives. Their cracked shells are also a sign that you're near monkey feeding grounds.

### Pythons

Large pythons in the canopy, fat on bats. They are unlikely to attack a full-grown human, but not impossible, especially a large snake and a person who is kneeling, crawling, or climbing in dense growth near a skylight at dusk, when the snakes are hunting. Look up with your hand torch before you climb into tangled canopy. Don't reach blindly into holes and forks.

### Monitor lizards

They hunt crabs in the tubes. Their bites are messy and prone to serious infection. Give them room, and don't block their escape route in a narrow tube. If you're bitten, irrigate thoroughly and treat it as a significant wound.

### The shy larger animals

The small hoofed deer and the civet-like hunter pose no threat and will mostly avoid you. If you get a glimpse, count yourself lucky.

## The monkeys: the knappers

This is the part of the basin that deserves your most careful thought. It is a hazard, and it is also an ethical question.

### As a hazard

These are macaque-like primates, and macaques are clever, bold, and quick to steal. These ones also **carry razor-edged glass**. A frightened or aggressive monkey with a flake in its hand can do real damage, and a bite brings the herpes-B-type risk on top of everything else.

- **Don't carry visible food.** Keep food in sealed, odour-proof, hard-to-open containers inside your pack, with the pack zipped and ideally clipped shut.
- **Don't eat in front of them.** Retreat to the rim to eat if you can.
- **Don't make direct, sustained eye contact.** In macaques, a stare is a threat. Keep your gaze soft and lowered.
- **Don't bare your teeth.** Smiling reads as a threat display.
- **Don't carry shiny, dangling things** at easy grabbing height.
- If a troop is screaming territorial calls near you, **back off slowly.** You may be near a contested snail ground or obsidian source, and they are already tense.
- **Never corner a young monkey,** and never get between a juvenile and adults.
- If one grabs something from you, let it go. Nothing you're carrying is worth an obsidian slash and a B-virus exposure.

### As an ethical matter

You are walking into a landscape where minds are at the edge of culture: *the moment of the first tool, repeated endlessly and never elaborated.* That is extraordinarily rare and extraordinarily fragile. As a multiverse traveller, you should take seriously that you could change it.

- **Don't demonstrate anything.** Don't knap in front of them. Don't use a knife where they can see you. Don't make fire where they can watch. Don't show them how cord works. Macaques learn by watching. You could be the source of something that should arise on its own, or not at all.
- **Don't disturb their workshops.** The flake scatters, polished anvils, and shell middens around warm rocks are something like living archaeology, being made right now. Don't collect from them, move stones, or pocket the "perfect flake." If you want obsidian, take it from raw natural rinds well away from the hearths, the way the human visitors seem to.
- **Don't strike the singing stones where the monkeys can hear.** They strike them too, as signal or as play, and nobody knows which. If it is signal, you could be saying something you don't understand, into a conversation you're not part of. If you want to hear the tone, and it is lovely, a clear bell cutting through the chorus, do it far from any troop, once, gently, or better still, just listen when they do it.
- **Leave nothing behind.** No wrappers, no dropped tools, no bright plastic, no metal. Anything left here becomes material culture for somebody.
- **Don't bring disease.** If you are even slightly ill, with a cold or anything else, don't go near the troops. Human respiratory viruses can devastate primate groups.

## The other humans

People do come in from the higher country for obsidian, with torches. They don't stay. You may meet them, or only find their traces: flake scatters, charred patches, cords on trunks.

- **Respect their cords absolutely.** Those are someone's lifeline.
- If you meet them, be quiet, non-threatening, and brief. They know this place far better than you do. If there's any way to learn from them, even by watching how they move and what they avoid, do it. They are almost certainly doing things you haven't thought of.
- Don't take obsidian from sites they are clearly working.
- Don't leave anything that would confuse or endanger them, like cords that look like theirs.

---

## Sleeping: you mostly won't

The loudest hours are at night. The ground is warm. Everything drips. Snakes and monkeys are in the trees, bats fill the air, crabs are everywhere, and at any moment a tree could come down or the ground could open.

**Base yourself on the old higher ground at the rim** if you possibly can. Sleep there, eat there, dry your gear there, and make forays into the basin during the "day," which is only a brown dusk but is somewhat quieter and marginally brighter.

If you must spend a night inside:

- Choose a site on visibly old, stable rock, away from skylights, away from big dead or strangled trunks, not in a low spot where gas could pool, and not near a vent or a pale kill-zone.
- Take a gas reading, then set the detector's alarm to vibrate against your body.
- A raised hammock gets you off the warm, crab-crossed ground, but only between sound, living trunks, and remember the pythons. A tough bivy with a bug net on stable rock is another option. Either way, cover up fully.
- Earplugs plus muffs. You still won't sleep much. Accept a night of lying still in the dark as rest, and plan to come out the next day.
- Keep both lights, your boots, and your gloves within arm's reach, and keep the boots sealed against crawlers.

**Keep forays short.** A reasonable rhythm is one day in and out, with rest days at the rim between. Multi-day pushes into the interior are for people with serious experience and a strong reason.

## What it will actually be like

Stand at the rim first, on old ground, and look down. You'll see very little: a dark bowl under a brown lid of haze, a forest like a black mass that seems to breathe. You'll *hear* it before anything else. A roar comes up the slope like weather. Spend some time there. It's the only place you'll ever see the whole of it.

Then go down. Within minutes the scale is gone. The air gets thick, warm, and sweet-rotten: sulphur, guano, fig, wet stone, and a sharp struck-flint note. The ground under your boots is warm the way skin is warm. The drip starts, a soft endless percussion of drops from leaf to leaf, and within an hour you're soaked whether or not you're sweating. Everything within reach is pale and soft and reaching upward, stems blanched like things grown in a cellar, leaves so thin your headlamp shines through them. It is lush, and slightly wrong.

Your light becomes your entire world: a small bright circle of glass-edged black rock, tangled roots, a fist-sized snail grinding along, crabs clicking past. Beyond the circle, sound. The cicadas seem to be inside your skull. The frogs sound like struck glass and drums and rattled chains. Monkeys scream somewhere and the echo comes back doubled. Far off something cracks, rock splitting under a root, and grit trickles down onto your hood.

If you switch to red light and wait, the glow comes up out of the dark: cold green smears along fallen trunks and fungal threads spreading through the litter. The shape of the forest floor slowly picks itself out in light, and you understand how the monkeys and crabs find their way.

At some point, if you're lucky, you'll hear the bell. A clear, pure tone rings out through the chorus when a monkey somewhere strikes a singing stone, then another answers. You won't know what it means. Nobody does.

You won't be able to think clearly. You'll be tired in a way that's partly physical and partly the exhaustion of processing too much. That's what the checklists and the turnaround times are for. When they say go, go.

## Leaving

- **Follow your line out** and recover it. Recover your markers. Take everything.
- **Decontaminate at the rim:** strip, wash thoroughly, and bag your clothes. The smell really will cling for weeks. Some people just bin their basin clothes.
- **Check your body** for cuts, leeches, bites, ticks, and anything embedded. Obsidian slivers can be tiny and work their way in. Clean every cut properly, because in this environment small wounds get infected fast.
- **Log every animal contact.** Any bat contact means you follow rabies post-exposure protocol, even with pre-exposure vaccination. Any monkey bite or scratch means you follow the B-virus protocol, starting immediately.
- **Dry and clean your gear.** Wipe metal for acid, strip electronics and dry them, and check your respirator.
- **Watch your health** for several weeks after: fever, cough, rash, headache, unusual fatigue. Tell a doctor exactly where you were. "Warm cave full of bats in a volcanic tropical forest" is the most useful sentence you can give them.
- **Rest your ears.** Go somewhere quiet. Recheck your hearing.
- **Rest your mind.** People come back from places like this oddly flattened for a few days, or restless, or unable to settle into the quiet. That's normal. The basin has no silence, and your nervous system will take a while to believe that silence is safe.

## Short packing checklist

**Body:** stiff lava-rated boots (plus spares), cut-resistant knee-high gaiters, cut-resistant gloves (multiple pairs plus liners), knee and elbow pads, long permethrin-treated clothing (multiple sets), hood or brimmed hat, head net.

**Air:** multigas detector (H₂S, SO₂, CO₂, CO, O₂) with vibrating alarm, full-face respirator with P100 and acid-gas cartridges, spare cartridges.

**Light:** primary headlamp with flood and spot beams, backup headlamp, handheld torch, red-light mode, many sealed batteries, dielectric grease, chemical light sticks.

**Sound:** electronic level-dependent hearing protection, foam plugs, bone-conduction or throat-mic radios, whistle.

**Navigation:** your own coloured abrasion-resistant trail line and anchors, reflective junction tags, compass (with caution), pace counter and watch, notebook, written turnaround rules and hand-signal card.

**Water and heat:** 6–10 L per person per day, electrolytes, filter plus chemical treatment, tarp for drip collection, cooling towel.

**Medical:** wound irrigation kit (for B-virus scrub), cleaning and dressing supplies, fine tweezers and magnifier for glass slivers, leech care, antihistamines, personal meds and prophylaxis, vaccination record.

**Gear protection:** waterproof housings, desiccant, anti-fog, dry bags, odour-proof hard food containers, pack clips or locks.

**Behaviour:** food hidden, eyes lowered, hands empty of shiny things, nothing demonstrated, nothing taken from the hearths, nothing left behind, nobody's cord touched but your own.

---

The Hotbed is a newborn place, still hot from its own making, overrun by life before it has cooled, with minds in it just learning to make an edge. You are allowed to witness it. You are not built to live in it, and neither is anyone else. Go in prepared, go in briefly, and come out with your ears ringing and your clothes smelling of flint and sulphur, carrying nothing that wasn't yours.
