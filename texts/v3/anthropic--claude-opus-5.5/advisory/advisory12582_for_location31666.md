# Preparing for the Warm Throat

## What You're Actually Walking Into

Before the practical details, it helps to understand what kind of place this is.

The Warm Throat is not a cave you explore. It is closer to a body you are allowed into briefly. It is a small, hot, saturated, crowded room that has spent centuries tuning itself to its own rhythms: the light crossing the wall, the pool rising and falling, the afternoon rain, the bats' exodus and return. You are the only thing in it that is loud, fast, bright, and dry. Nearly all of your preparation comes down to one goal: be less of those things.

The second thing to understand is that it's physically harder than it looks. The room is small and gentle-seeming. It has a flat stone to sit on. But it is also a sealed, near-100%-humidity hot room reached through a squeeze you have to climb back up. It is full of bat droppings, and it is acoustically dead, so nobody outside will hear you if something goes wrong. Its beauty makes it easy to underestimate.

## Part One: Before You Leave Home

### Can your body fit?

The throat is "barely wider than a person's shoulders," and it bends twice. This is the first and most absolute filter.

- **Measure yourself honestly.** Measure shoulder width, and also chest depth with a harness on. If you're broad-shouldered, assume you won't fit until proven otherwise.
- **Practice squeezes before you go.** Build a test gap from boards or furniture at a width slightly narrower than your shoulders. Practice getting through it with one arm leading overhead, then practice reversing out. Coming back up the throat is harder than going down, because you're pushing against gravity on slick stone.
- **Know your claustrophobia threshold.** The throat is short, but it's warm, wet, dark, and smells of rot. It presses on you from both sides, and the fig roots form a kind of wooden basket around you. If you have ever panicked in tight spaces, do a practice session in a real cave squeeze with an experienced guide first. Panic in a squeeze is how people get stuck.

### Physical conditioning

You'll need:

- **Chimneying and stemming ability.** You'll brace against walls with your back, feet, and palms.
- **Grip endurance on wet surfaces.**
- **Rope ascent competence** if you use a rope. You should, as a safety line at minimum.
- **Heat tolerance.** This is the one people neglect, and it gets its own section below.

### Heat acclimatization

The room is the temperature of a warm bath, and the air is saturated. That combination is physiologically serious. In dry heat, sweat evaporates and cools you. In saturated air it doesn't. Sweat just runs off you and your core temperature climbs slowly and steadily. You can feel "fine, just sweaty" right up until you feel very bad.

In the two weeks before your visit:
- Do daily sessions in a sauna or steam room, ideally a steam room, since humid heat is what matters. Build up gradually.
- Learn your personal early signs of heat strain: headache, a thudding heartbeat, clumsiness, irritability, sudden fatigue, chills despite the heat, nausea, confusion.
- If you have any cardiac condition, blood pressure issues, or take medications that affect sweating or heat tolerance (some antihistamines, diuretics, beta blockers, stimulants, certain psychiatric medications), talk to a physician first.

### Medical preparation

This matters more than it might seem.

- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** Hundreds of bats, some possibly more. You may never touch one, but you'll be in their air, under their roost, and possibly in the throat when a straggler comes through. Pre-exposure vaccination is standard for cavers working in bat roosts. Start the series weeks in advance.
- **Histoplasmosis awareness.** Bat guano can harbor fungal spores that cause lung infections when inhaled. The room's wetness probably keeps the floor from being dusty, but you will be walking on a mattress of droppings and litter. Every step puffs something up, and your hands will be in it on the way down and back. Bring a respirator (below).
- **Leptospirosis and water-borne risks.** There are rats, and there is warm standing water. Cover any cuts before you go. Treat any scratch you pick up inside as contaminated.
- **Warm-freshwater amoebae.** This is critical. Warm, still freshwater is the environment where certain brain-invading amoebae thrive. They enter through the nose. **Never put your face in the pool, never splash it near your nose, and never let pool water get into your sinuses.** This is non-negotiable.
- **Tetanus booster** if you're not current.
- **Your own world's pathogens.** As a multiverse traveler, you should assume that this world's microbes are not ones your immune system has met. Take whatever quarantine and decontamination precautions your traveling practice normally uses, and be especially careful here. A warm, wet, closed room full of animals is an ideal incubator.

### Biosecurity: protect the room from you

The reverse matters just as much. The room is a sealed community. It has eyeless fish adapted to one pool, frogs that absorb everything through their skin, a luminous film found on nothing else, and bats that pack together in hundreds. Diseases like white-nose syndrome have devastated bat populations after hitchhiking on cavers' gear.

- **Clean everything that has been in another cave, anywhere, in any world.** Boots, clothes, rope, helmet, gloves. Ideally use brand-new gear or gear that has never been underground.
- **Wash and decontaminate your boots thoroughly.** Seeds and spores in your treads could colonize that soft, rich floor.
- **Do not wear insect repellent, sunscreen, lotions, or fragrance into the room.** The frogs, shrimp, and fish would absorb it, and the moths and crickets would be poisoned by it. You'll need sun protection on the surface, so solve that with clothing, a hat, and shade rather than chemicals, or wash thoroughly before descending.

## Part Two: Timing Your Visit

The room runs on a schedule, and your plan has to fit around it.

### The daily cycle

| Time | What happens | Implications for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Before dawn** | Bats stream back in through the throat for nearly an hour | **Do not be in the throat.** |
| **Dawn** | The faint "sweating" | Quiet, subtle, humid |
| **Morning–midday** | Light column enters and begins crossing the far wall; bats asleep | Best window to descend |
| **Late afternoon** | The rain, about half an hour; bell-flowers open, snails emerge | Stay still, get wet, watch |
| **After the rain** | Room steams heavily for about an hour | Hottest-feeling period; monitor yourself |
| **Dusk** | Bats stream out through the throat for nearly an hour | **Do not be in the throat.** |
| **Night** | Glow emerges (after full dark adaptation), moths orbit, rats forage | Exit possible once the exodus ends |

### The two hard rules

1. **Never be in the throat during the bat streams at dusk and dawn.** You'd physically plug the only passage for hundreds of animals. You'd be struck repeatedly, possibly bitten, and you'd cause real harm to the colony. It could also trap you in a squeeze while panicking animals pour around you.
2. **Decide in advance which window you're exiting in, and stick to it.** Your exit windows are either before the dusk exodus or after it ends and before the dawn return. If you miss the evening window, you're committed to the night.

### Recommended itineraries

**Option A: The day visit (best for a first time)**
- Descend late morning.
- Watch the light move, meet the room, sit on the stone.
- Stay through the rain.
- Leave during the steaming hour after the rain, with plenty of margin before dusk.
- Total time underground: roughly 4–6 hours.

**Option B: The full cycle (for experienced, heat-tolerant visitors)**
- Descend early afternoon.
- Experience the rain and the steaming.
- Watch the bat exodus from inside the room, sitting well away from the throat mouth. It's one of the most extraordinary things you'll ever feel: a pressure change, a warm current of wings, and a roar you can't hear.
- Sit in total darkness for 30–45 minutes until the glow comes up.
- Watch the lowered sky and the moths orbiting it.
- Climb out in the dark, well before the dawn return.
- Total time underground: roughly 8–10 hours. This is a lot of hours in saturated heat. Only do this if your acclimatization is solid and you have a partner monitoring you.

**Option C: Two visits.** If you want both the daylight and the glow, the safest choice is two separate descents on different days. Recover fully in between.

### Seasonal timing

Once a year, after the heaviest rains, the frogs lay egg strings among the roots trailing in the pool. If you visit in that season, give the pool's edge an even wider berth. Consider not going to the pool at all.

## Part Three: Gear

### What you wear

- **Helmet** with a chin strap. The ceiling near the walls is low, there are root pillars everywhere, and the throat bends.
- **Lightweight synthetic long sleeves and long trousers.** Cotton will become a heavy, hot, sodden wrap within minutes. Long coverage protects you from centipede bites and spider contact, and it protects the room from your skin.
- **Knee pads and elbow pads**, thin ones, for the throat.
- **Thin gloves with rubberized palms.** Bare hands give you the best grip on that skin-warm stone, but the stone is coated in condensation and whatever lives in it, and there are centipedes. Nitrile-dipped work gloves are a good compromise.
- **Soft-soled, well-cleaned boots** with good grip for the throat. On the floor, soft soles will spare the litter.
- **Sun hat and light long layers for the surface**, which you'll leave at the top.

### What you carry

- **A respirator.** A P100 or N95 half-mask is enough for the guano. Here is the paradox: in saturated air a respirator is miserable to wear, because it gets wet and breathing feels harder. Bring spare filters or masks, and consider wearing it mainly while you're moving and stirring the floor, less while you sit still.
- **Primary headlamp with a red-light mode**, plus two backup lights. You'll hardly use white light at all, but you must have light for the throat, especially if you exit at night.
- **Water: much more than you think.** Plan on at least 1 liter per hour underground, plus electrolytes. You're sweating continuously, and none of it cools you. You cannot drink from the pool.
- **Electrolyte tablets or powder.** Plain water alone, drunk in these quantities, can cause its own problems.
- **A small thermometer and hygrometer**, or a single heat-stress meter if you have one. Knowing the actual numbers helps you make honest decisions when your judgment is already getting soft from the heat.
- **Waterproof dry bags for everything.** The rain will reach everything, and the steam gets into places rain can't.
- **Waterproof notebook and pencil.** Pens fail in this humidity.
- **A pee bottle.** Pack out everything.
- **Emergency blanket, first-aid kit, and wound dressings.**
- **Optional but wonderful: a bat detector.** A handheld ultrasonic receiver that drops bat calls into your hearing range. Switch it on and the silent room suddenly roars. The bats have been shouting the whole time, and you can hear the ceiling map itself. Keep the speaker volume very low.

### On cameras

- Cameras will fog badly. Your gear arrives from outside cooler and drier than the room, and the room's moisture condenses on every lens instantly. Keep your camera sealed in a dry bag with desiccant, and let it sit inside the room for half an hour to reach air temperature before you open the bag.
- **No flash.** Not near the bats, not near the geckos, not near anything. Use long exposures on a small tripod or a stable rock that isn't the flowstone or the handprint wall.
- Honestly, consider not bringing a camera at all. Much of what makes this room remarkable, like the silence, the heat, the pressure change when the bats leave, and the slowness, doesn't photograph.

### What you don't bring in

- Food with crumbs, or food at all if you can avoid it. The rats and beetles will find it, and they shouldn't. If you must eat, choose sealed items like gels, eaten carefully, with all wrappers packed out.
- Scented anything.
- Anything you'd be upset to lose. Dropping something in the throat or the litter can mean it's gone forever.

## Part Four: The Surface

Don't let the drama of the underground make you careless up top.

- **The surface is hot, dry, and exposed.** Pale limestone reflects sunlight up at you. You'll arrive at the throat already a little dehydrated and heated unless you plan. Drink before you descend. Rest in shade before you commit.
- **Watch where you put your hands and feet.** Cracked limestone in dry scrub often shelters scorpions, snakes, and ants.
- **The fig tree.** Admire it, but don't climb it, cut it, or tie anything to it. It is the room's life source. Its roots are the lining of the throat, the ceiling of the chamber, and the scaffold of the whole hanging forest. Damaging it damages everything.

### Rigging a line

- **Anchor to rock, not to the fig.** Use a natural rock anchor or a stake well away from the roots. If you're in a place where bolting is appropriate and permitted, a bolt is better than wrapping a sling around the fig.
- **Protect the roots from the rope.** Run the line where it won't saw back and forth across roots as you climb. Use rope protectors at pinch points. A rope rubbing across a root dozens of times per visit is a small wound that stays open.
- **A pack will not fit through the throat.** Put your gear in a haul bag on a separate trailing line and lower it after you, or have your surface partner lower it.

### Your surface partner

You need one, and here's why: **sound doesn't leave this room.** A shout inside becomes nothing. A shout from the room to the surface is inconceivable. Someone at the top needs to know when you went down, when you intend to come out, and what to do if you don't.

- **Agree on a hard return time.** If you're not out by then, the surface partner raises the alarm. They don't come down after you alone.
- **Agree on rope-tug signals.** For example: one tug means "I'm okay," two means "take in slack," three means "I'm coming up," and repeated pulling means "trouble." This works when voice doesn't.
- **Radios may or may not work through the rock.** Test before you rely on them.

## Part Five: The Descent

- **Descend feet first, slowly.** Keep your breathing slow. The air coming up at you is thick, warm, and wet, and it smells of rot and something sweet. It's unsettling. Breathe through it.
- **The walls are skin-warm and slick.** Use the roots for grip where they are thick and firmly anchored in the rock. Take weight gently. Test before you trust. Avoid pulling on thin roots and root-tips at all.
- **The two bends.** At each bend, pause and think about the way back up. Where will your hands go? Which way will your shoulders turn? Memorize it. You'll be doing this later, tired and heat-softened, possibly in the dark.
