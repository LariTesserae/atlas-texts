# Preparing for the Standing Roar

This place is not hostile in the way a storm or a predator is. Nothing here wants anything from you. But it is relentless in several ways at once: altitude, light, sound, wind, cold, and dryness. The stillness of the scene hides how hard all of it pushes on a body. Your preparation is mostly about standing up to constancy. Nothing will surprise you here, and nothing will let up either.

---

## 1. The Air: Altitude First

"Every breath has to be taken twice" suggests elevation on the order of 5,000 meters or more, where each breath carries about half the oxygen of sea level. This is the most likely thing to kill an unprepared visitor, and it will do it quietly, long before the edge or the wind has a chance.

**Before you go:**
- **Acclimatize in stages.** Do not step through a portal directly onto the plateau. Spend a week or more at progressively higher altitudes, in your own world or an intermediate one. The approach route up the lower slopes exists for a reason: the people who came here walked up, and their bodies adjusted on the way.
- **Talk to a doctor about acetazolamide**, and carry dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies. Learn the signs of altitude sickness (headache, nausea, poor sleep), of fluid on the lungs (breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, pink froth), and of brain swelling (confusion, stumbling, strange behavior).
- **Carry a pulse oximeter.** You will not be able to hear your own breathing or your companion's. You'll need numbers.
- **Consider supplemental oxygen** for any extended stay, or at least an emergency canister.

**The crucial complication:** the roar will mask the early warnings. Headache, pressure behind the eyes, nausea, and disorientation are all symptoms of both altitude sickness *and* intense low-frequency sound. You will not be able to tell which one is hurting you. Treat any worsening of these as altitude sickness until proven otherwise. The one cure for altitude sickness, descent, is also the cure for the sound.

## 2. The Light: Protect Yourself From Below

The description is precise about something most mountain travelers never face: **the light comes from underneath.** The cloud-floor is a reflector stretching to the horizon. It works like snowfield glare, except it is below you and everywhere, and it never moves.

- **Glacier glasses with side shields are not enough.** You need eye protection that blocks light from *below*: wraparound goggles with a full lower seal, or glasses with a lower shield added. Snow blindness (sunburned corneas) is excruciating and leaves you blind for a day or more on a cliff edge.
- **Apply sunscreen to the places sunscreen never goes:** under the chin, under the jaw, inside the nostrils, the undersides of the ears, the eyelids, under the nose. Use a high-SPF lip balm and reapply constantly. At this altitude and under this violet sky, the UV is brutal.
- **Cover your face with fabric** (a balaclava or buff) and wear gloves even when warm. The backs of hands burn fast here.
- **Expect your depth perception to degrade.** Shadows are "thin and faint and seem to come from nowhere," and shadows are how the brain reads the shape of the ground. Stones will look flat. Grooves in the rock will be hard to see until your boot finds them. Move slowly and deliberately.
- **The squint is real.** Expect eye fatigue and tension headaches. Bring lubricating eye drops; the dry wind will dry your eyes out even behind goggles.

## 3. The Edge: The Most Dangerous Illusion

**The cloud-floor looks like ground.** It is level, white, unbroken, and it goes to the horizon like a plain. Your eyes will read it as a surface. Add the flattened shadows and the missing depth cues, and the edge of the escarpment becomes very hard to judge. People who stay long enough to relax may stop *feeling* that the drop is there. It is thousands of feet.

- **Decide on a minimum distance from the edge before you arrive**, and mark it physically, with a line of low flat stones like the old cairns or a staked cord, so you don't have to judge it by eye.
- **The wet band runs along the edge and it is slick.** The granite is permanently beaded with condensation and crusted with lichen. Wet lichen on smooth rock is like ice. Do not cross the wet band toward the edge without being roped and anchored.
- **Crampons or microspikes are a bad idea here.** They would scar lichen that took millennia to grow. Use soft, grippy rubber soles, and use rope for security instead.
- Oddly, the wind itself may protect you near the lip. Air pours up the face and over onto the plateau, so it pushes you *away* from the drop. Do not rely on this. Where the rock ribs break the flow there will be eddies and reversals, and a sudden change in wind pressure is exactly the moment you lose your footing.

**Never try to descend into the cloud.** Whatever is below it is outside this description, and the air rising from it is warmer and heavier, from another country entirely.

## 4. The Wind: Nothing Loose Survives

The first rule the landscape teaches is written in its geology: *nothing loose remains.* No pebbles. No sand. Whatever is not fixed down is gone. That includes your gear.

**Gear discipline:**
- **Tether everything.** Lens caps, sunglasses, hats, maps, notebooks, water bottle lids, food wrappers, gloves you take off for a moment. Use leashes, carabiners, and lanyards. Anything you drop will be carried inland across the plateau and lost, and it will litter a place that has never known litter.
- **Wear no clothing that can act as a sail.** Close-fitting windproof layers, hood cinched down, nothing flapping. A flapping jacket won't make any sound you can hear, but it will beat against your body for hours, and that is exhausting.
- **A normal tent will be destroyed.** Learn from the cairn-builders: *low and flat.* Camp far from the Throat, well back from the edge, in the lee of a rock rib. Use a bivy sack or an extremely low four-season shelter, staked and weighted with anything available, including your pack and your body. Some travelers might choose not to sleep on the plateau at all, making day visits from a camp lower on the approach.

**Approaching the Throat:**
- The flow from the mouth of the Throat "would knock a standing person flat." **Do not walk into its axis.** Approach from the side, along the fluted walls, never across the stream. The pale tongue of the wet band fanning out from the mouth marks the main flow for you. Treat it as a river.
- Near the Throat, **move low.** Crouch, crawl, keep three points of contact. Be anchored to a companion or a fixed point.
- **Windchill will be severe and never lets up.** There is no calm hour to warm up in. The dry wind also strips moisture from your body with every breath. Pack more insulation than the air temperature suggests, and remember that "warm in the sun" rock will be "very cold in the lee."

## 5. The Sound: The Hazard Nobody Is Ready For

This is what makes the Standing Roar unlike any mountain you've climbed. The sound is not background. It is a physical force acting on your body all the time.

**Understand what you're facing.** The description gives three layers:
- **A low fundamental, felt in the breastbone and teeth before it's heard.** This is low-frequency sound, probably reaching into infrasound. Earplugs do little against it; it passes through your skull and chest directly. Prolonged exposure to intense infrasound is linked to nausea, dizziness, anxiety, fatigue, pressure in the head and chest, and possibly visual disturbances, since the eyeball has its own resonant frequencies.
- **A constant surf-like hiss.** Broadband noise at a level that drowns out your own voice a mile away. This is the part that causes permanent hearing damage.
- **A glassy high overtone.** High frequencies cause tinnitus and ear fatigue.

**Protection:**
- **Double hearing protection is mandatory:** high-rated foam earplugs plus sealed over-ear earmuffs. Wear both from the moment the roar becomes loud, which will be long before you can see the Throat.
- **Get a baseline hearing test before the trip and another after.** Expect some temporary threshold shift. Watch for tinnitus that doesn't fade.
- **Limit your time near the Throat.** Plan it in minutes, not hours. The mile-off distance is already very loud; the lip of the Throat, where "sound stops being sound," should be a brief, deliberate visit, like diving to depth and coming back up.

**The adaptation trap:** after a day or two your brain will stop presenting the roar. You will "hear silence." This is a real phenomenon and it is seductive, but your cochlea is still being damaged and your body is still under stress. **Adaptation is not safety.** Keep your protection on even when you feel like you're in silence. That feeling is exactly when you'll be tempted to take it off.

**Losing a sense:**
- You will not hear rockfall, a companion calling for help, a slip, a fall, or your own gasping.
- **Develop a non-verbal communication system before you go,** and practice it until it's automatic: hand signals for *stop, danger, come here, I'm unwell, turn back, look.* Include a signal for "I'm okay," given regularly.
- **Carry a small mirror or signal light.** Use it the way the swifts use their white wing-crescents. Glint communication works superbly in this light. (But see the note on the birds below.)
- **Use touch.** A shared tether can carry tug signals: one tug for "okay," three for "stop," continuous for "emergency."
- **Try the grazers' method.** Tapping or stamping on granite carries through the rock. You can feel the herd's patter through your palm, so you may be able to feel a companion's too.
- **Radios with bone-conduction or throat-contact microphones** might work at a distance from the Throat, though the vibration may overwhelm them close in. Test them in a loud environment beforehand.
- **Carry a waterproof slate or notebook** for anything complicated. You'll be writing a lot.

## 6. Water, Food, and the Body

- **The plateau is dry, except for the wet band.** There is no standing water beyond a thumbnail's depth. Bring all the water you need, or plan to harvest it.
- **Condensation harvesting is possible:** a fine mesh net strung low in the wet band, facing the flow, will collect moisture as it pours over the lip. Test and filter it. The air carries aerial plankton and who knows what else from below the cloud. Stake the net very securely, and never let it go.
- **Dehydration will be rapid.** Dry wind, high altitude, and heavy breathing all strip water from you. Drink on a schedule. Your usual cues won't be reliable.
- **Appetite drops at altitude.** Bring calorie-dense food you actually like and eat on a schedule.
- **Expect insects in your face.** The wind carries a constant drift of gnats, winged ants, ballooning spiders with their silk threads, and aphids. Near the Throat, where the harvest is "thickest," they'll be pelting you. Goggles help. A fine mesh over the mouth and nose lets you breathe through your mouth, which you'll have to do at this altitude, without inhaling midges.
- **Human waste must be packed out.** Nothing decomposes quickly up here, there's no soil, and the wind carries everything.

## 7. The Life: Walk as if Every Step Costs a Century

Everything living here is slow, old, and precisely balanced. You are a sudden large animal in a place where change is measured in hair's breadths per lifetime.

- **Lichen grows "a fraction of a millimeter per century."** A single boot print through a rosette can destroy something older than recorded human history in your own world. Keep to bare granite wherever you can. Where the wet band is crusted solid, cross it at one chosen point and reuse your own path.
- **Never stand on a cushion plant.** They are dense enough to bear your weight, and that is the temptation. Some of them "the size of a sleeping animal" may be thousands of years old.
- **If you see the cushion plants flowering,** that is a rare, one-day event. Watch, photograph from a distance, and do nothing else.
- **Stay out of the Throat during nesting.** The nests are glued into the fluted walls, and chicks hatch into the roar. Your presence could keep parents away from them. You would also have to climb into the most violent air on the plateau to reach them, which is dangerous in itself.
- **Be careful with mirrors and signal lights around the swifts.** Their whole social life runs on flashes of white in sunlight. Your heliograph might read as courtship, alarm, or noise. Point signals *down* or across the plateau, never up into the colony.
- **Watch for fast birds near the Throat lip.** They streak past as "dark lines." A collision with your face at that speed could hurt both of you. Goggles again.
- **Move gently near the grazers.** They communicate through the rock, so your heavy footfalls might be heard as a loud, meaningless shouting. Walk softly, stop often, and put your palm to the stone.
- **The vultures are not a threat,** but notice if they start circling low over you or a companion. They are experts at recognizing an animal that is failing, possibly before you are.
- **Clean your boots, clothes, and gear of seeds and spores before arrival.** Anything foreign you bring will be picked up by a wind that never stops and scattered across the whole plateau.

## 8. The Traces: Leave Them Exactly As They Are

- **Use the cairn line as your route.** It was built by people who understood the wind, and it leads toward the Throat along the path they judged safest.
- **Do not add to the cairns, restack them, or build your own** (except temporary edge-markers, which you remove when you leave).
- **Do not disturb the cache.** Do not take the drilled bones. Do not "just look" and put them back. The cord is brittle with age, and handling could destroy it. Photograph it from where you stand. Someone "meant to return, or wanted to leave something." Either way, it isn't yours.
- **The worn sitting-hollows in the rock ribs** are the best places on the plateau: backs to the wind, facing the Throat, shaped by generations. You may sit in them. That is what they're for. You are joining a practice, not intruding on it.
- **Do not bring an instrument to play.** Nothing could be heard. Let the bone flutes stand as their own answer.

## 9. The Mind: Constancy Is Its Own Weather

Most travel hazards come from change: storms, nightfall, predators. Here the hazard is **that nothing changes.** Same wind, same pitch, same light, same scene every morning.

- **Expect sensory monotony to do strange things to you.** Time distortion, a dreamy detached feeling, trouble concentrating, and, near the Throat, the sense that "thinking stopped." Some people will find this ecstatic. Some will find it terrifying. Many will find it both within an hour.
- **Decide your purpose and your time limit before you arrive,** and write them down. The place gives you no natural reason to leave. A day at the Throat blurs into two, then three. Set a hard turnaround time and have your companion hold you to it.
- **Never go alone.** Not because of predators, but because you won't be able to hear yourself or judge yourself. A companion who can see your face, your gait, and your oximeter reading is your early-warning system.
- **Do buddy checks on a schedule** using your signal system. Ask: are you drinking? Eating? Is your head worse? Are you confused? Do you know what time it is?
- **The stars at night are "hard and many and very near."** Go out for them, briefly and well bundled. The wind doesn't stop at night, and cold plus windchill in the dark at altitude can kill quickly.

---

## 10. Leaving: Going Deaf in Reverse

The people who returned said the ordinary quiet of the valleys "sounds hollow and frightening, like a held breath." Take them seriously.

- **Plan a staged descent.** Spend a night or two partway down the approach, where the roar is distant, before dropping into true quiet.
- **Bring a white-noise source** (an app, a small speaker, a fan) for your first nights home. It gives you something to lean on as your brain recalibrates.
- **Expect tinnitus for a while,** and possibly anxiety or insomnia in silent rooms. Your nervous system has been braced against a constant force; it may take days to stop bracing.
- **Get your hearing tested within a week.** If there's any lasting loss or tinnitus, see an audiologist.
- **Expect to feel the note in your chest for a while, even after you can't hear it.** The ancient visitors seem to have come here specifically for that feeling. They went "somewhere so loud that thinking stopped" and carried the note back down. If you're lucky, you will too.

## Packing Summary

**Essential:**
- Acclimatization plan and altitude medications (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine)
- Pulse oximeter
- Double hearing protection: foam plugs plus sealed muffs, with spares
- Wraparound goggles with lower shielding; spare pair
- High-SPF sunscreen for all surfaces, including undersides; lip balm; eye drops
- Close-fitting windproof shell, insulated layers, balaclava, gloves, cinched hood
- Soft-soled, grippy boots (no crampons)
- Climbing rope, harness, anchoring gear for edge and Throat approaches
- Tethers and leashes for every piece of loose gear
- Low-profile shelter or bivy, heavily staked, or a plan to camp below the plateau
- All the water you need, plus a condensation net and filter
- Calorie-dense food
- Waste bags to pack out everything
- Signal mirror, signal light, waterproof slate or notebook
- A pre-agreed hand, touch, and tug signal system, rehearsed

**Useful:**
- Bone-conduction or throat-mic radios (tested beforehand)
- Supplemental oxygen
- Mesh mouth and nose cover for insects
- White-noise device for the return
- A camera with a leashed lens cap, for the lichen pelt, the glinting colony, and the flutes of the Throat

**Leave behind:**
- Any instrument
- Any urge to take a souvenir
- The expectation of silence, or of change

---

You are going somewhere that has held one shape for longer than anything you know. Arrive slowly, move softly, protect your eyes and ears and breath, and stay well back from the bright floor that isn't a floor. Sit where the others sat, with your back to the wind and your face to the Throat. Let the note fill your chest for as long as you planned, and no longer. Then walk down into the frightening quiet, carrying it.
