# Preparing to Visit the Hush Geode

## A Note Before Anything Else

Most dangerous places are dangerous because of what they do to you. The Hush Geode is dangerous because of what *you* do to *it*, and because of what your own body does in a place where every sound becomes a physical object. Nothing here wants to hurt you. The real threats are your cough, your zipper, your stomach, your gasp of wonder, and your sobbing if the beauty undoes you.

It is also a place where your visit may already be written into the walls. You aren't just preparing to explore. You may be preparing to keep an appointment.

Prepare for three things: **silence, light, and the possibility that you belong here.**

---

## Part One: Who Should Go

The geode is small, sealed, bright, and nearly silent, and its entrance closes behind you. Be honest with yourself about the following.

**Do not go if you:**
- Have claustrophobia, panic disorder, or a history of dissociation under sensory deprivation. The room is chapel-sized, but it is sealed, egg-shaped, and slowly closing. You will know it is closing.
- Have a chronic cough, asthma, allergies, or a cold. Even a mild cold is disqualifying. One sneeze sends a burst of glass needles outward from your face.
- Snore, grind your teeth, or tend to nod off when still. You must not sleep here.
- Have loud joints (knees or ankles that crack), frequent hiccups, or a noisy digestive system you can't manage.
- Use hearing aids that whistle, a CPAP machine, an insulin pump that beeps, or any medical device that makes sound. Check every device you depend on.
- Tend to exclaim, laugh, or narrate your experiences aloud without meaning to.

**Go alone, or with at most one other person.** The marks in the chamber (the handprints, the stair, the arch, the word) suggest one visitor, or one visitor returning. A group multiplies breath, warmth, condensation, and noise, and a crowded chamber of fragile animals can't absorb that. If you bring a companion, consider leaving them **on the surface, near the crack.** More on why below.

## Part Two: Training in Silence

This is the most important preparation, and it takes weeks.

### Measure yourself
Get a sensitive decibel meter and a quiet room, ideally an anechoic chamber if you can arrange time in one. Learn exactly what sounds your body makes:
- Your breathing at rest, and after climbing a flight of stairs.
- Your footsteps on different surfaces.
- The sound of your clothes when you raise an arm, kneel, or turn your head.
- Your swallowing. Your stomach an hour after eating, and three hours after.
- What happens when you are startled.

Every one of these sounds will become glass. A careless exhale becomes "a faint frost of needles in the air." You want your sounds to be so small that they settle as dust the chime-grazers will clean up within minutes.

### Breathe like a sleeper
Practice slow, nasal, diaphragmatic breathing until it is automatic even when your heart is racing. Practice it while frightened: watch unsettling footage, take cold showers, stand at heights. The geode will surprise you. Rain will fall from nowhere, the light will turn to starlit night without warning, and you will see your own handprint. Your breath must not break when your composure does.

### Move like a thief
Practice moving through a room without sound: rolling heel-to-toe, lowering your weight slowly, placing your hands before you lean. Practice kneeling and rising. Practice reaching overhead. Do it blindfolded, because you may be squinting against glare for your first minutes inside.

### Rehearse the emergency
You *will* feel a cough, sneeze, or hiccup coming at some point. Train the response:
1. Bring a thick felt pad (see gear) to your mouth and nose.
2. Press it firmly and let the sound happen *into* it.
3. Hold still for a moment. Let the crystal grow into the felt rather than the air.
4. Then move slowly one or two body-lengths away from where it happened.

That last step matters. Sound-crystal grows *outward from its source*, and the Listeners follow the filament back to where it began. You don't want to be at the point it points to.

### Rehearse the word
Near the entrance seam lies a single spoken word, crystallized. It is not dusty or eaten, and it is "shaped like a voice that was speaking to someone." It may be yours.

You should decide before you go what you would be willing to leave behind as a permanent object. You don't need to script it. Your intuition should tell you that trying to *force* the word is a mistake. But know who you would speak to if you spoke. Spend time with that question. When the moment comes, you should not have to wonder.

## Part Three: Health and the Body, the Days Before

- **Two weeks out:** Avoid anyone sick. Get any dental work done, since a toothache makes you clench and grind.
- **Three days out:** Stop carbonated drinks, beans, dairy if it troubles you, and anything else that makes your gut vocal. Eat simply.
- **The night before:** Sleep well. You cannot rest inside.
- **The day of:** Eat a small, bland meal three to four hours before entering. You want to be neither full (gurgling) nor hungry (growling). Use the toilet thoroughly.
- **Caffeine:** A small amount is fine for alertness. Avoid enough to make you jittery or send you to relieve yourself.
- **Antihistamines:** Consider a non-drowsy one. The floor is dusted with crystalline "flour," the residue of old sounds, and it will get kicked up.
- **Limit your visit** to a few hours at most. Everything in your body is harder to control over time: bladder, fatigue, restlessness, emotion.

## Part Four: Clothing

You're dressing for four things: **silence first,** then cool damp air, intense light, and a floor strewn with sharp glass.

### Fabrics
- **Wool** is your best friend. Merino base layers and a soft boiled-wool or felted outer layer. Wool is silent, stays warm when damp, and doesn't crinkle.
- **Soft fleece** is acceptable if it's the brushed, non-pilling kind.
- **Silk** works for inner layers.
- **Avoid entirely:** nylon shells, Gore-Tex and other waterproof membranes, ripstop, waxed canvas, stiff denim, and anything that swishes when your thighs or arms pass each other. In ordinary life this sound is nothing. Here it grows a thicket of whiskers at every step.

### Fasteners
- **No zippers.** No velcro, no snaps, no metal buttons, no buckles. Their sounds are sharp and distinct, and sharp sounds make sharp crystals.
- Use soft ties, wooden or horn toggles wrapped in thread, wrap-style garments, or pull-overs. Tape down anything that could tap.

### Footwear
This is a real dilemma. Bare feet are quietest, but the floor has "fresh fractures, clean-edged and sharp," plus shards of old noises, some of them shaped like coughs.
- Wear **thick felted moccasins or felt-soled tabi boots** with a cut-resistant inner layer of knitted aramid or Dyneema sock liners.
- Wear no hard soles and no treads that might click or squeak.
- Walk in them at home until they're broken in and silent.

### Hands
- **Knit cut-resistant gloves** (quiet, flexible) for moving around.
- But know that **you may need to take one off.** The handprints on the wall are bare. If the moment comes, you'll feel it.
- Keep gloves on otherwise. The soft stone near the fault "takes a fingerprint," and whatever touches the wall is remembered in both directions of time. Don't leave marks casually.

### Head and face
- A soft wool cap. Hair brushing against collars makes more sound than you'd think.
- **Eye protection is essential.** See the next section.

### Layering for rain
When the fault pours the light of a rainy day, actual water falls, a fine drizzle across a room-sized space. You can't wear a rain shell. Wool will handle an hour of drizzle well enough. Bring a spare dry wool layer in your pack.

## Part Five: The Light

Don't underestimate this. You're underground, but you'll be standing in **full noon sun**, sometimes summer noon, sometimes a **snow-glare morning**. That second one is the light that causes snow blindness.

- **Glacier glasses** with side shields, or wraparound category-4 sunglasses, but with rubber or silicone fittings so they don't click. A soft leash, not a metal chain. Keep a second, lighter pair (category 2) for gold afternoons and rainy greys.
- **Sunscreen,** reef-safe and unscented, applied before you enter. Assume the light carries its full UV. It came from actual summer noon.
- **Brimmed cap:** soft wool or felted, not stiff.
- **Adjusting to dark:** "One startling interval of blue-dark night with stars visible *in the pools*." It will come without transition. Don't move when it does. Stand still, let your eyes adjust, and watch. This is one of the most beautiful things you will ever see, and the moment you're most likely to stumble into something sharp.

### Don't sit in the pools
The light *pools* in low places. It is slightly warm and thickens as it ages. At the edges of old pools it "begins to set, turning cloudy, then milky, then hard." The chamber's oldest shelves contain "the sealed shapes of things that did not move in time."

You can wade your hand through a pool. That's fine; it's a gift, so do it. But:
- **Do not rest your belongings in pools.** Keep your pack on your body.
- **Do not sit or kneel in low places for long.** Choose raised, hardened stone.
- **Do not fall asleep.** Beyond snoring, you might wake partly set into the floor.
- **Don't stand still in one spot too long.** Shift gently every few minutes.

### Your shadow
Shadows lag here, sometimes by hours, and they point *toward* the light, not away. You'll see your own shadow reaching toward the fault. When you move, it will stay behind for a while, then follow. Expect to be disoriented by walls crowded with shadows of things no longer there. Don't mistake your lagging shadow for another visitor. Don't mistake another shadow for yours.

The Folds sometimes hunt empty moth-shadows. One might slide over *your* shadow. Let it. That's harmless.

## Part Six: Gear

Pack in a **soft wool or felt bag** with no hard frame. Wrap every hard object individually in cloth so nothing clinks against anything else.

### Bring:
- **Felt cough pads,** two or three: dense, thick, palm-sized. Keep one in your hand or at your chest at all times.
- **Water** in a soft, flexible pouch (a hydration bladder with the bite valve), sipped slowly. No bottles, no caps that twist or pop.
- **A small amount of soft, silent food:** dates, soft bread, cheese. Nothing crunchy or in wrappers. Probably you won't eat.
- **A silent timekeeper.** Digital, with every beep and alarm disabled and verified disabled. The light won't tell you what time it is; it tells you what time it is somewhere else, and changes by the hour. A mechanical watch is unacceptable.
