# Preparing for the Cold Rise of Asker

## The Short Version

Asker will not try to kill you, but it will wear you down. Nothing there is hostile. The skerries won't hit you, the drip-lice only investigate, and the gleaners want dead skerries, not you. The dangers are environmental and cumulative: cold water that never stops, noise that never stops, darkness that never lifts, rock sharp enough to cut, and canals that can kill you in under a minute. Most visitors who get into trouble don't meet a single catastrophe. They get slowly wet, slowly deafened, slowly disoriented and sleepless, and then they make one bad step near a channel edge.

Prepare for four things: **wet cold, sound, darkness, and edges.** Then prepare your mind, because the place is an overwhelming sensory experience and your nervous system will not know what to do with it at first.

---

## 1. When to Go

**Go first in the grey season, if you possibly can.** For a few weeks a thin light comes over the eastern rim and the fog lifts. You will be able to see the shape of the city, or at least its silhouette: the ledges, the canal lines, the drop-offs. That visual map will stay in your head when the dark returns, and it is worth more than any equipment you can carry. The colony's roar is also at its lowest then.

**Avoid a first visit near the middle of the long night.** That is when the roar peaks and the young are being born deep in the warm cells. The noise will be at its worst, and you would be moving among nurseries.

If you arrive in the long dark anyway, expect to spend your first days in the keepers' quiet districts learning to move before you go anywhere loud.

---

## 2. The Cold That Is Wet

This is the most important physical preparation. The air sits a degree or two above freezing and is completely saturated. Every surface weeps. There is always a few millimeters of moving water underfoot. **Nothing will ever dry.** Plan everything around that.

Wet cold near freezing is more dangerous than dry cold well below it, because water pulls heat from your body far faster than air does, and because you will not feel dramatically cold. You will feel damp, then tired, then clumsy, then stupid. By then you are already hypothermic.

**Clothing:**
- **Outer layer:** Do what the keepers do and wear close-fitting waxed layers. Waxed cotton or waxed wool shells work well: they shed water, they are quiet (important for hearing, see below), and they don't snag on sharp rock the way thin synthetic shells will. Loose, flapping gear is bad here. It catches on edges, and it makes noise that masks the echoes you need to hear.
- **Insulation:** Wool or synthetic only, which keep some warmth when wet. Absolutely no down. Down in Asker becomes a cold wet sponge within a day.
- **Vapor-barrier layer:** Consider a vapor-barrier liner, especially for sleeping. Since you can't stay dry from outside, at least keep your insulation from soaking up your own sweat.
- **Sealed dry kit:** Carry one full set of sleeping clothes in a truly waterproof roll-top bag, and **never wear it outside.** Having something dry to sleep in may be the difference between recovering each night and slowly declining.
- **Hands:** You face a real conflict. You need to protect your hands from cold and from rock sharp as a broken plate, but you also need your fingertips to read the keepers' carved wall markings and feel your way. Bring thin, cut-resistant, tactile gloves as your main layer, with heavier waterproof overmitts you can pull on and off. Also consider fingerless cut-resistant liners, so fingertips can be exposed briefly for reading carvings and then covered.
- **Feet:** This is critical. Wear waterproof boots with stiff, puncture-resistant soles. Fresh fractured basalt and volcanic glass can slice through soft soles. The soles must also grip on wet glass. Grippy lugged rubber is usually good on raw fractured rock, but the green glass seams will be slick under a film of water. Bring several pairs of wool socks and change them compulsively. Trench foot is a genuine risk after days of wet feet near freezing. Dry and air your feet every night, inside your shelter.

**Heat:** Nothing on Asker burns. There is no wood, no plant matter, nothing. Bring all your fuel. A small stove for hot drinks is a morale device as much as a survival one. Chemical heat packs are extremely valuable for hands, feet, and the sleeping bag. Eat a lot. You will burn calories just staying warm.

**Shelter:** Ask the keepers where visitors can rest. Their districts are older, quieter, and inert, which makes them better sleeping ground in every way. Bring a bivy or small shelter anyway, one that can go up on sloped, dripping stone. Bring a closed-cell pad; warm stone or not, you don't want to lie in running water. The rock arrives only a few degrees above freezing, so it won't keep you warm.

## 3. Sound: Protect Your Hearing, But Don't Lose It

This is the paradox of Asker. Sound will hurt you, and sound is also your main sense for getting around.

In the dense districts the noise is *loud enough to feel in the teeth.* My intuition is that levels in places like the Tallow terrace approach or exceed a hundred decibels, sustained for hours, and for you it will never stop. That causes permanent hearing damage and tinnitus with enough exposure. It also causes something less discussed: exhaustion. Continuous loud noise raises stress hormones, wrecks concentration, and makes rest nearly impossible.

**What to bring:**
- **Electronic hearing protection** (the kind used on shooting ranges) is ideal if you can get it. It passes normal sound through and compresses loud sounds. You could hear a companion, a canal, or your own clicker's echo, while the roar is capped.
- **Flat-attenuation musicians' earplugs** as backup. They lower the volume evenly without muffling, so echoes and textures stay audible.
- **Foam plugs for sleeping.** Even the quiet districts are loud compared with anything you know.
- Test your hearing before you go and after you return.

**Budget your exposure.** Treat loud districts like deep dives: planned, time-limited, and followed by recovery in quiet stone. Standing under Tallow is one of the things you'll want to do. Do it for a while, with protection, and then leave.

**Learn to click before you arrive.** Humans can learn basic echolocation: a sharp tongue-click, then listening for the returned sound off walls and openings. Blind people use it every day. Practice for weeks beforehand in dark rooms and corridors. You won't be as good as the keepers, but you will understand what they are doing and learn from them much faster. Once there, ask whether a keeper will make or trade you a clicker. The bone or glass instruments presumably produce a sharper, more distinct sound than a tongue does, one that stands out against the colony's fizz.

Expect echolocation to work better in the quieter districts than the loud ones. In Tallow the roar swamps everything, which is probably one reason the keepers live where they do. In the loudest places you'll navigate mostly by touch.

**Communication:** Speech is useless in the dense districts. Before going anywhere loud, agree with your companions on a **touch code**: squeezes and taps on the shoulder or arm meaning stop, go back, edge, danger, I'm cold, I'm not okay. Practice it until it's automatic.

## 4. Darkness: Don't Count on Light

For most of the year there is no sunlight. Your eyes will eventually give you almost nothing.

Bring lights anyway, but understand their limits. The air is **saturated cloud**, and a bright headlamp in fog mostly lights up a glowing white wall a meter from your face, which is often worse than nothing. That may be part of why the keepers rarely carry lamps. The other reason is likely courtesy to the colony and the simple fact that they don't need them.

**Light recommendations:**
- A **handheld lamp, held low**, angled toward your feet and the ground just ahead. Low-angle light cuts fog glare and shows the water film, the edges, and the glass seams.
- A **red or dim setting** for general use. It preserves what little night vision you have and is less intrusive.
- Spare batteries in waterproof storage. Cold drains them fast. Keep the batteries you're using warm inside your clothing.
- Use bright light sparingly, and never shine it into the roost-walls. You are walking among nurseries and hundreds of thousands of sleeping and waking animals. You're a guest.

**Navigation:**
- **Don't trust a magnetic compass.** Fresh basalt is often strongly magnetic, and there are engines in the mantle doing things no one understands. Expect local deflections.
- Satellite positioning, if it exists in your frame at all, will have no meaning there.
- Your best tools are **the keepers' carved wall vocabulary**, which you should ask them to teach you; the **sound of the canals and the falls**, whose direction is constant from anywhere in the city; **the tempo of the drips**, which keepers use to identify ledges and which you'll gradually learn to hear; and **a guide**.

**Hire or befriend a keeper. Seriously.** Asker is miles of stacked cliffs, canyons, and ledges barely wide enough for two people, many ending over drops or canals. It changes over time, though slowly. Its landmarks are invisible. Keepers have lived there for generations. Offer them something of real value: fuel, warm dry textiles, food with flavor, good tools, knowledge from outside. Then do what they tell you.

**Time:** The sky gives you no day and night, only the colony's rhythm. Bring a reliable watch and keep a written log of sleep and waking. Your body clock will drift within days. Without daylight, many people slide into disordered sleep, low mood, and confusion. Keep a strict routine of meals, sleep, and warm drinks. If you can, bring a small bright light for your shelter to reset your body clock each "morning", used privately.

## 5. Edges and Water: The Real Killer

**The canals.** They are black, perfectly straight, cold enough to numb a hand in seconds, flowing swiftly, and running toward falls at the plateau's edge. If you fall in, cold shock will make you gasp involuntarily within seconds. Within a few minutes you'll lose the use of your hands. The current will then carry you toward the drop. **Falling into a canal is very likely fatal.**

The worst part is that the ledges beside the canals are narrow, wet, dark, and sometimes "end abruptly over a canal." The keepers carve markings at shoulder height for exactly these places.

- **Use a rope** near canals and on any narrow ledge. A short tether between you and a companion, or a guide line along known routes.
- Keep **one hand on the wall** and your weight toward it.
- Don't move fast. Nobody in Asker hurries except the skerries.
- Consider a **buoyancy aid** under your outer layer when you're near water. It won't save you from the cold, but it may keep your head up long enough for a companion to pull you out.
- Carry a **throw line** and practice with it.
- If you *do* fall in and get out, **strip and get into dry layers immediately**, then get warm. Wet clothing will finish what the canal started.

**The rock.** Unweathered basalt and glass have crisp edges that have never been dulled. Expect cuts on hands, knees, and shins if you slip or brace carelessly. Knee pads are worth considering if you'll be crouching or climbing. Bring a proper first-aid kit with wound closure strips, antiseptic, and dressings. Wounds heal slowly in cold, wet conditions, and you'll be pressing your hands against walls covered in microbes and small animals all day.

**Heights.** Some districts tower hundreds of meters, and terraces and stairs are stacked in tiers. A fall from a ledge edge isn't only a canal risk. Treat the whole city like an unroped scramble in the dark, because that's what it is.

## 6. Water, Food, and Health

**Drinking.** The text says the glass seams are the best places to drink, and the keepers presumably do. Your body is not theirs. The water that runs off Asker's walls:
- carries leached iron, sulfur, and "reactive compounds that exist nowhere else on the surface" — possibly acidic, possibly carrying metals your gut isn't used to;
- is alive with mineral-eating microbial films;
- runs through, past, and under a colony of millions of warm-blooded animals, their waste, and their dead.

Bring a **good filter plus chemical or UV treatment**, and **pH strips** to check the water. Start with the glass-seam water the keepers recommend, and drink modestly for the first days to see how your body responds. Carry some clean water from home as a reserve. The iron-and-flint taste is part of the experience. Just don't let the experience be a week of stomach trouble.

**Food.** There is nothing to forage. No plants, nothing green, nothing that roots. The only large animals are an ecosystem you have no business eating from. **Bring every calorie you'll need, plus margin.** Warm, fatty, dense food. Hot drinks. Something that tastes good, because morale matters in the dark.

**The skerries.** They're warm-blooded, flying, colonial, fur-covered animals living at huge density: in other words, bat-like, and bats are serious disease reservoirs in many worlds. They don't collide with you, but they will brush your face constantly.
- Get **pre-exposure rabies vaccination** or the local equivalent before you go.
- Never handle a skerry, living or fallen, with bare hands. Let the gleaners and the keepers deal with fallen ones.
- Wear **clear protective glasses or goggles.** They keep wingtips, drip water, and debris out of your eyes, and the fog will make them a nuisance, so choose anti-fog and accept the frustration.
- Consider a **face covering** or light mask in dense districts. Wings flick across lips and noses. Also, a colony that size produces enormous amounts of waste. Most seems to be washed away by the endless water, which is probably why the place smells of struck match and earth rather than ammonia, but pockets may exist. If you enter somewhere that smells sharply of ammonia, leave.

**The drip-lice.** Put your hand on any wall and they'll walk over your fingers within seconds. They're almost certainly harmless, grazers interested in the microbial film, not you. Prepare mentally, though, because it happens on *every* wall, and you'll be touching walls constantly. Some visitors find it unbearable for a day and then stop noticing. Don't put your fingers in your mouth. Wash or sanitize before eating.

**Longcombs and gleaners.** Longcombs are blind and slow and live in the canals, where you shouldn't be anyway. Gleaners are larger than a dog, stilt-legged, and feel ahead with long antennae. They eat fallen skerries, not people. Still, if an antenna touches you, freeze and let it investigate and move on. Don't step into their path on a narrow ledge. You don't want to wrestle anything on a wet shelf above a canal. The keepers may know more about whether they're "animals at all." Ask. Their answers may be the most interesting thing you hear.

**Altitude.** You climb through a cloud layer to reach the plateau. If it's high, expect some altitude effects: breathlessness, headache, poor sleep. Ascend over days if you can, and carry medication for altitude illness if appropriate.

## 7. How to Behave

**Remember that you are casting a vote.** The engines raise stone where bodies and voices concentrate. As a single visitor your weight is nothing next to a million skerries, but understand the principle. Don't set up a noisy camp and loiter in one place. Don't gather a crowd for a spectacle. The keepers spend lifetimes deliberately adding a few centimeters to a wall, and your idle noise is, in a very small way, participating in their politics. If you want to join in, ask. Keepers sometimes go out in groups to clap stone and whistle, night after night, to ask the city to lift. Being invited to add your voice would be a rare honor. Taking it upon yourself would be rude.

**Stay out of the loudest nurseries during birthing time.**

**Don't bring ultrasonic or sonar devices.** Echolocating animals navigate by sound in a world with no light. Flooding their soundscape with artificial pulses may disrupt a colony that has never collided with anything. Don't be the first thing they collide with.

**Don't obstruct the canals.** Keepers spend real effort keeping them clear, because clogged channels flood the ledges. If you drop something, don't try to retrieve it from a canal.

**Don't take stone or animals.** Fresh rock is the basis of all life here. It's tempting to pocket a shard of green glass. Ask first.

**Let the keepers set your pace.** They move by touch and echo, slowly, precisely, without light. That's the only competent way to move in Asker. Match it.

## 8. Preparing Your Mind

This is the part people underestimate.

You will spend days or weeks in near-total darkness, in constant wet cold, in noise that doesn't stop, surrounded by invisible streams of animals that brush your face and walk across your hands. Your eyes will be useless. Your body clock will fail. The ground itself is rising under you, and you may sense it as a vast, patient pressure through your soles at the edge of perception. That's probably infrasound or vibration. It is not an earthquake, and it is not your imagination.

Some people find this unbearable: panic, claustrophobia in the open dark, sensory overload, dread. Others, often after a hard first few days, find it is one of the most extraordinary experiences they will ever have, a place you know entirely through ear, skin, and feet, a city built by consensus with a population of flying animals.

**Before you go:**
- Spend time in the dark. Navigate your home blindfolded. Walk familiar routes with your eyes closed and a guide beside you.
- Spend time in loud environments with hearing protection, to learn how you react to sustained noise.
- Spend a night or two camping in cold rain, deliberately staying damp, to learn your own early signs of hypothermia.
- Practice echolocation clicks and the touch code with your companions.

**While there:**
- Plan retreats. Know how you'll get back to a quiet district if you're overwhelmed.
- Go with at least one companion. Being alone in Asker's dark and noise is where people make fatal mistakes.
- Let yourself be humbled. The keepers describe their lives' work as a few centimeters added to walls the colony built. They seem to have found peace in that. You may too.
