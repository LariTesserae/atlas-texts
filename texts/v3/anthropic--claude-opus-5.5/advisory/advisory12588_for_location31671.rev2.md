# Preparing for the Walled Shelf

## The Short Version

This is a polar under-ice dive in a labyrinth that never ends, in water at or below the freezing point of seawater, with no animals, no landmarks that differ from each other, and possibly no way to reach the surface from where you arrive. It is not a hike or a sightseeing trip. It is closer to a cave dive, an Antarctic ice dive, and a wilderness expedition combined, in a place where you will be the only living animal.

It is also, if you prepare properly, one of the strangest and most beautiful places you could go. You would be drifting over the rooftops of a continent-sized handmade world while the whole forest breathes around you on waves you can't see.

An ordinary person can do this. They cannot do it casually, alone, or on a whim.

---

## 1. The First Problem: You Arrive in the Water

The description says: *"When you first arrive there is nothing to see at all, only the pressure of cold water on every surface."* That sentence changes everything. You do not step onto a beach and wade in. Your arrival point is submerged, probably under ice, probably in darkness.

This means:

- **You must cross over fully kitted and already breathing from your equipment.** Suit sealed, mask on, regulator or rebreather loop in your mouth before the transition. If your crossing method needs a moment of fumbling on the other side, you don't have that moment. Rehearse the transition in a pool or tank at home until putting on the gear is automatic.
- **You may not be able to reach the surface.** For most of the year the ice is a ceiling, and first-year sea ice can be one to two meters thick. You cannot cut through that from below with hand tools while holding your breath or rationing gas. Treat the ice as a solid roof unless you know otherwise.
- **Your return route must work underwater.** Before committing, confirm that you can leave from the arrival point, or from anywhere, while submerged. If the only way home requires reaching air, you need a surface team that has already cut and marked holes, or you must time your visit to the open-water season.
- **Mark your arrival point immediately.** Before you do anything else, anchor a strobe or a line spool at the exact spot. Every enclosure looks like every other enclosure. If you drift ten meters in the dark and lose track of where you came in, you may never find it again.

If the arrival mechanics can't be made safe, a scout crossing by a remote camera or drone should come before any human crossing.

---

## 2. When to Go

The Shelf has roughly three seasons, and they are very different visits.

**Deep winter / brine season.** Total darkness, thick ice, cold brine running through the channels. The ice booms and creaks and the forest grows slowly on stored reserves. This is the most atmospheric season and the most dangerous. You would need artificial light throughout, surface access would be nearly impossible, and the brine pools in the low enclosures are the coldest and possibly the least oxygenated water here. **Not recommended for a first visit.**

**The green season (early).** The sun returns, the ice thins, and dim greenish light filters down. There is enough natural light to see the shapes of walls and canopy, and the forest is starting its growth burst. The ice is thinner but may still be continuous, and it is unpredictable to walk on. **This is the best season for an experienced visitor with surface support.**

**Melt and storm season (late green).** The ice breaks, meltwater spreads out on the surface, and there may be open water. You would have real surface access, but storms stir the silt off the courtyard floors and visibility can drop to nothing for days. The canopy is at its thickest and most tangling. **Best for first-timers who prioritize a guaranteed exit**, as long as you plan around silt events.

If possible, send a probe ahead to check ice thickness, light levels, and visibility before you commit to a date.

## 3. Cold: The Thing That Will Actually Hurt You

Seawater freezes at about -1.9°C. Water under sea ice sits right at that temperature, and the dense brine pooling on the paving can be colder still without freezing because of its salt. The stones "never lose their chill." You will be immersed in water colder than any ordinary recreational diver has experienced.

### Thermal protection
- **A drysuit is mandatory.** Wetsuits are not an option. A crushed-neoprene or trilaminate drysuit with heavy undergarments (thick fleece or Thinsulate-type layers) is the minimum.
- **An active heating vest and heated socks,** battery powered, with the batteries in sealed canisters. Test them in cold water for longer than your planned dive.
- **Dry gloves with a ring system.** Wet gloves at -2°C make your hands useless within minutes, and in a labyrinth full of kelp, useless hands can kill you. You need to be able to hold a knife.
- **A full-face mask** rather than a mouthpiece regulator. Your lips and jaw will go numb and lose their grip on a mouthpiece. A full-face mask also protects your face from the cold-shock reflex and allows through-water voice communications.
- **A thick hood** under or integrated with the mask.

### Regulator freezing
At these temperatures, standard regulators can freeze into free-flow and dump your gas uncontrollably. You need:
- **Environmentally sealed, cold-water-rated first stages.**
- **Two fully independent regulators on two separate first stages,** so one can be shut down if it free-flows.
- Consider a **closed-circuit rebreather**. It gives you far longer duration, warmer and moister breathing gas (a real comfort in cold water), and near silence. But rebreather scrubbers perform worse in cold water, and rebreathers need specialized training. Don't bring one you aren't fully certified on.

### Drysuit and BCD inflators
Inflator valves can freeze open. An inflator stuck open causes a runaway ascent, and here that means smashing into an ice ceiling. Learn to disconnect your inflator hose instantly by feel with gloved hands. Practice until you can do it blind.

### Dive duration
Even well equipped, most people get dangerously cold within 45 to 90 minutes in water this cold. Plan short dives. Your body will tell you it's fine long after it has stopped being fine, because cold dulls your judgment first. Set a hard time limit and obey it no matter how wonderful the view.

### The surface
Coming out wet onto polar ice, possibly at -20°C or colder in wind, is its own emergency. You need a **heated shelter directly over your entry hole**, like the dive huts used in Antarctic research, with a stove, dry clothing, and hot drinks waiting. Getting out of a drysuit with numb hands in the open is how people lose fingers.

## 4. Depth and Gas Planning

The Shelf is shallow by ocean standards, 20 to 30 meters to the paving, but that is not trivially shallow.

- At 30 meters on air you have roughly 20 minutes before you need decompression stops. Under an ice ceiling you can't do a free ascent anyway, so plan your profiles carefully.
- **Use Nitrox** to extend your no-decompression time and reduce fatigue.
- **You don't need to go to the bottom to see this place.** The kelp canopy tops out several meters above the paving, and the walls are only about a person's height. Most of the view is from the level of the canopy top. Cruising at 15 to 20 meters gives a vastly longer, safer dive.
- Mild **nitrogen narcosis** at 30 meters, combined with darkness, cold, and a maze of identical rooms, is a recipe for confusion. Another reason to stay shallower.
- Follow the **rule of thirds** for gas (a third going out, a third coming back, a third in reserve), as in cave diving. Everything is overhead here, either ice or canopy.

## 5. The Labyrinth: Navigation

This is the danger people will underestimate. The description is explicit: the lattice continues forever, looks the same everywhere, and has no open ground. You can travel for a day and still see low rounded walls, pink crust, brown kelp, and a channel somewhere nearby.

### Treat it as a cave
- **Run a continuous guideline** from your entry point, exactly like cave divers do. Tie off to wall stones or lintels, not to kelp, which moves and can break. This line is your only reliable way home.
- **Line markers** (directional arrows and cookies) point toward the exit.
- Don't let the line pull through the canopy. Kelp will grab it, tangle it, and saw at it in the current. Run it along wall tops.

### Supplementary navigation
- **A compass,** but verify that it works. You don't know what this world's magnetic field is like. Test it in the first minutes against a known direction.
- **An inertial or acoustic navigation unit,** if you can get one. An acoustic beacon at your entry hole that your wrist unit can home in on would be invaluable.
- **Use the channels.** They are the only large-scale structure, and they run consistently downslope. The brine flows downhill along them in winter, and the combed kelp inside them points with the flow. If you know whether your exit is upslope or downslope, a channel is a highway. But brine pools collect at the downslope end, so going downhill ultimately leads into the coldest, stillest, least oxygenated water.
- **Count walls.** It sounds tedious but it works: "Fourteen walls north along the channel, then left." Write it on a slate as you go.

### Don't do this
Don't "just look over the next wall." The next wall is identical to this one. Neither is the next one after that any different. People get lost in featureless environments exactly one wall at a time.

## 6. Hazards Specific to the Shelf

### Kelp entanglement
Every enclosure is filled "to its rim" in the growing season. Tough, leathery stipes several meters long, gas-bladders, and holdfasts are gripping everything. Kelp wraps around tank valves, fin straps, and regulator hoses, and around a panicking diver it gets worse.

- Carry **at least two, preferably three, cutting tools**: a line cutter or trauma shears on your chest, a blunt-tip knife on your waist, and a backup where either hand can reach it.
- Streamline your gear. Nothing dangling, and a valve cage or protective rolls on your cylinder.
- **Swim above the canopy, not through it,** wherever the gap between canopy and ice allows. Where it doesn't, move slowly, part the stipes with your hands, and never kick hard in dense kelp.
- If you get caught, **stop, breathe, and cut one strand at a time.** Twisting makes it worse.

### Overhead environments within the overhead
- **Don't enter the beehive chambers.** They are corbelled, ancient, slumped, with holdfasts working into their joints, and stones do knock loose. Being pinned by a collapse inside a crawlspace under ice is not survivable. Look in with a light from outside.
- **Don't hang onto walls or pull yourself along them.** The stones are loose and have been levered by roots for millennia. The "dull knock" you'll hear now and then is a stone that finally gave way. Don't let it be the one you were holding.
- **Culverts** where channels pass under enclosures are tunnels. Don't swim through them.

### The peat rooms and the silt
Some enclosures are filled with compressed, rotting kelp, a spongy dark floor decomposed only by bacteria, possibly for centuries.

- **Don't stand on, kneel on, or touch these deposits.** You could sink in. Your fins would lift clouds that ruin visibility instantly.
- Cold, poorly mixed, bacterially decomposing organic matter often means **anoxic zones and hydrogen sulfide**, possibly methane. Water directly over these deposits and in stagnant brine pools may be low in oxygen. It won't affect your breathing gas, but it tells you the water is stagnant and the silt is foul. Keep exposure off your skin, rinse your gear afterward, and don't sample it without gloves.
- **Silt-outs.** Fine silt carpets the courtyard floors. One careless fin kick, or a storm, and visibility drops to zero. Learn **frog kicks and back kicks** that push water horizontally, not down. If you're silted out, keep one hand on your guideline and follow it out by touch.

### Water layers and buoyancy
The Shelf's "weather" happens in layers: dense brine near the paving, fresher water above, fresh meltwater on top in summer, with sharp boundaries between them.

- **Crossing layers changes your buoyancy.** Drop into dense brine and you'll suddenly rise. Swim up into a fresh meltwater lens and you'll sink. Expect to adjust constantly.
- At the boundaries, mixing water creates a **shimmering blur (a halocline)**, like heat haze. It can make you dizzy and ruin your depth perception.
- **Internal waves** lift and lower these boundaries by meters over an hour. If you're sitting near a layer line, the layer itself may rise past you, dropping the temperature and shifting your trim without you moving at all. Watch the kelp. When whole districts start leaning together, a wave is passing. It's beautiful, and it's also a buoyancy change coming your way.

### Ice
- In the melt season, surface ice may be thin, rotten, or breaking into floes. Use proper ice-safety procedure (spikes, probes, tethered movement) when on the surface.
- Under the ice, **never lose track of your hole.** The standard ice-diving practice is to be **tethered to a surface tender** by a line, with rope signals. That is strongly recommended here.
- The **booming and cracking** of the ice is loud and can be startling. It is almost always normal. But if you're working near a thin area or a pressure ridge in the melt season, pay attention.

### The absence of animals: a question mark
There are no animals at all. No fish, no crabs, no worms, no plankton that swims. Some of this is explained by the description, but you don't actually know *why* no animals live here. It could simply be that none ever arrived. It could also mean something in the water is hostile to animal life: a toxin, a trace chemical, a pathogen, unusual water chemistry.

Be cautious:
- **Test the water** before your first dive: pH, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and screens for heavy metals and common toxins.
- Keep skin exposure to zero, which the drysuit gear already does.
- **Don't drink it, don't taste it,** and rinse your equipment in fresh water after every dive.
- Monitor yourself afterward for any unusual symptoms.

The kelp is thriving, so it's probably ordinary seawater. But "probably" is not a plan in a place that has no animals.

## 7. Biosecurity: The Most Important Ethical Duty

This section matters as much as your own safety.

The Shelf's entire character depends on having no animals. The forest grows unchecked because nothing grazes it. If you bring in a single copepod, amphipod, snail egg, or sea urchin larva, even microscopic, and it survives and breeds, it could spread across a continent of ungrazed kelp with nothing to stop it. You could permanently transform an ecosystem that has been stable for an almost unimaginable length of time. You would be the ancestor of every creature that ever lives here.

Therefore:
- **Use brand-new equipment,** or equipment that has never touched natural water.
- If any gear has been in a sea, lake, or aquarium, **sterilize it thoroughly**: soak in a biocide, dry completely for weeks, and inspect every crevice. Drysuit seams, BCD bladders, and the inside of regulator second stages are where tiny organisms hide.
- **Don't bring any organic material into the water** that you don't have to. No snacks inside your mask, no bait, no specimens from elsewhere.
- **Your own body** is covered in microbes, and there's nothing to do about that entirely, but drysuits keep most of it contained. **Don't urinate into the water.** Use a sealed urine-collection system (a P-valve that vents out is a real consideration here; prefer an internal collection bag or keep dives short).
- **Leave nothing behind.** Recover every line, marker, and weight.

You are the first animal in this sea in a very, very long time. Try to make sure you're also the last.

## 8. Food, Water, and Supply

- **Nothing here is edible except the kelp.** Brown kelp is edible in principle, since kombu is a kelp. In a true emergency, it could stave off starvation, but it is very high in iodine and should be eaten only in small amounts, cooked, and only after water testing has ruled out toxins. Plan never to need it.
- Bring **all food and all drinking water,** or a way to melt and treat snow and ice from the surface. Sea ice becomes low in salt as it ages; multi-year ice melts into nearly fresh water. Newly frozen ice is salty.
- **Fuel** is life on the surface. You need stoves to heat your shelter, melt water, and dry gear. Plan generously.
- **Batteries** die fast in cold. Carry extra, keep them warm inside your clothing or shelter, and choose lithium chemistries rated for low temperatures.
- The surface world above the ice is unknown to you. It may be as lifeless as the water. Don't count on anything you didn't bring.

---

## 9. Light and Seeing

- In the dark season or under thick ice, it is **completely black.** Bring a primary canister light and at least two backup lights. That's the standard cave-diving minimum and it applies here.
- **Wide, soft beams** are better than narrow spots. Kelp creates dense shadows, and a narrow beam turns the forest into a confusing tunnel of flickering stipes.
- Silt causes **backscatter.** Hold lights to the side, not directly in front.
- If you visit in the green season, try **turning your lights off for a while** once you're sure of your line. Let your eyes adjust. The dim green-grey light falling through thin ice onto an endless lattice of walls, with the canopy a brown ceiling and the enclosures below as darker grids, is the view this place actually has. Your light shows you one room. Darkness shows you the pattern.
- A **strobe** at your entry point and at each line junction makes a huge difference.

## 10. Team and Training

An ordinary person can visit the Shelf, but an ordinary untrained person should not. Before going, you want:

- **Advanced open-water certification,** plus **drysuit, ice diving, and cave or overhead diving** specialties. The cave training is arguably the most important, because the navigation logic is identical.
- **Many hours in very cold water.** Train in the coldest diving you can reach at home: a winter quarry, a glacial lake, the White Sea, or McMurdo if you're lucky.
- **Practice kelp diving** in a real kelp forest (California, Tasmania, Norway, Chile) so entanglement and gear handling are familiar.
- **A team**: at minimum a dive buddy and a surface tender, and ideally a separate surface support person to manage the hut, heat, and emergency response. Solo diving under ice in an infinite labyrinth is not a reasonable risk for anyone.
- **Emergency medical preparation**: hypothermia treatment, rewarming protocols, frostbite care, and decompression illness protocols. Oxygen at the surface. There is no hospital, no recompression chamber, and no rescue service in this world, so you must know how to get an injured person home through the crossing.

## 11. The Psychological Side

This part is easy to dismiss and important to prepare for.

**The silence.** Real seas crackle with shrimp, fish, and countless small clicking things. The Shelf lacks all of that. People who have never heard true underwater silence often find it deeply uncomfortable. Your own breathing will be enormous in your ears. On a rebreather it will be quieter still. Then there will be long stretches of nothing, broken by the far-off boom and creak of ice, the soft woody groan of kelp stems rubbing when the current rises, and occasionally a single stone knocking loose, with a gritty trickle after it, somewhere you can't see. That knock will make you jump every time.

**The sameness.** Wall after wall after wall. The mind searches for landmarks and doesn't find them. Some people become anxious. Some drift into a trance-like state that feels peaceful and is actually a loss of situational awareness. Keep a task, such as counting walls or checking gauges on a schedule.

**The made-ness.** This will be the part that stays with you. Everything here was made by hands. Every stone was chosen and turned until it sat right, across a whole continent. The querns still have their dished hollows. There are doorway lintels. There are rows of posts that once stood in air. And there is no one. Not the builders, not any animal, not even a snail. You may feel that you are trespassing in someone's home, or that the place is waiting. It is common, in places like this, to feel watched in an empty room. Know in advance that this feeling will come and that it is your mind responding to an environment built for people but containing none.

**The scale.** The description says it's hard to hold in mind, and it is. Knowing that the lattice goes on past any horizon, under ice that "no one will ever reach," can produce something between awe and dread. Let it come. Don't let it hurry you.

**Isolation.** You will be farther from other people than almost anyone has ever been. Plan regular communication with your team, and after the trip, talk about it.

---

## 12. Etiquette and Respect

- **Take nothing.** Not a loom weight, not a quern, not a stone from a wall. They're part of a pattern that has outlasted its own structure. Every stone you move makes that pattern slightly less complete, and there's no one left to put it back.
- **Touch as little as possible.** The coralline crust took ages to grow, and the walls are fragile.
- **Photograph everything.** Nobody knows who built this or why. Careful documentation of enclosure shapes, channel layout, sluice slots, and artifacts may be the only evidence the rest of the multiverse ever sees.
- **Record the sounds.** A hydrophone recording of the ice, the kelp, and the stones may be the most evocative thing you bring home.
- **Leave no trace.** No lines, no weights, no markers, no organisms.

## 13. What It Will Actually Be Like

You come through into black water so cold it seems to push against you. For a moment there is nothing: no up, no down, only your breath roaring in your mask and the ache of cold beginning at your wrists and face even through the gear. You snap your strobe to a stone you can't yet see, and in its pulse you glimpse a shape. It's a low, rounded ridge, pink-crusted, with brown straps rising from it into darkness.

You turn on your light. The beam finds a wall, then another wall a few paces beyond it, then the kelp filling the space between them like a room packed with hanging cloth. You rise until you're just above the canopy, and your light skims across the tops of the blades, and below them you can make out lines. They run straight into the dark and cross other lines, and those cross others still, room after room after room.

Far away, the ice booms. The sound goes on and on, fading into a long wavering creak, and you realize it came from somewhere so distant you'll never see it.

You wait. After a while, the kelp all around you begins to lean, slowly, together, as if something vast were exhaling underneath the forest. It takes minutes to lean and minutes more to settle. No current you can feel moved it. It was a wave traveling along a boundary between two waters, rolling across a thousand walled rooms that someone, once, made by hand.

A stone knocks loose somewhere in the lattice. Then silence.

Then your timer goes off, and you follow your line home.

- [ ] Line markers

- [ ] Primary light plus two backups
- [ ] Surface tether and tender

- [ ] New or fully sterilized equipment

- [ ] Zero-trace plan

- [ ] All food, water or ice-melting capacity, stove fuel

- [ ] Camera with housing and lighting

- [ ] Trained buddy, surface tender, surface support
