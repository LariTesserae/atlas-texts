# Preparing for the Stokemoor: A Field Guide for the Ordinary Traveler

## The First Principle: You Are Going Blind

Every preparation follows from one fact. For the whole visit you will see nothing. It will not be dim or shadowy. There will be no light at all. And your usual fallback of bringing a lamp **will probably not work**, because on the Stokemoor fire burns dark. Sparks give no light, and lightning is only thunder and heat. Assume that everything that normally emits light, including your headlamp, glow sticks, phone screen, and LED indicators, will emit nothing visible. Test this on arrival, but do not stake your life on the test coming out differently.

That means your phone is a dead brick unless it talks to you. Your GPS display, your watch face, and your paper map are useless. Every tool you bring must work through sound, touch, or temperature.

The second fact matters almost as much and is easier to miss: **the Stokemoor's climate may be at or beyond the limit of human survival.** The description says it is "warm the way the inside of a mouth is warm" and "wet the way breath on glass is wet." That means air near body temperature at close to 100% humidity. Your body cools itself by evaporating sweat, and in saturated air at mouth temperature, sweat cannot evaporate. It runs off you uselessly. Physiologists put the survivable limit at roughly a 35°C wet-bulb temperature, and even well below that, sustained hard exertion becomes dangerous. Pushing through dense undergrowth is hard exertion.

So plan your trip around two dangers: the dark will disorient you, and the heat can cook you slowly. Everything else is secondary.

## Part One: Before You Go (Months, Not Days)

### 1. Learn to live blind

This is the single most important preparation, and it cannot be done in a weekend.

- **Take orientation-and-mobility training** from an instructor who teaches blind people. Learn long-cane technique: two-point touch, constant contact, and shorelining along edges. The cane will be your primary organ on the Stokemoor.
- **Learn basic echolocation.** Tongue-click echolocation, as taught by practitioners like Daniel Kish, can be learned by sighted adults to a useful degree in a few weeks of practice. On the Stokemoor it will tell you where a rock wall stands, where a stack-tree trunk rises, and where the undergrowth opens. Practice until you can find a doorway by clicking.
- **Live blindfolded** for extended periods: a full day, then several consecutive days. Cook, dress, pack, and use the toilet this way. Most of the misery of blindness is logistical, so solve the logistics at home.
- **Practice setting up your entire camp blindfolded,** in a warm shower if possible. You should be able to pitch your shelter, find your medical kit, and change your socks without sight, with water pouring over you.

### 2. Heat and humidity acclimatization

Spend at least **10 to 14 days** doing graded exercise in a hot, humid environment, such as a heat chamber, a tropical climate, or a sauna room with the humidity raised. Acclimatization increases your plasma volume, lowers your core temperature at rest, makes you sweat earlier, and reduces the salt in your sweat. It will not make saturated body-temperature air safe. It will buy you margin.

Learn the early signs of heat illness in yourself **without relying on sight**: dizziness, headache, nausea, a racing pulse, muscle cramps, confusion, and the moment you stop sweating or start shivering in the heat. Confusion is the dangerous one, because it arrives just as your judgment leaves.

### 3. Medical clearance

Get a thorough physical. Do not go if you have:
- any cardiovascular condition, since heat strain and cardiac strain compound each other
- respiratory conditions, given the sulfurous vent gases and the saturated air
- epilepsy, or a pacemaker or other implanted electronics (a pacemaker should keep you well away from the electrified cables and the cablevine)
- claustrophobia or a history of dissociative episodes, because total darkness combined with constant noise is a severe psychological environment

### 4. Learn your gear by touch

Mark every item in your kit with a distinct tactile code: notches, tape bands, knots, or rubber bumps. Every pocket should have one purpose and only one. On the Stokemoor, an item you put down and forgot is gone for good. It will sink into the mantle-fern sponge and you will never find it.

## Part Two: Equipment

### Sensing and Navigation

**A long cane.** Use a sturdy one, fiberglass or carbon, since you don't want metal near the cables. Give it a large rolling or ball tip, because the ground is mostly soft, rounded, and spongy, and a fine tip will stab into the peat. Bring a spare.

**A thermal sensory-substitution device.** This is your most valuable piece of technology, and it takes advantage of something the description implies. The creatures here sense heat directly, and you can feel a fire column's heat on your face from a hundred meters away. Both facts mean **infrared radiation works on the Stokemoor** even though visible light does not. A thermal camera should therefore still function. Its screen will not. What you need is a thermal camera connected to a **sonification or haptic output**, either an audio interface that turns the heat image into tones, like the vOICe system for blind users, or a haptic vest or tongue display.

With it you can do what the local fauna does:
- the buried steam mains become audible rivers of warmth
- the dark fire columns become bright tones that warn you off
- the **quietcat**, which is almost certainly warm-bodied, becomes detectable (see below)

The main technical problem is condensation. In saturated air, anything slightly cooler than the air fogs over instantly, and a fogged germanium lens makes a thermal camera blind. Choose a unit with a lens heater, or rig a small heating element to keep the lens a degree or two above ambient. Bring lens cloths in a sealed pouch. Train with the device at home for weeks until reading heat by sound feels natural.

**A handheld infrared thermometer with spoken readout.** Point it at a surface before you touch it. Crusted pipes range from pleasantly warm to hot enough to burn you, and you cannot tell which by looking.

**An ultrasonic obstacle detector,** such as a vibrating cane handle or a wearable ranger. Ultrasound sits above most of the moor's audible chorus, so it may cut through the noise better than your ears do.

**Inertial and step-counting navigation.** There is no sky, and probably no satellites or at least none reachable through the lid. **Your compass will be unreliable.** The ground is laced with iron pipe, the rock is "old iron," and even the rivetbacks are magnetic. Bring a talking pedometer and a device that records your path by dead reckoning and speaks your heading and distance back to you. Expect drift and correct it against landmarks.

**A talking watch** or a vibrating one with tactile hands. You also need a way to **count Strokes**, because the Stroke is the local clock. A simple mechanical tally clicker on a lanyard works well.

### The Homing Beacon (Do Not Skip This)

On arrival, **plant a sound beacon at your entry point** before you do anything else. It should be a waterproof unit that emits a distinctive, unnatural pattern: something you will recognize instantly and nothing native to the moor could produce. It should run for far longer than your planned stay.

Two design details matter:
- **Time the beacon to sound during the Stroke's aftermath or the chorus, not the quiet.** A loud artificial sound in the silent gap would be the only sound on the moor at that moment. It might carry beautifully, but you don't know what it might draw, and the thing that hunts in the silence is exactly what you don't want to call.
- Its frequency should sit where the moor is quiet. Listen for a few minutes on arrival before choosing, if your beacon lets you adjust.

Without a beacon, finding your exit point again in a lightless, pathless, featureless landscape of rounded hills and dense fern is close to impossible. The beacon is your way home.

### Tethering

Everything you carry should be leashed to your body or pack with short coiled lanyards: cane, thermal device, thermometer, water bottle, knife, and anything else you would hate to lose. **If you travel with a partner, you should be tethered to each other** with a short line, and you should agree on a system of rope-tug signals: one tug for "stop," two for "come to me," three for "danger." You cannot always count on being heard over the Exhale or the chorus, and you can never see each other.

### Clothing

This involves real trade-offs.

- **Light, long-sleeved, loose synthetic clothing.** It will be soaked within minutes and stay soaked for your entire visit, so it is not there to keep you dry. It protects you from scrapes, the rusty sourness of bloom-lichen, the mild shocks of cablevine, and the leaking condensate full of iron and sulfur. Avoid cotton, which rots, chafes, and gets heavy. Choose clothing that drains and doesn't hold water in thick layers.
- **Boots:** electrical-hazard rated, drainable, with **soft sticky rubber or felt-cleat soles** of the kind anglers use on slick river stones. The channel beds are "polished so smooth that bare feet slide," the peat saddles give underfoot, and the domes are smooth. You'll need grip on smooth wet stone, flotation on peat, and insulation from both heat and current. Consider gaiters against peat and debris.
- **Wraparound shatterproof glasses.** They won't help you see. They keep dripping mineral water out of your eyes, and they protect you from branches and stems you will walk face-first into.
- **A wide stiff-brimmed hat.** Your cane covers the ground and the hat brim covers your head. The brim will bump overhanging leaves and branches before your face does, and it gives an early warning of low obstacles.
- **Gloves: carry two kinds.** Thin, grippy work gloves let you keep some touch sensitivity for general travel. Heavy insulated, electrical-rated gloves are for anything near pipes or cables. You will be tempted to go bare-handed because touch is your main sense. Keep bare-handed touching to surfaces you have already checked with the thermometer.

### Shelter and Sleep

- A **bivy or small shelter with fine mesh**, against oil moths and whatever else crawls in the undergrowth. Practice pitching it blind.
- A **closed-cell foam pad**, because inflatable pads puncture and the ground is a wet sponge. Closed-cell foam doesn't care about water.
- **Hammocks are worth considering.** They get you off the seeping ground and away from ground-level crawlers, but be careful where you hang one. Stack-trees are soft-wooded and hollow and ring with every Stroke, so sleeping slung between two of them means sleeping inside an organ chord. Test before committing.
- **Earplugs** for sleep and for moments near vents. Use them sparingly, since your ears are your eyes.

### Air Safety

A **multi-gas detector with audible and vibrating alarms** for hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, oxygen, and combustible gas (LEL). This is non-negotiable.
- The vents release sulfurous gas. **H₂S is deceptive:** at low concentrations it smells of rotten eggs, but at high concentrations it deadens your sense of smell, so the moment you stop smelling it can be the moment it becomes dangerous.
- Ruptured gas lines burn in dark columns. There may also be unburned leaks.
- CO₂ and other heavy gases **pool in hollows**, and hollows are exactly where condensation collects and where you'd instinctively go looking for water or shelter from wind.

Also bring an **escape hood or respirator** with acid-gas and organic-vapor cartridges. Understand that filters will saturate fast in this humidity and that breathing through a mask in body-temperature saturated air is exhausting. It is for emergencies, not for walking.

### Electronics

Everything must be **sealed to IP68 or better.** In saturated air, water gets into every seam, and the dissolved minerals will corrode contacts quickly. Bring spare batteries in sealed bags, silicone grease for seals, and redundant units of anything critical. Assume at least one device will fail. Test radio early, because the old cables and towers may cause interference.

## Part Three: The Heat Problem in Detail

Your life on the Stokemoor depends on managing heat.

**Move slowly.** Push through undergrowth at half the pace you think you can manage. Rest often. Sit rather than stand when you rest.

**Monitor your core temperature.** An ingestible core-temperature pill paired with a speaking or vibrating receiver is ideal. Failing that, use an ear thermometer with spoken output. Set a hard threshold, for example 38.5°C: when you reach it, you stop everything and cool down. If you pass 39.5°C, you abort the trip.

**Seek cooler places, and understand that the living world will lead you away from them.** Every plant here grows toward heat, so the lushest corridors lie along buried steam mains, which are the hottest ground. The **thinning of vegetation is your clue to cooler ground.** Other options:
- The **high ridges and domes** may be cooler, since they sit farther from the warm peat saddles.
- The **buried water mains** that "gurgle" underfoot may be cooler than the steam mains.
- **Near the waterfalls** the air may be moving, which is precious. But see the warning below.
- Moving air helps even when it's saturated. A small battery fan pointed at your face and neck will help a little. Bring one.

**Use water deliberately.** Wetting your skin normally cools you by evaporation, which won't happen here. Immersion in water **cooler than your body** still works by conduction. Probe any stream or pool with your thermometer before stepping into it. If you find water in the low 30s °C or cooler, lying in the shallows is a real cooling tool. Check for eels first (see below).

**Replace electrolytes.** You'll sweat heavily without that sweat cooling you. Bring electrolyte tablets and drink them on a schedule rather than waiting until you're thirsty.

**Beware the Exhale.** Twice a day the air goes "hot and dense" and rain falls "warm as bathwater." Bathwater can be 38–40°C, which is warmer than your body. For that hour you will be gaining heat rather than losing it. Do not be exerting yourself during the Exhale. Know when it's coming (see the daily rhythm below), find a safe rest spot beforehand, sit still, and wait it out.

**Short trips.** For a first visit, **plan 24 to 48 hours** and stay within a few kilometers of your arrival point. The combination of heat stress, constant wetness, and sensory load will wear you down faster than you expect.

## Part Four: Water and Food

**Do not drink the local water untreated.** It is "thick with dissolved iron and sulfur and stranger things." A standard backpacking filter removes bacteria and protozoa but does not remove dissolved metals or sulfur compounds. You need either:
- a **reverse-osmosis unit**, a hand-pump marine version, or
- **distillation**, which the Stokemoor makes easy. It is covered in hot surfaces and saturated air, so any cool surface beads with near-pure condensate. A small still set against a warm pipe (checked with your thermometer) with a condensing surface can produce drinking water. Test the output with a TDS meter that speaks its reading.

Carry at least a day's worth of clean water in case your system fails, and treat the rest of your supply as needing work.

**Bring all your food.** Do not eat anything native. The plants run on chemosynthetic bacteria, concentrate metals, and carry unknown compounds. Rivetbacks are magnetic from the iron they eat, so their meat is probably loaded with iron at toxic levels. The eels live in mineral water. None of it is food.

**Your food will spoil quickly once opened.** Everything warm and wet rots fast. Bring **single-serving sealed pouches**: dehydrated meals, nut butters, bars, and electrolyte drinks. Open each one and eat it right away. Pack it out, since the moor will do strange things to your waste.

## Part Five: Hygiene and Body Care

You will be **wet for your entire stay.** Nothing will dry, not your clothes, your skin, or your socks. This has consequences.

- **Warm-water immersion foot** develops when feet stay wet in warm conditions: soggy, white, painful, cracking skin that can become infected. Coat your feet in a thick water-repellent balm, such as zinc oxide paste or petroleum-based foot balm, before you arrive and reapply it every few hours. Take your boots off whenever you rest safely.
- **Chafing** will be severe. Apply anti-chafe balm generously to thighs, armpits, feet, groin, and under your pack straps.
- **Fungal infections** will start quickly. Bring antifungal cream. Powders will just turn to paste.
- **Ear infections** (otitis externa, "swimmer's ear") are very likely with constant moisture and mineral water. Bring acetic acid or alcohol ear drops and use them at night.
- Here is a trick worth knowing: **a warm but not scalding crusted pipe or sinter shelf is a drying rack.** Checked with your thermometer and approached carefully, it can partly dry socks, gloves, or your own feet. It may be the only dry heat on the moor.

**First aid must be done by touch.** Practice blind before you go:
- **Burns** from pipes, vents, and dark fire. Bring a generous supply of burn dressings and hydrogel. Remember that you won't see the redness, so learn to feel for heat, blistering, and swelling.
- **Electric shock.** Learn to check for breathing and pulse by touch, and learn CPR by feel.
- **Wound care** in a wet, dirty environment. Bring antiseptic and broad-spectrum antibiotics if your physician will prescribe them.

## Part Six: On Arrival (The First Hour)

The first hour matters more than any other, because your mistakes compound from here.

1. **Stand still.** Do not take a step until you have anchored yourself. Crouch and touch the ground.
2. **Plant and activate your homing beacon.** Anchor it to something solid or stake it firmly, and confirm it's working.
3. **Test for light.** Try your lamp. If it works, which is unlikely, your trip becomes much easier. If it doesn't, you have already planned for that.
4. **Start your gas detector** and let it take a reading.
5. **Feel the Stroke. Count several cycles.** The description is ambiguous about the timing. It might be a Stroke every 41 seconds with the last 11 falling quiet, or a 41-second rise followed by an 11-second quiet, making a 52-second cycle. **Learn the local rhythm yourself** with your watch or clicker. You need to know in your body exactly when the quiet falls, because the quiet is when the predator moves.
6. **Listen for the waterfalls.** The continuous roar tells you which direction the plateau's edge lies in and roughly how far away it is. Note it.
7. **Scan with your thermal device.** Find the heat lines, the steam mains, and any fire columns. Locate anything warm and animal-sized.
8. **Find your first landmark** by touch and sound: a stack-tree grove chord, a pump-house rhythm, a warm main underfoot. Record its direction and distance relative to your beacon.

Only then begin to move.

## Part Seven: Moving Across the Moor

### How it will feel

You will not walk through the Stokemoor. You will **push** through it. Leaves wider than your arm span, warm and thin and damp "like touching skin," will press against you from every side. They'll pour water down your neck at the slightest touch. Mantle-fern will give under your feet like soaked carpet. Every step is a negotiation. Expect to cover **very little distance**, perhaps one or two kilometers an hour on good ground and far less in thick undergrowth.

### The buried mains are your roads

Walking a buried steam main, you'll feel "heat rising through your soles in a long straight line." These lines are the Stokemoor's highways. They are straight, predictable, and followable, and they often lead to pump-houses. The catch is that they are also the hottest ground and the thickest vegetation, since every plant leans toward them. Walk *parallel* to a main, a few meters off, using its heat as a guide rail with your thermal device, rather than walking directly on top of it. That keeps you cooler and in thinner growth.

Buried water mains gurgle underfoot. Cables hum. Learn to tell the three apart early. Press your cane tip, or better, your jaw or ear, against the stone. The cable's thin electric whine is distinctive.

### THE EDGE WILL KILL YOU

The standard survival rule on Earth, **follow water downhill to find help,** will take you over a cliff here.

On the Stokemoor all water runs **toward the plateau's edges**, and the edges drop off in waterfalls "that may be miles high." The streams are old, their walls soft and fluted, their beds polished smooth. You could follow a gentle, pleasant stream, feeling the warm water tug at your ankles, and it would carry you, slipping, to the lip of a drop you would never see coming.

- **Do not follow streams downhill.** Cross them where you must, perpendicular to the flow, tethered to your partner.
- **Track the waterfall roar.** If it's getting louder, you're heading toward an edge. Know its volume at your arrival point and set yourself a hard limit: if the roar reaches a certain loudness, turn back.
- Near an edge you may feel **updrafts** and **cooler moving air**, and the ground may start to feel **wet with spray from below.** Treat any of these as a stop signal.
- When uncertain, **sweep the ground ahead with your cane in wide arcs** before each step. A missing ground contact means stop and back up.

### Channels and hollows

- **Don't walk in channels.** The polished beds are slick, the eels are there, and during the Exhale they become torrents.
- **Don't rest in hollows.** They collect condensation, gas, and floodwater. Your gas detector should tell you if you're descending into bad air.
- **Rest and camp on the rounded domes,** partway up. Stay off the very tops, especially if there are iron towers nearby, and out of the hollows and saddles.

## Part Eight: The Stroke and the Daily Rhythm

### Living by the Stroke

The Stroke is not just a curiosity. It structures everything.

- **The chorus after each Stroke** is when animals call, and it's when you can move with the most information around you. The moor is full of sound and your ears can read it.
- **The quiet before the next Stroke** is when the quietcat moves. In those seconds, **stop.** Stand still. Listen, and scan with your thermal device.
- **The Stroke itself** is a small lurch, "the width of a fingernail." It shouldn't knock you over, but on a slick surface or near an edge, don't step at the moment of the Stroke.

Some travelers report that the rhythm becomes hypnotic, that after a day your body starts moving by it without being told. Let it. That is you adapting to the place.

### The Exhale (twice a day)

Learn the Exhale's timing in the first day. Count Strokes if you have to. It is announced by the vents opening all at once in a "white roar." Before it arrives:

- **Get away from any vent mouths.** Stone-lipped shafts, iron chimneys, and ruptured pipe-mouths will all release pressurized steam. Steam at that pressure will scald you severely and invisibly. Your thermal device should show vents as hot even when they're dormant, so mark them and give them a wide berth.
- **Get out of channels and low ground.** Torrential rain for an hour means flash flooding.
- **Stay away from iron towers.** Lightning comes with the storms, and the towers are already live and sparking.
- **Sit down and wait.** You'll be in "sheeted water" at bathwater temperature. Protect your electronics, keep your breathing slow, and let it pass.

The Exhale-storms also wander, colliding and spinning, so you may get storms at other times too. Treat any sudden heat surge and booming overhead as an Exhale.

## Part Nine: Invisible Hazards

### Dark fire

This is one of the Stokemoor's deadliest features for a visitor. A ruptured gas line burns in a **roaring column you cannot see.** From a hundred meters you can feel it as heat on your face. Up close it will kill you. What warns you:
- **the roar,** a distinct low rushing sound
- **the smell** of burning gas
- **radiant heat** on your exposed skin, especially your face
- **your thermal device,** which will show it clearly as extreme heat

Train yourself to register rising heat on your face and to stop. **Never walk toward increasing radiant heat you can't account for.**

Remember too that **your own flame won't be visible.** If you use a stove or light a fire, you won't see it. Handle any flame as though it were invisible, which it is. Better still, don't bring an open flame at all. Use a flameless heating pouch if you need hot food, or rely on the pipes.

### Electricity

- **Cables** buried or exposed hum with current.
- **Cablevine** grows only on electrified lines, so wherever you hear its thin whining buzz, there is live current.
- **Towers** on the high ground carry current, sparking darkly at their tops.
- You are standing in water in an environment of iron. **Your whole world is conductive.**

Rules:
- If you hear buzzing or a thin whine, **stop and locate the source before moving.**
- **Touch unknown objects with the back of your hand first.** Electricians use this rule: if current makes your muscles contract, the back of your hand pulls away from the surface instead of your fingers closing around it.
- Keep your electrical-rated gloves on near any cable, pipe, or tower.
- **Do not touch cablevine.** The shock is "not quite painful," but it isn't nothing, and a slip against a thicket could put you in contact with the line underneath.

### Hot iron and steam

Pipes break the surface "knuckled with crust," many warm and some "hot enough to burn." The crusts form columns and shelves, beautiful to touch and dangerous. **Always check the temperature before you touch anything.** Your infrared thermometer is your fingertip's advance guard.

## Part Ten: Fauna

### Bellows-birds

Goose-sized, flightless, and loud. They are almost certainly harmless to you. More than that, **they are your best friends,** for two reasons:
1. **Their calls map the land.** With practice you may learn to hear where their calls come from and how they echo, and read the terrain from them as they do.
2. **They are your alarm system.** When bellows-birds go silent in a patch of moor, something is there. Most likely it's a quietcat.

Don't trample them, don't scare them off, and don't mimic them unless you know what you're saying.

### Rivetbacks

Armored, many-legged herbivores grazing lichen off the pipes. They sound like "distant workmen hammering." They are probably harmless unless you corner or step on one. Their magnetic shells will scramble your compass further if you stand near a herd. Give them space.

### Condensate eels

Long, slick, sometimes **several meters**, hunting by pressure waves in the water. A wading human makes a *lot* of pressure waves. **Do not wade in channels if you can avoid it.** If you must cross water, cross quickly and at the shallowest point, and don't linger. You'll know they're near by a hum you "feel through your feet." If you feel the hum, get out of the water.

### Oil moths

Swarms around leaking engine joints. They're furred and mostly silent, but a swarm makes "a thick, soft roar like distant rain." They're probably harmless in themselves, but walking into a swarm in the dark means a face full of moths, possibly in your nose and mouth. Wear a head net near pump-houses and old machinery, where they gather.

### The quietcat

This is the one that matters.

It is the only large predator. It **moves only in the quiet before each Stroke** and **freezes** when the chorus resumes. It makes no sound, so its prey, which navigate by listening, cannot find it.

What you know, and how to use it:

- **It moves in bursts of a few seconds at a time.** It can only close distance in that brief window each cycle. That limits its speed, and it gives you a rhythm to watch.
- **The warning sign is a "silence with a shape."** A patch of moor where the chorus has stopped, where the bellows-birds have gone quiet and the rivetbacks have stopped clacking. **Learn to hear the holes in the sound.** Practice at home by listening to crowded soundscapes and identifying which direction has gone quiet.
- **You have an advantage the native animals lack: you don't rely only on hearing.** Your thermal device doesn't care whether the quietcat is silent. A warm-bodied predator will show up as a distinct heat shape moving in the quiet and frozen in the chorus. **Scan during every quiet window.** This may be your best defense.
- **You yourself are very warm and very large.** The moor is full of heat-sensing creatures. Assume the quietcat can sense your heat and knows exactly where you are. Hiding won't work.
- **Don't be alone.** Travel with a partner, tethered, back to back when you stop.
- **At rest, put your back to something solid:** a pump-house wall or a rock face. Sleep in the most enclosed defensible spot you can find, and take turns keeping watch with the thermal device.
- **Carry a loud noisemaker** such as an air horn. It is unknown whether sudden noise will drive a quietcat off or merely make it freeze, but a creature that has built its whole life around silence is likely to find sudden loud noise alarming. Treat this as a hypothesis, not a guarantee. Also carry a stout pole or walking staff. It won't win a fight, but it might discourage one.
- **Set up perimeter alarms around your camp:** tripwires attached to rattles or noisemakers. The quietcat moves softly, but a wire across its path doesn't care how softly it moves.

## Part Eleven: Structures

### Pump-houses

These are the Stokemoor's waystations, found "every few days' walk." They're round basalt structures with running machinery inside, hot and full of steam, crowded with wildlife, and overgrown with stack-trees and cablevine.

They're valuable for **navigation** (their piston rhythm carries far), **shelter** (solid walls at your back), and **drying** (dry heat off the machinery). But:
- **The machinery is moving.** Pistons, gears, and valves can crush or scald you, and you won't see them. Enter slowly and cane-sweep constantly. Keep well away from anything with a mechanical rhythm.
- **The inside is very hot.** Check your core temperature before entering, and don't linger.
- **Cablevine on the walls** means there's current in the building.
- **Wildlife is crowded in,** and you're a very large surprise. Enter noisily in the chorus so you don't startle anything at close range.

### Iron towers

Avoid them. They carry current, spark, and draw lightning. Their singing in the Exhale winds is a good landmark to hear from a distance, and that's the only way you should use them.

### The strange things

A great gear that turns once every few days. A warm iron door in a cliff with a steady thrumming behind it. A lone chimney that breathes in time with the Stroke, and **voices** far below it.

Your curiosity will pull you toward these. So be clear about your purpose. An ordinary traveler on a first visit should **treat them as landmarks, not destinations.** Do not stand near the gear when it turns. You won't see it coming, and it moves with the weight of a hillside. Do not try the door. And if you find the chimney and hear the voices, consider that you are hearing something nobody has ever reached and come back to describe. Listen, remember, record what you can, and then go home.

## Part Twelve: The Mind

### Sensory deprivation

Total darkness does odd things to the brain. After hours without light, your visual cortex will start generating its own images: patterns, shapes, faces, flashes, sometimes full scenes (the "prisoner's cinema"). **This is normal.** Know it in advance so it doesn't frighten you. Nothing you "see" on the Stokemoor is real. Close your eyes if it helps, since it makes no difference to what you can see.

### Sound overload

The Stokemoor is loud everywhere and all the time: the waterfalls, the chorus, the Stroke, the stack-tree chords, the cablevine whine, the Exhale. Your hearing is working constantly just to keep you alive. This is exhausting in a way that's hard to describe until you've lived it. Rest during the quiet windows, and let your ears relax whenever you're somewhere safe.

### Time

There are no days here, no dawn and no dusk. Your body clock will drift. The Exhale, twice daily, gives a rough rhythm, and your watch gives an exact one. **Keep to a schedule by your watch,** sleeping and eating at set times, or your internal clock will come apart.

### The Stroke in the body

Most accounts suggest the Stroke gets under your skin. You'll feel it in your knees, then in your chest, then in your sleep. Some people find it comforting, like a great heartbeat or a sleeping giant's breath. Some find it oppressive, a reminder that something enormous and unknown turns over beneath forty miles of rock. Either reaction is fine. Just don't let it lull you into carelessness during the quiet.

## Part Thirteen: Leaving

- **Follow your beacon home.** Keep your dead-reckoning device running so you can check the beacon's direction against your recorded path.
- **Leave with margin.** Start back well before your water, batteries, food, or body run low. Heat exhaustion builds up over time, and your judgment will be at its worst just when you most need it.
- **Collect your beacon when you leave,** or leave it only if you intend to come back.
- **Afterward,** seek medical care promptly for any burns, cuts, or skin problems, and for your ears and feet. Cool down properly, rehydrate, eat, sleep, and let your eyes adjust to light again slowly. Expect the ordinary world to seem shockingly quiet and bright, and a little flat.

## A Last Word: What It Will Actually Be Like

You will arrive into a warm, wet, total darkness, and within minutes you'll understand that darkness is the least of it. What you'll notice first is the sound: the roar of distant waterfalls, the pulse of the chorus, then the silence. Then the ground lifts under you, a gentle shrug you feel in your knees, and a low round *WHUMM* rises through the stones and through your bones, and the whole world rings like a bell.

You'll feel the heat pressing on you like a hand. Your breath will come back to you as a film on your lips. Leaves as warm as skin will touch your face. You'll walk a heat-river underfoot and hear the moor drinking. If you've trained well, you'll start to read the land as its creatures do, as echoes and warmth and rhythm.

Go slowly and stay a short time. Respect the heat, the edge, the fire you can't see, and the silence that has a shape. Keep your beacon calling you home.
