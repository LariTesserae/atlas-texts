# Preparing for Weir-in-the-Cloud

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

Most travel preparation is about getting through a place. Here it's more about being absorbed into it without damaging it or yourself. Weir-in-the-Cloud is not a dangerous place in the dramatic sense. There are no bandits in the description and no hostile gods. But it is relentless. You will be wet from the moment the fog finds you on the trail until the moment you leave, and probably for a day after. You will not hear silence. You will not see a dry surface, except perhaps the inside of your own mouth. The ground will be soft and the stones will be slick, you will be surrounded by animals, and the weather will change faster than you can put on a coat.

The people here have spent five hundred years learning to live inside a sponge that screams. Your job is to arrive humble, arrive prepared for water, and let them show you how.

---

## When to Go

This is the most important decision you'll make, because there are really two Weirs.

**Migration season** (two windows of about six weeks each, in the "cool shoulders" of the year, roughly spring and autumn analogues) is the Weir of legend: skies full of swifts, hawks sliding past at rooftop height, exhausted birds on the rafters, nets heavy, markets busy, everyone shouting the count. It is overwhelming and unforgettable. It is also when the town is working hardest, when strangers are most likely to be underfoot, and when the ritual rules matter most.

**Off-season** is quieter only by comparison. You still get frogs, monkeys, pigs, laughingthrushes, the jay, and the water-clappers. You'll have more of people's attention and patience, and you'll see the maintenance life of the town: the lashing, the re-seating of stones, the digging of drains.

**If you can, arrive a week or so before the migration begins.** You'll have time to acclimatize to the wet and the noise, learn a few words, and find a household that will host you. Then the birds arrive around you, and you'll understand what you're seeing.

**Consider the first-fog night carefully.** Nobody nets on the night of the first fog of the season. It is clearly a significant night, and it may carry observances the description doesn't mention. Ask about it before it arrives. Don't be the stranger who does something loud or careless on that one night.

## The Approach

You'll come up a switchback trail through lower forest, likely from either the eastern or western valley, since traders come from both. Some things to know:

- **The trail is long, and the last part is blind.** You may see the saddle from below as a dark green mass steaming with smoke, and then the fog will take it. You'll climb the final stretch navigating by sound. Stay on the trail. Don't try to shortcut toward the noise.
- **The lower paths are where the leeches are.** They quest from every leaf. Before you begin the climb, tuck your trousers into your socks, and if you can, wear leech socks: tightly woven, knee-high cloth overstockings. Check yourself at every rest stop: ankles, behind the knees, waistband, neck. Leeches aren't dangerous, but their anticoagulant makes bites bleed for a long time, and in constant damp a bleeding bite is an invitation to infection.
- **Get your ash-and-salt pouch early.** Everyone in Weir carries one. Salt or ash on a leech makes it let go cleanly. Pulling one off can leave its mouthparts behind. Bring your own salt, and once you arrive, adopt the local pouch. It's a small, visible sign that you're paying attention.
- **Travel with a trader or pony train if possible.** They know the trail, they know when a slip has moved a section overnight, and arriving alongside familiar faces makes you less of an oddity.
- **Pace yourself for altitude.** The saddle sits in a cloud forest in a high range. It's probably moderate altitude, high enough that the climb will tax you but likely not enough for serious sickness. Still, the combination of altitude, steep switchbacks, cold, and wet is exhausting. Plan to arrive tired.

## The Central Problem: Water

Everything is wet, always. Water doesn't just fall here. It condenses directly out of the air onto every surface, including you. Your preparation should be built around this.

### Clothing

- **No cotton. At all.** Cotton wet is colder than nothing. It will never dry here.
- **Wool is your best friend.** Wool stays warm when wet, resists odor over many days, and is something the local culture will likely recognize. Bring a wool base layer, a wool mid-layer, and wool socks: several pairs, more than you think.
- **A synthetic or waxed outer shell** for the squalls, with a hood. Understand that in fog, a waterproof shell doesn't keep you dry, because you'll sweat inside it and the fog soaks everything anyway. What it does is block wind, and wind is what turns wet into hypothermia.
- **A wide-brimmed hat.** Moss beards drip constantly and eaves drip in "beaded curtains." A brim keeps water off your face and out of your eyes, and if you wear glasses it makes them usable. Glasses will fog and bead anyway. Bring a cloth and consider going without them if you can.
- **Keep one sealed set of dry clothes for sleeping only.** Never wear them outside. Keeping one set dry is the difference between a miserable night and a restful one.
- **Temperature:** it's cool, not frigid. The saddle is sheltered from the worst wind, and mosquitoes are few because it's too cool for them. Think of a raw, wet, chilly day that lasts the entire visit, dropping sharply whenever the fog wall rolls through. You'll feel the temperature fall "at once."

### Footwear

This matters more than anything else you pack.

- **The stone paths are polished granite with moss on the edges and a water-filled dish in the center of every step.** They'll be slick. Soft-soled, grippy footwear is better than hard boots. Think of what fishermen wear on wet rock.
- **Off the path, you sink to the shin.** Don't step off the path unless someone local shows you where. You'll ruin your footwear, and you'll damage the sphagnum root-mat the whole town floats on.
- **Bamboo walkways and ladders:** rungs worn "concave" on the old oak ladders, lashed bamboo bridges at head height. You'll want footwear that's flexible enough to feel what you're standing on. Many locals may simply go barefoot or in something minimal on the upper level. Watch them and imitate them.
- **Waterproof boots will fill up and stay full.** A better strategy is fast-draining shoes plus wool socks, accepting wet feet but keeping them warm and changing socks often.

### Foot and Skin Care

This is the health risk most travelers will underestimate.

- **Trench foot and fungal infections** are real dangers when feet stay wet and cool for days. Every night, dry your feet completely by the fire, inspect them, and let them air. Bring antifungal powder or ointment. Rotate socks.
- **Skin maceration:** hands and feet will wrinkle and soften. Small cuts heal slowly in constant damp. Clean every scrape. Bring a barrier balm.

### Gear and Belongings

- **Everything goes in waterproof bags inside your pack,** not just rain-covered from outside. Double-bag anything precious.
- **Paper will fail.** Notebooks swell, ink runs. Use pencil and waterproof paper, or accept the losses.
- **Anything electronic or mechanical from another world will suffer.** Condensation gets into everything. Pack it with desiccant, keep it sealed, and don't expect it to work reliably. Better yet, leave it behind. Weir is not a place for devices.
- **Fire-starting:** nothing is dry here. The locals clearly manage their cookfires, but if you're ever out alone you won't find dry tinder. Carry a reliable firestarter in a sealed container, though realistically you'll depend on your hosts' hearths.

## Noise

Weir is never quiet. Let this sink in before you arrive.

At night there are frogs "until the air vibrates," thrushes calling overhead in the dark during migration, the uncountable clatter of hundreds of water-clappers, pigs under the floor, chickens in the eaves, and running water in a thousand pipes. At dawn the swifts come down screaming, then the dogs, the monkeys, the children with slings, the market, the ponies, and the shouting across the lanes.

- **Bring earplugs,** soft ones you can sleep in, and expect them to only partly help. Some of these sounds you'll feel through the floor.
- **Expect a few nights of poor sleep.** Most people adapt, and eventually the noise becomes texture rather than intrusion. If you're sensitive to sound or prone to sensory overload, plan quiet recovery time, and know that you may need to walk down the trail for a day to find stillness. There's no quiet corner in the saddle.
- **Learn to talk loudly.** People here "always have to talk loudly." Speaking softly may be taken as timidity, secrecy, or simply not heard. Project your voice. It's not rude here.
- **Fog changes sound.** Close sounds get closer and far sounds vanish. This is disorienting. In fog, don't trust your sense of how far away something is.

## Navigating the Town

### Two Levels

Traffic runs on two levels: the stone paths below (people, pigs, ponies, goats) and the bamboo walkways above (people, dogs, children).

- **Bamboo bridges are built for local bodies and local loads.** If you're large, or carrying a heavy pack, ask before you use the upper walkways. Don't bounce, don't crowd, and don't lean on the lashings.
- **Children will be much faster than you up there.** Step aside for them.
- **Mind the eaves and the bamboo pipes.** Water channels cross lanes on "little trestles" at unpredictable heights. You'll bang your head at least once. More importantly, don't knock one out of alignment, because that water is going to someone downhill.

### In the Fog

- The fog can drop visibility from half a mile to an arm's length in twenty breaths.
- **Stay on the stones.** The worn centers of the steps are clean because feet use them. If you feel moss under your feet, you're drifting to the edge.
- **Water flows downhill,** and the whole town is plumbed to pass water down toward the lower terraces and the far valley. If you're lost, the direction the water runs tells you which way is down.
- **Stop, call out, wait.** Someone will answer. Everyone's close. The town is crammed together partly because "the fog makes people want to be close."
- **Stay away from the upper edge where the nets are,** and from the cliff-feet, unless you're with someone.

### Weather Awareness

- **Squalls come without warning** and turn the pass into a wind tunnel: sideways rain, falling branches, torn thatch. When you hear everyone running, run with them to shelter. Don't stand under the old oak limbs heavy with epiphytes during high wind, because branches come down.
- **Sudden sunbreaks** make everything glitter and steam. This is when the birds "erupt." Have your eyes ready. It's some of the most beautiful light you'll ever see, and it lasts minutes.
- **Ground movement:** paths slide, walls slump, and houses tilt as their footing softens. If you're offered lodging in a house that's visibly leaning, it's probably fine, since it has probably been leaning for years. But pay attention after heavy rain, and notice if cracks or tilts appear overnight.

## Health Hazards Beyond the Wet

### Water Supply: Where You Drink From Matters

This is subtle and important. The town's water passes downhill "from household to household, used, and passed on," first for drinking, then washing, then pigs, then taro beds.

- **Drink only from the top of a chain,** from upper roof catchments and cisterns meant for drinking. Water from a trough or lower basin may already have been used for washing or animals. Your hosts will know which is which. Ask.
- **Consider bringing a way to boil or purify water,** at least for the first days, until your stomach adjusts.
- **Never help yourself to water from a channel.** Water rights are inherited, and some channels are older than the families who tend them. Taking water from the wrong pipe may be less like drinking from a public tap and more like siphoning someone's property. Always ask your host.

### Animals and Disease

This town is densely packed with wild birds, bats, macaques, pigs, dogs, chickens, and goats, in constant close contact with humans. For a traveler from elsewhere, that's a real disease environment.

- **Macaques:** don't feed them, don't make eye contact in a challenging way, don't try to intervene in the rack war, and don't let them get close. Macaque bites and scratches can transmit serious infections, including herpes B in many real-world macaque species, which can be deadly to humans. If bitten or scratched, clean it immediately and thoroughly with soap and running water for a long time. Know that this is a genuine medical emergency and plan your evacuation route in advance.
- **Bats:** the cliff caves pour out a "black river" of bats. Don't enter the caves. Bat guano can carry fungal diseases, and bats can carry rabies-like viruses. Never handle a grounded bat.
- **Dogs:** assume rabies is possible anywhere animals are dense and medicine is traditional. Consider pre-exposure rabies vaccination before you travel if that's available to you.
- **Wild birds:** handling and processing large numbers of migrating wild birds is how avian influenza and other bird-borne illnesses move into people. If you help with plucking or smoking, and you might be invited to, wash your hands thoroughly, avoid touching your face, and don't handle obviously sick or dead-before-netting birds. Birds roosting on the rafters mean droppings in the living space. Keep your food covered and your bedding clean.
- **Pigs** root under every house. Eat pork only well cooked.
- **Vaccinations to consider before you travel,** depending on what's available in your home world: tetanus (all those cuts and scrapes on old stone and bamboo), rabies pre-exposure, hepatitis A, typhoid.
- **A basic medical kit:** wound cleaning supplies, antiseptic, bandages that stay on when wet, antifungal cream, anti-diarrheal medicine, oral rehydration salts, any personal medications (double-sealed), and a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics if you can get them prescribed.

### Smoke

Houses have a chimney hole, not a chimney. Interiors are smoky, and smoke keeps the damp and insects at bay. If you have asthma or respiratory sensitivity, bring your medications and expect some irritation. Sleeping nearer the floor or nearer the walls may help, though smoke behavior varies by house.

### Insects

Mosquitoes are few, which is good news. Moths come to the fires in "blizzards," which is harmless but startling. There will be beetles in the thatch, and possibly in your bedding. Shake out everything before you put it on.

## Culture and Conduct

The people of Weir have lived here for five centuries and keep their rules "old and strictly." As a visitor you're entering a working, crowded, precarious community. Your conduct matters.

### The Netting Rules

Learn these and honor them absolutely:

1. **No net may be strung across the central gap.** It must stay open. Don't obstruct the central gap in any way: no lines, no gear, no standing where you block the flight path if you're told not to.
2. **Nobody takes the hawks.** Don't harm, catch, or handle a raptor. If one comes through at rooftop height, duck like everyone else.
3. **Nobody nets on the night of the first fog of the season.** Ask what else that night means.
4. **The first bird caught each year is released from the highest roof by the youngest child who can climb there.** If you're present for this, it's a privilege. Stay back, stay quiet if you can manage it, and let it be theirs.

**Don't touch the nets.** They're fine nettle-fiber cord, hard to make, and easily torn. They're the livelihood of the town.

### If Bird-Netting Troubles You

The town's livelihood is killing migrating songbirds by the thousand. If you come from a culture where that's distressing, prepare yourself emotionally before you arrive, or reconsider whether this is a place you want to visit. This is not a practice you'll change, and moralizing about it would be both rude and pointless. This is a community that has sustained itself with explicit restraint for five hundred years: an open gap, protected hawks, a sacred night of rest, a released first bird. That's an old conservation ethic of its own. You don't have to eat the smoked birds, though declining food in a subsistence community should be done gracefully. You do have to let them live their lives.

### The Moss and the Growth

- **Don't pull moss off walls.** Families press moss into their walls on purpose, for warmth and windproofing. What looks like neglect to you is insulation to them.
- **Don't pick orchids.** Medicinal orchids are a trade good. The white orchids on the roofs may be cherished. Ask before touching.
- **Don't step on roof gardens** or treat the living roofs like terrain.
- **Don't cut or break branches,** especially on the great oaks, which are family seats. The trees are homes.

### Stones and Walls

- Walk in the worn centers of the steps like everyone else, and don't scrape moss off the edges.
- Don't climb, sit on, or lean heavily on the leaning retaining walls. They're held up by roots and generations of patient patching.

### Hospitality and Contribution

- **Bring trade goods, not just money.** The market runs on salt, iron knives, dried fish, cotton cloth, smoked birds, honey, nettle cord, and orchids. If you arrive with salt, good steel needles, fishhooks, iron tools, or durable cloth, you'll have something valued. Salt is light, universally useful, and doubles as leech treatment.
- **Offer to help with repairs.** The town is always being fixed. Someone is always lashing a pole, re-seating a stone, or digging a drain. Pitching in, even clumsily, under direction, is probably the fastest way into people's good graces. After a squall, when everyone comes out to fix things, come out too. Carry what you're asked to carry.
- **Join the monkey war only if invited.** Guarding the drying racks with slings is the children's job. If you're asked to help, it's fun. Don't take over.
- **Birds in the house:** if exhausted migrants roost in the rafters at supper, leave them alone. They're too tired to fear you. Don't grab them, and don't shoo them away unless your hosts do.
- **Ponies are stabled in the hollow bases of the oaks,** which are also family seats. Treat those spaces as someone's home.

### Language

Traders haggle in three languages. You probably won't speak any of them. Learn, at minimum:
- greetings and thanks
- "may I?"
- "where is water to drink?"
- "which way is down?"
- numbers, for the market and the net counts

Being loud and cheerfully wrong will go further than being quiet and correct.

## Lodging

You'll most likely stay in a stilt house or be given a platform in one.

- Floors are a man's height above the wet, with woven walls, steep sedge thatch, a smoke hole, pigs below, chickens in the eaves, and possibly birds in the rafters.
- **Expect little privacy.** Houses crowd together with eaves nearly touching. Family life is lived in the open and loud.
- **Ladders** may be old and worn smooth. Go slowly, three points of contact, especially when wet, which is always.
- **Bring a lightweight, quick-drying sleeping bag or wool blanket** in a waterproof sack, plus a thin mat. Local bedding may be plentiful or scarce.
- **Sleep near the hearth if you're offered the spot.** It's the warmest and driest place in the house.
- **Bring a small gift for your host family** from your own world: something useful, durable, and waterproof. A good knife, fine needles, sealed salt, a metal cooking vessel.

## What to Pack: Summary List

**Clothing**
- Wool base layers (2 sets)
- Wool mid-layer
- Windproof/waterproof shell with hood
- Wide-brimmed hat
- Many pairs of wool socks
- Leech socks / overstockings
- Fast-draining, grippy, flexible shoes
- One sealed set of dry sleep clothes

**Health**
- Earplugs (several pairs)
- Medical kit (wound care, antiseptic, antifungal, rehydration salts, anti-diarrheal, antibiotics if available)
- Foot powder and barrier balm
- Personal medications, double-sealed
- Water purification method
- Pre-trip vaccinations as available (tetanus, rabies, hep A, typhoid)

**Gear**
- Waterproof bags / dry sacks, lots of them
- Lightweight wool blanket or quick-dry sleeping bag
- Sealed firestarter
- Pencil and waterproof notebook
- Small cloth for wiping eyes and glasses

**Trade and Gifts**
- Salt (lots)
- Steel needles, fishhooks, a good iron or steel knife
- Durable cloth

**Leave behind:** cotton, anything electronic you care about, paper you want to keep, heavy boots, and your need for quiet.

## What It Will Actually Feel Like

For the first day or two, it will be too much. You'll be cold in a damp, clinging way that no fire fully fixes. You'll slip on stones and bang your head on bamboo pipes. The noise will seem hostile. The monkeys will scream at you and the jay will steal something of yours and shout about it. You'll feel huge and clumsy on the walkways and helpless in the fog.

Then, somewhere around the third day, something will shift. You'll start stepping in the worn bowls without thinking, the way everyone else does. You'll stop hearing the frogs as noise and start hearing them as the sound of evening. You'll know which clapper by your host's house knocks slow and which knocks fast. You'll watch a wall of fog pour over the western lip like a slow gray waterfall, and instead of bracing, you'll wait for the moment it tears open and the sun hits wet moss and every surface blazes. You'll see a hawk slide past at the height of your head. You'll eat supper under a row of sleeping brown birds that crossed a mountain range to rest on the rafters above you.

You'll understand why the people here have never left, and why they've never finished anything. Nothing here is ever finished. It's a town the mountain wears into its own shape, loudly and patiently, forever.

Arrive with salt, wool, dry socks, and open hands. Leave the moss on the walls, the hawks in the sky, and the central gap open. When you go down the trail on the last day and the noise fades behind you into the fog, you may find the silence on the far side almost unbearable.
