# Preparing for the Singing Saddle

The Saddle is small, quiet in its dangers, and very easy to damage. Most places in the multiverse can hurt you. This one is more likely to *remember* you. Almost everything below follows from one fact: **in the Saddle, sound becomes stone, and anything left still in the wet becomes part of the mountain.** You are going somewhere that records.

---

## 1. Understand What Kind of Trip This Is

This is not an expedition into a hostile realm. It is a hard mountain walk that ends in a warm, wet, deafening, fog-filled bowl about the size of a cathedral. The physical risks are ordinary ones in an odd arrangement: cold on the way up, wet heat inside, sharp glassy rock, poor visibility, lightning, and sensory overload.

The subtle risks come from the rule of the place:

- **Your voice builds things.** Talking, coughing, sneezing, snoring, clanking gear, and stamping feet all get laid down as rock, in a place that forgets nothing for centuries.
- **Stillness gets you fused.** A pebble is cemented to the floor by noon. A boot, a pack strap, a dropped glove, or a coil of rope will be too.
- **Everything is always wet.** Your gear will take on a mineral skin.

The right mindset is closer to entering a sound-recording studio, a cave, and a nesting colony all at once than to mountaineering. Move slowly. Stay quiet. Keep your feet moving. Leave nothing behind.

---

## 2. Before You Leave

### Fitness
You need a full day of uphill walking through boulder fields and heather on old frost-split granite. Expect ankle-turning ground, route-finding, and a steep last stretch below the col. If you cannot comfortably do a long, rough hill day with a loaded pack, train until you can. You will arrive tired, and tired people are clumsy and loud.

### Health: who should think twice
- **Anyone with a cold, cough, hay fever, or chest infection.** A coughing fit on the Saddle floor leaves a lasting mark, and suppressing coughs for hours is miserable. Postpone the trip. If you get sick on the way up, turn back or stay below the line.
- **Heavy snorers.** This matters if you are tempted to sleep anywhere near the bowl (see camping, below).
- **People with pacemakers, implanted defibrillators, cochlear implants, or hearing aids.** The masts emit strong low-frequency pressure you feel in your sternum and teeth. Nobody knows how that interacts with implanted electronics. Keep well away from the masts, and consult whoever understands your device.
- **People prone to anxiety, panic, migraine, or vertigo.** Infrasound is associated with unease, dread, nausea, and visual flicker. Add sourceless light, sub-forty-pace visibility, and sudden roaring bird-floods out of the fog, and the place can overwhelm. It is survivable, but know your limits and plan short visits.
- **Anyone with tinnitus or noise-induced hearing damage.** The dawn chorus is enormous in an enclosed bowl. Protect your ears.

### Weather
Learn the forecast methods of the valley people and, if you can, ask them directly. They live below the line and understand it. **You do not want to be in the Saddle during a thunderstorm** (see section 8). Go in a settled spell. Accept that "settled" still means fog in the bowl, because the cloud lives there. Clear days only matter for the view from the ledge at the top of the stairs.

### Talk to the locals
The few people who live below the line have the only real body of practical knowledge about the place. Ask them:
- where they camp,
- which routes across the floor are safe underfoot,
- which areas are newly grown since the last storm,
- how the swifts are behaving this season,
- and what they consider rude.

They have learned to whisper, so you should practise before you arrive. Their customs are your best guide.

### Practise silence
Spend a few days before the trip rehearsing:
- **Whispering.** Keep it genuinely soft and breathy, without a stage-whisper hiss.
- **Hand signals** with your companions for basic fog communication: stop, come here, look, danger, turn back, I'm fine, I'm not fine, time to leave.
- **Soft walking.** Roll heel to toe with no scuffing.
- **Stifling a sneeze.** Press the tongue to the roof of the mouth, or press under the nose. It's not foolproof, but it helps.

## 3. Base Camp: Sleep Below the Line

**Do not sleep in the Saddle.** The locals don't, and the reason is snoring. You also cannot control coughing, sleep-talking, or a nightmare shout. Beyond that, anything lying on wet stone for eight hours, including your tent floor, sleeping mat, and pack, will be partly cemented to the bowl by morning.

Camp on the old granite, below the line, as close as the ground allows. That gives you:
- dry(ish) ground,
- ordinary cold mountain conditions, which you know how to handle,
- a short, known walk up to the lip for dawn.

If you want the dawn chorus, and you should, you will make that walk in darkness and fog. **Scout it in daylight the afternoon before.** Count paces from camp to the line and from the line to your chosen dawn spot. Note landmarks. In the dark, the masts' hum may help you orient, since it becomes a steady note at distance and a chest-pressure up close. Treat it as a supplement, not your navigation.

## 4. Kit

### Clothing: two climates in one day
You will climb through cold, windy upland, then step over a line into air that is warm, saturated, and constantly condensing. Then you will step back out into the cold, wet through. **That re-exit is the most straightforwardly dangerous moment of the whole trip.** Being soaked on a cold col at dusk is how people get hypothermia.

- **Base layers:** synthetic or thin merino that dries fast and stays warm when damp. Avoid cotton entirely.
- **Inside the bowl:** strip down to a light layer. A waterproof shell is nearly pointless in there. You'll get as wet from sweat inside it as from fog outside. Wear it only if you need armour against the thorns.
- **A complete dry set in a sealed dry bag,** kept inside your pack: base layer, socks, insulating layer, hat, gloves. **Change into it at the line when you leave**, before you start descending or return to camp. Do this every time.
- **Hat or hood.** Condensation and mineral film in your hair is unpleasant, and beards and eyebrows stiffen with grey crust over a long visit.

### Hands
Wear **durable gloves** (leather palms, or good work gloves). The new stone has "sharp edges nothing has touched yet." Glassy, unweathered rock cuts like broken bottles, and the eastern wall is covered in thorns "fine as glass." Bring a second pair, because the first will get soaked and stiff with mineral. You can take them off briefly to feel the warm, ticking stone at the line. It's worth doing once, gently.

### Feet
- Boots with **very grippy rubber**. You'll be walking on wet, glassy surfaces laced with warm braided rivulets.
- Stiff enough soles to resist sharp edges.
- Gaiters help keep grit and mineral water out of your boots.
- **Never stand in a rivulet for long.** Every few minutes, lift and resettle your feet. This matters most in the half-hour after the dawn chorus, when growth is fastest and rivulets run thick and milky. A boot left in running water through dawn may come away with a stone shell, or not come away cleanly at all.

### Eyes and head
- **Eye protection.** Clear or lightly tinted safety glasses or goggles. Swift parties appear "suddenly all around you" at speed in fog. The thorns can be at face height. Stone spider-lace shatters into slivers.
- **Anti-fog treatment**, plus a way to clean lenses with clean water you carried in. The film that forms on wet surfaces will form on your glasses, and wiping mineral grit across lenses scratches them. Rinse first, then wipe. Bring a spare pair.
- **A light helmet** if you plan to go anywhere near the eastern wall. Thorns can snap, and falling glass needles are no joke.

### Ears
- **Earplugs or earmuffs** for the dawn chorus. They protect only you and change nothing in the bowl, so wear them without guilt. Consider earmuffs you can lift on and off, so you can take the plugs out during the midday hush and hear the ticking, the dripping, and the mast-note.

### Breathing
- A **light, breathable face covering** (a buff, or a mask designed for humid conditions), and **a proper particulate mask** if you'll be anywhere near broken stone. The glassy rock is likely silica-rich. Its fine dust and slivers are not something to inhale, and fragments from snapped thorns or shattered lace will be sharp at the microscopic level too.
- Nobody knows what breathing mineral-laden mist for hours does, especially the warm drizzle at the base of the masts, where the cloud is "loaded with mineral." It is probably harmless for a short visit. But the rule of the place is that mineral water plus sound pressure equals stone, and your airways are wet and full of sound when you talk. I have no evidence that this causes harm. I would still limit time in the mast drizzle, cover my mouth and nose there, and not speak while standing in it.

### Water and food
- **Do not drink the Saddle's water.** It is carrying stone. Even if it tastes fine, you don't want to find out what it does in kidneys or gut.
- **Carry all your water from below the line**, and treat it as usual.
- **Keep bottles closed and inside your pack.** An open bottle collecting fog will grow a grey skin, then scale, then worse.
- Choose food that makes no noise. That rules out crackling wrappers and crunchy crackers. Soft bars, pre-opened pouches, and bread all work. Eat at the rim or below the line when you can.

### Sitting and resting
Bring a **small closed-cell foam pad**. Sit on it, not on the floor. **Pick it up and move it every half hour or so.** Never put your pack down directly on wet stone. Keep it on your back, or on your lap on the pad. Anything set down and forgotten will be a smooth bump by morning.

### Metal and noise
- Tape or pad anything that clinks: carabiners, stove parts, trekking-pole tips (fit rubber caps), zipper pulls, cup against bottle.
- Put a whistle *on you* for genuine emergencies, but understand what a whistle blast does here. It is a loud, sustained tone that will build something. **Use it only if someone's life depends on it.** For non-emergency signalling, use lights, touch, and prearranged line-tugs.

### Zippers, buckles, hinges
Mineral film will crust in teeth and mechanisms. Before you go, rub zippers with wax or a zipper lubricant. After the trip, flush everything with clean water. Expect some permanent stiffening on anything that spends a whole day in the bowl.
