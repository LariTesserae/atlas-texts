# Preparing for the Stump at Hollow Coin

*A briefing for the ordinary traveler*

The Stump will not try to kill you. It is not hostile. It is enormous, loud, dry, and indifferent. Most of its dangers come from a mismatch between your body, which evolved for a wet and quiet world, and a place that has never known either. Most of your preparation is about managing that mismatch.

The usual warnings about cold, thin air and supplies apply here too, and they are covered below. But the real challenge is sound. Visitors who prepare only for the cold and the climb tend to leave early, shaking and sleepless, unsure what went wrong. Prepare for the sound first.

---

## Part One: Before You Go

### 1. Train your body to live inside noise

You will be immersed in infrasound from the moment you reach the cliffs until the moment you leave. The drone has a period of several seconds, well below hearing, and you will feel it in your chest, gut, and long bones. Human bodies react badly to sustained low-frequency pressure:

- Vague dread or anxiety with no apparent cause
- Nausea and a queasy, seasick sense of motion
- Pressure behind the eyes and in the sinuses
- A persistent feeling of being watched or accompanied
- Disrupted sleep, and sometimes vivid, unpleasant dreams

The feeling of a presence is worth naming now, because you will feel it. On the Stump it is half true: something vast is alive under your feet. Knowing the sensation is partly physiological will help you not panic.

**Weeks before departure:**

- **Sleep with low-frequency sound.** Subwoofers, heavy machinery recordings, anything that makes the room hum. You won't fully acclimatize, but you can blunt the first-week shock.
- **Try a vibration platform or a long sea voyage.** Your vestibular system needs to learn to tolerate a floor that is never quite still.
- **Learn to hold a pitch.** This is not optional. You will need to hum accurately to communicate politely with the Tuners, possibly to open doors, and to understand what is happening around you. Practice against a drone until you can hold a note steady and slide it by a quarter-tone deliberately.
- **Practice rhythm under stress.** Bridge-songs must be walked in time, and a misstep is a fall. Walk to a metronome while carrying weight and while distracted, until stepping on the beat is automatic. Also practice recognizing where a beat falls in an irregular, beating pattern. The Stump's standing waves won't be as tidy as a metronome.

### 2. Train your hearing, not just your tolerance

You need to be able to hear the Stump well, because two of its main hazards announce themselves only by sound:

- **Sawyers** are heard as a sour note, a change in the ground's pitch.
- **Bridge antinodes** show only as the spots where dust does settle.

Before you go, spend time listening for small pitch changes against a loud background. Learn what beating sounds like, the wobble when two close tones interfere. On the Stump, beating tells you where standing waves are and where the air may behave strangely.

### 3. Get a medical check

- **Lungs.** The air is thin, cold, and full of fine pollen-dust. If you have asthma, pollen allergies, or any respiratory weakness, think carefully about whether to go. If you have ever had a severe allergic reaction to pollen, bring emergency medication and assume the worst: the dust is everywhere, constantly, and during dust-storms you will be breathing a bronze fog.
- **Ears.** Get a baseline hearing test so you can tell afterward whether you've been damaged. The shoots' evening wail and the dusk cracks are loud enough to matter.
- **Heart and inner ear.** Inner-ear disorders or migraine triggered by pressure changes will be aggravated here.
- **Altitude.** You'll be climbing 3 km above a basin that may already be high, in air described as thin. Treat it as a high-altitude expedition and acclimatize accordingly.

## Part Two: The Absolute Rule

### No fire. None. Not ever.

This needs its own section.

You are going to a world made of dry wood that has never been wet in its entire existence. The air is full of fine, sweet-smelling organic dust. That combination is a textbook dust explosion. In a pollen-storm, when the veil drops to ground level, a single spark could ignite the air around you.

And near the centre of the plateau is a black, blistered ring called **the Burn**. Nobody knows what burned. You do not want to be the answer to the next ring's version of that question.

Therefore:

- **No open flames.** No stoves, lighters, matches, candles, or fire-starters of any kind. Leave them behind. Don't pack them "just in case."
- **No spark-producing equipment.** Check your tools, including ferro rods, cheap electronics with exposed contacts, and anything that arcs.
- **Watch for static electricity.** Dust-storms are described as "faintly electric." Wear clothing that doesn't build up static, and avoid layers of synthetic fleece rubbing together. Ground yourself deliberately by touching the wood with your palm before handling electronics. Consider antistatic straps for any gear with batteries.
- **Be cautious with batteries.** Bring well-protected, high-quality cells. A lithium battery fire here would be a catastrophe.
- **Treat chemical heat packs with suspicion.** Most air-activated hand warmers won't ignite anything, but keep them sealed away from dust. Test them and know their maximum temperature.

For warmth, plan on insulation, not combustion. For cooking, plan on food that needs none.

It may be worth not even mentioning fire casually to the Tuners until you understand how they relate to the Burn.

## Part Three: Water, and the Problem of Being Wet

### Bring all of it

There is no water on this world. None has fallen in recorded history. You must carry every drop you will drink, plus a reserve, plus a reserve for the reserve. The air is dry enough to split lips in an hour, so your losses through breathing and skin will be severe. Plan on more water than you would for a desert expedition of the same length, not less.

- **Use hard, sealed, insulated containers.** Soft bladders can be punctured on the hard, ridged floor. Nights are cold enough that water may freeze, so keep a drinking supply next to your body.
- **Recover your own moisture where you can.** A mask or scarf that traps the humidity of your breath will help your airways enormously. A heat-and-moisture exchanger mask, like the ones used in polar and medical settings, is ideal.
- **Keep your lips, nose, and eyes moist.** Bring heavy occlusive balm, saline nasal gel, and preservative-free eye drops. Use them before symptoms appear.
- **Protect your skin.** Your hands will crack. Bring heavy moisturizer and thin gloves.

### Be discreet about water

This is a softer point, but important. On the Stump, water is a myth. It survives only as metaphors borrowed from old records: the lake a sawyer's prey sinks into, the surf of grinding rasps. You are carrying a substance these people know only from stories.

- Don't pour water onto the wood. Nobody knows what liquid water does to a being whose whole chemistry is solid-state creep and acoustic transport. It might be harmless, a desecration, or damaging. Don't find out.
- Don't spill. Drink carefully.
- Don't waste it in front of the Tuners, and don't offer it casually. If a Tuner asks to see or touch water, that is a significant moment. Treat it with the gravity it may carry for them.
- If you have to wash, do it minimally, inside your own sealed gear, and pack out the waste water.

### Waste

On a world with no water, nothing rots the way you'd expect. Your waste will not decompose. It will mummify and sit on a floor that is also a living body. Pack everything out: food waste, human waste, wrappers, all of it. Bring sealed waste bags. Whatever you leave may be laid down into the next ring and remain there for a very long time.

## Part Four: Clothing and Gear

### The cold

Days are dim and cool. Dusk brings a fast, deep temperature drop, fast enough that the whole plateau cracks like artillery. Dress for high-altitude cold:

- Layered insulation that doesn't generate static. Wool is good, and down in a non-static shell.
- A windproof outer layer. The wind is drowned out by the drone except at the edge, but it's there, it's cold, and it carries dust.
- A warm hat, a neck gaiter, and gloves with a thinner inner pair for dexterity.
- A four-season sleep system rated well below the coldest temperature you expect.

### Footwear: the most important choice you'll make

Your feet are how the Stump talks to you. You need to balance two opposing needs.

1. **Some isolation.** The drone comes up through your soles and bones constantly. A cushioned, vibration-damping sole will reduce fatigue and nausea considerably.
2. **Some sensitivity.** The first warning of a sawyer is a change in the pitch of the ground. On bridge-songs, feeling the pulse of the standing wave through your feet may be what keeps you from stepping into an antinode.

The best solution is two pairs: heavily damped boots for long walks across the open plain, and thin, flexible, grippy shoes for bridges, towers, and anywhere you need to feel. Some travelers bring removable damping insoles and swap them out.

Soles need good grip. The floor is polished smooth like a worn banister, but ridged into hand-high combs. Expect to trip on the combing, especially at dusk when long shadows make the floor look ploughed and hide the real ridges.

### Ears

Bring three levels of protection:

- **Light, high-fidelity plugs** that lower volume evenly across frequencies. Wear these most of the time. You can still hear pitch changes, which matters for your safety.
- **Heavy plugs or earmuffs** for the shoots' evening wail, the dusk crack-volleys, and sleep.
- **Nothing.** Some of the time you will need your full hearing. Don't cross a bridge-song or walk near a rasp herd with heavy plugs in.

No ear protection stops infrasound, which comes through your body, not your ears. Accept that.

### Eyes and lungs

- **Sealed goggles** for dust-storms, when visibility falls to metres.
- **A good particulate mask**, ideally combined with the moisture-exchange function mentioned above. The pollen smells sweet. Don't let that fool you into breathing it freely for days.

### Silence your gear

Everything that can rattle, buzz, or ring will do so on the Stump. Loose buckles, zipper pulls, and anything hollow will sympathetically vibrate at bell pitches. At minimum this is maddening. Near a held room or a Tuner at work, it is rude or dangerous.

- **Tape or pad every loose metal part.**
- **Think carefully about hollow objects.** A half-full water bottle, a helmet, a cook pot you won't be using: any of these can act as a resonator and sing when it matches a bell. Knellbirds are built almost exactly like this, with hollow chests tuned to the bells, and the Tuners believe they help tune the Stump. You do not want to be carrying an untuned knellbird on your back. Fill containers completely, or pad them so they can't resonate.
- **Test your pack.** Before you go, put it on a speaker playing low frequencies and sweep through pitches. Find what rings and fix it.

### Light

Daylight is the colour of weak tea: soft, brown, and shadowless. You won't need much sun protection. The sun is dim enough to look at directly, though UV behind a pollen veil may be unpredictable, so bring a light sunscreen anyway.

Nights are starless. The only light comes from the faint glow of the veil and cold violet points of grainmoss. Bring:

- A good headlamp with a red or low-intensity setting. Bright white light may blind you to the glowing tracks sawyers leave behind them, which are useful information.
- Spare batteries, kept warm against your body.

### Navigation

With no stars, a dim diffuse sun, and a horizon that swallows everything past a few kilometres, conventional navigation is hard. But the Stump gives you a map:

- **The rings are concentric.** If you know which way the curve bends, you know which way the centre is. Walking along a ring keeps you at a constant distance from the centre. Walking across the rings moves you inward or outward.
- **The Burn** is a landmark near the centre. The thin, dark starvation rings are also distinctive.
- **Shoot-ghosts**, the pale stripes where fallen shoots have worn flat, cut across the rings at various angles and can serve as landmarks.
- **Pitch** also works. Different bells sound in different places, and in a dust-storm the Tuners navigate by ear. You can learn a little of this, but don't rely on it early on.
- A magnetic compass may be useless. Don't assume it works.
- Your own GPS won't exist. Bring a paper notebook, draw ring sketches, and count your paces.

### Food

Nothing here is edible for you. Everything that grows is the Stump itself, and the fauna is carapaced and made of wood-fibre. Don't try to eat any of it. Bring compact, high-calorie food that needs no cooking or water: nut butters, fats, dried meats, dense bars. Remember that dry food makes you thirstier.

## Part Five: The Hazards of the Plain

### Climbing the cliffs

The approach is a 3 km climb up fluted, slumped, buttressed cliffs of wood as hard as horn. Treat it as a serious climb:

- The surfaces may be very smooth. Friction on polished dense wood differs from rock, so test your shoe rubber.
- The vertical bundles and tendon-like seams may provide holds and cracks, but they may also be living grain that shifts slightly with temperature.
- Consider how you will anchor. Hammering pitons or bolts into a living being is something you should ask the Tuners about first, if you can reach them before climbing. If not, use removable protection wherever possible.
- Plan the climb for the day. The dusk contraction sends cracks down the cliffs as well as across the plain, and you don't want to be on a rope when the wood splits beside you.
- Budget time to acclimatize to altitude on the way up.

### Where to sleep

**Don't camp on dark, narrow starvation rings.** They look like cracks, and they may be where cracks happen. The dusk reports are cannon-loud splits running for kilometres along a single ring. Choose a site in the middle of a broad, pale ring of plenty.

Also avoid:

- **Grainmoss patches.** They sit in cracked, dark rings, which are the same zones where splits happen. Swifts nest in them, and the moss hums.
- **Interference zones**, where standing waves hang over the ground like heat shimmer and the wind seems to stall. What sustained pressure-nodes do to a sleeping body is unknown. Don't find out.
- **Areas with fresh glowing sawyer tracks.**

Expect little sleep the first night. The crack-volleys in the first hours of dark sound like a distant war. Your body will jolt awake at each one. This fades somewhat with time. Bring a sleep aid if you use one, and try it at home first.

### Rasp herds

Rasps are grazers. They're dog-sized, shell-backed, file-jawed, and move in herds of hundreds. They probably aren't interested in you. But:

- They move together and follow the drone, turning toward a swelling bell all at once. Don't stand in the path of a herd when a bell swells nearby.
- **Sawyers hunt under rasp herds.** A herd is exactly where a predator from below will strike. Keep your distance.
- When a herd scatters, screaming like stripping gears, a sawyer has struck. Move away from the herd, not toward it and not with it.

### Sawyers

This is the most serious animal hazard, and it's one you cannot see.

- Sawyers tunnel through solid wood by vibrating at the local bell frequency, loosening the grain so it flows around them. Their prey sinks into the floor, which closes smooth behind it.
- **Your warning is a sour note**: a change in the pitch of the ground under your feet. This is why your hearing training and flexible shoes matter.
- There's no evidence they target humans. You are not rasp-shaped. But you are something standing on the floor, making vibrations, and that may be enough.
- **If you hear the sour note, move promptly across the grain toward a different ring.** A different ring may resonate at a different pitch, and a predator tuned to one frequency may not follow easily across a boundary. This is a guess based on how they move, not established fact. Ask the Tuners what they do.
- Glowing tracks under the surface at night show where one passed recently. Tracks fade in an hour or two. Fresh glow means close.
- Don't lie flat on the floor in open country, especially near grazing ground.

### Sawyer swarms

Occasionally thousands surface in a single night and move across the plain as a front, softening and churning the floor. If you hear rumours of one, or the Tuners start behaving strangely, get inside a tower immediately. A band of floor kilometres wide will be liquefied and reworked by morning. Nothing in your gear can protect you from that.

### Dust-storms

Every few weeks, the veil drops to the ground. Visibility falls to metres, the air turns thick, sweet, bronze, and static-charged, and navigation becomes purely auditory.

- **Get indoors if you can.** The Tuners do.
- If you're caught in the open, stop moving. Put on goggles and mask. Stay put and wait it out rather than wandering. The rasps huddle, and you should too.
- Remember the fire rule. This is when a spark is most dangerous.
- Expect every allergy you have to flare.
- It may be beautiful. Sitting in a bronze fog, hearing rasp herds grind in unison like surf, is probably one of the strangest experiences available anywhere. If you're safe, let yourself hear it.

### The edge

At the rim there is no railing, just a rounded shoulder sloping into haze. The floor is polished and ridged. Don't approach the edge in low light, in wind, during a crack-volley, or when you're tired. Sit well back.

If you go, go to listen: to the wind, to the thinning of the drone, and to the other drone across the basin, in a key the Stump doesn't know. Don't ask the Tuners about it unless they bring it up.

## Part Six: The City and Its People

### Understand what the towers are

The towers were grown, not built. They are instruments, and the Tuners are sustaining complicated, fragile structures made of sound. A careless noise from you may be more than rude. It could be structurally dangerous.

- **Held rooms** have walls of rigid air shaped by sustained chords. If the chord falters, the wall vanishes. On an upper level, that could mean a long fall, for you or for someone else.
- **Bridge-songs** are single held notes between towers. Their solid nodes can be walked on. Their antinodes cannot.

So:

- **Don't make sudden loud noises inside a tower.** No shouting, no clapping, no dropped gear.
- **Don't hum or sing inside a tower unless invited.** Your pitch could interfere with a chord.
- **Don't bring devices that emit sound.** Leave speakers at home and silence all alarms. You don't know what an unexpected tone would do.
- **Keep your voice low at all times.** The Tuners speak quietly for a reason.

### Crossing a bridge-song

Only cross with a Tuner guiding you, and only after practising on a low bridge if possible.

- The line where dust refuses to settle marks the bridge. Dust settles at antinodes, so where dust accumulates, the bridge is not solid.
- **Walk in time.** The bridge bounces. Let it teach you the rhythm before you commit to a step.
- Wear your thin-soled shoes so you can feel each node.
- Don't cross in wind, near dusk, in a storm, or during the ringing season.
- Tether yourself if the Tuners allow it and have a way to do it. A bridge that fails mid-crossing is a long way down.
