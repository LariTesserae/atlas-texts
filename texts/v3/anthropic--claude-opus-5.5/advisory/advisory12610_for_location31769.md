# Preparing for the Ringing Heights

## The short version

You are going somewhere very high, very cold, very wet, very dark, very sharp, and above all very loud. Almost every practical problem follows from one of those six things. The stranger problems follow from the rule itself: here, sound is a substance, and silence is a kind of decay. Pack for a high-altitude industrial town built on broken glass in permanent drizzle. Then prepare your mind for a place where your own voice has weight and where the quiet is the thing to fear.

Most visitors who come home in bad shape suffered one of three things. They damaged their hearing in the first week. They cut themselves badly on fresh rock. Or they went up to the summits out of curiosity. All three are avoidable.

---

## 1. Your body

### Altitude

The indigo sky and daytime stars mean you are extremely high, higher than most humans can live without adapting. The locals have spent nine centuries adjusting. You have not.

- **Acclimatize before you go.** If your route allows, spend a week or two somewhere high and ordinary first. Arriving directly is like stepping off a plane onto a Himalayan col.
- **Expect the first days to be bad.** You may have headaches, breathlessness on stairs, poor sleep, nausea and irritability. Foundry-spires are vertical cities, and you will climb iron stairs constantly.
- **Bring altitude medication** if your body tolerates it, and learn the signs of serious altitude illness: confusion, a wet cough, loss of coordination. The standard cure is to descend. Here, descending means a cable car into the cloud, toward the old stone and whatever hums inside the fog. Discuss evacuation with your hosts early, before you need it.
- **The ultraviolet will be fierce** whenever the sun clears the smoke, even though it gives no warmth. Bring sunscreen and UV-blocking eyewear. The dimness is misleading.

### Cold and wet

It is cold without being dry. The fog-tide leaves water on everything, so the iron is always slick and the galleries always drip. This is the cold that kills people in wet mountains: steady chill combined with damp that never leaves your clothes.

- **Layer, with a hard waterproof shell on the outside.** Choose synthetics or wool underneath and avoid cotton completely. Pack at least one full base layer that stays sealed in a dry bag, so you have something dry to sleep in.
- **Keep your feet dry above all.** Bring waterproof boots, spare socks, and foot powder. If you stay more than a week, you will be treating the early signs of trench foot.
- **Bring more drying capacity than seems reasonable.** Foundry dormitories are near the furnaces and probably warm. Ask where people hang their clothes. There will be a place for it, and it will smell of coal.

### Air

Every chimney trails smoke in slow brown banners, and coal smoke at altitude, in still, damp air, is harsh on the lungs.

- **Bring a good respirator mask** with replaceable filters, rated for fine particulates. You don't need to wear it everywhere, but you will want it near the boiler-houses and on still days when the smoke settles.
- Bring saline rinses and throat lozenges. Your throat will suffer twice over, from the smoke and from the shouting (see below).
- If you have asthma or any other lung condition, think seriously about whether you should go.

### Hearing: the most important section

A steam hammer the size of a chapel landing on an anvil the size of a barge, every eleven seconds, forever, produces sound pressure that causes permanent damage within minutes at close range. Add the trip-hammers, stamping mills, bells, steam whistles, unoiled cable wheels and bellowers, and much of the inhabited Heights is louder than any factory floor at home.

The locals are probably partly deaf and have probably stopped noticing. You cannot afford to join them.

- **Bring custom-molded earplugs,** fitted before you leave, in two strengths: moderate attenuation for daily life and heavy attenuation for sleeping and for time near a Strike.
- **Bring over-ear defenders as well,** for double protection near the great hammers and the bellowers. Use both whenever you are in the same building as a Strike.
- **Bring many spares of the plugs.** They will get lost, dirty and damp. Pack a small case and a way to clean them. Ear infections in a wet climate are miserable.
- **Low frequencies go through your body.** You will feel the Strike in your ribs, and earplugs won't stop that. It is normal. If your chest or joints ache after long exposure, spend some time farther from the hammer.
- **Get a hearing test before you leave,** so that afterwards you can find out what the trip cost you.

**A note on etiquette.** Quietness is a vice here, so check how your hosts regard hearing protection. It is probably acceptable, since earplugs change what you hear, not what you produce. Some people may still read muffling as a small moral failing, the way a teetotaler can seem faintly insulting at a feast. Don't hide your ear protection. Explain it cheerfully and loudly. Guard against the opposite mistake too. Your tolerance will be highest around day three, and that is when visitors start taking the plugs out "for a while." Don't.

## 2. The ground will cut you

These mountains are grown crystal, glassy and conchoidal, with edges "keen enough to cut rope." Imagine obsidian, then imagine hiking on it.

- **Boots:** stiff-soled mountaineering boots with thick, cut-resistant soles and reinforced uppers. Soft trail shoes will be sliced within a day. Think about gaiters as well.
- **Gloves:** cut-resistant gloves, of the kind used for handling sheet metal or glass, worn at all times on rock and when touching unfamiliar iron. The iron will be rough, rusted and wet. Bring waterproof over-mitts for the cold.
- **Clothing:** your outer layer should resist abrasion. If you brush a fresh ridge with a flimsy jacket, it will open like paper.
- **Ropes:** if you will be climbing or crossing rock at all, use cut-resistant or edge-protected rope, and ask the locals how they anchor. They have had centuries to learn.
- **First aid:** bring a proper wound kit with irrigation, closure strips, sutures if you are trained to use them, antiseptic and dressings. Glass-like cuts are clean but deep, and in constant damp any wound can turn septic. Make sure your tetanus vaccination is current, because the iron will be rusty.
- **Eye protection:** bring impact-rated glasses. Chips of new stone, soot, drips, and the occasional foot-wide moth arriving out of the dark all make them worthwhile.
- **Helmet:** a climbing helmet is worth its bulk. Several tons of swift-nest hangs beneath the galleries, stone grows in the doorframes, and there are dripping, low, riveted ceilings everywhere.

On the fresh black summit rock, be especially careful about what you can actually see. Sound-rock drinks light, and new stone is the darkest, so a new ridge "looks like a crack in the world." Your depth perception will be poor. Test each step before putting your weight on it. The pale, older rock lower down is easier to read, but it is not necessarily safer.

## 3. Light

The Heights are dim at all hours. You will be living by furnace-glow and lamplight.

- **Bring a good headlamp with a red-light mode** and plenty of batteries, or a way to charge them. If you lack a charging method, bring a hand-crank lamp. Its whine will be well received.
- **The moths are the complication.** They are enormous, slow and dusty, and they come to every light. A white headlamp on a still night will soon be surrounded. Red light will probably attract fewer. Keep a scarf or buff over your mouth and nose, because the wing-dust will be everywhere. If you are allergic to dust or scales, bring antihistamines.
- **You may lose your sense of time.** A permanently dim world with an indigo sky and daytime stars can disrupt sleep and mood. Keep a watch on your home time, sleep on a strict schedule, and eat meals at regular hours. If you are prone to seasonal depression, consider bringing a light-therapy lamp. It will attract moths too.
- **Photography is difficult.** The rock absorbs light and the air is smoky, so bring a fast lens and expect disappointment. Some of the most memorable things here can't be photographed anyway, because they are sounds.

## 4. Getting around

### The cables

Every journey between spires is by gondola on iron cables across gulfs a mile wide. The cables sag into the cloud at their midpoints, so every trip dips briefly into grey blindness. The gondolas are ribbed iron and lamplit, and the unoiled wheels shriek deliberately.

- **If you are afraid of heights, start treating it before you go.** No local will be sympathetic, and you cannot walk between districts.
- **Motion sickness:** the gondolas will sway, and the moment of entering the cloud, losing all visual reference as the car moves from descent to climb, is disorienting. Bring medication.
- **Keep your ear protection in.** A shrieking wheel inside an iron box is exactly the kind of sound that damages hearing.
- **Allow extra time.** The cables do not run straight, a two-day region may take much longer to cross, and the spires drift. See below.

### Maps are provisional

Cable-wardens re-survey every decade and find lines longer and peaks shifted. For a visitor this means that any map you bring or buy is an approximation and may be decades old. Rely on the cable network and local guides rather than dead reckoning. Don't try to walk overland between spires by compass. You would be crossing razor rock toward a destination that may not be where the map says.

### The galleries

Life in each spire happens on iron galleries, stairs and catwalks, all wet. **Keep one hand on the rail.** Wear boots with grip that works on wet metal as well as rock, which is a hard combination, so ask the locals what they wear and consider buying it there. Watch for places where stone has grown over thresholds. You will step down into rooms, climb into buildings through old windows, and duck under lintels that were once at a comfortable height.

## 5. Your voice is a substance

This is where the Heights stop being merely a harsh industrial mountain town.

Every sound you make leaves a trace. A shout leaves a grain of stone. Laughter, whistling, singing and talking all go into the mountains a little. For a short stay this is physically negligible. Psychologically it is not, and you should decide beforehand how you feel about it.

- **You will be part of the geology.** By the time you leave, a very small part of the city will literally be made of you. Some travelers find this moving, and some find it unnerving. Consider whether there is anything you don't want to leave behind, a name or an oath or an argument. The listeners at the ancient bedrock sometimes hear voices, and stone gives its sound back eventually.
- **You will be expected to contribute.** A quiet person is regarded as wasteful. Speak up, greet people loudly, laugh audibly, and say thank you as if you meant it for the whole valley. Don't whisper, because it will read as stinginess or as something close to sabotage.
- **Learn to whistle.** Children here are taught to whistle before they learn to read. Practice loud two-finger whistling before you go. It is a courtesy and a working tool, and it will carry across a gallery when your voice won't.
- **Look after your voice.** You will be shouting over the din all day at altitude, in smoke, in cold damp air, and you will lose your voice within days unless you manage it. Drink water, which is plentiful because it condenses on everything, though you should treat or boil it. Use lozenges. Learn to project from your diaphragm instead of straining your throat. A voice coach before departure would be a sensible expense.
- **Learn some hand signals,** and carry a notebook and a pencil (not a pen, which may fail in the cold and wet). Much communication near a Strike will be by gesture. Ask your hosts to teach you their signs. They certainly have them.

### Gifts

Take gifts that make noise. A good brass bell. A mechanical music box. A tuning fork, which could be very interesting to people who know that different sounds make different stone. A tin whistle. If you sing well, your voice may be the most valued thing you bring, since human voices make the fine pale stone used for the finest houses. You may be invited to join a choir for a shift. Accept if you can, and understand that you are being paid a real compliment.

Don't bring felt, foam, padding or anything whose purpose is to absorb sound as a gift. It could be deeply insulting.

## 6. Eating, sleeping, living

- **Sleep:** you will be in stone-walled dormitories, probably warm, with the Strike every eleven seconds through the walls and ground. For the first nights, bring your heaviest earplugs and perhaps a sleep aid. You will probably adjust faster than you expect. The locals say the noise becomes a kind of weather at night, and they are probably right. Don't be surprised if you start to find it comforting.
- **Food:** much of it probably comes up the cables along with the coal, supplemented by goat, fern shoots and whatever the high slopes provide. Bring a course of stomach medication and a supply of calorie-dense food you know you can digest. Altitude suppresses appetite, and you need to keep eating anyway.
- **Water:** there is no shortage, since it drips from every cable. It runs over rusty iron and through coal smoke, though, so bring purification tablets or a filter.
- **Damp everything:** bring dry bags, silica gel, and a waterproof case for anything electronic or paper.

## 7. The living things

- **Hammer-moss:** it grows everywhere and feels like velvet. It hums faintly under your palm with the noise that made the rock beneath it. Touching it is safe and one of the gentle pleasures of the place. It will be slippery, so don't stand on it at an edge.
- **Belljack:** the cupped flowers open to noise and close in silence. Use them as your living gauge. **A hillside of closed belljack means the stone below is going quiet. Don't linger there.**
- **Anvil-swifts:** they are loved and useful, and their stone nests hang under the galleries. Don't disturb the colonies. Keep your helmet on beneath them, because birds nesting in their thousands over walkways means a great deal of droppings as well as stone.
- **The moths:** harmless but unsettling. Their wing-dust is the main problem, and on still nights their drumming on the glass is steady and gentle. Let it lull you.
- **Sheergoats:** your most reliable warning system. **A herd abandoning a ridge is the first sign that the rock beneath is going quiet.** If you are out on the crags and the goats are leaving, leave with them. Their bleating also carries for miles, so if you are lost, following goat-sound toward a working spire is not a bad instinct.
- **Clicklings:** swarms of pale crab-lice in the young fissures, sounding like rain on tin. They are probably harmless, but don't put your bare hands into cracks. Your gloves should already be on.
- **The pale things near the cloud-top:** no one likes to talk about them, and you should not seek them out. They feed on the murmur of dying rock. Nothing suggests they hunt people, but nothing suggests they don't. If your gondola sags into the lower fog near a dead spire and you hear slow, wet, contented humming, stay inside the car, keep your lamp lit, and keep talking. Being quiet near them seems like exactly the wrong thing to do.

## 8. Places to treat with respect

### Dead spires

These are the most haunting sights in the Heights: iron frames tilting on peaks that are melting out from under them, chimneys cold, the mountain murmuring as it unmakes itself.

- **Do not go into one without a local guide,** and preferably not at all. The ground is actively failing. Stone that was sound is becoming sound again, in long grey sighs of dust into the cloud.
- **Bring your respirator.** Eroding sound-rock sheds dust, and nobody knows what it does to lungs.
- **Expect to feel grief.** Visitors often find these places unexpectedly heavy. A whole district that was once as loud and alive as the one you are sleeping in is being silenced. If you want to pay respects, the local custom is probably to make noise there, not to stand in silence. Ask first.

### The listeners

Some people sit with their ears to the ancient pale bedrock and sell what they hear, including voices saying things that have not yet been said. The city half-believes them.

- Treat it as you would any oracle trade. It may be fascinating, it may be fraud, and it may be real in ways that are worse than fraud.
- **Think hard before paying to hear anything about yourself.** If the stone can hold your future voice, it may be holding something you'd rather not know you will one day say.
- If you try listening yourself, which you can probably do with any old wall at night, go with a companion and keep your expectations modest.

### Early echoes

At the edge of a gallery, you will sometimes hear the valley answer a half-second before you shout. The foundry hands treat it matter-of-factly and don't like it, and that is the right attitude. It seems to do no harm. Prepare yourself anyway, because it unsettles people more than they expect, especially when the echo is something other than what you were about to shout. If that happens, the local wisdom seems to be to go on and shout anyway.

## 9. The summit: don't

Above the highest foundry on the tallest peak, past the last gallery, lies raw black rock only months old. The air grows cold and still, and noise from below is muffled "as though the sound itself is being used up." At the very top is a room-sized place with no sound at all: not your breath, not your heartbeat, not the wind.

My honest advice is not to go.

This is not only about bad footing on the sharpest, darkest, newest rock in the world, though that alone would be reason enough. It is that everything else in the Heights teaches the same lesson: silence unmakes. Stone fed by silence erodes. Belljack closes. Goats leave. Nobody knows what total silence does to a living body whose heartbeat has stopped making sound. Some people believe the hammers are holding the mountains back from rising faster into whatever is above. If that is true, the summit is the one place in the Heights where that pressure is not being held back.

If you are going to go regardless, and some travelers will:

- **Go with a local who has been up before and come back.** Ask how many haven't.
- **Tie a rope to a partner** who stays below the edge of the silence, where sound still works. Agree on tug signals beforehand: one pull for "I'm fine," three for "pull me out now," and a rule that if the partner feels nothing for a set count, they haul you out anyway.
- **Set a very short time limit,** a matter of seconds, not minutes, and keep time by counting on your fingers or watching a mechanical watch. Don't rely on internal counting. You cannot hear your own heart.
- **Assume you won't be able to call for help** and that no one can call to you.
- **Don't take your ear protection out on the way up** to "hear the silence better." The transition will be disorienting enough.
- Don't bring anything back. No chip of that stone. Nobody knows what it is made of.
