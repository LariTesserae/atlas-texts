# Preparing to Visit the Combing Stones

## The Short Version

You are going to a place that is cold, wet, high, silent, enormous, and fragile, in roughly that order of importance. Nearly everything that will go wrong for an unprepared visitor comes from one of those six words. Nearly everything that will go right comes from slowing down. The city does not reward ambition. It rewards the kind of attention a person gives to a stone before setting it down.

Plan to be damp for your entire visit. Plan to be lost at least once. Plan to see very few people and to be heard by more of them than you realize.

---

## 1. When to Go

The seasons here are not scenery. They set the danger level.

- **Late summer is the best window for a first visit.** The fog is thick and the city is at its wettest, but the temperatures are survivable. The cushion plants are flowering for their two brief weeks, the cisterns are full, and the walls are most stable because the thaw collapses are over and the autumn storms haven't begun.
- **Early summer** is also reasonable, but walls weakened by the thaw may still be settling. Some that looked sound in spring will slump in June.
- **Autumn** gives you the finches passing through, and the eastern districts are beautiful then. But the afternoon storms become violent enough to bring walls down. If you go, be under cover by early afternoon, not by five.
- **Winter** is for experienced cold-weather mountaineers only, and even they should think hard. The fog freezes on contact. Rime ice builds inches thick, and ice feathers an arm's length long grow from every edge. That ice is heavy, and it breaks walls. The lanes become frozen tunnels, the windward districts become glazed and nearly impassable, and falling ice and stone are a constant risk. It is said to be staggeringly beautiful. It is also the season most likely to kill you.
- **Spring thaw** is the season of collapse. Capstones drop into cells, and courses slump. Do not sleep inside a domed cell during the thaw. Do not linger in the roofed lanes. If you come in late spring, though, you may be able to watch, or even help with, the regathering and relaying of fallen walls. That is probably the closest a visitor can come to understanding the place.

## 2. Your Body

### Altitude

The plateau rises above the lowland cloud layer. The vegetation tells you a great deal: cushion plants, knee-high junipers a century old, pika-like animals, and lichen as the dominant life form. That is an alpine or high subalpine environment. Assume you will be at a height where altitude sickness is possible.

- Acclimatize before you ascend the escarpment. Spend a few days at a moderate altitude elsewhere if your route allows it.
- Learn the symptoms: headache, nausea, poor sleep, and breathlessness out of proportion to effort. Confusion and loss of coordination are emergencies. Descend.
- Descending is complicated here. "Down" means either the escarpment, which is a cliff, or one of the old outwash channels. Know your descent route *before* you need it (see Section 9).

### Cold and Wet Together

This combination is the real danger. It is more dangerous than cold alone. Dry cold at night, saturated fog in the morning, and rain in the afternoon will soak you and chill you all day long. Hypothermia here will not arrive dramatically. It will creep up on you in a quiet lane in the fog while you are being careful and looking at lichen.

- Watch yourself and any companions for the "umbles": stumbling, mumbling, fumbling, grumbling. If you notice them, stop, get dry, and eat.
- Eat more than you think you need. Your body will burn a lot of fuel keeping warm while damp.

### Feet

Your feet will be wet much of the time. Trench foot, meaning tissue damage from prolonged cold and wet, is a genuine risk on a multi-day visit.

- Carry several pairs of wool or synthetic socks and rotate them.
- At night, dry your feet fully. Keep one pair of socks sacred and dry, worn only for sleeping.
- Inspect your feet daily for whitening, wrinkling that won't go away, numbness, or pain.

### Hands

You will be touching wet stone constantly, and you will drink by cupping your hands under cold weeping joints. Your hands will crack and chill. Bring:

- Thin gloves you don't mind soaking
- A warm, dry pair for camp
- A good balm for cracked skin

### Sun

At noon, when the cloud lifts, the light comes back "hard and clean" at altitude. Bring sunglasses and sunscreen even though you'll spend half the day in fog. In winter, glare off the rime can cause snow blindness.

### Fitness

You won't need speed. You'll need endurance, balance, and a strong back and knees:

- The lanes are uneven, and the oldest steps are worn into saddles that roll ankles.
- You'll spend a lot of time crouching through low roofed passages.

Train with a loaded pack on rough, wet, uneven ground. Train your ankles specifically.

### Claustrophobia

Be honest with yourself here. The lanes are rarely wider than one person. Many are roofed, forming stone tunnels lit only by gaps. The fog reduces visibility to a few walls' length. There is "almost no open ground." If tight spaces trouble you, practice in caves or narrow slot passages first, or reconsider the trip. There is no quick way out of the middle of this city. It is the size of a small sea.

## 3. Clothing

Dress for sustained cold drizzle and wind, like a highland winter in a wet climate, even in summer.

- **No cotton, anywhere.** Not underwear, not socks, not a T-shirt for sleeping. Cotton will stay wet and cold for your whole visit.
- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic, top and bottom. Bring a spare set kept dry for sleeping.
- **Mid layer:** fleece or synthetic insulation. Down is dangerous here unless you can guarantee it stays dry, and you can't. If you bring a down jacket for camp, keep it in a dry bag and never wear it in the fog.
- **Shell:** a genuinely waterproof, breathable hard-shell jacket with a good hood, plus waterproof trousers. They will be used every day.
- **Hat and neck gaiter.** The morning fog is cold enough that you'll want both.
- **Gloves:** a thin wet-use pair, a warm dry pair, and waterproof over-mitts for winter.

### Footwear

This matters a great deal. The stones in the windward districts are "slick and black," and the channels are coated in tar-like lichen films and snail trails.

- Choose boots with soft, sticky rubber soles that grip wet rock well. Avoid hard, deep-lugged soles that skate on slick stone.
- Pick ankle support over lightness. The saddled steps and rounded cobbles will test your ankles constantly.
- Waterproof boots will slow the soaking, but they will not prevent it. Water will get in from above, and waterproof boots dry slowly. Some experienced wet-country walkers prefer non-waterproof boots that drain and dry faster, paired with warm wool socks. Decide based on your experience.
- **Winter only:** bring crampons or microspikes, but understand that metal points will scratch stones that have never borne a tool mark. Use them only where you must. Walk carefully enough that you rarely need them.

## 4. The Pack and Your Shape

In the city, **your width matters.**

- Choose a **narrow pack**. Large, wide expedition packs with side pockets, external frames, or strapped-on gear will jam in the lanes. They will scrape walls and dislodge stones. Choose something tall and slim, and keep everything inside.
- **Do not strap anything to the outside.** A sleeping mat sticking out sideways will catch on every corner. If it must go outside, strap it vertically.
- **Keep your total load modest.** You'll be ducking and squeezing, and a heavy, top-heavy pack makes you fall on wet stone.
- **Waterproof from the inside.** Line the pack with a heavy liner bag, and put everything important in its own dry bag as well. Pack covers are not enough. Water here comes from every direction, dripping from above, splashing from channels, and condensing from the air itself.

### Be Aware of Your Footprint

Treat every wall you pass as something you could knock down. Don't lean on walls. Don't brace against them in the narrow parts more than you must. Don't pull yourself up by them. Don't climb them. Walls in this city are built "by fit and weight alone." Many are in balance rather than at rest, leaning at gentle angles that may be the last angle they will hold.

## 5. Water

This is the city's great gift, and you should accept it gratefully and carefully.

### Drinking

The water weeping from the walls is condensed fog. It is essentially distilled from cloud. Freshly beaded water on clean stone is likely among the purest water you'll ever drink. However:

- It runs over lichens, mosses, and snail tracks. Bird-perch sites are marked by orange lichen, which means droppings. The cisterns are standing water, sometimes for a long time.
- As a multiverse traveler, your gut has no local immunity. **Carry a filter or purification tablets** and use them, especially for cistern and channel water. Drinking directly from a fresh weeping joint in the morning, as the locals do, is probably low risk. But it's your risk to take, and you shouldn't take it on the first day.
- Condensed water is very low in minerals. Over a long stay, add electrolyte tablets. This matters most if you are exerting yourself.
- You will not lack water. You can carry less than you would on most treks, perhaps one litre, and refill constantly. Water is heavy, and you want a light pack.

### Protecting the Water

This is the most important rule of conduct in the entire city.

**The channels, gutters, and cisterns are the city's drinking supply.** They are connected across whole districts. Anything you put into them travels.

- **Never urinate or defecate into or near a channel, gutter, or cistern.** Given how the city is built, with wall against wall, lanes funnelling runoff, and water finding every joint, that means almost anywhere in the city proper.
- **Bring waste bags (WAG bags or equivalent) and pack out everything, including urine if you can manage it.** At minimum, learn where the locals go. There will be a practice. Watch for it, or ask quietly and politely. Don't assume.
- Do not wash dishes, bodies, or clothes in the channels. Do not let soap, sunscreen, or insect repellent run off your skin into them. Collect water in a container and wash away from the water paths. Even then, remember the paths are nearly everywhere.
- Do not stand in channels if you can avoid it.

## 6. Food and Fuel

**Bring all your food.** Nothing edible grows in the city. Whatever the inhabitants eat is carried up the escarpment or brought in from the peat meadows at the far northern edge. Every mouthful they have was hauled in by someone. Do not expect to buy food, and do not accept food casually. If you are offered some, understand what it represents.

- Pack calorie-dense, compact food that tolerates damp: nuts, fats, dense bars, dried meals.
- **Do not forage.** The cushion plants belong to the pika, which harvests them. The juniper seeds belong to the autumn finches. The dry cells stocked with cushion plants are pika granaries. Do not disturb them, and do not sleep in them.
- Pack out every scrap. The ecosystem is extraordinarily sparse. Food waste could feed ravens into unnatural numbers, or poison the few animals there are.

### Fire and Stoves

The cells smell of old smoke, so people do burn fuel. But fuel here is precious. Presumably it is peat from the north or something carried up from below. The junipers are protected by long-standing custom.

- **Do not burn anything you find.** Bring a stove and your own fuel.
- **If you cook inside a domed cell, ventilate it.** A corbelled beehive with a small gap at the capstone is exactly the kind of space where carbon monoxide builds up. Cook in the doorway, or in a lane, or with the stove near an opening. Never run a stove while you sleep.
- Hot drinks are one of your best defenses against the chill. Budget fuel for plenty of them.

## 7. Navigation: Finding Your Way Without Signs

There are no inscriptions, no carvings, no signs, and no street names. The city covers something like the area of a small sea, and in the morning fog you'll see only a few walls' length ahead. Getting lost is not a possibility. It is a certainty. Prepare for it so that being lost is unsettling rather than dangerous.

### Bring

- **A compass**, if magnetism behaves normally in this world. Test it before you depend on it. Granite and slate are unlikely to deflect it much, but check.
- **Any satellite or electronic navigation** you rely on elsewhere may not work across worlds. Treat it as a bonus, not a plan.
- **A notebook and pencil.** Pencil works on damp paper. Ink runs.
- **A headlamp** with spare batteries kept warm and dry, for the roofed lanes and for dusk.

### Learn to Read the City as the Locals Do

The city is organized, just not in words. Before you go, and during your first days, learn these cues:

- **The grain of the walls.** The main walls run crosswise to the prevailing drift of the fog, like the teeth of a comb. The fog comes up the escarpment from the west and drifts inland toward the east. So the main walls tend to run roughly *north–south*, and moving *along* them keeps you at the same distance from the escarpment. Moving *across* them, which usually means threading through gaps and courtyards, takes you east or west. Check this against your compass on day one, because the local wind may not be due west.
- **Wetness.** The west is wetter, and the east is drier. Stones that are slick and black, channels running all day, and moss cushions in the joints mean you are windward, toward the escarpment. Pale walls crusted with grey-green lichen, and deeper cisterns, mean you are toward the lee, to the east.
- **Which face the growth is on.** Moss on the windward faces, lichen crust on the lee faces. A single wall can tell you which way the wind comes from.
- **Sound.** Water grows louder toward the cisterns. In fog, when you can see nothing, your ears are your best instrument. Stand still, stop breathing loudly, and listen.
- **The fog front itself.** In the morning, the fog rolls inland from the west. If you are ahead of it in the eastern sun, you can watch its grey wall come toward you. That is a direction.
- **Afternoon cloud streets.** The cumulus line up above the city along the grain of the walls. Looking up in the afternoon shows you the orientation of the whole city.
- **The raptor.** In the late afternoon it rides the updraft along the escarpment rim. If you see it hanging motionless, it is probably near the western edge.
- **Towers.** There are a few high towers. At noon, when the cloud lifts, climbing one (if permitted, and if it's sound) will give you the only real overview you'll get. Use it to fix your position and plan the next day. Don't be on a tower in the afternoon, when storms build.

### Do Not Leave Marks

The instinct of a lost traveler is to mark the route. **Don't.**

- No chalk, flagging tape, scratches, or paint.
- **Absolutely no cairns or stacked stones.** In this city, placing stone on stone is building. Moving a stone to mark your way means altering the city, and possibly weakening a wall you took it from. The locals mark nothing. Follow their lead.
- Record your route in your notebook instead: "Left along the weeping wall. Courtyard with juniper. Tunnel with slipped capstone, bright gap. Cistern sound ahead."

### Go Slow and Stop When It's Thick

- The morning fog is the worst time to try to navigate. Consider staying put through it, or moving only in short, known stages.
- Noon, when the cloud has lifted and light is "hard and clean," is your best travel window.
- In fog, if you are uncertain, stop. Being lost in a wet lane in daylight is survivable. Blundering around in fog toward the escarpment edge may not be.

## 8. Hazards

### Falling Stone

Capstones drop. Courses slump. Ice feathers break walls in winter. Frost lifts stones a hair's width each night.

- **A helmet is not absurd here**, especially in spring and winter and in the roofed lanes. A lightweight climbing helmet is reasonable.
- Before entering a cell, look up at the corbelling. Are courses sagging? Is the capstone seated? Is there fallen stone on the floor? Fresh rubble means recent collapse.
- Don't linger under walls that lean noticeably, even though many have leaned for centuries.
- Be most wary during thaw, during and just after heavy afternoon rain when the whole city runs at once, and in winter when rime loads the walls.
- If you hear stone shift, move out from under it. Don't wait to see.

### The Escarpment

The western edge of the city is a cliff. In the morning the fog climbs it, so you may be walking in cloud toward a drop you can't see. **Know where the edge is.** Treat the wettest, blackest, most moss-heavy districts with extreme caution in poor visibility.
