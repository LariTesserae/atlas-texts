# Preparing to Visit the Deep Fan

## First, the hard truth

You cannot visit this place with your body. You can only visit it inside something.

At four thousand meters the water presses on every surface with roughly four hundred times the force of the air at sea level. That is about four tonnes on every square centimeter-sized patch of you, or a small car balanced on each fingernail. Unprotected, a human body dies here instantly and completely. No training, no breathing technique, and no acclimatization changes this. Everything in this guide assumes you will spend your entire visit sealed inside a rigid pressure hull at one atmosphere, and that you will touch the Deep Fan only through steel fingers, hydrophones, and instruments.

Accept this before you go. Travelers who arrive hoping to "feel" the place directly tend to be disappointed, or they make fatal choices trying. The Fan is not a place you walk in. It is a place you sit inside of, listening.

## The arrival problem

This is the single most dangerous moment of the trip, and it is specific to multiverse travel.

If your crossing opens any kind of aperture between a normal-pressure space and the basin floor, water at 400 atmospheres will come through it. It will not flood. It will cut. A pinhole jet at that pressure can slice through metal and flesh, and a larger opening turns into a hydraulic ram. Your crossing must meet one of two conditions:

- **You arrive already enclosed.** The vehicle crosses whole, sealed, with its hull already rated for the depth. This is the only method I would trust.
- **The aperture opens into a pressure lock**, a chamber built to take the full ambient load, which then sheds that pressure slowly through a controlled bleed. This takes hours, and the equipment is heavy.

Also consider where you arrive. The basin floor changes by the hour. A point that was bare nodule when your coordinates were taken could now be under a hand's depth of fresh ooze, or under a turbidity current in progress. Arrive a few meters above the bottom, never on it or in it. Arriving inside mud means arriving buried.

## Choosing your vessel

You want something closer to a deep research submersible than a diving suit. Articulated hard suits are not rated anywhere near this depth. The requirements:

**Hull.** A titanium sphere, or something equivalent, rated with a large margin to at least 6,000 meters. The nominal depth is 4,000 meters, but this basin is a slump inside a basin, and you don't know exactly how far down the floor has settled. Get a hull that has been cycled and inspected. You will hear it creak on the descent or arrival as it takes the load. That sound is normal and still frightening.

**Viewports.** Small, thick, conical acrylic. Bring them for morale more than for use. The broth will make them almost useless.

**Buoyancy and ballast.** The water here is denser and more viscous than ordinary seawater because of the suspended load, so your trim will behave differently from anything you tested at home. You need adjustable ballast and **jettisonable** ballast: drop-weights that release mechanically even if all power fails. Your last-resort escape from burial is shedding mass and rising.

**Suction release.** Deep mud grips. A vehicle that settles into soft clay can be held down by suction far stronger than its positive buoyancy. Your skids, manipulator arms, sampling baskets, and any other part likely to be buried should be designed to break away. Plan to lose hardware. It's cheaper than losing yourself.

**Propulsion.** Low-wash thrusters with guarded, silt-tolerant intakes. Every movement you make lifts grains that won't settle, and a careless burst of thrust creates a private storm around you that blinds your sensors for hours. Crawler tracks will not work on this floor. The nodules roll, rock, and shift under load. Hovering is the correct posture.

**Life support.** Oxygen, CO₂ scrubbing, and power for your planned stay plus at least three days of reserve. Nobody is coming to get you. If there is a rescue, it is you rescuing yourself.

## Cold

The water is a constant two degrees Celsius, and the hull is a large metal heat sink sitting in it. Within hours the inside of your sphere will be cold, and the interior walls will sweat condensation.

- Wear layered wool or synthetic insulation, with a windproof shell against the chill radiating from the hull.
- Bring insulated seating pads. Sitting in direct contact with a titanium hull at four degrees drains heat faster than you would believe.
- Bring warm liquids in vacuum flasks. Bring gloves thin enough to work controls in.
- Keep a towel for the condensation, and make sure your electronics are sealed against drip.

Expect to be cold for your whole visit. It won't be dangerous if you prepare, but it will be constant, like everything else here.

## Sensing a place you cannot see

Here is what visiting the Deep Fan is actually like: you will almost never see it.

**Lights.** Your floodlights will light up the broth itself, not whatever lies beyond it. Every suspended grain scatters light back at you. The result is a glowing grey wall about an arm's length from the viewport, like headlights in heavy snow, only thicker. Use low-intensity, low-angle lighting set well off the camera axis. Red or amber wavelengths may reduce backscatter a little. Mostly, accept that your visual range is measured in centimeters.

**Turning the lights off.** Do this deliberately and often. Only in total darkness will you see the blue smears of bioluminescence, soft and fogged, blooming and fading. It may be the most beautiful thing you experience here, and the only way to see it is to give up seeing everything else.

**Sonar.** High-frequency imaging sonar is attenuated by the suspended sediment. Bring lower-frequency systems that trade resolution for range. You will "see" the floor as a coarse grey texture of lumps, and animals will appear rarely, as brief returns.

**Touch.** Manipulator arms with force feedback, if you can get them. Much of what you learn here you will learn by touching: the botryoidal surface of a nodule, the give of clay, the resistance of a sponge stalk. Pressing between two nodules and finding a third, as described, is a thing you will feel through a machine.

**Hydrophones.** Bring good ones, with headphones. The hiss of grain on grain is the voice of the place, and you should hear it. Listen also for the low grinding of nodules during storms. Your hull and machinery will add their own noise, so take quiet periods with non-essential systems powered down.

## The warning thud

This is the most important operational rule for your stay.

Turbidity currents start as slope failures up on the continental rise, possibly many tens of kilometers away. The failure sends a pulse through the rock, and sound in rock travels at kilometers per second. The mud flow itself travels at perhaps several to twenty meters per second, as fast as a river in flood or faster, but far slower than the seismic signal. So the thud arrives first, sometimes by an hour or more.

**Bring a seismometer or a hull-coupled geophone and monitor it continuously.** Set an alarm for low-frequency events above a threshold. A thud from the rise means a flow is probably coming.

Rehearse your storm response before you arrive, and pick one strategy in advance:

1. **Rise above it.** The flows are "many meters thick," but in a trapping basin that could mean tens of meters. Ascend well clear, hold position in the water column, and wait. A flow that runs underneath you is harmless. A flow that catches you low is not. This is the safer choice if you have the buoyancy control and power.
2. **Do not hunker down unless you have no choice.** Anchoring against a flow means accepting burial. Burial means suction, and suction means you are now depending on your breakaway hardware and drop-weights working perfectly.

Watch for the slower benthic storms, too: the eddy-driven ones that spiral down from surface currents. They give no thud. You'll know them by increasing hiss on the hydrophones, rising turbidity on your optical sensors, and your vehicle starting to drift. Currents come from every direction here. There is no lee to shelter in, so don't bother looking for one.

## Navigation in a place with no landmarks

The floor is the flattest land there is, with swells so gentle you notice them only as a lean in the current, and the surface is rewritten every hour. You cannot navigate by what you see, and landmarks you record today may be buried tomorrow.

- **Inertial navigation plus a Doppler velocity log** to track your own movement. Both will drift, especially in turbulent, sediment-laden flow.
- **Acoustic transponder beacons**, placed at your arrival point and around your working area, to give you fixed references. Mount them on tall, weighted, streamlined stands so a few centimeters of new ooze can't bury them. Expect to lose some of them anyway.
- **Your arrival and exit point is sacred.** Mark it with redundant beacons of different designs. Know its position through several independent systems. If you can't find your way home, you stay, and "everything that falls in, stays" becomes a statement about you.

## Communications

Assume you have none with your home universe. Within the basin, acoustic modems will work poorly through the broth, at short range and low bandwidth. If you come as a team, keep your vehicles close, agree on regroup procedures in advance, and plan to work alone when separated.

## The ethics of disturbing it

The storms churn this place constantly, so you might think your disturbance doesn't matter. It matters more than you think, for small reasons.

- **Move slowly.** Your wake adds to the drift. It won't change the basin, but it will blind you and may bury the frost-pale bacterial mats that are "always beginning."
- **Don't touch the standing glass sponges.** They are few, most have already been knocked down, and they are brittle beyond belief. Their skeletons lie among the stones like dropped lace, and you should leave them as lace.
- **Leave the crinoids alone,** especially in a lull, when they open to feed. That window is their whole life.
- **Leave the octopus's ring alone.** It rebuilds its wall after every storm. The storm always wins, but you shouldn't be another storm. If you find it, watch with the lights low and your thrusters idle.
- **Leave the made stones.** The net-sinkers, flints, and dished grinding stone are the one human presence already here, and they are on their way to becoming nodules. Photograph them, touch them gently if you must, and put them back exactly as you found them. The oldest has its first millimeter of manganese coat. Taking it interrupts something that is going to take the rest of time.
- **Don't drop anything.** Anything you lose here stays here. Tie off tools. Account for every sampling tube.

## Taking things home

The place's rule is that nothing leaves. You can technically break that rule, but think about it first.

Living things here are built for the pressure and the cold. Brought up fast, most die and fall apart. If you must sample biology, bring pressure-retaining sample chambers. It is more honest to collect sound, images, and measurements, and leave the matter where it lies. A small sample of loose ooze taken from the surface skin, which the next storm will replace anyway, is probably the most defensible souvenir. The nodules have been growing longer than the continents have held their present shapes. You don't need one on your mantelpiece.

## The psychological weight

This is the part travelers underestimate.

You will be in a small sphere, maybe two meters across, cold, damp, lit by dim instrument panels. Outside is total or near-total darkness, pressure enough to erase you instantly, and a sea of silt that ends your vision at the glass. Your hull will tick and creak. The hydrophones will give you a hiss that never stops, which after a few hours stops being a sound and becomes the condition of existence, as the description says. There is no day or night and no seasons, only storms and the gaps between them.

Some people find this unbearable. Others find it the calmest place they have ever been. Know which you are before you go.

- Do real confinement training: long sessions in a small dark space, with no exit you can use.
- Bring a clock and keep a strict schedule of sleep, meals, and work. With no light cycle, your sense of time will dissolve.
- Bring something personal and small: music, a photograph, a familiar-smelling cloth.
- Have a rule, agreed before arrival, for when you leave early. Write it down. Obey it without renegotiating.

The subtler danger is being absorbed. The place is so old, so indifferent, and so complete in its rules that some visitors report a quiet sense of their own insignificance that can tip into fatalism. You will feel how the plain makes no distinction between what was made and what was found. Remember that you are a visitor, and keep your exit protocols sharp.

## What a good visit might look like

You cross inside your sealed sphere and arrive five meters above a floor you can't see. The hull settles into its load with a sound like a knuckle cracking in a large room. You wait for the silt your arrival stirred up to thin, which takes an hour. You drop your beacons.

You descend with the lights low. The floor appears as a sonar texture first, then, at arm's length, in the lamp glow: black-brown lumps packed like the cobbles of an old street, furred with rust and cream films. A manipulator presses between two of them and finds a third.

You turn the lights off. For a long time there is nothing. Then, far off, a blue bloom soaks outward through the fog and fades.

You listen. The hiss. Your own breath. The hull ticking as it cools.

Six hours in, the geophone chimes: a low, dull thud from up the rise. You check the timing, drop trim ballast, and rise twenty, thirty, forty meters until the turbidity sensors show you are above the thickest layer. Beneath you, over the next hours, the hydrophones fill with a gale's worth of grain-noise and a deep grinding like millstones. You hang in the dark above a storm you cannot see, feeling it only as a slight push against your thrusters.

When it eases, you descend. The floor is different. Where there was bare nodule there is fresh grey ooze, rippled in a direction that will be gone within the hour. Your nearest beacon is buried to its waist. Somewhere on the western swell, a pale octopus is dragging stones back into a ring.

Then you go home, carrying nothing but what you heard, and leaving the Fan exactly as you found it, the way its own storms always do.

## Checklist

- Pressure hull rated with margin to at least 6,000 m, recently inspected
- Arrival method that never opens an aperture to ambient pressure
- Arrival target a few meters above the floor, not on it
- Mechanically released drop-weights; breakaway skids and arms
- Low-wash, silt-guarded thrusters; hover-capable trim
- Oxygen, scrubbers, and power for mission plus 3+ days
- Cold-weather layers, seat insulation, flasks, condensation towels
- Low-angle, low-intensity lighting; ability to go fully dark
- Low-frequency sonar; force-feedback manipulators
- High-quality hydrophones and headphones
- Seismometer or hull geophone with an alarm, for the warning thud
- Rehearsed storm protocol, rise preferred
- Inertial navigation plus DVL; redundant acoustic beacons on tall stands
- Exit point marked by several independent systems
- Pressure-retaining sample chambers, if sampling at all
- Tethers on every tool
- Confinement training, a clock, a fixed schedule, a written abort rule
- Something small from home

Go carefully, and make sure you are one of the things that leaves.
