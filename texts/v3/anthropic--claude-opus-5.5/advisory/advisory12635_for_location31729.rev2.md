# Preparing for Hollin Bottom

Hollin Bottom is not dangerous the way a cliff or a war zone is. It is dangerous the way a working mill is: there is force in motion, heat, water under pressure, and a great deal you can't see. It also seems to be a single living organism with habits of its own, and you will be walking around inside it. Prepare for a small, hot, wet, loud, occupied place. And prepare for the fact that, emotionally, it will be much bigger than it is.

---

## 1. Before You Leave the Village

**Talk to the oldest people first.** This matters more than any equipment. The one serious unknown in the Bottom is the generational silence, and the villagers are the only record of it. Ask:

- When did the knock last stop? How long did the silence last?
- Has anyone noticed warning signs beforehand, such as the knock drifting in tempo, the steam changing colour, or the cable running hotter or colder?
- Has anyone gone in and not come back, or come back changed? Listen for the stories they tell sideways, and for the ones they won't tell.
- Is there anything locals don't do down there? Old places often gather rules that sound superstitious but encode real experience: don't sit on the pipes, don't take the red stones, don't stay past dusk.

If the last silence was thirty-five or more years ago, you are inside the window. Seriously consider postponing, or at least keep your visit short and stay near the edge.

**Tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back.** Give them a hard deadline. The Bottom is small, but it is packed, and a person who falls through the root mat into a void could be two meters from open air and completely invisible.

**Go with a companion if you can.** One of you might stay at the lip of the basin, where the noise is quieter and the air is cooler, as a fixed point to call to or return to.

**Ask about the cable.** Someone may know where it goes, or have a guess, or have been told as a child never to follow it. Whatever they say, take it seriously.

---

## 2. Timing Your Visit

- **Go in daylight, early.** The light inside is already green and dim. It gets much worse toward dusk, and you do not want to be feeling your way along wet lattice by torchlight with a beam engine knocking somewhere ahead of you.
- **Go on a cool day if you can.** The Bottom runs several degrees warmer than the pasture. On a warm day it will feel tropical. On a cold day the contrast is pleasant, although you will steam when you climb out.
- **Avoid heavy rain.** The pasture is already soft. Rain makes the approach a bog and the rim embankment slick, and it will make the whole Bottom even more saturated than usual.
- **Plan two to four hours at most.** That is enough to see the edge, the machinery, the engine house and the chimney, and to leave without becoming exhausted or disoriented.

---

## 3. Clothing

You are dressing for a hot glasshouse, a flooded cellar and a factory floor all at once. These needs conflict, so accept some compromises.

- **Footwear is the most important choice.** Wear tall waterproof boots, knee-height wellingtons or good waders-style boots, with aggressive tread. The root mat is half a meter of sponge that wells warm water with every step. Ordinary hiking boots will be soaked within minutes, and warm, wet feet held for hours invite blisters and maceration. Pack **spare socks** in a sealed bag.
- **Gaiters**, if you wear anything shorter.
- **Light, breathable, quick-drying layers.** Don't bother with a waterproof jacket inside, because condensation will soak you from within anyway. Synthetic or merino, long-sleeved (see skin, below), and loose.
- **Long trousers and sleeves**, despite the heat. You will be brushing against sap, resin, iron, rust and red-brown seep water. Some of it may irritate your skin, and some of it will certainly stain.
- **Work gloves** with good grip, preferably thin leather or rubber-palmed. You will need handholds on fused stems, pipes and brickwork. The gloves protect you from rust, sharp iron edges and whatever the sap is. Carry a spare pair, because they will get soaked.
- **A hat with a brim**, mostly to keep the constant drip out of your eyes.
- **Clothes you don't mind ruining.** Iron-rich water stains everything a permanent orange-brown, and the pine-and-hot-metal resin probably won't wash out.
- **A dry change of clothes left at the rim or with your companion.** Climbing back out soaked into a cool, wet pasture wind is how people get chilled.

## 4. Equipment

**Essential:**

- **Hearing protection.** Bring foam earplugs or, better, earmuffs you can lift on and off. The noise is mill-yard loud, and the engine house will be louder, with the knock hitting like a blow. Hours of that wear on you. Muffs are better than plugs here because they're easy to lift when you want to listen, and you will want to.
- **A walking pole or probe stick**, to test the mat ahead of you. You are walking on living lattice over buried rail, drowned settling tanks, a pump shaft and who knows what else. Probe before you trust any stretch that looks especially soft, dark or level. Level usually means something built underneath, and that can mean a hollow.
- **A short length of rope** and a way to anchor it. You're not climbing, but it's useful for descending into the engine house chamber, crossing a doubtful stretch, or hauling a companion out of a hole.
- **Headtorch** with fresh batteries, sealed against moisture. The canopy is low and the light is green and thin, and it is thinner still in the central chamber.
- **Drinking water, and plenty of it**, plus **electrolytes**. You will sweat heavily in that humidity without noticing, because nothing can evaporate off you. **Do not drink the Bottom's water.** It is warm, iron-laden, standing, and has been through something that behaves like a circulatory system. It may be merely unpleasant, or it may carry the usual stagnant-water risks (leptospirosis and the like), plus whatever the plant contributes.
- **A basic first-aid kit**, with antiseptic wipes for cuts. Any scratch from rusty iron in warm, wet, bacteria-friendly conditions needs cleaning promptly. Check that your tetanus protection is current *before* you travel.
- **Waterproof bags** for anything that must stay dry: phone, notebook, spare socks, snacks.

**Useful:**

- **A notebook and pencil**, not pen. Pencil writes on damp paper.
- **A watch with a second hand.** You will count the knocks anyway, so you might as well time them. Recording the interval is a genuinely useful small contribution. If anyone ever works out the long cycle, it will be from records like yours.
- **Anti-fog wipes** for glasses. Any glasses, camera lens or visor will fog on the way down and stay fogged.
- **A small towel.**

**What won't work well:**

- **Magnetic compasses.** Between the buried iron, the machinery and the live cable, don't trust one. The Bottom is small enough to navigate by sound anyway. The knock is the centre, the cable hum is east, and the warmth and coal smell are north toward the chimney.
- **Delicate electronics.** Condensation gets into everything. If you bring a camera, let it acclimatise in a sealed bag first and expect fogging. Near the cable, sensitive devices may behave oddly. Don't bring anything with a magnetic storage medium you care about.
- **Anything you'd hate to lose.** Drop something into the root mat and it's gone. The mat will close over it.

---

## 5. Preparing Your Body

- **Heat tolerance.** If you come from a cool climate, the humid closeness may hit harder than you expect. Pace yourself and rest often. Headache, dizziness or nausea mean it's time to climb out and cool down.
- **The hum.** The low tone near the bottom of hearing is almost certainly partly infrasound, felt in the teeth and the chest. Infrasound is known to cause unease, pressure in the ears, a vague sense of dread, even slight visual flicker in some people. **Know this in advance.** If you suddenly feel you are being watched or that something is wrong, part of that may be the frequency working on your inner ear rather than your instincts. Part of it may not be. Either way, it isn't a reason to panic, but it is a reason to take a break at the rim.
- **Respiratory sensitivity.** The air is heavy, wet and dense with organic life. If you have asthma or mould sensitivities, bring your inhaler and consider a light mask. Near the chimney, the steam smells of coal smoke even though nothing burns. Nobody knows what that smoke actually contains. **Do not linger downwind of the chimney or in any low, enclosed pocket near it.** If you get a headache, grow drowsy or feel confused there, move out immediately. Treat it as possible carbon monoxide or something like it until proven otherwise.
- **Claustrophobia.** It isn't cramped, but it is *full*. Nothing has space around it, and that includes you. People who are fine in caves can find this unsettling, because the walls are alive and they pulse. Decide before you go in how far you'll go, and give yourself permission to turn back.

---

## 6. Hazards Inside

**The engine.** This is the one to respect absolutely. The beam is a living limb a meter thick. It rises slowly, hangs trembling, then *falls*, and the pump rod slams into the shaft hard enough to shake the whole basin. Wood or iron, that is a machine delivering enormous force on a schedule.

- **Never stand under the beam, beside the pump rod, or near the shaft mouth.**
- **Never put hands, tools or anything else near the gear train**, where living wooden teeth mesh with iron ones. It turns slowly, but it will not stop for you.
- Watch at least five or six full cycles from a safe spot before you move anywhere in the chamber. Learn exactly what moves and where, and keep your distance from all of it.
- Remember that the *walls* contract too. Don't wedge yourself into a gap between ribs.

**The pipes.** They are warm and under pressure. Cracked joints have been bandaged by the plant, but not every crack may be sealed. Don't lean your weight on the pipes, don't sit on them, and don't disturb the resin collars.

**Hidden voids.** The rail line that dives under the moss and doesn't come back up, the settling tanks, and the pump shaft all tell you the Bottom is honeycombed underneath. Probe as you go. Keep to fused lattice that feels structural. Stay away from the dark settling-tank water. It's warm, still and deep, and the brick edges are slick with moss.

**Falling objects.** Nodules drop free when ripe. A fist-sized lump that is mostly iron, falling from a few meters, can hurt you. Look up occasionally, especially near the lower chamber walls where they cluster.

**The cable.** It is warm, buzzing and live with *something*. The description says people have touched it where it's bare and come to no harm, but you don't know what it carries or at what intensity, and the ground around it is saturated. Brief contact through a dry glove is probably the most you should allow yourself. Don't grip it, don't stand in pooled water while touching it, and don't try to cut, dig at or follow it underground. People with pacemakers or other implanted electronics should keep away from it entirely.

**Getting lost.** It seems impossible in a basin you could throw a stone across, but when every direction is the same wall of green and you can't see the sky, it happens. **Orient by sound**, as above. The knock is always the centre. Walk away from it and uphill, and you'll reach the embankment.

## 7. Conduct: Visiting Someone's Body

This is the part most people won't think about, and it matters most.

The Bottom behaves like one organism, and you are inside it. It maintains itself with care: it bandages cracks, grows replacement cogs and seals joints. Whether it's aware of you, nobody knows. Act as though it might be.

- **Don't cut it.** It's tempting to test the sap pressure, or to clear a way through. Don't. The plant has to spend resources healing every wound, and you have no idea what you're cutting into. That stem might be part of a vessel feeding the engine. The edge is the only place where cutting could possibly be excusable, and even there it's better not to.
- **Don't tamper with the machinery.** Don't tap the gauges, turn valves, pull at rivets or pry at wooden teeth. This is a working system that has been kept running for decades in a way nobody understands. Leave it running.
- **Don't take the nodules.** I know. They're beautiful, layered like pearls and heavy as ingots, and there are heaps of them. But they are the only thing the Bottom *makes*, and you don't know what they're for. There may also be a local rule about them. Ask the villagers first.
- **Don't eat anything.** Obviously.
- **Move gently.** Step, don't stomp. Rest your hands on things rather than grabbing them.
- **Don't disturb the heron** if you're lucky enough to see it. It seems to have a relationship with this place you won't understand, and it will leave without hurry if you give it room.
- **Leave the beetles alone.** Watch where you put your hands on the pipes, both to avoid crushing them and because they're a small, rare, strange species.

---

## 8. Biosecurity: This Is Serious for a Multiverse Traveller

You move between worlds, and that gives you responsibilities that an ordinary visitor doesn't have.

- **The hexagonal seeds.** In the Bottom they never take root, because there's no room. Somewhere with room, they might. A plant that consumes a whole landscape, absorbs machinery and fills every niche so completely that nothing else can live is not something to introduce into another world, even by accident. **Check every pocket, cuff, boot tread, bag seam and fold of clothing before you leave the area, and certainly before you cross between worlds.**
- **The beetles.** They cling magnetically to iron. Check any steel on your gear: buckles, tent pegs, tools, zips, the frame of your pack.
- **Spores and root fragments.** Brush your boots down at the rim. Better still, wash them in the pasture and dry them. Bag the clothes you wore inside, and wash them hot before you travel on.
- **Anything you absolutely must take out**, like a water sample, a photograph of a gauge or a fallen seed casing for study, should be sealed, documented and handled as you would handle a potentially invasive specimen. Better still, don't take anything.

---

## 9. The Silence

If the knock stops while you are inside:

**Leave.** Don't investigate, don't wait to see if it restarts, and don't go toward the engine house. Turn your back on the centre and walk out steadily, uphill, now.

You don't know what happens during the silence. Nobody does. You know that the plant *does something*: it adjusts its internal workings and replaces parts entirely. You know that the only people who have ever explained it chose not to go and find out. And you know that when the knock returns, it comes *without warning*. You do not want to be anywhere near a meter-thick beam, a pump shaft or a contracting ribcage of living wood when a system that has been reconfiguring itself for days starts back up.

If you happen to be visiting during a silence that has already begun, stay at the rim. Watch from the pasture. Note the time. Note whether the steam has stopped and whether the cable is cold. That observation will be worth more than anything you might find by going down.

---

## 10. What It Will Actually Be Like

Knowing this ahead of time will help.

It starts with the noise, before you even see the place clearly, and then the realisation that the noise is regular. The descent is a change in the air. It turns warm and thick, and your glasses fog, and within minutes you are wet through from sweat and condensation and have stopped caring.

Your footsteps squelch warm water. There is dripping everywhere, a thousand small pitches like rain on a roof, without rain. The walls you're moving between aren't walls, or aren't *only* walls. You'll put your hand on a trunk and feel a rivet. You'll lean on what you take for a log and find it's a lagged pipe, warm and hissing faintly under your palm.

Without deciding to, you will start counting. Forty seconds. The knock. The shiver through everything, and through you. Forty seconds. Within half an hour your breathing may drift toward the rhythm. That's normal. Let it happen, but notice it.

The engine house will stay with you. The first sight of that living beam rising, trembling at the top, then *falling* will affect you in a way that's hard to prepare for. It's like watching a heart that is also a machine, or a machine that has become a heart. Give yourself a few minutes to just look. Take the earmuffs off for one cycle and hear it properly. Then put them back on.

You may feel unease, or awe, or a strange sense of being tolerated. You may feel sad for the dozen men who built this and are long gone, while their work goes on without them. You may feel that you're intruding on something private. All of these are reasonable responses.

## 11. Leaving

The climb out is cooler than you expect. Stop at the rim, change into your dry clothes, drink water, and check yourself and your gear for seeds and beetles.

Then turn around and look. From up here it's a green hump with a thread of steam, and you could mistake it for a copse. Stand and listen for a while as the dripping fades out of hearing, then the hiss, until only the knock is left.

Walk back across the pasture slowly. Somewhere beneath your feet the cable runs off, warm and humming, toward wherever it goes. You'll want to follow it. Most people probably do.

Note that impulse, and save it for another trip, with another set of questions for the oldest people in the village.
