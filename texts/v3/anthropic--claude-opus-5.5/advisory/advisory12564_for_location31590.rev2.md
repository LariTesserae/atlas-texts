# Preparing for the Singing Saddle

## The One Thing to Understand

Almost everything in this guide follows from a single fact: **in the Saddle, sound becomes stone.**

This is not a metaphor you can safely forget once you arrive. Every noise you make in that bowl is a physical act with permanent consequences. A shout is a ridge. A dropped cooking pot is a scar. A laugh that gets away from you is a small crescent of rock that will outlast your grandchildren. Nothing you do there will hurt you as much as a fall on wet glass, but your voice is the one thing that will change the place.

So the most important preparation is not packing. It is **training yourself to be quiet in a way most people have never needed to be.**

The second fact to hold onto: **anything wet and still becomes part of the floor.** A skin forms in about an hour. A pebble is fused by noon. Your gear, your boots, and your body are not exempt.

Everything else is detail.

---

## Before You Go: Training the Body

Start weeks in advance. This matters more than any piece of equipment.

**Unlearn the yelp.** When you slip, stub a toe, or are startled, you almost certainly make a sound: a gasp, a curse, a sharp "ah!" You will slip in the Saddle. Every surface is wet, glassy, and new. Practise, deliberately and repeatedly, converting the startle into a silent exhale through the nose. Have a friend startle you. It sounds absurd, but the difference between a trained and an untrained visitor is a scatter of small permanent ridges wherever the untrained one lost their footing.

**Learn to whisper properly.** A true whisper is unvoiced breath, with no vocal cords. Most people's "whisper" has a voiced edge that carries. Practise holding a whole conversation in unvoiced breath, and practise hand signals with anyone you'll be travelling with.

**Walk softly.** Practise rolling heel-to-toe, placing your feet rather than dropping them. Test yourself on a hard floor in socks, then in the shoes you'll wear.

**Deal with the involuntary sounds.**
- *Snoring:* find out whether you snore. Ask a partner or record yourself. It matters less if you sleep outside the Saddle, as you should, but you need to know.
- *Coughing and sneezing:* do not go with a cold. If you have allergies, take antihistamines before you cross the line. Carry lozenges. Learn to sneeze into your elbow with your mouth closed, which muffles it.
- *Humming and whistling:* many people hum without noticing, especially when concentrating or content. Ask people who know you whether you do. The Saddle's pervasive tone invites you to hum along. Don't.

**Build fitness for a full day's climb over boulder fields**, followed by hours of slow, careful, tiring movement on slippery ground, followed by a descent. The Saddle is small, but you will be moving tensely the whole time.

---

## The Approach

The climb is ordinary mountain country. It is still mountain country, and should be treated with the usual seriousness.

- The granite is old, rounded, and lichen-covered. Lichen on wet rock is slick, and boulder fields are where ankles break. Take your time.
- Expect weather typical of high heather-and-granite uplands: cold, wind, sudden cloud, rain. Pack as you would for any exposed day in the hills: layers, a real waterproof, a hat, gloves, map and compass, food, and more water than you think.
- Bring **all the water you'll need, or know where the last clean stream is below the line.** You cannot use the Saddle's water. More on this below.
- If there are people living in the valley below, stop and talk with them before you climb. They know when the swifts arrive and leave, when storms come over the ridge, and what the silence after a storm feels like. They may have customs about the place. Listen to them. Bring something to give.

## Where to Sleep: Below the Line

**Make your base camp on the old granite, outside the Saddle.**

This is the single best decision you can make. Camp a sensible distance below the line. It creeps outward only a finger's width at a time, but give yourself room. Go into the Saddle for the day. Come out to eat, drink, rest, clean your gear, and sleep.

Reasons:
- You cannot sleep safely on the bowl's floor. Anything lying still on wet stone for hours will be skinned over. Your snoring, your sleep-talking, even a restless groan would be written into the rock around you.
- Outside, you can talk. You can cook on a hissing stove. You can laugh. After hours of enforced silence, this is psychologically important.
- Outside, you can dry things. Inside, nothing is ever dry.

It will be colder out on the granite than in the warm bowl, so pack a proper sleeping system for exposed upland nights. You will crawl out of the milk-warm fog into wind and heather, and the contrast will be sharp.

If you must stay inside for some reason, the only plausible place is the ledge at the top of the singing stairs. You'd have to be certain of your own silence, and it is still not recommended.

## Clothing: Dressing for a Place That Plasters You

You will be damp from the moment you cross the line until the moment you leave. The cloud condenses on everything, including you, and the water carries stone.

**Choose smooth, hard-faced fabrics.** A tightly woven, slick nylon shell sheds water and mineral best. Avoid fleece, wool, cotton, or anything fuzzy on the outside. Fibres hold water and film, and over an afternoon they will stiffen and crust. A fleece left in the Saddle for a day would come out as a board.

**Wear old gear.** Whatever you bring in will come out greyer, stiffer, and faintly scaled. Don't bring anything you love.

**Silence your clothing.** This is where most visitors fail.
- **No Velcro.** The ripping sound is exactly the harsh, tearing-cloth texture of the swifts' alarm call. Imagine what that could grow.
- Avoid rustling, crinkly fabrics like stiff hardshells and "crisp" waterproofs. Test by walking in them in a quiet room.
- Tape or pad metal zips, buckles, and snaps. Pull-tabs rattle; replace them with cord loops.
- Big-toothed plastic zips resist mineral fouling better than fine metal ones. Wax them before you go and after each day.

**Layer light.** The bowl is warm: blood-warm water, rock like a living flank. It is also saturated air with no wind. You will sweat inside your shell. Wear a thin synthetic base and a shell, and carry an insulating layer in a dry bag for when you come out into the cold.

**Clean everything each evening.** Rinse clothing, boots, and gear in clean water below the line before the film dries. A day's deposit rinses off. Three days' deposit might not.

---

## Footwear: The Most Important Gear Decision

You need three things that pull against each other: **grip on wet glass, quiet, and resistance to sharp edges.**

- **Sticky rubber soles**, like good canyoneering or approach shoes. The new stone is smooth and wet, with sharp edges nothing has worn down. Hard lugged mountain soles will skate on it and clatter.
- **No metal.** No crampons, microspikes, or hobnails. They would ring, scratch the fresh stone, and clatter with every step.
- **Tough uppers.** The glassy rock and the stone thorns will scuff and slice soft mesh. Leather or heavy synthetic uppers are best.
- **Gaiters**, to keep mineral-heavy water from filling your shoes.
- **Laces:** these will cement if left wet. Use flat laces you can rinse, or a lacing system that doesn't rely on small metal hooks.
- **Carry spare laces and a small tube of shoe adhesive.** The rock eats rubber.

**Never stand still in a rivulet.** The rivulets are warm and pleasant and you'll be tempted. But the deposition works on anything stationary. If you stop to watch something, step onto a raised, relatively dry rib of rock. Every few minutes, shift your feet. When you stand quietly at dawn to feel the stone swell under you, and you should, keep it short and lift your boots afterwards. Check the soles.

**Leave trekking poles behind**, or fit them with rubber tips. Carbide tips click on stone with every step. That's a dotted line of tiny marks across the floor behind you, and a tip planted in a rivulet will foul.

## Hands, Eyes, and Head

**Gloves.** Thin, grippy, abrasion-resistant work gloves. The new stone has sharp edges. The thorns on the eastern wall are fine as glass. The stone-lace spiderwebs shatter into shards. You will want to put your hands on things for balance, and that's fine with gloves on.

**Eye protection.** Wear clear glasses or light goggles for three reasons:
1. Swifts. Thousands of them, moving at speed through fog with forty paces of visibility, "invisible until they are suddenly all around you." Collisions are unlikely because swifts are superb fliers, but not impossible, and a bird to the eye is serious.
2. Falcons stooping through the flocks in the afternoon. A stooping falcon is one of the fastest things alive, and in fog it will not see you until late.
3. The warm drizzle near the masts is mineral-laden. You don't want it in your eyes.

**Anti-fog treatment and a cloth.** Everything fogs in there. Wipe lenses often. Mineral film builds up on glass, so rinse them with clean water from your bottle every hour or so rather than smearing them.

**Don't wear contact lenses.** A mineral film on a contact lens is not something to experiment with.

**A light helmet** is worth considering if you'll be anywhere near the walls, especially the eastern thorn thickets and especially at dawn, when "the walls creak and pop audibly as new layers set." Fresh ledges and thin spines could crack free.

**Hair and beards.** Anything that stays wet and relatively still will collect film. Tie long hair up and keep it under a hood or cap. Bearded visitors should rinse regularly. You don't want to find your beard gone stiff and grey by evening.

**Your skin.** Wipe your hands and face with a clean damp cloth periodically. Warm water carrying dissolved mineral may be mildly irritating over hours. Nobody knows. Check your skin at the end of the day, and wash properly below the line.

---

## Water and Food

**Do not drink the Saddle's water. Do not cook with it. Do not wash in it.**

This deserves emphasis because the rivulets are warm, clear-looking, and constantly underfoot, and after a long climb you will be thirsty. But this water turns pebbles into bumps in the floor overnight. It is saturated with stone. What that would do in a stomach, a kidney, or a bladder is not something to find out. Think of kidney stones and multiply.

- Carry in all your water for the day. Refill from clean streams below the line.
- If you want to test the water scientifically, bring pH strips and a small sealed sample vial. Don't taste it.

**Food.** Eat outside the line if you can. If you snack inside:
- Use quiet packaging. Cloth wraps, silicone pouches, or food pre-unwrapped in soft containers. The crackle of a foil wrapper is a surprisingly loud, textured sound.
- Choose soft food. Crunching an apple or a cracker in a silent bowl is louder than you'd think, though it's probably too close to your head to build much. Soft, chewy food is kinder.
- Don't drop anything. A crumb in a rivulet becomes a fossil. Pack out everything.

---

## Silencing Your Pack

Before you go, fully load your pack and walk around a quiet room. Jump a little. Listen.

- Pad and tape everything that touches anything else.
- Carabiners should be taped shut or stowed inside the pack. Two clipped carabiners chatter constantly.
- No bottles that slosh loudly. Fill them completely or use soft bladders.
- No bells, including bear bells. Yes, people forget.
- Stow the emergency whistle somewhere you can reach but won't blow by habit. It is for real emergencies only (see below).

**Never set your pack down on the floor.** Keep it on your back, or hang it briefly from something. A pack left in a rivulet for an hour will be glued down, and ripping it free will be loud and damaging.

---

## Electronics and Recording

**Silence every device.** Turn off phones, or set them to fully silent with no vibration, since vibration against stone is sound too. Disable every beep: cameras, GPS units, watches, alarms. A watch alarm going off at 6 a.m. on the floor of the Saddle will leave its little stutter in the rock forever.

**Never play sound aloud.** No speakers. No playing back recordings to check them. No music. You have no idea what a recording of the dawn chorus would build if played back in the wrong place. Worse, you might play something that isn't a swift's song at all.

**Recording is one of the best things you can do there.** A good quiet audio recorder with a windscreen, stashed in a waterproof housing, will capture the dawn roar, the midday hush, the ticking stone, the masts' tone, and the stairs' melody. It makes no sound. Use headphones only.

**Protect everything from the wet.**
- Dry bags, silica gel packs, and waterproof housings.
- Camera lenses will fog and film over. Use a lens hood, a clear protective filter you can rinse, and keep a clean cloth sealed in a bag.
- The light is pearl-grey and sourceless, so a fast lens or a small tripod helps. But a tripod's feet in a rivulet will foul within the hour, so keep sessions short or stand the legs on raised rock.

**No drones.** The whine is continuous and high-pitched. It would draw a line of stone wherever it flew, and in fog it would hit swifts.

**Compasses and GPS** may or may not behave. The masts "are not metal," but they are clearly doing something. Check your compass against a known bearing before you cross the line and again inside. Don't rely on either absolutely.

## Moving Through the Fog

The bowl is small, about two hundred paces across. You cannot get truly lost. You will be constantly disoriented, though, because distances beyond forty paces "go to rumour."

**Navigating:**
- **Use the hum.** The masts' tone is a pressure in the chest near them and a note farther away. With practice you can triangulate by feel.
- **Use the walls.** They are never more than about a hundred paces away. The eastern wall bristles with thorns, and the western wall is smooth and terraced. If you know which one you're touching, you know where you are.
- **Use the floor.** Long low swells run concentric around the masts, overlaid by finer ripples. Following a swell is like following a contour line.
- **Don't build cairns or leave markers.** Anything you set down becomes permanent. A cairn would be swallowed into a lump within a day and remain as an odd, unexplained bump in the geological record. Leave nothing.

**Travelling with others:**
- Stay within sight. In this fog that means close, within twenty paces.
- Agree on hand signals in advance: *stop, come here, look, danger, I'm going out, I'm fine.* Make them large and slow so they read in poor light.
- A dim red headlamp or a small light worn on the back of the pack helps partners track each other in the grey without blinding anyone. Lights make no sound.
- Don't use a rope between you. Trailing on the floor, it will be cemented in rivulets.

## The Daily Rhythm: Planning Your Day

The Saddle runs on a schedule set by birds. Plan around it.

**Dawn: the chorus.** An enormous, high, sawing roar fills the bowl. Stone grows fastest in the half-hour after it. Steam rises, rivulets run milky, and walls pop and creak.
- **Bring earplugs**, or at least have them ready. The sound may be genuinely painful up close, and long exposure matters. Flat-response musician's plugs let you hear the texture while taking the edge off.
- **Stay away from the walls.** This is when fresh rock is setting and may crack.
- **Stay out of the rivulets.** Deposition is at its peak.
- This is the time for the experience the place invites: standing still, mouth closed, feeling the floor swell under your boots. Do it on a raised rib, briefly, and then move.
- If you camp below the line, you'll need to cross in before dawn, in the dark, on wet glass, with a dim light. Do this only once you've walked the route in daylight.

**Morning: settling.** The chorus fades. The stone is soft near the masts and firming elsewhere. Good light (relatively speaking) for looking at the floor patterns.

**Midday: the hush.** The swifts rest. You'll hear the ticking of the stone, the drip and trickle of a thousand rivulets, and the masts' low tone underneath. **This is the best time for a first visit.** Cross the line at midday on your first day. It is the gentlest introduction and gives your nervous system a chance to adapt before the noise.

**Afternoon: the hunt.** Falcons come over the rim, especially the eastern rim. The swifts' alarm call is a harsh tearing-cloth sound from hundreds of throats.
- Keep eye protection on. Stay away from the eastern wall's thorn thickets. That's where the falcons come in, and it's where the needle-thin spines are.
- Don't try to watch a stoop from directly beneath the flock.
- Don't make any sound resembling the alarm call. You might confuse the colony.

**Dusk: the moths and the ledge-trill.** Pale moths settle in furred patches on the warm walls. The swifts feast. Colonies trill in chorus, and ledges grow beneath them overnight.
- It is beautiful and worth seeing. Watch the western wall, where the terraces are.
- Leave while there's still enough light to cross the line safely. Plan your exit for dusk, not after.

**Night:** Unknown and unrecommended. Be on the old granite.

## The Masts

The masts are the least understood thing in the Saddle, and the most important to treat carefully.

**Their hum is infrasound near the base**, "a pressure in the sternum and the back teeth." Low-frequency sound at intensity can cause:
- Nausea and motion-sickness feelings
- Unease, dread, or a sense of being watched
- Pressure in the ears and sinuses
- Visual strangeness at the edges of sight
- Difficulty concentrating

Know that these feelings may come from the sound and not from your own mind. If you feel sudden anxiety near a mast, step back. It usually eases with distance.

**If you have a pacemaker, other implanted device, inner-ear disorder, severe migraines, or are pregnant**, be especially cautious about approaching the bases. Nobody knows how the field behaves.

**Don't linger at the bases.** Growth is fastest there. The fluted skirts are "still soft enough to take a thumbprint if you are quick," which means soft enough to take your boot, and to set around it. The warm drizzle is heaviest there. Take a look and leave.

**Consider a mask or buff over your mouth and nose near the masts.** You'll be breathing warm mist that is actively depositing stone. A day of it probably doesn't matter. Nobody has checked, and a damp cloth filter costs you nothing.

**Don't touch them.** They are seamless, oily-dark, and iridescent. They are not metal and not stone, and no one knows what they are. They may be perfectly inert to a touch. They may not be. There is no reason to find out with your hand.

**Don't climb them.** They narrow to thread and to nothing. There is no top.

---

## The Living Things

**Swifts.** Thousands of them, nesting in the walls. Real swifts are migratory. Ask the valley people when they're present and when they're breeding.
- Stay off the western wall's terraces and away from nesting cavities, especially in breeding season.
- Don't touch the ferns lining their nests.
- Remember that they built this place, and that they're still building it. You're walking through their home and their work.

**Falcons.** Hunters. Give them room. Don't approach a falcon on a kill.

**Salamanders.** Finger-long, black, speckled with mineral, grazing the fresh flowstone. **Don't handle them.** Many salamanders have toxic skin secretions, and these ones eat stone film. You don't know what's on them. Also, the oils and salts on your hands may harm them. Watch, don't touch.

**Moths.** The furred patches on warm walls at dusk. Leave them be. They're swift food.

**Spiders and their stone lace.** The oldest webs are mineralised, "brittle and beautiful," and shatter "with a sound like a dropped wineglass." Don't brush against them, for three reasons:
1. The shards are glass-sharp.
2. You destroy something that took a very long time to form.
3. The sound of it shattering, a bright crystalline smash, will be written into the stone.
Look carefully before reaching toward the thorn thickets. The webs are hard to see in the grey.

**Snails.** Translucent, slow, on the damp floor. Watch where you put your feet.

**Moss.** Electric green, in the wettest rivulets. The brightest colour in the Saddle, and the most slippery surface. Don't step on it: it's precious, and you'll fall.

**The silver fern.** The only fern here, gripping new rock directly on the western wall. Don't pick it. There may not be much of it, and the swifts need it.

## The Hut and the Slab

This is where you will most want to do something, and where you should be most restrained.

**The hut.** The doorway is swallowed. One window remains, "a small square of wavy old glass."
- **Don't break the window.** The sound of breaking glass would be written into the stone around the hut. You would also let the fog in, and whatever is inside — the table, the chair, the chalked grid on the wall — would begin to be buried.
- Look through it. Bring a small torch and shine it in through the glass to see the chalk markings. Photograph them if you can. The grid on the wall may be a key to the slab outside.

**The slab.** Notation carved into polished stone, partly softened by flowstone. Along one edge is a later hand's transcription of the swifts' ledge-trill.
- **Bring a waterproof notebook and pencil** to copy it. Pens fail in the wet, and pencil on waterproof paper works in anything. Photograph it too, from several angles, because raking light from a torch will show carved marks that flat grey light hides.
- Don't trace the marks with your fingers. The flowstone is slowly filling them. Your skin oils won't help.
- **Do not read the notation aloud. Do not sing it. Do not hum it. Not a single phrase.**

This is the most important rule in this guide after "don't drink the water." The people who made that slab came to tune a mountain. They meant to sing something specific into being, "a shelter or a tower or a road." They left, or stopped, or were overtaken. You don't know which. You don't know what a fragment of their score does in that bowl, sung near their masts. You don't know what they were making, or whether it was finished for a reason. A human voice, singing their notation, might be the thing that place has been waiting for.

Take the notation home. Study it somewhere safe, somewhere the stone doesn't listen.

The same goes for the transcription of the swifts' ledge-trill. A human singing it in the Saddle might grow ledges wherever they stood. That's harmless in theory, but it's a permanent human intrusion into the swifts' architecture.

---

## The Singing Stairs

On the southern wall, the steps are "sung into existence, one note per stair." They are uneven, each riser a different height.

- **Walk them.** This is one of the few things in the Saddle you're invited to do. Your footfalls ring and trace the melody. It's the one place where your sound is meant to be heard.
- Go slowly. The uneven risers will catch you out, and they're wet. Use your hands.
- Walk them in order to hear the melody properly.
- The ledge at the top gives the view, on the rare clear days. If the cloud ever lifts during your visit, that's where to be.
- Resist the urge to sing the melody back. It is tempting to hum what the steps are telling you. Don't. You'd be adding to someone else's composition without knowing its shape.

---

## Storms: Leave

**When weather comes over the ridge in earnest, get out of the Saddle and off the col.**

Consider what the Saddle is: a mountain col, the high point between summits, with five tall thin spires standing in a bowl. Those are exactly the conditions under which lightning kills people. The masts "flare with a sheen" when thunder strikes. They may draw lightning. They may channel it. Ground currents spreading across wet stone from a strike near a mast could be lethal well away from the mast itself.

Then there's the thunder. It builds walls. "Whole new ribs of stone sweep across the floor in great curving bands," knee-high and steaming within hours. Nobody knows what happens to a person standing in the path of a thunder-roll as it rings around the bowl, and you shouldn't be the first to find out.

- **Check the weather before every crossing.** Ask the valley people how storms build here.
- At the first sign — darkening from the west, rumbling, static on your skin or hair, a change in the masts' sheen — **leave immediately.**
- Descend off the col to lower ground. Don't shelter by the hut, near a mast, or on the stairs' ledge.
- Know your route out in advance. You don't want to be picking a way down a granite boulder field in a thunderstorm in the dark.

**After a storm:**
- The rivulets run "thick as milk." Deposition is at its most intense. Stay out of them entirely.
- The new ribs are steaming, soft, and growing. Don't climb on them.
- The birds are silent for a full day. This is extraordinary and may be worth experiencing, the Saddle without its voices. But watch where you step, and feel how strange it is.
- Stay well clear of the old storm-rib near the western edge "that the swifts still remember." They avoid its shadow. So should you. They know something.

## Emergencies

**If there is a genuine emergency — an injury, a fall, someone missing — make whatever noise you need to.**

Blow your whistle. Shout. The ridge you leave behind is a small, sad thing compared to a life. The rest of this guide asks you to value the place. It does not ask you to value it above a person.

Otherwise:
- Carry a proper first aid kit, with emphasis on cuts (the glass-sharp stone and thorns), sprains, and eye injuries.
- Carry a way to call for help from outside the line, and know where signal exists, if anywhere.
- If someone falls into a rivulet and is hurt and can't move, **get them out of the water.** Don't leave an immobile person lying on wet stone for hours.
- If someone's foot or gear becomes stuck in soft flowstone near a mast base, pull free sooner rather than later, and slowly. Don't wait for it to harden.

## What You'll Leave Behind

Every visitor leaves something in the Saddle. A careful visitor leaves only the faintest traces: the dragged scuff of a slip, a whisper that settled somewhere, a few footfalls.

This is a place where the "leave no trace" principle takes on new meaning. **Here, leave no trace means leave no sound.**

- Don't take stone samples. Breaking off a piece is loud, and it damages a record that is still being written. If you find a small fallen shard of thorn or lace, loose on the ground, consider whether you really need it.
- Don't carve or scratch anything. Someone already added to the slab. That was their choice to make; it isn't yours.
- Don't sing, even quietly, even beautifully. Especially not beautifully.
- Pack out every crumb, wrapper, and hair tie.

There's an argument for leaving one deliberate mark: a single whispered word, soft and considered, a crescent of stone that says *someone came and was careful.* That's for you to decide. If you do, choose the word before you arrive, and choose it knowing it will outlast you.

## What It Will Actually Feel Like

Preparation isn't only equipment. Here is what to expect, so it doesn't knock you over.

**The crossing will be startling.** One step you're on ancient, soft-weathered granite, and the next you're on rock that's warm like a living thing and ticking faintly under your palm. Most people stop and stare at that line for a long time. Let yourself.

**The light will disorient you.** Pearl-grey and sourceless, with no real shadows, and a sun you can look at. You'll lose your sense of time of day. Carry a watch and check it.

**The noise will be relentless**, and then suddenly not. The dawn roar is overwhelming. The afternoon alarms are harsh and anxious. The midday hush is so quiet you'll hear the stone and the water. Your nervous system will be pulled back and forth all day. Expect to be more tired than the physical effort accounts for.

**The silence you're keeping will weigh on you.** Not being able to speak, exclaim, or laugh at something wonderful is hard. You'll find yourself bursting to say "look at that." Your companion will be too. Some people find it meditative. Some find it claustrophobic. When you come out below the line, you may find yourself talking more than usual, or much less.

**The hum will get into you.** That pressure in the chest and teeth doesn't feel like sound. It feels like a mood. You may feel uneasy, watched, or strangely tender, and you may not know why. It isn't you; it's the masts.

**Everything will be wet, and warm, and moving.** Rivulets under your feet. Film on your sleeves. Steam at dawn. Birds appearing and vanishing. Nothing sits still, nothing is dry, and nothing is finished. After a day, the ordinary still, dry, cold world below the line will feel oddly dead.

**You will feel recorded.** That's the deepest strangeness. In most places, your passage leaves no mark and your voice vanishes into air. Here, everything you do is being written, slowly, in stone. Some people find this frightening. Some find it moving. Most, eventually, find both.

## Packing List

**For the approach and base camp (outside the line):**
- Full mountain kit: layers, waterproof, hat, gloves, map, compass
- Tent and warm sleeping system for exposed upland nights
- Stove and fuel (use outside only)
- All water for your stay, or knowledge of clean streams below the line
- Water for rinsing gear each evening
- Gifts for the valley people

**For crossing into the Saddle:**
- Smooth-faced, quiet, synthetic shell and light base layer
- Insulating layer in a dry bag
- Sticky-rubber, tough-uppered shoes; gaiters
- Thin grippy work gloves
- Clear glasses or goggles, anti-fog treatment, lens cloth in a sealed bag
- Light helmet (optional, recommended near walls)
- Cap or hood; hair ties
- Buff or cloth mask for near the masts
- Earplugs (flat-response if possible)
- Dim red headlamp; small torch for the hut window and slab
- Waterproof notebook and pencil
- Quiet audio recorder, waterproof housing, headphones
- Camera with protective filter, lens hood, and dry bag
- Silica gel packs
- Day's water in full bottles or soft bladders
- Quiet, soft food in quiet packaging
- Lozenges; antihistamines
- First aid kit, especially for cuts and eye injuries
- Emergency whistle, for emergencies only
- pH strips and sealed sample vial (optional)
- Spare laces, shoe adhesive, tape for silencing gear

**Leave behind:**
- Velcro, bells, speakers, drones
- Crampons, microspikes, and metal-tipped poles
- Crinkly packaging
- Contact lenses
- Anything you love
- Any song you might be tempted to sing

## A Final Word

The Saddle asks one thing of you: attention. Pay attention to where you put your feet, to what your body does without your permission, and to what you want to say and can't.

If you've prepared well, you'll come out with sore legs, grey-crusted boots, a notebook of copied marks, a recording of the loudest dawn you've ever heard, and a strange new respect for your own voice.

And if you stood still at dawn, mouth closed, and felt the floor swell under your boots, you'll carry that for the rest of your life. You stood on a song that wasn't finished, and you were careful not to sing.
