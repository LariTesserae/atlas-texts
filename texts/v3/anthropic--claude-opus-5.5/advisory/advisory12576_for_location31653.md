# Preparing for the Seaward City

## What Kind of Trip This Is

This is not a dangerous place in the way a war zone or a volcano is dangerous. Nothing here hunts you, and the few people you meet will almost certainly leave you alone. The risks are quieter. The ground is made of something close to glass. Your eyes will be lied to all day. There is no running water. The whole region is shaped by a pull that affects light and water but not you. Most travelers who get into trouble here do it one of two ways. They cut themselves on a seam they didn't see, or they walk toward a stay they thought was close, run out of daylight and water, and sit down on warm stone in the dark with no idea how far they've come.

Prepare for sharp, dim, damp, disorienting, and lonely. If you do, this is one of the most beautiful and restful places you could visit.

---

## 1. Your Feet and Hands: The Ground Is Glass

Treat the lay as a landscape of fused bottle glass that hasn't had time to wear down. Every seam is a broken edge, and most of those edges are hidden under moss, ferns, and creepers. The greenest, softest-looking line on a plate is exactly where the knife is.

**Footwear**
- Stiff, thick-soled boots with a puncture-resistant midplate. Soft trail runners will get sliced along the sides where your foot rolls into a seam.
- Soft, sticky rubber in the tread. Faceted glass-stone beaded with dew is slick in a way ordinary rock isn't. The fine crystalline grain may even give it a direction, grippy one way and slippery the other. Test it on a flat plate before you trust it on a slope.
- Gaiters or high ankles. You will step into seams by accident, and the edges will catch your shins and ankles.
- A second pair. Your first pair will be damp for most of the trip.

**Hands**
- Cut-resistant gloves are the single most important item you can bring. You will want to touch everything here: the carved writing, the seams, the root-flares of the stays, the arcade walls. Instinct says to run your palm along the stone. Don't do it bare-handed.
- Wear them whenever you climb a terrace, steady yourself on a wall, or kneel at a seam to look at the snails.

**Knees and falls**
- Falling on this ground is falling onto a floor of broken tile. Consider light knee pads if you plan to crouch at seams or climb, and a pair of trekking poles with rubber tips (not carbide; they'll skate).
- Walk plates rather than seams. Cross seams deliberately and perpendicularly, with a long step. Never walk *along* one.

**Medical kit additions**
- Wound irrigation (a syringe and clean water), antiseptic, butterfly closures or steri-strips, and liquid skin adhesive. Glass cuts are clean, deep, and bleed a lot.
- Fine tweezers and a magnifier. The edges may shed slivers.
- Antifungal powder and cream (see *Damp* below).

## 2. Your Eyes and Your Balance: The Sky Is Wrong

Full noon here is dusk anywhere else, and the sun is smeared into a bar or split in two. Travelers underestimate how tiring this is. The problem isn't brightness. It's that your visual system keeps trying to solve a sky that won't solve.

**Expect**
- Eye strain and headaches, especially in the first two or three days.
- Something like motion sickness or seasickness when you look at the horizon, which curls up at its edges into a shallow bowl. Your inner ear says "flat," your eyes say "bowl," and your stomach takes sides.
- Depth perception you cannot trust past a few hundred meters.

**Bring**
- A hat with a wide, dark-lined brim. Its job isn't sun protection. It cuts the sky out of your peripheral vision so you can look at the ground and at what's near you, which is where the sense is.
- Motion-sickness medication (meclizine or similar) and ordinary painkillers for the first days.
- Nothing that makes the light dimmer. Leave sunglasses behind, or bring only a very light pair for the orange-glowing seaward edge.

**Practice**
- Look down and near. Look up rarely and on purpose, and when you do, stop walking.
- Let your eyes adapt. The dim world is full of color that only appears after twenty or thirty minutes without bright light: grey-green ferns with silver undersides, green-gold haze, the faint thread of light in the avenues. Every time you look at a screen or a bright headlamp, you throw that adaptation away.
- Sunlight probably won't burn you here, but don't assume. The lensing might occasionally focus it. A little sunscreen on your face costs nothing.

## 3. Navigation: Stop Believing Distances

This is the most important section.

Near the stays, light bends like water around a post. Everything that works by line of sight will mislead you: your eyes, binoculars, cameras, laser rangefinders. Radio is also light, so assume radio links may bend, fade, or behave strangely near the columns. There is no GPS. A compass may work, or may not; don't stake your trip on it.

A stay that looks an hour away may take half a day. One that looks far may appear around a corner. The residents navigate by counting seams, and you should too.

**Tools**
- **A tally counter** (the clicker kind), or two. Count seams crossed along a route. Plates vary in size, but seams are real, and the lensing can't move them.
- **A notebook and pencil**, not a phone. Write down routes as sequences: *pale avenue, 214 seams, stepped hall on left, bridge, turn toward nearer stay, 60 seams to wet ring.* You are building a map that can't be lied to.
- **A watch.** There's no real sunrise, and the light barely changes all day. Your body will lose track of time within a day or two.
- **Chalk or removable markers.** Only if the residents don't object. See *Etiquette*.

**Natural compasses**

The place is full of reliable direction signals once you know how to read them:
- **Water points to the nearest stay.** Pour a little water on a flat plate and watch which way the drops creep. Morning dew does it for you.
- **Shadows lean toward the stays**, not away from the sun. A stick stood upright is a stay-pointer.
- **Fog flows inland.** The afternoon sea-fog runs along the avenues from the west toward the east, so its direction of flow points you toward the forest.
- **Plate age tells you east from west.** Toward the sea (west), the plates are younger, sharper, and barer, with thin moss, and near the edge the seams are still warm or glowing. Toward the forest (east), the plates are older, the moss ridges are thicker, the seam-figs are larger, and eventually the stone begins to craze. If you're lost, look at how old the ground is.
- **The avenues run straight.** Their pale channels are the most honest lines in the city. At night their centerline threads glow. If you're on an avenue, stay on it.
- **The grain of the lay.** The fine parallel lines in the stone all run one way on a given plate. They may prove consistent across a district. Check this early; if they are, it's a free compass.

**Rules**
- Plan every walk for at least **three times** your estimated duration, and carry water and food for it.
- Turn back at a fixed clock time, not when things "look closer."
- On your first day, walk a known route out and back, counting seams both ways, and compare how long it took with how far it looked. Relearn your eyes.

## 4. Water: Damp but Not Wet

There are no rivers, streams, or springs. Water arrives as dew, fog, and brief rain, and then leaves: it creeps toward the stays and runs into the seams. You could be surrounded by moisture and still be thirsty.

**Carry**
- Enough water for your first full day and night.
- A filter plus a chemical or UV purifier. Every water source here has been sitting in moss, snail trails, and fig roots, or standing in black pools at the stay bases.

**Harvest**
- **Dew is your best source.** At the grey "dawn," every plate is beaded thickly with condensation that begins creeping almost immediately. Lay out a clean, absorbent cloth or a sheet of plastic on a bare plate before you sleep, and wring or funnel it in the morning. A small collapsible basin helps. Work fast; by midday the plates are dry.
- **Rain** comes every few days in heavy, warm bursts. A tarp rigged as a catchment will fill bottles in minutes.
- **Stay-base pools** have the most water and the worst quality. Filter and treat. Try not to disturb the waders, the plants, or whatever grazes there before dawn.

**Watch your containers**

Test this on arrival: leave a small open cup of water on a plate and see whether it drifts or leans. If open water migrates, then spills, sweat on your gear, and wet clothes left out may all wick or drip toward the stays more than you expect. Keep water sealed and gear packed.

**Flash flow**

When rain lands, it turns sideways and runs toward the stays in sheets, and the seams become channels. Never camp or leave gear in a seam, a sunken plaza, a half-sunk hall, or the wet ring around a stay.

## 5. Climate, Clothes, and Damp

It's warm, humid, and dim, with warm nights. The ground itself holds heat like a loaf of bread.

**Clothing**
- Light, quick-drying, long-sleeved and long-legged clothing. The long sleeves and legs are for protection from the stone, not from cold.
- A light rain shell. The rain is warm, so this is mostly for keeping gear dry.
- Clothes won't fully dry. Bring spare socks, lots of them, in sealed bags. Rotate them daily and dry your feet every evening.
- Antifungal powder for feet, groin, and anywhere cloth stays damp. Warm, humid, and dim is exactly how skin infections start.

**Keeping gear dry**
- Dry bags for everything: electronics, notebook, spare clothes, medical kit.
- Expect lenses to fog in the afternoon and mornings to leave everything beaded.

## 6. Where to Sleep

The best places are **high, bare, and away from stays**: the top of a terrace, a raised plate, or perhaps the span of one of those bridges that arch over empty stone. (They "may be anticipating a river." On a visit of days or weeks, the river is not coming tonight.)

**Good**
- A clean, unbroken plate, slightly raised, a comfortable distance from any seam, with the sharp edges in view so you don't roll into one.
- The stone's warmth rising through a thin sleeping pad. It's a real comfort here.

**Bad**
- Seams, sunken places, and stay-base wet rings. These are where the water goes, the larger animal grazes, and the lantern-flowers bloom.
- Directly beside a stay. The deep pressure from the columns, felt in the chest, is likely infrasound. Long exposure to strong infrasound in our own world causes unease, nausea, poor sleep, and a vague sense of dread. Visit the bases, but don't sleep at them.
- Arcades. They look like shelter but mostly shelter air. The wind moans through them on one note all night, and the floor may carry sheet water in rain.
- Any plate that is cracked. The city absorbs broken plates back into itself over weeks. That's slow, but nobody can tell you what it does to objects left lying there. Don't be one of them.

**Sleep aids**
- Earplugs. The stone ticks at random intervals all night, like a clock with no rhythm, and many people find this harder to sleep through than steady noise. The arcade moan carries far.
- An eye mask is unnecessary. The nights are dark and the days are dim, and your circadian rhythm will be the problem, not light. Keep to your watch, eat and sleep at consistent times, and consider bringing a small bright daylight lamp for ten minutes each "morning" if you're staying more than a week and are prone to low moods.

## 7. Light You Bring

- A headlamp with a **red or amber** mode, which you'll use almost exclusively. Red keeps your dark adaptation, and amber matches the city's own windows. White light is for emergencies and first aid.
- Keep lights low around the wet rings at night. The lantern-flowers open only in the dimmest hour, and the snails and moths live by faint light. A traveler with a white beam can blind an entire necklace of lanterns and everything feeding among them.
- Solar chargers will struggle here. Bring power banks for the whole trip, and use your electronics sparingly.

## 8. The Seaward Edge

This is the most spectacular part of the city and the one with real physical hazards.

- **Visit by day, from a distance.** The new ground rises mostly at night. You'll know it's coming when the sea goes flat, oily, and then pale. Hot stone is meeting water, and that means steam, possibly scalding. It may also mean gases; assume the fumes are not good to breathe.
- **Orange seams mean hot, and so may grey ones.** Fresh plates keep a glow for days, and "faded to grey" doesn't mean cool. Test with the back of a gloved hand held close before you step on any plate within sight of the water.
- **The maps are always wrong here.** New plates and new stays appear, so don't rely on routes more than a few weeks old.
- **Don't swim** near the edge. It's warm, possibly hot, uncertain, and the stone is still being made underneath.
- **Hear it instead of seeing it.** You don't need to stand at the edge at night to experience the laying of land. From anywhere in the lowland, on some nights, comes a single long, soft exhalation, like a sleeper breathing out. Listen from a safe plate far inland.

---

## 9. The Inland Edge

Here the city lets go. The stays stand slack, the stone crazes into gravel, the seams become gullies, and the jungle takes over.

- **This may be your relief zone.** Without active stays, the light should bend less, and distances may start telling the truth again. If the lensing is making you ill, an afternoon on the inland side, looking at a normal horizon, might reset you.
- **The ground itself is the new hazard.** Crazed stone and gravel over glass-sharp rubble, with gullies hidden under vegetation, is the kind of ground where ankles break. Use poles, step deliberately, and don't walk it at dusk.
- **It's a different ecosystem.** Dense, warm, dripping lowland jungle almost certainly has more insects, more animals, more leaves you shouldn't touch, and more things in the water. It's the one part of the region where insect repellent, a head net, and regular leech and tick checks might matter. Treat it as a separate trip with its own risks.
- **Seen from the city**, the forest looks like a green wave about to break, curled upward by the lensing. That's a good view. The jungle itself is for travelers with jungle experience.

## 10. Plants and Animals

**Plants**
- Eat nothing. Bring all your food. Nothing here has been tested on human bodies. The fleshy spiral vines in particular look edible in the way many poisonous things do.
- Don't cut, pull, or pry. The city has included the plants in its plans: arcades frame particular seam-figs, and broken plates are absorbed around the trees. A traveler hacking at vines is interfering with an ongoing design.
- The spiral vines always wind counterclockwise, seen from below. Look for yourself; it's one of the quiet marvels here. But don't try to make one turn the other way. Gardeners here have tried for a long time.
