# Preparing for the Stokemoor

## A Field Guide for the Ordinary Traveler

The Stokemoor is not a hostile place in the way a volcano or a battlefield is hostile. Most of it is not trying to kill you. It is simply built for creatures that are not you. Everything there assumes you can hear better than you do, feel heat through your skin, sense current in a cable, and live forever inside a warm, wet breath. You can do none of these things well, and you will be blind.

The most useful thing to understand before you go is this: **your biggest danger is not the quietcat. It is the air.** The second is your own disorientation. The quietcat comes third, but it is the one you will think about most, so it gets a long section too.

## Part One: The Thing Most Likely to Kill You Is the Warmth

The description says the moor is "warm the way the inside of a mouth is warm" and "wet the way breath on glass is wet." Take that literally for a moment. The inside of a mouth is about 36–37°C, and breath on glass means the air is saturated, near 100% humidity.

In air like that, sweat does not evaporate. It just runs off you. Your body's only real cooling system stops working. Physiologists measure this with the **wet-bulb temperature**, and the widely cited limit of human survivability is around 35°C wet-bulb. Healthy, resting, shaded people exposed to it for several hours overheat and die. Recent research suggests the practical limit is lower, closer to 31°C for many people, especially anyone exerting themselves.

Pushing through dense undergrowth is exertion. So if the Stokemoor really is mouth-warm and saturated, you cannot simply walk across it. You would cook slowly from the inside.

It may not be that extreme everywhere. Descriptions of alien places tend toward poetry. But prepare as though it is, and verify on arrival.

### Before you go
- **Heat-acclimatize** for two to three weeks in a humid heat chamber or a tropical climate. This does not raise your wet-bulb limit much, but it improves your efficiency, blood volume, and tolerance a great deal.
- **Get a medical screening.** Heart conditions, blood pressure medications, diuretics, some antidepressants, and antihistamines all impair heat tolerance. Talk to a doctor about every drug you take.
- **Learn the signs of heat illness by feel.** You will not be able to see a companion's flushed face or glassy eyes. Learn instead: hot dry skin in a place where everyone should be soaked, confusion, slurred speech, stumbling, irritability, a racing pulse you can feel at the wrist or neck.

### What to bring
- **An audio-output wet-bulb or heat-index meter.** A screen will be useless (see Part Two), so it must speak or beep its readings. Bring two.
- **A cooling strategy that doesn't rely on evaporation.** Phase-change cooling vests work only until they melt, and there may be nothing cold to recharge them. Look instead for the cool places:
  - **The plateau's edges near the waterfalls** will probably be the coolest ground. Falling water drives air movement, and air coming up from the lower world may be cooler and drier. This is your likely entry and exit zone, and your retreat.
  - **High ridges**, away from the buried steam mains.
  - **Running water**, if any of it is cooler than your skin. Immersion is the most powerful cooling there is. But see the eels.
- **Electrolytes in quantity.** You will sweat enormously and gain nothing from it. Bring salts and a real rehydration plan. Hyponatremia (too much water, too little salt) is a real risk.

### On arrival
Before doing anything else, sit at the plateau's edge and measure. Spend an hour there. Check your pulse and your core temperature (bring an audio-readout thermometer). If your core temperature is climbing while you sit still, the interior of the moor is not survivable for you, and your visit is going to be a series of short forays from the edge. That is fine. Plenty of the moor's magic is audible from the edge.

## Part Two: You Will Be Totally Blind

"Nothing here is visible. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it."

Fire burns dark. Lightning is only thunder and a blast of heat. The implication is that light itself doesn't work here, or doesn't reach your eyes. **Assume none of your light sources will function.** Test them on arrival out of curiosity: a flashlight, a chemical lightstick, your phone screen. But build your whole plan around total darkness. That includes every screen on every device you own. A GPS with a display is a brick. So is a camera.

This is not the darkness of a bedroom at night, where your eyes adapt and shapes emerge. It is absolute, and it does not end.

### What absolute darkness does to a person
- **Your brain will start inventing images.** In total, prolonged darkness most people begin to see phantom lights, colors, shapes, even faces. Miners and cave explorers call it "the prisoner's cinema." It is normal. It is not a quietcat. Know this in advance so you don't panic.
- **Your sense of time will disintegrate.** With no day and night, your circadian rhythm will drift. You'll sleep at odd times, wake confused, and lose track of how long you've been there. Cave-isolation experiments showed people drifting toward 25-hour or even 48-hour cycles without knowing it.
- **Your balance will be worse.** Vision is a large part of how you stay upright. On uneven, wet, rounded, slippery ground that gently rises and falls every forty-one seconds, you will fall a lot.
- **You may feel intense claustrophobia**, a sense of being pressed in. The lid overhead thunders, the leaves touch you like skin, and you cannot see open space. Some people find this unbearable within hours. Know whether you are one of them before you go.

### Training (this is the most important preparation you can do)
- **Take orientation and mobility training from blind instructors.** This is a real discipline, taught to people who lose their sight. Spend weeks with it: long-cane technique, trailing walls, protecting your head and body with your arms, crossing open ground, finding your way back.
- **Live blindfolded for at least a week, ideally two**, in a place you know, and then in places you don't. Cook, dress, pack, navigate. Find out what you lose and what you panic about.
- **Learn human echolocation.** Some blind people navigate by making sharp tongue-clicks and listening to the echoes. It is learnable by sighted adults. Training studies have produced basic competence in about ten weeks. On the Stokemoor, clicking may be drowned out during the chorus. But in the quiet it could tell you whether you're facing an open slope, a wall of stack-trees, or a pump-house.
- **Train your hearing directionally.** Practice pointing toward sounds with your eyes closed and checking yourself. Learn to turn your head to locate things. Your ears are about to become your eyes.

### Replacing your eyes with gear
- **A long cane made of fiberglass or another non-conductive material.** Not aluminum. The ground is threaded with live cables, and a metal cane that bridges one could shock or kill you. The cane is your forward sense: it finds drop-offs, pipes, pools, the edges of channels, the warm crust of a leak before your hand does.
- **A haptic or audio thermal sensor.** This is where you can borrow from the natives. The creatures here "see" heat, and a cheap infrared thermometer or thermal sensor, rigged to output a tone that rises with temperature or a vibration that intensifies, would let you sense the moor's invisible glow. You could sweep it to find buried steam mains (your roads), vents, and fire-columns, and most importantly **invisible fires and scalding surfaces before you touch them.** This may be the single most valuable device you carry. Bring two, waterproofed.
- **Ultrasonic or haptic obstacle detectors**, the kind sold as mobility aids for blind people, worn on the wrist or chest. They may be confused by the density of vegetation, but they're worth testing.
- **Everything labeled for touch.** You cannot read. Before you go, mark every item:
  - Medications in distinct container shapes, with notches or raised dots. Get this wrong and you swallow the wrong pill.
  - Food packets of different sizes or cut corners by type.
  - Gear in the same pocket, every time, always. The first rule of blind camping is *put it back where it lives.* An object set down on the moor is lost.
- **A tactile, vibrating watch**, or a talking one. You'll use the Stroke as your minute-hand, but you need an hour-hand too.

### Eye protection
Wear **clear safety glasses or goggles** the entire time, even though they do nothing for your sight. You cannot see branches, stems, frond-tips, or crusted edges coming. A sighted person blinks before something pokes their eye. You won't get that warning. Also protect your face generally: a brimmed cap does a surprisingly good job of taking hits before your forehead does.

## Part Three: The Stroke

Every forty-one seconds the plateau rises by a fingernail's width and falls, and *WHUMM* comes up through the stones.

### What it will feel like
At first it will startle you every time. A deep, round pressure will come through your knees and the soles of your feet and up your spine, then into your jaw and ears as every pipe and stone rings in sympathy. You may feel slightly seasick for the first day. A fingernail's width is tiny, but it is a rhythmic movement of *the ground*, and your inner ear will notice.

Within a day you'll anticipate it. Within a few days you will stop noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. And then your body will begin keeping time by it without asking you. Travelers to places with strong rhythms often report entrainment: breathing, walking pace, even sleep beginning to lock to the pulse.

Expect, when you go home, to feel the Stroke for a while afterward: phantom rises in quiet rooms, a startled pause every forty seconds or so. This is the same thing sailors feel as "land-sickness." It fades.

- **It is your clock.** Count Strokes. Roughly 88 to an hour, about 2,100 in a day. For short intervals ("rest for twenty Strokes," "we meet back here in fifty Strokes") it is more intuitive than any watch.
