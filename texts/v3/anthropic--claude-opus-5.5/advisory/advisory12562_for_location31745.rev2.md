# Preparing for the Foundry Blister

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Most dangerous places are dangerous because something in them wants something: prey, territory, attention, a sacrifice. The Foundry Blister wants nothing. That makes it easier and harder to prepare for.

It will not hunt you. It will not trick you. It is simply working. The real danger is that you are an unassigned part walking into a machine that assigns everything. Everything you bring, drop, shed, move, or leave behind may be taken up into a process that was already running before you arrived. Almost all of your preparation comes back to one principle: **enter as a closed system, leave as a closed system, and change as little as possible in between.**

My intuition is that the room will tolerate you the way a well-built engine tolerates a moth flying through the housing. It won't notice you unless you get caught in something. So don't get caught in anything.

---

## Part One: The Mountain Itself

Before you face the chamber, you have to reach it, and the approach is a problem of its own.

**Altitude.** The blister is near the summit, where the air is thin. Don't arrive straight from sea level. Spend several days acclimatizing at intermediate elevations, because you will soon be asking your body to handle thin air and extreme heat at once. Each of those raises your heart rate. Together they will punish anyone who hasn't adjusted. If you already get headaches or breathlessness on the slope, the chamber will be much worse.

**The slope.** It is raw, young volcanic ground: black glass and rust-colored scoria, "still settling." Treat it like unconsolidated scree that happens to be made of broken obsidian.
- Wear heavy leather gloves. Volcanic glass cuts like a scalpel, and you will put your hands down.
- Wear gaiters and tall, stiff-soled boots. Scoria gets into everything and shreds thin fabric.
- Wear eye protection, both for the glare and for glass dust.
- Travel with a partner, roped if the ground is loose, and test every step. Young slopes move.
- The ground is warm even at altitude. That is a sign of what is waiting below, not a comfort.

**Finding the seam.** Nothing is visible from outside, so you need precise directions or a lot of patience. The crack is about shoulder-width and angled. Look for the place where the slope feels warmer than it should, where the air above the ground shimmers without an obvious reason, and perhaps where you catch a faint smell of cut metal and crushed stems, the bellows plants exhaling through the only opening they have.

---

## Part Two: The Entry

A shoulder-wide, angled crack means you can't carry a large pack in, and you shouldn't try.

- **Pack small and flat.** Everything you bring inside should fit in a slim harness pack or chest rig that can be pushed ahead of you or dragged behind on a short tether.
- **Practice squeezing through narrow, angled gaps beforehand**, ideally in gear similar to what you'll wear. Knowing you can get out is essential. When you are overheating, the exit is everything.
- **Leave a partner at the mouth.** They hold a line clipped to your harness, keep time, and have agreed-on signals with you: tugs on the rope, or a whistle if sound carries. Their only job is to count minutes and pull you out if you stop responding.
- **Note the draft.** The entrance is the coolest part of the room, which is why the plants there are stunted and pale. That makes it your refuge and your staging area. It is also the only thing in the chamber whose purpose partly coincides with yours.

---

## Part Three: The Heat

This is the hazard that will actually hurt you if you are careless. Everything else in the room is strange. The heat is simply dangerous.

"An oven set low and left on for a century" suggests something like a very hot dry sauna, perhaps 70 to 100°C. The air is so dry "it seems to have been ironed," and there is no humidity at all. That dryness helps you, because your sweat will evaporate instantly and cool you efficiently. It also hides how fast you are losing water. You won't feel wet. You won't see yourself sweating. You will simply dry out.

**Time limits.** Plan visits in short blocks: **ten to fifteen minutes at most** for someone who is heat-conditioned, less for someone who isn't. Come out, recover fully in the open air, and go back in only if you're truly recovered. Several short visits are much better than one long one. The room is slow. You won't miss anything in the half hour you spend outside.

**Conditioning.** In the weeks before, build heat tolerance with regular sauna sessions, gradually lengthening them. Your body will learn to sweat sooner and lose less salt.

**Clothing.**
- Full coverage in loose, light-colored natural fibers. Wool is surprisingly good because it resists heat and doesn't melt. Bare skin takes the radiant heat directly.
- Wear a wide-brimmed hat or hood, even though the light comes from everywhere. Or rather *because* it does.
- A loose cloth over the mouth and nose will temper the scorching air you breathe in. Your airway is sensitive to heat.
- **Insulated boots with thick soles.** The heat comes up from the entire floor. Standing still in thin-soled shoes will burn your feet.
- **Remove or insulate metal**: zippers against skin, jewelry, buckles, watch backs. Metal gets hot enough to brand you. Take off rings before entering.
- A phase-change cooling vest (the kind with solid wax or salt packs, not water) can buy you a few extra minutes.

**Signs to leave immediately:** headache, confusion, clumsy hands, a pounding heart that won't slow, nausea, chills, dizziness, or the moment you stop feeling hot. Stopping feeling hot is the most dangerous sign of all. Your partner outside should know these signs too.

**Electronics** will probably fail or behave oddly. Battery chemistry doesn't like this heat. Plan for your devices to be unreliable and don't depend on them for timekeeping inside. Your partner at the crack is your clock.

## Part Four: Water

This deserves its own section, because I think it is the most important thing to get right.

There is no water in the blister. There never has been. There is "barely even the idea of it." And yet there is that single pale mineral ring on one wall, "left where something *might* once have evaporated but probably never did... a stain from the future... a flood that has not happened."

In a room where consequences are sometimes set in place before their causes arrive, a stain from a flood that hasn't happened yet is a warning. My strong intuition is that **you should not be the flood.**

- **Bring no water into the chamber in anything that could open, leak, or spill.** Drink deeply outside before you go in and again when you come out. If you must carry water inside for an emergency, use a hard-walled, sealed, screw-capped container inside a second sealed container, and don't open it inside.
- Don't pour water on your skin, don't wet a cloth, and don't spit.
- Your sweat is probably all right. It is tiny, it evaporates at once, and the room seems able to absorb what bodies unavoidably give off. Tears, too, if the place affects you that way. But don't bring water *as water.*
- Be very careful near that white ring. Don't touch it. Don't stand beneath it with anything that could spill.

Maybe the ring belongs to something much larger than any visitor, a future flood in the mountain's life. But the room builds its consequences first, and I would rather not find out that I was the cause it was waiting for.

## Part Five: How to Move Through the Room

### The Floor

About a third of the floor is finished: polished, dense stone. The rest is the ruled lattice, the undecided floor, "a promise the room intends to keep," which fills in "only when something is spent into it."

- **Walk on finished stone whenever you can.** Plan your route from the entrance before you move. The undecided floor holds your weight, but it is where things get absorbed.
- **If you must cross the lattice, don't look straight down at it for long.** When watched, it becomes gravel, and gravel shifts. It may be safest to let your eyes rest a little ahead, where it stays a plan, and trust your feet. That goes against every instinct you have. Practice walking without watching your feet beforehand if you can.
- **Drop nothing.** I mean *nothing*: a glove, a pencil, a button, a hair tie, a fingernail, a crumb, a thread. Anything that lands on the ruled floor may be spent into it, and the room will make ground out of your belongings. Maybe that does no harm. But you would be leaving part of your own world inside the permanent structure of this one, and it would stay there as long as the mountain does. Tether your tools. Close every pocket. Wear a snug hood so hair doesn't fall.

### The Flywheel Ferns

They turn once every eleven days, which is far too slowly to hurt you. The worn arcs on the columns show that their rims pass precisely and persistently. Don't stand, sit, or leave anything in the path of a rim. Nothing will happen in the minutes you're there, but I would still avoid putting myself in a groove the room has cut for something else. Don't touch the valves at the hub. They are under pressure that builds and releases on a schedule you know nothing about.

### The Bellows Plants

They exhale once a day or so, for almost a minute, a hot dry breath that smells of cut metal. It's probably harmless, but don't put your face over one. The breath may be much hotter than the room air, and your airway won't forgive you. If you hear the long sigh begin, step back and let it pass. Treat it as the room's ventilation, which it is.

### The Wire Vines

They carry current. Laying a palm across two of them produces "a mild numb buzzing." That is the account of someone who touched them and came away fine, but you should still be careful:
- **Never touch the vines with both hands**, and never with one hand on a vine and the other on a column. A current path across your chest runs through your heart. If you must feel the pulse, and I understand the pull, use one hand, one vine, and the back of your fingers, so that a jolt makes your hand pull away instead of clench.
- Anyone with a pacemaker or heart condition should not touch them at all and should probably stay well away.
- Don't bridge the circuit or break it. It "closes on itself" and powers "nothing except its own continuing." A closed loop that runs only to keep running is the most basic kind of machine there is, a clock with no face. Don't interrupt it.
- Note that they have begun to lean toward the dark patch. They are the one part of the room that is visibly going somewhere.

### The Tree and Its Fruit

The tree is producing units. The units are identical "to within a hair." Nothing collects them, and eventually some sink into the undecided floor and become ground.

**Don't take the fruit.** I feel strongly about this. The room says that "what is duplicated stays in step," and anything so perfectly identical may be tied to its siblings, or to the floor it hasn't yet become. You would be taking home a component with an open job. At best you would leave a gap in the floor that never fills. At worst you would carry something back through the crack that is still, in some sense, part of the machine.

Don't handle them either. Don't stand directly under a ripening one. Being ejected makes them projectiles, if slow ones. And don't pick up the ones that have "dust on them that has not yet settled from the air." They are showing you what the room does with order.

## Part Six: The Paired Stones and the Rule of Stepping

There are at least four pairs of objects that are "the same stone." Move one and the other moves.

- **Move none of them.** You can't be sure which stones are paired, and the pairs are hard to spot. So don't pick up stones. Don't kick them. If you brush one by accident, leave it where it ends up rather than trying to put it back, because adjusting it adjusts its twin somewhere you aren't looking.
- **Count your equipment going in and coming out.** This may sound paranoid. But the room economizes and reuses parts. If you come out with two identical carabiners where you carried in one, that tells you something. **Don't take the duplicate with you.** Leave it at the mouth of the crack and think carefully before going back in.
- More subtly, notice whether anything you're carrying has started to "stay in step" with something in the room. It's unlikely. But it's the kind of thing worth checking for.

## Part Seven: Before and After

This is the part that might unsettle you most, so prepare for it mentally.

Consequences in the blister are sometimes put in place before their causes arrive. The housing is finished before the engine is cast. That raises a real possibility: **you may find evidence of your visit before you make it.** A scuff on the finished floor exactly the shape of your boot. A mark on a column at the height of your hand. Something in a place you haven't been yet.

If that happens:
- **Don't panic, and don't try to prevent it.** You can't argue with a room that assembled its timeline in advance. Trying to avoid making a mark that is already there is how people make mistakes: they stumble, drop things, touch what they shouldn't.
- **Don't deliberately cause it either.** Just move carefully and let what happens happen.
- **Look around before you enter and after you leave**, and keep a rough mental map. If you do leave traces, you'll want to know whether they were yours all along.

I suspect that a careful visitor leaves almost nothing, and that the room, which already knows its own shape, will turn out to have had very little room for you in it. That's what you want.

---

## Part Eight: The Dark Patch

This is where the room is going next. All the shadows point to it, including yours. It is the only cool surface in the blister, and it is slowly growing. The wire vines lean toward it, and you can make out threading and flanges within it: "the next chamber, drafted but unpoured."

**You will want to go to it.** You will have been in brutal dry heat for ten minutes, and there on the far wall will be the one cool place in the room. The pull will be physical and simple. Your body will want to lean against it the way you lean against a cold window in summer.

**Don't.**

The rest of the room is finished or finishing. This is unstarted. It is a draft, and its heat "is gathering." I don't think it's a hostile place, but it is exactly where the room's future is being decided, and I wouldn't want to become part of what gets poured there. Keep your distance. Look at it if you like, since the details come out slowly if you look long enough, but don't touch it and don't rest against it. When you need relief, **go back to the entrance draft.** The cool that the room isn't using is safer than the cool it is saving.

Watch your shadow as you move. It will point at the patch no matter where you stand. That's normal. It is just the rule of the room. It doesn't mean you're being pulled, and you shouldn't let it pull you.

---

## Part Nine: The Knock

Very rarely, something far below completes one stroke. The columns vibrate, black glass dust sifts down from the flanges, and a new ridge of threading appears at the base of every column.

The odds of it happening while you're inside are low, since strokes come months or years apart. But:
- **Carry a respirator or a close-fitting mask with a particulate filter.** Volcanic glass dust is fine and sharp, and you shouldn't breathe it. Wear sealed eye protection too.
- If the knock comes, **stay still.** Don't grab a column. Let the dust settle. Don't panic about the new ridge appearing. That is only the gauge advancing.
- Then leave calmly, because it will be a long time before anything like that happens again, and you'll want to think about what you witnessed from somewhere with air in it.

If you are there for a knock, count yourself very lucky. I would hold it quietly as a private thing. It's the room's own count, and you happened to be present for one tick of it.

## Part Ten: What to Bring, Summarized

**Inside the chamber:**
- Slim, closed harness pack, everything tethered, every pocket sealed
- Insulated thick-soled boots, heavy gloves, loose natural-fiber full coverage, hood or hat, mouth cloth
- Sealed eye protection and particulate respirator
- Phase-change cooling vest (no water-based cooling)
- A safety line to your partner outside
- A small notebook and a graphite pencil on a cord, if you want to record anything. Ink and electronics may fail in the heat. Sketching may work better than photography anyway. The room seems to reward looking slowly.
- Nothing that can spill, shed, crumble, or fall off

**Outside, at the crack:**
- Plenty of water, electrolytes, and salt
- Shade, if you can make any on that slope
- A partner with a watch, first aid for burns and heat illness, and the authority to haul you out
- A record of everything you carried in, for counting on the way out

**Leave behind entirely:**
- Open water, food, anything living (seeds, soil, pets, cut flowers), anything you'd be unhappy to see incorporated permanently into another world's floor

## Part Eleven: The Mind

This is the part people forget to prepare.

The blister is beautiful in a way that is hard to be in for long. It isn't frightening the way a dark cave or a predator is frightening. It is purposeful without anyone's purpose. Everything moves, grows, turns, breathes, and conducts, and "none of the functions are for anyone." The bellows ventilate the room for occupants who aren't there. The vines power nothing. The tree makes units no one collects.

Standing in it, you may feel very strongly that **you are not needed.** No part of the place has a slot for you. Some people will find that restful: the relief of being in a place that asks nothing, where you can simply watch. Others will find it quietly devastating, the feeling of being a spare part in a machine too complete to need you.

Know which one you're likely to be before you go. If you tend toward the second, go in short bursts and have someone to talk to afterward. Remind yourself that the room isn't rejecting you. It's just busy, the way a clock is busy.

Prepare as well for the **silence.** It's heavy, dry, and textured, like felt. The sounds under it come very far apart: a single tick every few minutes, one long exhale a day, a hum at the edge of hearing. Your mind will reach for more stimulation than the room gives. Let it settle. If you can, spend one of your short visits simply standing still by the entrance draft and listening. That's when the place makes the most sense.

And prepare to feel its **patience.** "Time is slow, not because it is sluggish but because it is careful." You'll be tempted to wait for something to happen, a fern to visibly turn, a fruit to drop. It probably won't in the time you can stand the heat. That's fine. You aren't there to see it finish. You're there to see that it's working.

## Part Twelve: After You Leave

- **Rehydrate slowly and thoroughly.** Keep monitoring yourself for heat illness for several hours, since some symptoms come on late.
- **Check your skin for burns**, especially where fabric pressed against you, on your feet, and where metal might have touched you.
- **Shake out your clothes and gear before leaving the slope.** Glass dust gets everywhere, and you don't want it in your lungs or your eyes later.
- **Count your equipment.** Anything extra, identical, or oddly warm stays on the mountain.
- **Expect lingering impressions.** The smell of cut metal and crushed stems may stay in your nose for a day or two. You may find your sense of time a little off, catching yourself expecting consequences before causes, or feeling that the world outside is too fast and too careless. Some visitors may find they notice patterns more: the regularity of things, the way ordinary objects look manufactured or grown. That will pass. Or it won't, and you'll see the world a little differently. The blister has that effect.
- **Consider not going back.** Once is probably enough. The room didn't need you the first time. The kindest thing a visitor can do is leave no trace in a place that will someday, very slowly, finish itself exactly as it always intended, and know that for a few minutes you were allowed to stand inside the drafting.

---

## The Short Version

Acclimatize to the altitude and condition yourself to heat. Enter through the crack with almost nothing, tethered to someone outside. Stay only minutes at a time. Bring no open water and spill none. Drop nothing, take nothing, and move no stones. Walk on finished floor. Touch the vines with one hand at most. Leave the fruit where it lies. Seek relief at the entrance draft, never at the dark patch. Expect to be unneeded, and don't take it personally. Count everything on the way out.

The Foundry Blister is not waiting for you and does not want you. Treat that as a kind of permission: you can come in, watch it carefully for a little while, and go, as long as you leave it exactly as busy as you found it.
