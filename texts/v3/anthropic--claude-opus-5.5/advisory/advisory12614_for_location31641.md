# Preparing for the Governor's Room

## First, What Kind of Place This Is

The Governor's Room is not dangerous the way a battlefield or a predator's territory is dangerous. Nothing here wants anything from you. It is dangerous the way a hot tub in a cave is dangerous: quietly and physiologically, through heat, bad air, and edges you can't see. It is also fragile the way a soap bubble is fragile. It is a closed system that has spent years or decades tuning itself to one slow machine, and you are the first animal to enter it.

So preparation comes in two halves: keeping the room from hurting you, and keeping yourself from hurting the room. The second half matters more than it first seems to.

---

## The Air: Take This Seriously Above Everything Else

Read the description again and notice what is missing. There are no insects, no spiders, no worms, no bats, in a warm, damp, sheltered, lit space that should be crawling with them. The description offers the gentle explanation that whatever brought the seeds didn't bring anything that breathes. That may be true. But there is a second explanation, and you should plan around it until you have ruled it out.

**The room sits on a volcanic vent in a sealed basalt pocket.** Volcanic steam commonly carries carbon dioxide, sometimes hydrogen sulfide, sometimes other gases. CO₂ is heavier than air and pools in low places. The engine sits at the low end of the room, where the floor tilted, directly on the vent. Plants would thrive in CO₂-enriched air, which would explain ferns that are enormous for the light they get. Animals would not survive in it. An empty room with lush greenery and no animal life is exactly what a gas trap looks like.

Before you go:

- **Bring a multi-gas monitor** that reads CO₂ (not just O₂, since CO₂ becomes dangerous well before oxygen drops noticeably), H₂S, CO, and O₂. Calibrate it before the trip. Clip it at chest height, and carry a second sensor, or a probe on a pole, so you can test near the floor before stepping down into any hollow.
- **Do not trust your nose.** The description mentions no smell. That could mean the air is clean, or it could mean the smell is absent because the gas is odorless (CO₂), or because concentrations are high enough to deaden smell (H₂S does this).
- **Lower a sensor on a line before you lower yourself.** Leave it at the bottom for at least one full light cycle if you can. Gas output may rise with the vent's pressure. The dangerous moment may be the peak of the "day," not the dark.
- **Treat the area around the engine as a separate, more dangerous zone.** Approach it only with continuous readings, and never kneel or crouch there. Crouching puts your face into the heaviest air.
- **Pack an escape respirator** (a short-duration self-contained unit, not a filter mask; filters do nothing against CO₂ displacement). Know how to put it on in the dark with sweaty hands.
- **Never enter alone.** One person stays at the surface with a rope attached to you and the authority to haul. Agree on a check-in signal on a fixed interval, perhaps a tug or a call every five minutes. Silence means you come up.

If the air proves clean, wonderful. The room's lifelessness is then a mystery rather than a warning. But you have to find that out with instruments, not hope.

## The Heat

"The temperature of a held breath" means close to body temperature, around 35–37°C, and humid. It "sits against the skin like wet cloth."

This matters more than it sounds. Your body sheds heat in two main ways: radiating to cooler surroundings, and evaporating sweat. In air near skin temperature, radiation barely works. In humid air, sweat evaporates slowly. You will sweat heavily and gain little from it. Your core temperature will creep up while you stand perfectly still.

Prepare:

- **Acclimatize beforehand** if you can: a week or two of daily exposure to hot, humid conditions, such as a sauna with a wet towel or a tropical climate.
- **Plan short stays at first.** Perhaps 30–45 minutes, then climb out and cool down. Build toward longer visits only once you know how your body handles it. You will want to stay for a full cycle, which could be three to five hours. That may need to be done in shifts, or across several visits, rather than in one sitting.
- **Bring more water than you think you need, with electrolytes.** Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.
- **Know the signs of heat illness** in yourself and your partner: headache, nausea, confusion, clumsiness, stopping sweating. Confusion is the dangerous one, because the person experiencing it won't recognize it. This overlaps with CO₂ symptoms, which is another reason for the monitor.
- **A cooling vest** (phase-change packs, not ice, which will melt fast) can extend your safe time considerably.
- **Expect your equipment to suffer too.** Anything you bring down cooler than the room will fog and drip immediately. Glasses and camera lenses will become useless the moment you arrive. Let gear acclimatize in a sealed bag, bring anti-fog treatment, and keep electronics in dry bags with desiccant.

## Getting In and Out

The drop is about three body-lengths, perhaps five or six meters, through the broken floor of a ruined pump house. This is a rope descent, not a scramble.

- **Anchor to something outside the collapse zone.** The pump house above has already lost its floor. Its remaining walls and footings are suspect. Anchor to solid ground well back from the edge if possible, or to multiple independent points.
- **Do not anchor to, hang from, or touch the bowed iron joist.** It is holding up the lamp. It is also, in a sense, holding up the whole ecology of the room, and it is already smiling under strain.
- **Pad the edge.** Broken brick and fractured basalt will cut rope. Use rope protectors wherever the line crosses an edge.
- **Rig for ascent before you descend.** You need to be able to get out when you're hot, tired, possibly dizzy, and possibly in the dark. Practice single-rope ascent beforehand until it's automatic, or have the surface team set up a haul system.
- **Choose your landing point** on the rubble, not on growth, and not on the talus slopes against the walls.

## Footing and Edges

The collapse is fresh by geological standards. Nothing has been rounded, crusted over, or settled.

- **The basalt fracture faces are glassy and sharp enough to open skin.** Conchoidal fracture, the kind that makes obsidian into scalpels, produces edges that cut before you feel them. Wear **cut-resistant gloves** (rated, not ordinary work gloves), long sleeves of a tough but breathable fabric, and trousers you don't mind shredding. This is in direct tension with the heat. Accept the tension and lean toward protection. A cut here, on a rusted, damp, spore-laden surface, is a serious wound.
- **The talus is unsettled.** Angular rubble that hasn't "rolled far or long" hasn't found its angle of rest either. It will shift under your weight. Stay off the steep slopes against the walls. Test each step. Move slowly, which you will be doing anyway because of the heat.
- **Boots with stiff soles and ankle support**, with good grip on wet surfaces. Everything is filmed with moisture. Wet basalt and wet moss are both slick.
- **Knee pads**, if you intend to get close to anything low.
- **Beware the saturated hollows.** The darker patches of wet grit in low spots are low for a reason. They are where water collects, and possibly where gas collects too.
- **Do not step near the vent itself.** Ground around a steam fissure can be hot, crumbly, or thin. Steam can scald without warning when the pressure surges. Remember that the "day" arrives because the vent is strengthening.
- **Bring a first-aid kit** focused on lacerations: irrigation, closures, pressure dressings. Make sure your **tetanus vaccination is current.** Rust over rust, sharp edges, damp soil: this is the textbook setting.

## The Machine and the Wire

The engine is slow and old, and it looks harmless. It isn't entirely.

- **A turning flywheel, however slowly, has mass.** A belt and pulley will take a finger or a sleeve. The governor's arms swing out as it speeds up. Keep hands and loose clothing away from anything that moves, even if it has been still for an hour. It will start again on its own schedule, not yours.
- **The boiler holds pressure "the way a lung does."** It leaks, which is probably why it hasn't burst. Still, don't stand in line with its seams, and don't touch it. It is fed by volcanic steam and will be very hot.
- **The dynamo and cable are live during the light phase.** It's likely low voltage, but it runs through a humid room, partly bare, partly embedded in living wood, possibly with wet contact points you can't see. Don't touch the cable, the lamp bracket, or the fig roots that carry it. Assume anything near that vein of copper can shock you.
- **The engine sits a few degrees off true** on a fractured floor over a vent. Don't lean on it, climb it, or push it.

## Light and Time

Your own light is a problem, in two ways.

For your safety, you need it. There will be one or two hours of total darkness per cycle, in a room of glass-edged rubble. Bring a headlamp plus two backups, because the damp will be hard on electronics.

For the room, your light is an intrusion. These plants have tuned themselves to one amber source with an irregular rhythm. A bright white beam sweeping across the ferns in the dark phase is, from their point of view, a false dawn from the wrong direction. One visit probably won't harm anything lasting, but it will disrupt exactly the behavior you came to see.

So:

- **Use a dim, red or deep-amber headlamp**, and keep it low and pointed at your feet.
- **Pick a spot to sit before the dark phase begins**, somewhere stable, in clean air, away from the talus, and then turn your light off entirely. Use it again only to move.
- **Consider whether you need a light at all** during the lit phase. You probably don't; your eyes will adapt to the lamp.
- **Give your eyes time.** Full dark adaptation takes twenty to forty minutes. The room's slow dawn will take about the same. If you're patient, you'll see the lamp coming on before you can measure it.

Know also that **you cannot schedule this place.** The vent runs on "something deep in the rock that no one has measured." You may arrive at full amber, or at the start of a two-hour dark. Plan your safe-time limits around the heat and your air supply, not around the light. If you run out of safe time before dawn, leave, and come back. The room will still be here.

## You Are the Only Animal

This is the part of the preparation that is more ethical than practical, and I think it's the part that matters most.

The room contains no insects, no microbes you can see, no animal life at all. That makes it unusually vulnerable. You carry an ecosystem on your body: skin bacteria, fungi, possibly mites, possibly seeds in your boot treads, possibly an insect in a fold of your pack. The Governor's Room has never had a pollinator, a herbivore, or a decomposer arrive from outside. One fruit fly or one aphid could reorganize everything. A fig wasp would change the fig's entire future.

- **Clean all gear thoroughly** before you go. Wash or freeze clothing and packs to kill insects and eggs. Scrub boot soles. Use new or sterilized rope if you can.
- **Inspect everything** for hitchhikers on the surface, immediately before descent.
- **Bring no fruit, no open food, no plant material.** Eat on the surface.
- **Carry out everything**, including your urine and any waste. Bring a sealed bottle.
- **Touch nothing that grows.** Not the ferns, not the moss pelt on the bedplate, not the red rosettes near the vent. In particular, do not touch the fig-belt. It is half leather and half living wood, stretched once every few seconds by the engine. If you break it, the dynamo stops, the lamp stops, and the only sun this room has ever known goes out for good.
- **Take no samples** unless you've thought hard about why. A single unripened fig from the talus is tempting. It is also part of whatever strange reproductive stalemate that tree is in.
- **Protect yourself from them too.** The room is full of fern spores and whatever else is in the damp. A disposable particulate mask (worn under the escape respirator plan, not instead of it) reduces how much you breathe in. If you have asthma or respiratory allergies, factor that in seriously.

Your body will also change the room just by being in it. You are a 37°C, CO₂-exhaling, vibrating mass. The plants seem to anticipate dawn by sensing heat and vibration. Your footsteps may be felt through the rock and root mat. Move as little as you can, and set your feet down softly.

## What You Should Leave Unfixed

At some point you will see the problem: the fig is slowly closing over the lamp. Each year the light is smaller. You will feel the urge to prune it, to pull back a few roots from the bulb and give the ferns another few years.

Prepare for that urge before you arrive, and decide in advance not to act on it.

The room is not a garden. It is not yours to tend. The whole meaning of it is that it is "tended by nothing," a process finding out what it is. If the fig swallows the lamp, that is the room's own future, and it has a right to arrive at it. Watch it. Don't correct it.

## Kit Summary

**Air:** calibrated multi-gas monitor (CO₂, O₂, H₂S, CO), secondary sensor on a pole or line, escape self-contained breathing apparatus, particulate mask for spores.

**Access:** static rope, rope protectors, harness, descent and ascent devices, multiple anchor options, a surface partner with a haul system and a communication plan.

**Heat:** cooling vest with phase-change packs, abundant water with electrolytes, sealed pee bottle, a firm maximum-time rule agreed with your partner.

**Body protection:** cut-resistant gloves, tough breathable long sleeves and trousers, stiff-soled grippy boots, knee pads, a helmet (things still fall from above: drips, and eventually brick).

**Light:** dim red or amber headlamp plus two backups, all waterproof.

**Care:** first-aid kit for lacerations and heat illness, up-to-date tetanus.

**Other:** anti-fog for eyewear and lenses, dry bags with desiccant, a notebook in a waterproof sleeve (pencil, not pen), a watch you can read without a bright display, to time the cycle.

**Leave behind:** food, fruit, anything organic you can't account for, loud or crinkly gear, bright lights, and any plan to improve the place.

## What It Will Actually Be Like

Once the practicalities are handled, this is what I think you should prepare yourself for.

You'll come down the rope into warmth that feels like entering a mouth. Your glasses will fog white, and for a few seconds you'll be blind in a hot dark full of sharp things, which is why you practiced. When they clear, if the lamp is lit, you'll see a sphere of bronze and coppery black, ferns held up like hands to a fire, and the squat engine at the low end breathing out through its teeth once every few seconds.

You'll be uncomfortable the entire time. Sweat will run into your gloves. You'll be aware of your breathing, of the monitor on your chest, of the time. The discomfort is part of it. You are an animal where animals don't go.

Then you'll have to become very still. The room's events are slow: a drop every few minutes, a governor settling, a belt that is also a tree creaking. If you are restless, you will see nothing but a dim ruin. If you can sit, breathe quietly, and wait, you may hear, in the last part of the dark phase, that faint whisper of a thousand fronds turning toward a light that hasn't come yet. It will be the quietest thing you have ever heard. You may not be sure you heard it.

The hardest thing to prepare for may be the feeling at the end. As the lamp sinks to its last orange and the engine turns once in a long while, the room will look deliberate, built and devotional, like a chapel with a single candle. You'll feel a strong pull to stay for one more cycle, one more dawn. Your monitor and your partner and your core temperature are there to overrule that feeling. Climb out when your time is up. Sit on the surface in the cooler air, drink water, and let your heart slow.

Then check your pack and your boots one last time for anything alive, before you leave or go back down.
