# Preparing to Visit the Held Basin

## The Short Version

This is not a dangerous place in the way a war zone or a glacier is dangerous. Nothing here wants to kill you, except in the incidental way a mute wants to eat whatever falls. The Held Basin is dangerous the way a factory floor is, or a foundry, or the inside of a bell. The environment itself is the hazard, and it is continuous. You cannot step out of the sound, the dust, or the glass. There is no night off.

It helps to accept three things before you go:

1. **You will lose some hearing.** You can reduce the damage but not prevent it. The residents go deaf by middle age, and they have lived with this for generations.
2. **You will be a burden.** You are heavy, loud-bodied, clumsy on the lattice, and ignorant of rules that everyone else learned as infants. Your job is to be as small a burden as possible.
3. **Your stay should be short.** Plan in days, perhaps a couple of weeks. The residents' own warning is that outsiders lose themselves after about a month, and that is advice worth taking.

---

## Who Should Not Go

Be honest with yourself here. The sound is not just loud. It is physical, and it works on structures in the body that most environments leave alone.

- **Heart conditions**, especially arrhythmias, and anyone with a **pacemaker** or implanted defibrillator. Continuous infrasound in the sternum is exactly the wrong thing for these.
- **Inner-ear or vestibular disorders**: Ménière's, superior canal dehiscence, a history of vertigo. Intense low-frequency sound can trigger dizziness and falling in susceptible people. In a place where falling onto glass means lacerations, and falling off a raft means death, that is disqualifying.
- **Pregnancy.** The fetus is enclosed in fluid and receives sound well. The effects of prolonged exposure at these levels are unknown and probably not good.
- **Epilepsy or migraine** triggered by patterns or sensory overload. Between the geometric halos, the rhythmic beating of the chords, and the ultrasonic eye-ache, this place is close to a trigger by design.
- **Existing significant hearing loss.** You have less margin to lose.
- **Loose dental work, recent surgery, or implanted hardware** that might vibrate. The description says the tone sits in your back teeth. Loose crowns and fresh sutures will let you know about it.
- **Serious lung conditions.** The air is full of centuries of volcanic dust.
- **Severe claustrophobia, or reliance on sound for emotional regulation.** If you calm yourself with music, with your own voice, or with silence, you will have none of those here, except in the cancellation pockets.

## Before You Leave

### Get a baseline audiogram
Have your hearing formally tested before the trip and again after. This is the only way to know what you lost. Consider tinnitus screening too.

### Learn to communicate without sound
You cannot learn the Basin's sign language in advance. You can do things that will help:

- **Learn an existing sign language to at least a basic level**, or learn to fingerspell. The benefit is less the vocabulary than the habit of thinking spatially and watching hands. You will pick up local signs faster.
- **Build a touch code with your companions**, and drill it until it is automatic. Tap patterns for *stop*, *danger above*, *follow me*, *I need the pocket*, *I'm unwell*, *retuning coming*, and *yes/no*. Practice with your eyes closed and earplugs in. At night in the Basin, touch will be the only channel you have.
- **Practice reading faces and bodies.** Everyone around you will be communicating through posture, gesture, and pressure. Hearing people are usually bad at this.

### Train for the rafts
Travel between dwellings happens on woven rafts nudged along invisible node-lines, sometimes tens or hundreds of meters above black glass. Before going:

- Get comfortable with **climbing harnesses and tethers**. Practice clipping and unclipping by feel.
- Do some **balance training**. Paddleboards, slacklines, and small boats on choppy water all help. The rafts wobble when the chords beat.
- Test yourself for **motion sickness**, and find a medication that works for you without heavy sedation.

### Travel light, and weigh yourself
In the Basin, weight is an etiquette issue as well as a comfort issue. Dwellings sit on nodes with limited capacity, and "never build heavier than your node can bear" is one of the fundamental rules. When you step onto someone's raft or into their house, you are adding load to a system tuned to carry exactly what it carries.

Know your total weight with gear. Get it as low as you reasonably can. A hiker's 25-kilogram pack is a real imposition on your hosts, and possibly a danger to them. Aim for ultralight. Leave the luxuries at home.

### Prepare your equipment for vibration
Everything you bring will be shaken continuously for the whole trip.

- Put **threadlocker** on any screws in your gear, and check everything with moving parts.
- Avoid electronics with spinning drives or delicate mechanical parts.
- Assume **microphones and audio equipment are useless**, and possibly harmful to themselves.
- Pad and wrap anything fragile. Glass flakes on the ground "tremble and walk," and anything small and loose in your pack will do the same.

## Gear

### Hearing protection (layered)
- **Foam earplugs**, well inserted, with plenty of spares. They will get dusty and wet.
- **Over-ear muffs**, high-rated, over the plugs.
- Know the limits. Plugs and muffs do little against **infrasound**, which enters through your whole body, and nothing against **bone conduction** at these intensities. The tone will reach your inner ear through your skull. Protection buys you time and reduces the worst of the audible and ultrasonic ranges. It does not make you safe.
- If you can get one, a **wearable noise dosimeter** is worth having, with a vibration-only readout. A light that blinks when you have exceeded your daily dose is the kind of instrument you want.
- Ultrasound is a wildcard. The ache behind the eyes is a real signal. When it builds, move away.

### Respiratory and eye protection
- A **P100 or N95-class respirator**, with many spare filters. Volcanic glass dust is essentially silica, and centuries of it hang in the air. Silicosis builds slowly and is permanent. The dust sits in rows at the nodes, so some air will be cleaner than others, but you cannot see finely enough to tell. Wear the mask whenever you are moving through dusty strata, and always after a retuning, when the dust finally falls and gets stirred up.
- **Sealed goggles.** Glass microflakes in the eye are serious.
- **Saline eye wash.**

### Head protection
- A **lightweight hard hat or climbing helmet.** Things fall in the Held Basin: water beads the size of plums, clods of soil, gas-fruit soaked heavy, dropped tools, the prey of mutes, and in a retuning, everything at once. Most of what falls is light, but not all of it.

### Feet and hands
- **Boots with thick, rigid, cut-resistant soles.** Ordinary rubber soles will be sliced by the crests of pressure-folds. Look for puncture-resistant midsoles of the kind used in construction or firefighting. Carry a spare pair of soles, or a spare pair of boots.
- **Gaiters**, so you don't catch your ankles on glass edges.
- **Cut-resistant gloves** (Kevlar or similar) for any scrambling on the floor or handling rafts.
- **Knee pads**, lightweight. Kneeling on glass is otherwise not an option.

### Clothing
- The basin is **warm and humid**, and the fog bands are wet. Choose quick-drying synthetic layers, long sleeves, and long trousers. Skin exposed to the glass gets cut.
- **Close-fitting** rather than billowing. Loose fabric catches the sound and flaps, and loose objects fall or drift.
- A **light waterproof shell** for passing through fog bands.
- Nights are no colder than days. The ground stays at the warmth of a stone after sunset. You will not need much insulation. A thin sleeping mat mostly protects you from the glass.

### Water
- Bring **your own water** for the first days, and a **filter capable of removing fine particulates**. Water beads held in the nodes are harvestable, but they have hung in dusty air, perhaps for a long time. They are probably safe once filtered. Ask locals before harvesting. Water is the basin's scarcest resource, and nets and node-beads may well belong to someone.
- A **collapsible container**. Anything hard-sided is extra weight.

### Light
- **A red-light headlamp** plus a dimmable white light. The basin is always dim: a pewter glow by day, near-black at night. Your eyes will adapt, and you want to keep that adaptation. Use bright light sparingly. You don't know what your light means to the animals, or to the people, whose own lanterns are small.
- Spare batteries, well secured against vibration.

### Navigation
- A **compass** may not be reliable on fresh volcanic rock, which can be magnetized unevenly. Test it on arrival against something known.
- You will not see the sun.
- The residents navigate by the **halo figures**: diffraction patterns in the held droplets, unique to each Throat and visible only from certain bearings. Ask a local to teach you. Sketch each halo in a notebook with its bearing and the name of its Throat. This is the most useful knowledge you can acquire here.
- On the floor, the **concentric ribbing** of the glass around each column tells you which Throat you are near and roughly how far away it is.

### Communication
- A **small writing slate or waterproof notebook** with a grease pencil.
- A **short tether line** you can use to stay physically connected to a companion.
- **Bright visual signals** (a colored cloth, a light flash) for emergencies. Whistles are useless here.

### Medical kit
- **Laceration supplies**: irrigation syringe, wound closure strips, sutures if you know how, antiseptic, generous bandaging. Glass cuts are clean and deep and tend to bleed freely.
- **Tweezers and a magnifier** for glass splinters.
- **Anti-nausea medication.** Infrasound causes nausea in many people, and so do the rafts.
- **Painkillers** for headaches and jaw pain, and **sleep aids** that you have tested at home.
- Ear drops, eye drops, and lip balm. Your throat and sinuses may be sore from vibration and dust.

### Gas detection
- A **small multi-gas detector** (CO₂, H₂S, SO₂). The fissures "breathe warm air" from volcanic depths. Some cancellation pockets are in low places near fissures, and heavy gases pool in low places. The blind mammals and pale fungi obviously survive there, but don't assume you can.

## Arrival: The First Hours

**Arrive at the edge, far from any Throat.** You will hear the basin before you see it, and that is useful. Let it build gradually. Stop when the pressure in your sternum starts. Sit. Breathe. Notice what it does to you. Some people feel anxious or even afraid for no reason; infrasound does that. Some feel it as awe. Name the feeling, so you know which part is you and which part is the sound.

**Put your protection on before you think you need it.**

**Find a cancellation pocket and learn what one feels like.** The pockets are common land, and no one may be turned away, including you. Go there first. Take off your ear protection, speak a few words aloud, listen to your own heartbeat. Remember this feeling. It is your baseline and your refuge, and you will need to find it again, often in the dark and sometimes in a hurry.

**Find a guide.** Given the few thousand residents and the vast distances, finding one may take patience. Watch for dwellings, lanterns, rafts. Approach slowly and in plain view, since no one will hear you coming. Show your hands. Offer something light and useful, or simply offer work. Trade goods from outside may be interesting, but consider their weight. A small, dense gift can be a bad gift here.

## Moving Through the Basin

### On the glass floor

- **Never stand at a node.** The locals treat this as the first rule, and it is also plain physics. Things fall on nodes. Learn to spot them. Look directly overhead for held objects: water beads, seeds, dust in rows. If something is hanging above you, you are under a node. Move.
- **Walk the ribs, not the ridges.** The concentric ribbing gives a more even surface than the pressure-folds, whose crests can "part a boot sole."
- **Watch where the flakes walk.** Small glass flakes tremble and migrate in the sound. Their movement will show you where the vibration is strongest, and where it goes flat.
- **Use the roads if permitted.** The polished glass ribbons are built with extraordinary precision and almost unused. Walking them may be easier on your boots. Ask first. Something so carefully made and so rarely used may have a purpose you don't understand.
- **Leave the floor flora alone.** The black succulents, cushion mosses, and pocket fungi are delicate and intolerant of vibration. Don't step on moss in the runnels. Don't pick fungi; they live in the precious flat places.
- **Never carry the blind pocket-mammals out.** They panic outside the silence.
- **Let the lizards be.** They are deaf, calm, and the same color as the floor, so watch where you step. Their calm might be a good model for you.
- **Stay out of fissures** unless a local takes you in. Warm air, possible gases, sharp edges, and nowhere to go.

### On the rafts and in the lattice

- **Always be tethered.** To the raft, to a companion, to a vine. A fall from even the low tier onto glass means serious injury. From the middle tier, it is fatal.
- **Don't push, nudge, or steer** unless you have been taught. Moving along node-lines takes skill. A bad push can send a raft drifting off-line, or dump it into a gap between nodes.
- **Tether all your gear.** Anything loose that slips off is gone, and it falls onto whoever is below. A dropped water bottle from a hundred meters is dangerous.
- **Sit low and centered.** The rafts wobble when the chords beat. Keep your weight still. Move deliberately and tell your hosts first, by touch or sign, before you shift.
- **Don't touch the resonant plates** hanging beside dwellings. They are tuned to nudge a node a hand's width up or down. Playing with them is like adjusting someone's life support.
- **Never touch or cut the old vines** that tie floating fields to the ground. They are the oldest living things in the basin, and residents do not cut them.

### Near a Throat

- **Don't go close without a reason.** Near a column, sound stops being noise and becomes a medium. You won't be able to speak, shout, or hear your heartbeat, and your throat will buzz on its own. Treat this area like a high-radiation zone: limited time, planned exit, companion present.
- **Never touch a Throat.** This rule is universal and non-negotiable, and it would be a very bad idea physically anyway.
- **Watch for the insect swarms.** Near the columns they are dense enough to shimmer. They probably don't sting, but you'll be breathing through your mask for a reason.
