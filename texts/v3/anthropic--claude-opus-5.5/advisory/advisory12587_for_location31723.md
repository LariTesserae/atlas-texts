# Preparing to Visit the Hotbed

## The short version

The Hotbed is not dangerous because of any single thing. It is dangerous because everything is slightly hostile at once, all the time, and the place takes away the faculties you would normally use to cope. It is dark, so you can't see hazards. It is deafening, so you can't hear them, and after a few hours you can't think clearly about them either. It is hot and saturated, so your body is already struggling before you do anything strenuous. The ground cuts, burns, and sometimes gives way. Every wound will try to rot.

So the core principles are:

1. **Do your thinking before you go in.** Once you're down there, rely on decisions, rules, and turnaround times you already made. Don't count on judgment you won't have.
2. **Armour yourself against the ground.** Most injuries will come from the rock. Most serious outcomes will come from infected cuts.
3. **Carry your own air, water, and light.** Or at least the means to test and fix them.
4. **Go in short, come out often.** Sleep on the high ground. Treat the basin as somewhere you visit in forays, not somewhere you stay. The people who live nearby already do this. "No one stays" is the most important local knowledge there is.
5. **Leave the knappers alone.** They are the most interesting thing in the basin and among the most dangerous, and you have a real capacity to harm them.

## What it will actually feel like

It helps to picture this honestly, because the mental preparation matters as much as the gear.

You'll hear it long before you arrive, a roar coming up the slope like surf or distant traffic. From the rim the basin looks like nothing: a dark, blurred lowland under a brown lid. Walking down, the light goes first. It doesn't fade the way evening does. It turns muddy and sourceless, as if you're walking into a room with dirty windows. Then the canopy closes and you're in the dark at what your watch says is midday.

The heat comes up through your boot soles. The air is at body temperature or close to it and completely saturated, so sweat doesn't cool you. It just runs. Water drips on you constantly from above, never quite rain, and it's warm. The smell arrives in layers: rotten eggs, then something sweet and fermenting, then ammonia from the guano, then a sharp flinty smell that seems to sit at the back of your nose. Within an hour you stop noticing it, which is itself a warning sign (more on that below).

The noise is the thing people underestimate. The cicada whine sits at a pitch that feels internal, as if it's happening inside your head. On top of it the frogs pulse and clang, and the monkeys scream and the screams come back off the rock. Because the space reflects sound, there's no direction to any of it. You won't be able to tell where anything is by ear. Conversation at arm's length requires shouting. After a few hours you'll feel stupid in a specific way: slow, irritable, prone to fixating, bad at sequencing tasks. Veterans say "a place where you cannot think," and they mean it literally.

Underfoot is black glass and shattered crust, sticky with algae, often hidden under a mat of roots and litter that may or may not have rock beneath it. Around you everything is pale and soft and reaching, with blanched stems, translucent leaves, and vines like wet rope. It looks like a greenhouse left in a cellar for a century. There are moments of real beauty: cold green fungal light tracing a fallen trunk, a clear bell tone from a struck stone cutting through the chorus, the river of bats at dusk. There are also moments of real dread, such as a crack like a gunshot somewhere above you, or a deep rumble from under the ground.

Expect to feel exhilarated for the first hour and then worn down. Expect your first foray to be much shorter than you planned. That's normal, and it's correct.

## Before you go

### Medical preparation

See a travel medicine specialist and tell them you're going somewhere that is, for their purposes, a humid tropical volcanic forest with enormous fruit bat colonies, a macaque population, abundant standing water, and constant skin trauma. Things to discuss:

- **Tetanus booster**, if you're not current. You will be cut.
- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** Bats in numbers that "are hard to state," plus macaques and civets. Pre-exposure doesn't replace post-exposure treatment, but it simplifies it and buys time, which matters when you're far from help.
- **Hepatitis A, typhoid**, and whatever regional vaccines apply to a Southeast Asian–type tropical environment (Japanese encephalitis is worth asking about, since you'll be amid pools and mosquitoes).
- **Leptospirosis.** Warm water pools, rodents likely, animal urine everywhere. Doxycycline prophylaxis is sometimes used for high-exposure trips, and it also covers some other tropical infections. Ask.
- **Mosquito-borne disease.** "Larvae in every cup" means mosquitoes. Ask about malaria prophylaxis for the analogous region and take bite prevention seriously regardless.
- **Macaque B virus (herpes B).** Macaques can carry a virus that is harmless to them and potentially fatal to humans. Know the protocol before you go: if bitten or scratched, scrub the wound with soap and running water for fifteen minutes, then get medical care and antiviral treatment urgently. Carry written instructions for this, because you won't remember them clearly in there.
- **Respiratory baseline.** If you have asthma or any lung condition, the sulphur dioxide haze ("vog") may be a serious problem. Get your doctor's opinion and carry your rescue inhaler. Honestly consider not going.
- **Hearing baseline.** Get a hearing test before and after. The trip may cost you some hearing, and you'll want to know.
- **A broad-spectrum oral antibiotic** and a course of antifungal, prescribed in advance, with clear instructions on when to start them.

### Physical preparation

- **Heat acclimatisation.** Spend ten to fourteen days before the trip doing daily exercise in hot, humid conditions, or in a heat chamber or sauna protocol. It measurably improves your sweating, blood volume, and heat tolerance. It won't make the basin comfortable, but it might keep you from collapsing.
- **Scrambling fitness.** The basin is not hiking. It's slow, careful, full-body clambering over broken ground and through tangles, often on all fours, with a pack. Train your legs, grip, core, and balance. If you can, practise on real fresh lava fields (Hawaiian ʻaʻā or Icelandic lava is the closest analogue). Learn what shelly, gas-bubble crust feels like when it breaks.
- **Low-light movement.** Practise moving over rough terrain at night by headlamp. It's a skill, and it's exhausting until you have it.
- **Noise inoculation.** This may sound absurd, but spend time doing tasks such as tying knots, reading a map, and doing first aid while listening to very loud, chaotic, unpleasant sound through headphones. You'll learn how badly your mind degrades and which tasks you need to simplify.

### Planning preparation

- **Find a local guide.** People from the higher country walk in for obsidian. They know the ways in and out, what the cords on the trunks mean, which areas have gone bad lately, and how to deal with the troops. They are the most valuable resource you have. Hire someone, pay them well, and defer to them. If they say an area is wrong today, it is.
- **Write your rules down now.** Turnaround times. Maximum foray length. Gas alarm thresholds and responses. What you do if separated. What you do if someone is injured. Laminate them. In the basin you'll follow a card far better than you'll reason.
- **Set a base on the rim.** The old higher ground is outside the haze, quieter, cooler, and stable. Sleep, dry out, clean wounds, and recover there. Go down in forays.
- **Leave a detailed plan** with someone outside who will act if you don't return.

## Gear

### Against the rock (the most important category)

The rock is fresh, unweathered basalt with glassy obsidian rinds. It behaves like broken glass layered over rubble. It destroys soft gear and skin with astonishing speed.

- **Boots:** Heavy, stiff-soled, full-leather or leather-synthetic mountaineering or lava-field boots with thick lug soles and a protective rand. Not trail runners. The sole needs some heat tolerance too, since the ground is warm and near vents it's hot. Expect to ruin a pair on a long trip. Break them in beforehand.
- **Gaiters:** Tough, full-height ones. They protect your shins from glass and help against leeches.
- **Gloves:** Cut-resistant gloves (high cut rating, like those used for glass handling or butchery) with a grippy palm. Wear them constantly. You'll put your hands on the rock hundreds of times a day, often without seeing it. Bring several pairs, because they'll get soaked and foul.
- **Knee and elbow pads:** Low-profile, like those used for caving or construction. You'll be crawling and scrambling.
- **Clothing:** Long sleeves and long trousers in tough, lightweight, fast-draining synthetic fabric, ideally with abrasion-resistant panels at the knees, seat, and forearms. Cotton will stay wet forever and rot. Treat clothing with permethrin in advance against insects and leeches. Accept that clothes worn in the basin may be write-offs. The smell alone may make them unwearable anywhere else.
- **Helmet:** A light climbing or caving helmet. Grit falls when roots split rock. Branches, fruit, and guano fall constantly, and you'll hit your head on low roots and branches you can't see. It's also a good mount for your headlamp.
- **Eye protection:** Wraparound safety glasses, clear or amber-tinted, not dark. Obsidian flakes, falling grit, twigs at eye level, and acidic drips are all threats. Anti-fog coating is essential in this humidity and will still only partly work.

### Against the air

The vents produce volcanic gases. The haze-lid is sulphurous. Low points, lava tubes, fresh collapse pits, and areas near new vents can accumulate gases that are toxic or simply lack oxygen.

- **Multi-gas monitor:** A personal, calibrated, alarmed monitor that reads at least oxygen, hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), sulphur dioxide (SO₂), and carbon dioxide (CO₂). Clip it near your face. Treat it as non-negotiable.
    - **Why it matters so much:** H₂S at dangerous concentrations deadens your sense of smell, so the rotten-egg smell can seem to fade just as the gas gets worse. CO₂ has no smell at all and pools invisibly in hollows. You cannot rely on your senses.
    - **Rule:** If the alarm sounds, leave immediately, uphill and away from hollows, without discussing it. Discuss it later.
- **Respirator:** A half-face elastomeric respirator with combination cartridges: acid gas (for SO₂ and H₂S at low levels) plus P100 particulate filters. The particulate filters also protect against obsidian dust and against fungal spores from guano (histoplasmosis is a real risk in bat roosts and tubes). A respirator won't save you from oxygen deficiency or high CO₂. Only leaving will.
- **Carry spare cartridges.** Humidity degrades them.

### Against the noise

You face a real dilemma here. You need hearing protection, but you also need to hear falling trees, cracking roofs, hissing vents, and your companions.

- **Best option:** Electronic level-dependent earmuffs or earbuds (the kind shooters use). They amplify quiet sounds and clamp down on loud ones. Choose waterproof or water-resistant models, and expect humidity to shorten their life.
- **Backup:** Flat-attenuation "musician's" earplugs, which reduce volume without making everything muffled.
- **Always carry foam plugs** for sleeping or for the worst hours.
- **Communication:** Don't plan on talking. Agree on hand signals and light signals in advance, such as a circled light for "OK?", a steady light for "come to me", and rapid flashing for "danger or stop". If you can, use waterproof radios with throat mics or bone-conduction earpieces. Whistles won't be heard over cicadas. Tug signals on a shared line are a good backup in dense stretches.

### Light

Plan on functional darkness nearly all the time.

- **Two headlamps per person**, plus a small handheld torch, plus spare batteries or charging. Everything must be waterproof. Expect failures, since condensation gets into everything.
- **Adjustable output and red or amber modes.** This matters for two reasons:
    1. **Insects.** A bright white light in the basin will draw moths, flies, and katydids in clouds, into your face, mouth, and collar. Dim, warm-coloured light draws far fewer.
    2. **The glow.** The bioluminescent fungi trace the shapes of the forest floor, including fallen trunks, root masses, and litter. With dark-adapted eyes you can read the ground by it surprisingly well, and it's one of the loveliest things you'll ever see. Bright white light ruins your dark adaptation for twenty minutes or more.
- **Chemical light sticks** for marking a position, a companion
