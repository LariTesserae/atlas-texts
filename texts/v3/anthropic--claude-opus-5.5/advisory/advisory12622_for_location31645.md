# Preparing for the Throat

## First, Understand What You're Actually Doing

Strip away the poetry and the Throat is this: a cold, supersaturated geothermal jet of enormous force, on a high and geologically active mountain, enclosing a small wet chamber that you can only enter or leave by throwing your body through a moving wall of water during a gap that lasts a fraction of a second. Inside, you cannot speak, cannot hear, cannot get dry, and at night cannot see. The air is mostly spray. The floor is soft stone that remembers you.

It is also, by every account, one of the most quietly overwhelming places a person can stand in. People don't come out of it the same.

Prepare for both. The practical preparation keeps you alive. The other kind keeps you from being foolish once you're inside, when the place starts working on you and your judgment is the first thing to go numb.

---

## Part One: Your Body

### Cold-water conditioning
This matters more than anything else. You will be soaked within seconds of reaching the base of the column and will stay soaked until well after you leave. The water arrives "cold as a knife," probably just above freezing, and the chamber is filled with its spray. Outside, you are near the snowline in mountain wind.

Weeks before you go:
- **Practice cold immersion** in controlled conditions, with someone watching. Learn how your body reacts to the cold-shock gasp, the involuntary inhalation when icy water hits your chest. That gasp is what drowns people. You will feel it at the moment you throw yourself through the wall, and you need it to be familiar rather than a panic.
- **Learn your own hypothermia signs.** Clumsy fingers, slurred thinking, stubbornness, the sudden conviction that you're fine. In a place where you can't talk to your companion, you need to recognize these in yourself.

### Altitude
The Throat sits high, on bare rock above the vegetation and just under the permanent snow. Acclimatize properly in the valley and on the lower slopes for several days. Altitude sickness plus cold plus sensory overload is a terrible combination, and some early altitude symptoms (headache, nausea, confusion) look exactly like what the infrasound will do to you inside. Arrive already knowing how your body feels at that height.

### Impact
Those who've done it say the rising wall "hits like a falling tree." Take that literally. You are passing through a sheet of water moving upward at speed. It will try to lift you, twist you, and throw you back. Train your neck and core. If you can, find a powerful waterfall in your own world and practice passing through the edge of it, safely and with a spotter, until leading low with your shoulder, chin tucked, becomes instinct.

### Fitness in general
You need to scramble up fresh volcanic rock, crouch in cold spray while counting, lunge explosively, and still have enough left to do it again on the way out, colder and more tired. Leaving is harder than arriving. Train for the exit, not the entrance.

## Part Two: Your Mind

### The noise will change you
The sound is not just loud. It is continuous, directionless, and has a deep infrasonic floor, the mountain "resonating like a struck bell." Sustained infrasound does known things to human beings. It causes unease, dread, nausea, pressure in the chest, blurred or shaking vision, and a very specific feeling that someone else is present. Add continuous white noise, which the brain responds to by manufacturing patterns, and you get the "words" in the stutter.

This doesn't mean the words aren't real. I wouldn't presume to tell you what the Throat is or isn't saying. But you should go in knowing that your own nervous system will be producing meaning. Whatever you hear, you'll want to be able to tell which part came from the mountain and which part came from you. Go in steady. Don't go grieving, exhausted, or looking for a sign. The place will give you one whether or not it's real.

### Loss of voice, loss of self-talk
Visitors say that within an hour "you've forgotten you have one," meaning a voice. Many people rely on inner speech to stay oriented: *okay, the exit is behind me, I have forty minutes left.* In the Throat that inner voice gets drowned out. Before you go, practice holding a plan in your body rather than in words. Rehearse your exit so many times it becomes muscle memory. Decide your turnaround time before you're inside and never renegotiate it once you're in.

### Vection and dazzle
The light bands crawl constantly upward, so the whole room seems to rise. This is a powerful motion illusion. Your eyes say you're moving and your inner ear says you aren't, and some people get severe motion sickness from it. Others lose their sense of up and down. Practice fixing your gaze on something still, such as the floor, your own hands, or the basin of still water in the center. That bowl is probably there for more than drinking. It's the one thing in the room that doesn't move. Use it.

### Decide in advance about the long stay
Rule Five says that if you stay a full day and a night, the stutters begin to align into something repeating. This is the lure of the place, and it's why some people lose the count and never leave.

Decide before you ever reach the mountain whether you are a short visitor or a long one. A short visit (under an hour) is a serious but manageable undertaking. A full day and night is a survival expedition: continuous cold immersion, total darkness, no rest, no warmth, no voice. It should only be attempted with specialized gear, a partner, an outside support person, and experience from several shorter visits first. Don't let the room make this decision for you after you arrive.

## Part Three: Gear

The guiding principle is to **dress like a whitewater swimmer and pack like a diver.** Everything you wear must work while fully wet, and everything you carry must be tethered, because Rule One is true in the most literal way. Drop something and it becomes part of the room.

### What to wear
- **A drysuit** with latex seals, or a thick wetsuit at minimum. A drysuit with a warm fleece undersuit is far better, because it's the difference between an hour and a day. Wear a hood.
- **Neoprene gloves.** Thin enough to tie a knot, thick enough to handle the rock. The fissure edges are "sharp enough to cut rope," so they'll cut hands.
- **Sturdy boots with soft, grippy soles.** Fresh volcanic rock outside is razor-edged. Inside, the floor is slick with living film and sometimes soft "like wet sugar." Avoid aggressive lugs that will gouge the new stone. Consider neoprene socks under the boots.
- **A whitewater helmet.** The entry impact can throw your head into the rock, and there are delicate shelves and a stone ladder you could strike in the confusion of landing.
- **A buoyancy vest or padded impact vest.** It isn't for floating. It's padding for your ribs when the wall hits.

### For breathing and seeing
- **A dive mask.** The spray is so thick "it's barely air," and it will blind you. A mask lets you see through the curtain during entry and keeps your eyes open inside.
- **Something to breathe through.** Locals use a cloth, which works but saturates. Better is a cloth or buff over the mouth plus a snorkel-type tube with the opening pointed downward, so spray doesn't fall straight in. Practice breathing through it while getting drenched. Your breathing should feel boring before you go.
- **Anti-fog treatment** for the mask. Bring extra, and expect the mineral film to build on the lens over time. Bring a small soft cloth, tethered.

### For sound
- **Hearing protection, seriously.** A sound "felt on your teeth and your breastbone" is doing damage over hours. Wear foam earplugs under the hood. They won't block the infrasound, which comes through your body anyway, but they'll spare your cochlea from the collapse at the top and the tearing hiss of the shear. You can take them out briefly to listen if you choose. Keep them in by default.

### For light
- **Waterproof headlamp, plus two backups**, all tethered, with fresh batteries. At night inside the Throat there is *nothing*: no stars, no moon, only black and roar. If you're caught there after dark without light, you won't find the exit. You won't even be able to read your own watch.
- **Chemical lightsticks.** They're reliable, waterproof, and need no switches your numb fingers can fumble.

### For time and counting
- **A dive watch with a vibrating or clearly visible timer.** Set your turnaround time before entry. You won't hear any alarm.
- **Consider a second timer** set to buzz every few minutes, as a reminder that time is passing. Time dissolves in that noise.

### For communication
- **A diver's slate** with a grease pencil, which writes underwater and in spray. Tether it.
- **Pre-agreed hand signals** with your companion, practiced until automatic: *I'm cold. I'm leaving. Time. Help. Look. Where is the exit? Stop.* Keep them simple.
- **Your own knot-cord**, if you wish to take part in the custom. Make it of natural fiber, plant or hair, never synthetic. Decide on your knots beforehand. More on this below.

### For gas
This is my intuition rather than anything the accounts say, but it's worth taking seriously. Water pushed up from "deep and hot" rock, carrying that much dissolved mineral, very often carries dissolved gas too, commonly carbon dioxide and sometimes hydrogen sulfide. The top of the column is open, and the turbulence probably keeps the air mixed. But low places, such as the floor near the fissure and the hollows between shelves, could pool heavier gas. The accounts describe people lying on their bellies to peer at the pale thing, which is exactly the posture that would expose you.

- **Carry a small waterproof gas monitor** (CO₂ at minimum) clipped at chest height, with a vibrating alarm.
- **Treat sudden headache, air hunger, or confusion near the floor as a gas warning**, and lift your head immediately.

### For warmth afterward
You'll have to leave all of this outside, at a safe distance from the spray, ideally in a sheltered spot out of the wind:
- A complete set of dry clothes in a waterproof bag
- Insulated jacket, hat, gloves
- A stove, fuel, a pot, and something sweet and hot to drink
- An emergency shelter or bivvy
- A towel

The moment you come out of the Throat, soaking and exhausted into mountain wind, is the most dangerous moment of the entire journey. Hypothermia doesn't happen inside, where you're adrenalized and distracted. It happens afterward, on the rocks, when your body finally notices.

## Part Four: Before You Climb

### Never go alone
Tradition says people come "alone, or in twos." As a traveler with no local knowledge, you should be a two, with ideally a third person who stays outside. That person watches the curtain and the time, holds the warm kit, and goes for help if you don't come out by the agreed hour.

### Seek out someone who's been there
If there are locals who've visited, find them and ask with humility. Learn what the stutter feels like, how long the gaps last, where the downhill thin point is, how the room has changed. Remember Rule Four, though: *the room is always smaller than you remember.* As the fissure narrows, the jet becomes "stronger and louder and thinner." Any information more than a few years old is out of date, and the rhythm of the stutter may have changed. Trust what you observe with your own eyes on the day over any account.

### Clean your gear
You are carrying a whole other world on your boots and clothing. The Throat's living things are few and specialized: the mineral-feeding film, the domed mosses racing their own burial, and whatever the pale thing is. Scrub and disinfect everything you'll wear. Don't bring seeds, soil, or foreign microbes into a place that has almost nothing and grows slowly into permanence.

## Part Five: Learning the Count

Give yourself at least an hour, preferably much longer, outside the Throat before attempting entry. Stand on the downhill side at a safe distance and watch the fissure's narrowest point.

- **Watch before you listen.** From outside, you'll see the rising wall go "glassy and thin" when the pressure stutters. Watch many cycles. Count between them on your watch. Is it regular? Does it drift? Do some stutters last longer than others?
- **Feel it.** Put your palm on the rock. The stutter probably travels through the stone as a change in the vibration. Learning to feel the count, not just see it, will help you inside, where you'll be half-blind in spray.
- **Find your spot.** Identify exactly where you'll stand, which rock you'll push off from, and the line you'll travel. Note what the landing looks like, as much as can be glimpsed.
- **Rehearse the lunge** on dry ground nearby, over and over. Crouch, count, go low, lead with the shoulder, chin tucked, hands forward to catch yourself.

When you move in close, with your "back to the mountain" in the falling curtain, you'll be drenched and pounded. The cold will hit you hard. Let the gasp pass before you start counting. Give it thirty breaths. Don't go on your first chance. Wait until you've felt three or four cycles from inside the curtain and can predict the next one.

## Part Six: Entry

When you go, go fully. Half-hearted lunges get caught by the wall and thrown back, or worse, spun sideways into the fissure's cutting edges.

You'll hit the thinned wall, feel it try to lift you, and then suddenly land on your knees on wet stone in what the accounts call "a sudden bright hush that is not quiet at all."

What to do in the first thirty seconds:
1. **Stay low.** Don't stand up yet. Your balance will be gone.
2. **Check your breathing.** Get the cloth and tube sorted. Cough out any water.
3. **Turn around and look at where you came in.** This is the single most important thing you'll do inside. Fix the exit point in your memory: its relation to the ladder (north wall), the bowl (center), the biggest shelf, the nesting towers. This is your way out. You may not be able to leave a marker, since everything gets stoned, but a lightstick laid at the exit point will glow for hours, and you'll carry it out with you.
4. **Check on your companion.** Hand signal: *OK?*
5. **Look at your feet.** You've landed on soft, growing stone, probably on top of centuries of handprints. Be gentle from this moment forward.

## Part Seven: Inside

### Moving
Move slowly, low, deliberately. The floor is terraced and ridged, sometimes soft, slicked with living green film in places. The shelves at the base of the walls are frilled and delicate, "like frozen splashes." Break them and you damage something that took years to form and won't be repaired. Nothing here has "weathered edges," which means nothing here is strong yet.

Don't:
- **Climb the ladder.** It's "too fragile to climb" and part of the wall now. Breaking it would destroy something irreplaceable and would probably injure you.
- **Disturb the cairns.** They're figures in shrouds, and some are very old.
- **Touch the green film** on the shelves. It's a living skin, probably easily damaged by a gloved hand.
- **Crush the moss domes.** Each is winning or losing a race against the stone, one summer at a time.
- **Approach the nesting towers.** There are "never more than a handful" of fall-swifts, and each spring some don't come back. These birds are hanging on by a thread. Stay back, keep your light off their nests, and when they pierce the curtain, stay out of the path they use.

### The fissure and the pale thing
If you want to look down into the spring, do it with great care:
- Watch your gas monitor.
- Stay back from the edges, which are sharp and may be undercut.
- Don't reach in. Don't drop anything in. Don't shine a powerful light down for long. The creature has no eyes that anyone's seen, but it's colorless, which suggests a life lived in darkness. Light and heat and your presence may matter to it in ways you don't know.
- If it's truly as old as the fissure, or the only one of its kind, the least you owe it is not to be the visitor who harmed it.

### Breathing
Keep breathing through the cloth. Watch for the slow panic of feeling like you're not getting enough air. It's partly real (spray is not air) and partly the infrasound. Breathe slow and long. If you begin to feel air-hungry and dizzy, check your gas monitor, then move toward the center of the room, where the light and spray from above are strongest and the air likely freshest.

### The bowl
Everyone who comes drinks from it, and you may want to join that custom. A caution, though: deep geothermal water that "tastes of cold metal" may carry arsenic, antimony, or other dissolved metals. A ceremonial sip is almost certainly fine. A long drink isn't wise. Bring your own drinking water, and drink it. You'll be dehydrating without noticing, surrounded by water you shouldn't drink.

Look into the bowl, too. It's the only still surface. It will steady your eyes and your stomach.
