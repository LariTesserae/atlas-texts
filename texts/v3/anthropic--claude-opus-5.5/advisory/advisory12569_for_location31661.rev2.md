# Preparing for the Seam

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

The Seam is not dangerous the way a war zone or a wilderness is. Nothing here will chase you, and nobody wants to hurt you. The danger is chemical and mechanical, and most of it comes from carelessness. Everything around you is hot, sharp, moving slowly, or growing slowly, and the people who live there have built their whole culture around attentiveness. You will be safe if you arrive attentive, and at risk if you arrive distracted.

It also helps to understand the Seam emotionally before you go. It is a place in slow retreat. Every resident knows the town is getting smaller, and that one day it will be too small. That knowledge sits under everything, the way the engine stroke does. People will not be gloomy about it, but they will be serious about it. Come prepared to be quiet and to take small things seriously.

## Your Body

### Heat and Humidity

This is the first thing that will hit you, and it never lets up. You will be sweating within a minute of arrival and for your entire stay. Because the air is already saturated, sweat won't evaporate efficiently, so it won't cool you the way it's supposed to.

- **Acclimatize beforehand if you can.** Spend a week or two before the trip doing light exercise in hot, humid conditions, such as a sauna, steam room, or tropical greenhouse. Your body can learn to sweat earlier and lose fewer salts, and that genuinely helps.
- **Bring electrolytes, not just water.** You will lose salt constantly. Use oral rehydration salts, electrolyte tablets, or salted snacks. Plain water alone, drunk heavily, can leave you dizzy and weak.
- **Don't count on the local water until you've asked.** The brine is undrinkable. It is mineral-saturated and oily, and it is the raw material of an industrial product. The drizzle from above carries dissolved stone. The condensers at the top probably yield distilled water, which is likely what residents drink, but ask how it's handled. Steam from a sulphurous vent may carry volatile compounds. Bring your own supply for the first day and a purification method you trust.
- **Watch for heat exhaustion**: headache, nausea, clumsiness, irritability, or a sudden stop in sweating. The last one is a genuine emergency. Go up the forty stairs into cooler air.
- **Plan short first visits.** Two or three hours, then climb out. Extend gradually.

### The Air

The faint smell of eggs is hydrogen sulphide, or something like it. At low levels it is merely unpleasant. The trouble is that your nose stops detecting it after a while, so its absence tells you nothing.

Take the animal behavior seriously. Dogs refuse the last flight of stairs. Birds turn away. No insect lives there. The residents may have adapted, or they may simply be enduring something with long-term effects nobody has studied. You are not obligated to find out the hard way.

- **Carry a personal gas monitor** if your home universe offers one. It should cover hydrogen sulphide at minimum, and ideally carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and oxygen levels as well. Deep fissures can pool heavy gases, and furnaces produce combustion byproducts.
- **Know your warning signs**: persistent headache, eye irritation, a metallic taste, confusion, or shortness of breath. If you notice them, leave and don't argue with yourself about it.
- **Be most cautious on the lowest level**, near the furnace mouths and the throat. It is the hottest part of the Seam, the closest to the source, and the place most likely to have poor air.

### The Asbestos

The pipe lagging is described as cracked asbestos board. In a town where everything is chipped, scraped, and brushed against, fibers are almost certainly present in the air near the pipe bundles.

- **Pack a fitted respirator** with P100 or HEPA-grade filters. Wearing one in wet heat is miserable, so bring spare filters, because damp filters clog and become hard to breathe through.
- At minimum, wear it when walking past pipe bundles, during maintenance work, and anywhere chipping is happening.
- Don't touch the lagging, and don't brush your clothing against it if you can help it. Wash your outer layer after every visit, away from where you sleep.
- Be discreet about it. Residents may not wear masks, and you don't want to imply they're poisoning themselves, even if they are.

### Claustrophobia and Vertigo

The Seam is extraordinarily tight. You cannot stretch your arms out anywhere. The ceiling is a ribbon of darkness where two walls nearly touch. The walkways are gratings stacked six or seven levels high, with red furnace-light glowing up through the bars beneath you.

- If you have any claustrophobia at all, test yourself honestly beforehand in crawlspaces, caves, or the engine rooms of ships. This place will not give you a way to "step outside for a minute" except the forty stairs.
- Open grating over a red glow is hard on people with a fear of heights. You will be walking on it constantly.
- Be aware that the constriction and the heat together can cause panic attacks even in people who have never had one. Learn a breathing technique before you go. As it happens, the engine stroke is a nearly perfect breathing pace: inhale on the downstroke, exhale on the return.

## What to Wear

The environment asks for contradictory things. It is hot, so you want to wear little, but it is sharp, scalding, and dripping, so you want to be covered. Covered wins.

- **Gloves with leather palms.** They are non-negotiable, and they are what locals wear. Brass is hot enough to burn and iron is always warm. Bring two pairs, since one will always be damp.
- **Long sleeves and long trousers** in a tough, tightly woven fabric that breathes. The fracture edges of the basalt are sharp enough to cut a sleeve, which means they're sharp enough to cut the arm inside it. Avoid loose weaves and knits, which snag. Avoid heavy cotton, which will be soaked by sweat, never dry, and chafe. Choose something fast-drying.
- **A close-fitting cap or hood.** It protects against the constant mineral drizzle, which will otherwise leave a crust in your hair, and against low-hanging pipes and chains. Don't wear a wide brim, because there's no room for one.
- **Boots with excellent grip**, closed toes, and a heat-resistant sole. The gratings carry a wet mineral film, and slipping here means falling against something sharp or hot. Choose a snug fit, with no dangling laces or loops that could catch on grating or chain.
- **Nothing that dangles.** No scarves, lanyards, loose straps, long necklaces, or loose long hair. Braid or pin it back. The engines have swinging parts, and the Rules warn about "a swinging chain or a lurching gantry."
- **Eyewear.** Glasses will fog and collect mineral spotting from the drip, so bring a soft cloth and expect to wipe them constantly. Contact lenses may be uncomfortable in the sulphurous air. Consider safety glasses anyway, because chipping sends up flakes of sinter.
- **Plastic or coated fasteners over bare metal ones** where possible. See the next section for why.

## What to Bring and How to Carry It

### Pack Small

Bring a slim, body-hugging bag, not a backpack that sticks out. You will be squeezing past pipes and people constantly, and anything that protrudes will catch, scrape, or knock into things. Think in terms of what fits against your body.

### Tether Everything

You are walking on grating over a hot throat in the earth. Anything you drop may fall through several levels, into a furnace, or into the fissure itself, and it will not come back. Tie your tools, lights, and notebooks to yourself. Keep pockets closed.

### Fight the Sinter

This is the strangest preparation, and it is essential. The Seam's first rule is **nothing stays still unattended.** Mineral-laden condensation lands on everything and leaves stone behind. Bolts seize. Doors seal. Railings grow knuckles of rock in a season.

You will be visiting for days, not seasons, but you'll still notice it:

- **Metal zippers, snaps, and buckles** will stiffen and crust. Work them regularly, and wipe and flex them each evening.
- **Hinged or threaded equipment**, such as camera lens mounts, bottle caps, knives, and tripod screws, should be wiped and turned daily. Bring a small amount of lubricant, and a small brush or pick for clearing grooves.
- **Don't set things down and walk away.** Even in a few days, a bag left on a damp surface may come away spotted with white crust.
- **Paper curls.** The locals keep books in tins, and you should do the same. Bring your notebooks, documents, maps, and anything precious in sealed containers with a desiccant pack. Replace the desiccant often, because it will saturate fast.
- **Electronics** are at serious risk from the combination of humidity, heat, mineral deposits, and possibly corrosive sulphur compounds. Bring only what you need, sealed in waterproof cases, and expect to lose some anyway. Silver and copper contacts may tarnish from the sulphur.

### Light

Bring a small light, preferably a dim, warm-toned headlamp. The Seam rations its lamps, and blasting a bright white beam around may feel to residents like shouting. Use it for footing and close work, not for sightseeing.

### A Note on Color

This is easy to overlook. Sodium lamps render color very poorly, and the lower-pressure kind render almost no color at all. Everything looks amber, grey, and black, with a red cast from below. That means:

- Color-coded items become unreliable. Medications, maps, wiring, and cable markings may all be affected.
- Blood may be hard to distinguish from grime. Check small cuts by touch and in your own light.
- Label things by shape, texture, or position rather than color.

### Medical Kit

- Burn gel and dressings, for the hot brass.
- Wound closure strips and antiseptic. Basalt and volcanic glass make clean, deep cuts. Clean them thoroughly, since mineral grit may be embedded.
- Anti-chafing balm and powder, for your inner thighs, underarms, and feet.
- Eye wash.
- Antifungal cream. Constant damp skin invites problems, even if the Seam seems oddly free of fungi.
- Headache medication.
- Rehydration salts.

## Learning the Rhythm

The Seam's third rule, **keep time with the engines**, is the single most useful thing you can learn before you arrive.

The beam engines stroke about once every eleven seconds. The whole town moves to it without thinking. People pour tea on the downstroke and hammer on the up. That shared rhythm is a safety system. When everyone moves together, chains swing predictably, gantries shift predictably, and people step aside predictably.

**Practice before you go.** Set a metronome or a timer to an eleven-second interval, and live with it in the background for a few days. Count "down... two... three... four... five... up... two... three... four... five... six." Learn to feel where the stroke is without counting. Walk to it. Breathe to it.

When you arrive, spend your first half hour just standing somewhere safe with your feet on the iron, feeling the engines through your soles until the rhythm is in your body. Watch how the locals move. Don't rush past a moving machine on the wrong beat. If you're unsure, wait one full stroke. Nothing in the Seam happens quickly, and nobody will mind the pause.

## Social Preparation

### Speak Low

This is both custom and safety. Voices ring off the hard rock, and a raised voice means emergency, specifically a burst line or a jammed jack. If you shout to get someone's attention, or laugh loudly at a joke, people will freeze and look for the danger.

- Practice speaking at a murmur before you go. It's harder than it sounds, especially when you're hot, excited, or frustrated.
- **Learn what an actual alarm sounds like** on your first day, and ask what to do when you hear one. You will want to know where to go.
- If you genuinely need emergency help, raise your voice. That's what raised voices are for.

### When the Rock Cracks

At some point you may hear a deep, grinding crack from far back in the stone. It is the walls taking another fraction of their thumbnail.

**Do what the locals do: go still.** Don't run or speak. Wait until the drip resumes and the engines take their next stroke. Someone will note the time in a ledger. Once you're sure things are normal, it is appropriate to quietly ask whether anyone needs a hand checking their jacks.

This will likely frighten you the first time. Your instinct will scream *earthquake, get out*. Prepare yourself mentally for the fact that it is normal, that these people have lived with it for decades, and that bolting in panic up a crowded iron stair is more dangerous than the crack itself.

### Respect the Shrinking

The town can only get smaller. Nothing new is built, only rearranged or cut down.

- **Don't offer to build anything**, and don't suggest improvements, expansions, or "wouldn't it be nice if you added…" ideas. It will land as either naive or cruel.
- **Be gentle about lost rooms.** When an old resident tells you about a room that no longer exists, listen. It may be something like a grief story, or a story about a childhood home. Don't treat it as a curiosity.
- **Don't take up space.** Your presence costs room in a town that has less every year. Be compact. Stand against walls when people pass, keeping in mind that the walls are sharp. Offer your spot.

### Don't Touch What You Don't Understand

- **Leave the small copper lines alone.** Nobody knows what they carry, and the locals won't remove them for exactly that reason. Take that caution seriously.
- **Don't pick the ferns.** They grow toward the lamps, and in the dark you could map the town by them. They may be a quiet navigational aid. They are also some of the only living things there.
- **Don't scrape the copper-green algae** off the warm pipes out of curiosity.
- **Handle seam-salt with care.** Its composition isn't agreed on, and people are vague about why it's valued. Don't taste it, don't rub it on your skin, and think carefully before carrying it back across universes. If you buy some, seal it well and label it.
- **Don't lean on anything.** It's hot, it's sharp, or it's someone's jack assembly.

### Offer Your Hands, Carefully

The crews do two kinds of work: cutting the town narrower, and chipping it free of stone. It is endless. If you stay longer than a day or two, offering to help with chipping bolts, freeing hinges, or scraping sinter may be the most respectful thing you can do. Ask first, follow instructions exactly, and work on the stroke.

## Sleep and Time

There is no day or night in the Seam, only shifts. Your internal clock will drift.

- Bring a watch you trust, sealed against moisture.
- Ask about the shift schedule and try to sleep when the town sleeps.
- Lodging will be tiny, hot, and damp. Bring a thin, quick-drying sleeping sheet and a light towel. You will wake up sweating.
- Many people find the engine rhythm deeply sleep-inducing. Others lie awake counting strokes. Bring earplugs just in case, but try sleeping without them first. The engine sound is also information, and if it stops, you want to know.

## The Silence That Isn't There

Prepare yourself for something that sounds minor and isn't: **there are no animals.** There are no rats, flies, spiders at the lamps, or scratching in the walls.

Most people don't consciously realize how much they rely on the small sounds of other living things until those sounds are gone. The Seam's soundscape is full of drips, ticks, hisses, and the engine's breath, but none of it is alive. Some visitors describe it as peaceful. Others say that by the second or third day it produces a low, persistent unease they can't name, a feeling of being in a place life has declined to enter.

It helps to know this in advance so you can recognize the feeling when it comes. It also helps to remember what that absence might mean for your health, as discussed above. Listen to your body as well as to the rock.

## What It Will Actually Be Like

Here is what my intuition tells me.

You'll come down the forty stairs and the heat will close over you, and for the first ten minutes all you'll feel is discomfort: the sweat, the smell, the tightness, the sharp edges everywhere. You'll be too big and too loud and out of rhythm. You'll bump things. You'll want to leave.

Then, somewhere in the first hour, the engine stroke will get into you. You'll notice you've stopped counting and started moving with it. You'll notice you're whispering. The amber haze will stop feeling murky and start feeling close, like lamplight in a small room. You'll see someone pour tea exactly on the downstroke and it will seem obviously correct.

At the end of a shift, you'll find yourself standing in front of a thick glass vein, watching it glow green, knowing perfectly well that it isn't glowing. Nearly everyone does this. Let yourself.

When you leave, the forty stairs up will feel like surfacing. The quarry air will be startlingly cold and dry, and the sky will seem enormously, almost indecently wide. For a few days afterward, you may catch yourself pausing mid-motion to wait for a stroke that isn't coming. You may find ordinary voices too loud. You may miss the drip.

And you may find yourself thinking about the Seam at odd moments, about the fact that it is still there, a little narrower than when you left, with someone chipping a hinge free by sodium light and someone writing the time of the last crack in a ledger, all of it kept alive by attention.

## Quick Checklist

**Body:** heat acclimatization, electrolytes, water supply, gas monitor, respirator with spare filters, a claustrophobia self-test, and a breathing technique.

**Clothing:** leather-palmed gloves (2 pairs), long tough breathable layers, a close cap, grippy heat-resistant boots, nothing dangling, eye protection and a cleaning cloth.

**Gear:** a slim body-hugging bag, tethers, sealed tins for paper, desiccant packs, sealed electronics, a dim warm headlamp, a brush/pick and lubricant for fasteners, and labels that don't rely on color.

**Medical:** burn care, wound closure, antiseptic, anti-chafing balm, eye wash, antifungal cream, headache medication, rehydration salts.

**Skills:** the 11-second rhythm, speaking at a murmur, freezing at the crack, and knowing the alarm.

**Manners:** don't shout, don't lean, don't build, don't touch the copper lines, don't pick the ferns, don't leave things still, listen to stories of lost rooms, and offer your hands.

**Speak low. Listen to the rock.**
