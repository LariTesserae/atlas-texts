# Preparing for Orrin Low

## The Short Version

You are going to a place that never goes quiet, never dries out, and never stays still. The ground will try to cut you and the air will try to dampen you. The noise will get into your bones and stay for a while after you leave. Pack for sharp rock, cold fog, constant pounding, and a community that works hard and speaks in short sentences. Plan on staying at least eleven days, because that is how often the boat comes.

---

## 1. Timing Your Arrival and Departure

**The steamer is your lifeline.** It calls every eleven days. Whatever door you use to reach this world, assume that leaving by conventional means requires that boat. If you miss it, you wait another eleven days. Build in slack. A two-day visit is not really possible here. The honest minimum is one steamer cycle.

**Arrive by sea if you can.** Coming in on the steamer, you will hear the stamps before you see the town. It will be a faint throb across the water, then a drumming, then something you feel through the deck. By the time you step onto the jetty you'll have had an hour or two to adjust. If you step directly into the town from elsewhere, the noise will hit you all at once, and it is disorienting.

**Consider arriving in late morning.** The fog will have burned off and you'll see the field in its full mosaic. Arriving at dusk is beautiful, with the petrels circling and the windows glowing yellow. But you'll be finding your footing on unfamiliar, treacherous ground in failing light, with tens of thousands of birds overhead.

---

## 2. Your Feet: The Most Important Preparation

The field is described as cutting boots and dog pads. Take this literally.

- **Boots:** Heavy leather work boots with thick, stiff soles and full ankle support. Lugged rubber or hobnailed leather both work. Soft trail runners or light hiking shoes will be shredded by the clinker within days. If you have Vibram-soled mountaineering boots, bring them. Break them in beforehand, because you'll be walking constantly. The town is a kilometer long and nothing is close to anything else.
- **Bring a spare pair,** or at least resoling supplies. The steamer may bring boots, but not in your size and not soon.
- **Gaiters.** Clinker gets into boot tops, and fern-trenches are damp and full of slow-worms.
- **Wool socks, many pairs.** Everything will be slightly damp all the time. Rotate socks and dry them near a stove whenever you can.
- **Foot care kit:** moleskin, blister dressings, antiseptic, and something for fungal infections. Feet that stay damp for weeks will give you trouble.

**Learn to read the ground.** You will be walking across three kinds of surface:

1. **Ropy flow with deep moss:** soft, springy, mattress-like. It is pleasant, but the moss can hide cracks and voids. Test with your weight before committing. The cold air the moss breathes out is a sign of open space in the stone beneath.
2. **Clinker:** loose, sharp, glassy rubble. Slow down. Don't put your hands down to catch yourself if you slip, because this is how people lose palm skin.
3. **Crushed gravel and slurry-pond margins:** in the stripped zone behind the Stamp House. The gravel is easy walking. The rust-orange algae crust on the ponds is very slippery. Don't walk the pond edges.

**Collapsed lava tubes** are trenches three to four meters deep with near-vertical walls. They're full of shoulder-high ferns, which means the edge can be hard to see until you're at it. Uncollapsed tube roofs exist too, and nobody has surveyed which ones are sound. Stick to paths the locals use. There will be worn routes between buildings, and the tramway and pipe trestles make good landmarks.

**Gloves.** Bring sturdy leather work gloves. Any time you touch the rock, whether climbing, scrambling, or helping with anything, you'll want them.

---

## 3. Your Ears: The Noise

This is what will define your visit.

Forty-eight stamps, each falling about once every two seconds, add up to roughly twenty-four heavy impacts per second. They're staggered, so they blur into a continuous rolling pound that is too complex to count and too regular to tune out. It has not stopped in thirty-four years except for three days.

**Bring hearing protection in several forms:**
- **Foam or silicone earplugs,** a large supply. Locals use waxed cotton, and yours will be better.
- **Earmuffs** for time inside or near the Stamp House. Do not go inside without serious protection. Plugs plus muffs is not excessive.
- **Musician's earplugs** (flat-attenuation filters) for general wear around town. They reduce volume while letting you still follow speech, which you'll need.

**Sleep will be the hard part.** At the far end of town the stamps become a low uneven drumming. The basalt carries sound well, so you'll feel it through the floor and the bed frame. Then, after dusk, the petrels start: a purring "like a cat purring into a bottle," from burrows possibly directly under the house you're sleeping in. It goes all night, and it pulses in time with the stamps.

Some travelers find this deeply soothing, a kind of mechanical-organic lullaby. Others lie awake for three nights and then collapse. Bring:
- Earplugs rated for sleep
- A sleep mask. This is a far-north-feeling coast, and depending on season, nights may be short.
- Whatever mild sleep aid you trust, for the first few nights
- A folded blanket or foam pad to put under your bedroll, to dampen vibration coming up through the floor

**Learn some hand signals before you go,** or be ready to learn them on arrival. Stamp House workers talk with their hands. Even outside, people speak "loudly and bluntly, often in short phrases." Being able to point, gesture "wait," "yes," "no," "come," and "danger" will help you.

**Expect the rhythm to follow you home.** After a week or more here, many people will feel the pounding for days after leaving, a phantom beat in the chest and feet, and an odd unease in silence. Remember the petrels who would not come ashore when the stamps stopped. Give yourself quiet recovery time afterward, and don't be alarmed if silence feels wrong.

---

## 4. Your Lungs and Eyes: The Grit

The whole purpose of the town is to crush volcanic rock into a fine, hard, glassy abrasive. That means dust.

- **A good respirator mask** (N95/P100 or equivalent) for any time near the Stamp House, the sorting sheds, or the tramway when grit is being tipped. Fine glassy rock dust is the kind of thing that ruins lungs over years. For a short visit it's more irritant than danger, but there's no reason to breathe it.
- **Safety glasses or goggles.** Grit in the eye is extremely painful. Bring eyewash or saline.
- **Sunglasses.** The light is clear and flat, glancing off black rock and wet surfaces at midday. It's not snow-blindness bright, but it's tiring on the eyes.

The fog and damp keep much of the dust down outdoors. Inside the sorting sheds is another matter.

---

## 5. Clothing: Damp, Cool, Never Quite Wet

The climate here is cold sea fog, thin grey rain one day in four, heavy dew every morning, and cool bright sun between. It is a place that is "damp without ever being wet."

**Dress in layers, wool first:**
- **Base layer:** merino wool. It stays warm when damp, which matters more than anything here. Avoid cotton against the skin.
- **Mid layer:** a wool sweater or fleece.
- **Outer layer:** a waterproof, breathable shell with a hood. Waxed cotton or oilcloth would fit in with local style and handles sparks and grit better than thin synthetics.
- **Durable trousers.** Canvas or heavy wool will survive clinker scrapes. Lightweight hiking pants will tear.
- **A brimmed hat.** It shades your eyes in low sun, keeps drizzle off your face, and protects against the petrels at dusk. Tens of thousands of seabirds circling low over the town means droppings.
- **A scarf or neck gaiter** you can pull up over your mouth for fog, chill, or dust.

Bring a way to dry things. A mesh bag and cord for hanging clothes near a stove is enough. Nothing will dry outdoors.

---

## 6. Water and Food

**Water** comes from two sources:
- **The fog-net cistern:** tastes of tar and iron. It's presumably drinkable, since the town drinks it, but your gut is not their gut. Bring a filter and purification tablets, at least for the first days.
- **The condenser trough:** warm, distilled engine condensate. It is likely very clean, though distilled water is flat and mineral-poor. It feeds the gardens, so ask before drawing from it.

Water here is caught with effort. Every drop has been pulled out of fog or engine steam. Don't waste it, don't wash in the cistern, and ask how the household you're staying with manages it.

**Food** will be simple. There's kale, potatoes, rhubarb, and watercress from the moving gardens, plus whatever the steamer brings. Most likely that means flour, oats, salt meat, tinned goods, and tea. Fish and gulls' or petrels' eggs may be eaten seasonally. Ask, don't assume. There may be mutton or a few chickens.

- **Bring some supplementary food:** dense, calorie-rich things like nuts, chocolate, dried fruit, and hard cheese. You'll be cold, damp, and walking constantly, and you'll burn a lot.
- **Offering food is a good gift.** Something like coffee, sugar, dried fruit, spices, or good tea will be appreciated in a town that gets resupplied every eleven days.
- **Vitamin C** is probably fine with all that kale and cress, but a multivitamin is cheap insurance.

---

## 7. Lodging

There is no mention of an inn. Orrin Low is a working mill town where people live close to the work and move their houses. Your likely options:

1. **Boarding with a family.** Offer payment in whatever currency works, or goods, or labor. This is the best option for understanding the place.
2. **A bunk connected to the Stamp House or sorting sheds,** if there are workers' quarters. It will be very loud.
3. **One of the left-behind buildings** near the old quarry face. These are shells overgrown with moss and fern, quiet by local standards, and far from everything. Don't just move into one. Ask. Abandoned doesn't mean unowned, and it may matter to someone that their grandmother's house was left there.

If you stay in a sledge-house, know that petrels may nest underneath. Don't block or disturb the gaps. The purring under the floor is part of the house.

Bring your own **sleeping bag** rated for cold, damp conditions, and a **headlamp or lantern**. Only the Stamp House, sorting sheds, and doctor's house have electric light. Everyone else uses oil lamps. Bring extra batteries or a way to charge. There's a single dynamo line, and it isn't for you.

---

## 8. Health and Medical

There is a doctor, and the doctor has electric light. That's a good sign. Still:

- **Wound care is critical.** Clinker cuts are jagged and dirty. Bring antiseptic, sterile dressings, butterfly closures, antibiotic ointment, and ideally a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics from your own world's doctor.
- **Tetanus shot:** make sure it's current. You'll be around rusted iron, animals, and sharp rock.
- **Painkillers and anti-inflammatories.** Your legs, feet, and head (from the noise) will ache.
- **Any personal medications** in more-than-sufficient supply. Remember the eleven-day boat.
- **Stomach remedies** for adjusting to new water and food.

---

## 9. Animals

- **Petrels:** harmless, beautiful, everywhere at dusk and night. Don't reach into burrows or fissures. There may be chicks, and you may scare adults off nests.
- **The grey fox:** small, clever, a thief. Keep food sealed and off the ground. Don't feed it.
- **Dogs:** the town has them. Working dogs in a harsh place may not be friendly to strangers. Let their people introduce you.
- **Starlings:** they mimic the engine whistle and tram brakes. When you hear a whistle, it may not be the engine. This matters around the tramway, so look, don't just listen.
- **Gulls:** at the jetty, bold, and they'll steal food out of your hand.
- **Slow-worms:** legless lizards in the fern-trenches, harmless. Don't mistake them for snakes and panic.
- **The unnamed wading bird** at the settling ponds: if you know birds, bring a field guide from a comparable world and see if you can identify it. The town might appreciate knowing.

---

## 10. Hazards of the Machinery

This is a steam-age industrial site with no apparent safety regulations.

- **The tramway:** narrow gauge, carrying heavy loads of grit. The brakes shriek, but starlings mimic that too. Never walk along the rails.
- **The pipe trestles:** carry steam, water, and compressed air. Don't touch pipes. Steam lines burn, and a failed joint will scald. Watch your head where lines sag low.
- **The Stamp House:** do not enter without an escort and hearing protection. Cams, shafts, and falling iron columns the height of a man. Don't touch anything.
- **The winching:** once a week or so, the entire Stamp House is winched forward along its rails. Cables under tension, heavy loads moving. If a winching is happening, stay well back but do go watch. It's the heartbeat of the town's slow migration.
- **The boiler:** peat-fired, and it once failed. Everyone remembers.

---

## 11. Social Preparation

**Speak plainly.** People here talk loudly, bluntly, and briefly, because they've had to for thirty-four years. This isn't rudeness. Don't take it as such, and don't be offended if your long polite phrasings are cut off. Match them.

**Don't complain about the noise.** Everyone knows it's loud. Complaining about the stamps is like complaining about someone's heartbeat. And never, ever say anything like "wouldn't it be nice if they stopped for a while."

**The three days.** The boiler failure is spoken of quietly, "the way people speak of an illness." If it comes up, listen, don't joke. It was a time when the town's world briefly broke. The birds didn't come home.

**The oldest birch.** Everyone knows which tree it is. Ask someone to show you. It is a good way to start a conversation, and a sign that you understand what matters here. Don't carve anything into it, lean your gear against it, or break twigs.

**Offer to help with a house move.** If a family is dragging their house across the moss on oiled logs while you're there, pitch in. This is a communal event, and joining it is the fastest way to be accepted. Bring your gloves.

**Ask about "following the warm water."** It's their saying. Let them tell you what it means to them.

**The moss patterns.** Locals don't think much about them: "it is simply how moss grows here." You will find them astonishing. Try not to be the tourist who gushes endlessly about something the locals consider ordinary. But do ask, politely, whether you can go up on the Stamp House roof to see them. From the ground they're subtle. From above, they are concentric rings, nested curves, and radiating lines, like the figures sand makes on a vibrating plate. Look ahead of the Stamp House, onto the untouched rock, where faint rings are already forming a meter or two past where the stamps have reached. It's like seeing the future written in moss.

**Don't walk on the patterned moss** near the Stamp House unless there's a path. It won't hurt anyone, but it seems like the right courtesy.

**The town hasn't decided its future.** In sixty years the grit runs out. Some will want to follow the stone inland to poorer rock, and some will want to stop. Don't offer opinions unless asked. It's a live, unresolved question and probably a sore one.

---

## 12. Currency and Gifts

You'll need to figure out what they use for money. Likely it's a regional currency of the inland factory economy, or company scrip if the Stamp House is company-owned. Barter goods travel well:

- Good tea, coffee, sugar, tobacco (if appropriate to your ethics), spices
- Sewing needles, strong thread, good knives, files
- Quality boot leather or rubber
- Books or newspapers, since news arrives every eleven days at best
- Seeds of hardy vegetables that grow in cool damp conditions, though ask before introducing anything
- Earplugs. Genuinely. Modern silicone or foam plugs would be a marvel to people who use waxed cotton.

---

## 13. Useful Extras

- **Notebook in a waterproof bag, and pencils** (ink runs in the damp)
- **Binoculars** for the birds, the fog-nets, and the coast
- **A small hand lens.** Look at the grit up close, at the moss, at the algae crust.
- **A thermos,** for hot drinks on foggy walks
- **A walking stick or trekking pole** with a rubber tip, for testing moss over voids and steadying yourself on clinker
- **Waterproof bags** for everything electronic or paper
- **A stethoscope,** if you're curious. Put it, or your ear, against a boulder a kilometer from the Stamp House. You will hear the stamps in the stone.

---

## 14. What It Will Actually Feel Like

The first day you'll be overwhelmed. The pounding will seem unbearable, and you'll wonder how anyone lives here.

The second day you'll notice you've stopped hearing it consciously. It's become the floor of the world. You'll start noticing everything layered on top: the chuff of the engine, the clatter of the screens, starlings copying the tram, the hiss of the fog-nets in the morning like rain that isn't falling.

Somewhere around the fourth or fifth day, you may catch yourself walking in rhythm. You'll dash across open clinker in short bursts, like the voles, timed to the beats. You'll find yourself pausing in speech in the gaps. You'll lie in bed at night listening to the petrels purr beneath the floor, pulsing with the stamps, and realize you're breathing in time with it too.

You'll learn to love the mornings: fog thick off the cold sea, dew beading on every black edge, the nets hissing, the moss exhaling cold stone-breath when you step on it. Then there's the midday light, when the fog lifts and the whole field snaps into color. It's a mosaic of black basalt, chemical-green fern, rust-orange ponds, grey-white gravel, dark iron pipe. Then dusk, with thousands of petrels spiraling in against a pale sky, and the Stamp House windows glowing, the only electric light for many kilometers.

You'll walk out to the old quarry face, to the abandoned houses swallowed by moss and fern, and understand that this is what happens to everything here that stops moving. You'll walk back along the crushed-gravel scar to the front, where the iron shed is chewing its way, a few centimeters a week, into forty-one-year-old stone. Everything here is young: the rock, the trees, the moss. Everything is moving forward together.

When the steamer's horn sounds on the eleventh day, you'll feel a pang. And when you step off the boat somewhere else and the ground is silent under your feet, it will feel, for a little while, like something is missing.

Bring earplugs. Bring good boots. And bring the patience to let the rhythm get into you. That's the only way to really visit Orrin Low.
