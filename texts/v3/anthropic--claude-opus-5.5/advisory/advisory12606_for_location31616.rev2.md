# Preparing for Kessel Flat

## The Short Version

Kessel Flat is not dangerous in dramatic ways. Nothing here will attack you, the people will mostly leave you alone, and the buildings will not fall on you if you're sensible. It is dangerous in slow, chemical, cumulative ways. The air will not let you cool down. The water will not let you drink it. The ground will not let you trust it. And the whole environment is working, patiently and without malice, to corrode everything you bring, including your skin.

Most visitors who get into trouble here do it by treating the place as a hot, humid, abandoned town with some birds. It is really a **caustic chemical landscape that happens to have a town in it.** Prepare for that and it becomes one of the strangest and most beautiful places you will ever stand.

---

## Part One: Before You Leave

### Understand the heat before you feel it

The most important line in the description is this one: *Sweat does not evaporate here.*

Your body cools itself almost entirely by evaporating sweat. In dry heat you can survive remarkable temperatures because sweat flashes off and carries heat away. In saturated air it just runs down your body and drips off your elbows, taking almost no heat with it. The relevant number is not the temperature but the **wet-bulb temperature**, which combines heat and humidity into a measure of how well evaporation can work. Healthy people doing light activity start struggling at wet-bulb readings around 28–31°C. Somewhere in the low-to-mid 30s, even resting in shade is not survivable for long.

A below-sea-level basin with a saturated lake, standing brine, and a permanent haze is almost designed to push wet-bulb readings up toward those limits at midday.

So:

- **Bring a thermometer-hygrometer, or better, a small digital wet-bulb or WBGT meter.** Look at it several times a day. The number will tell you when to stop long before your body does.
- **Acclimatize before you go if you can.** Ten to fourteen days of daily exertion in hot, humid conditions (a sauna, a humid greenhouse, a tropical city in summer) trains your body to sweat earlier, sweat more dilute sweat, and increase blood volume. It is the single most effective preparation you can make. If you can't, plan your first three or four days in Kessel Flat as nearly idle.
- **Be honest about your health.** Heart conditions, blood pressure medications (especially diuretics and beta-blockers), anticholinergics, some antidepressants and antihistamines, recent illness, and poor fitness all reduce heat tolerance. This is not a place to discover that you're more fragile than you thought.

### Time your visit

The basin changes character dramatically through the year, and you should decide which version you want.

- **The dry months** are the flamingo breeding season. The colony is at full strength, the pans are at their most vivid, the lake has pulled back to leave trona crust and mudflats. Also the saltiest, dustiest, and in the afternoons the most abrasive. This is the season most people would want to see.
- **The wet months** bring the heavy rains, a swollen lake, berm patching, and probably a washed-out, muddier, more exhausting town. The fly drifts dissolve, the roads may flood, and the rail line out might be disrupted. Fewer birds, more water, fewer reasons to come.
- **The gap after the flamingos leave**, the few days when the basin suddenly looks abandoned, is worth seeing if you can catch it. The description says that only then do you see how much of the pink was birds. It would be a melancholy, revealing week.

Within any season, **fly emergences** come in waves lasting a few days. If you're sensitive to insects crawling on you (even harmless ones), try to learn the local cycle and arrive between peaks.

### Plan your exit before your entrance

There is one line out: a standard-gauge freight railway that runs **twice a week**, climbing west to a port. There is no mention of roads, buses, or airfields. That means:

- Find out, before arriving, whether the freight train carries passengers, will carry you, and on what terms. Company towns often carry workers, mail, and a few outsiders on freight, informally, with the crew's permission.
- **Know the departure days.** If you miss a train you are staying three or four more days, whether your water supply, your health, or your nerves agree.
- Have a contingency if you get sick. Find out whether the plant has a clinic, a first-aid station, or a radio link out. With six hundred people and working kilns, there will almost certainly be *something*, but it may be minimal.

The climb out of the basin will probably be a relief so intense it feels like a physical event: the air thinning, drying, cooling as the train gains elevation, sweat suddenly evaporating off you for the first time in days. Look forward to it.

## Part Two: The Chemistry of the Place

This is the part most people underestimate.

### The brine is caustic

The lake is a **soda lake**. Trona, the mineral being mined, is a sodium carbonate compound, and soda ash is sodium carbonate. Lakes like this are strongly alkaline, often pH 10 or higher, and in the concentrated pans higher still. Alkaline solutions are in some ways worse on skin than acids. They don't sting much at first, they saponify fats, they soften and penetrate tissue, and the damage can keep advancing after you think you've rinsed it off.

- **Do not wade, swim, or put your hands in the pans or the lake.** Especially not the red pans, which are saturated brine. The color is lovely. It is not an invitation.
- **Protect your eyes above all.** Alkali in the eye is a medical emergency. Splash, spray from a sluice gate, wind-blown brine mist, a rubbed eye after touching a handrail: all of these can do real harm. Carry an **eyewash bottle of clean water** on you at all times and know how to use it: flush immediately, and keep flushing for fifteen minutes or more. Don't try to neutralize alkali with vinegar or anything acidic.
- **The handrails matter.** That "gray, salty paste on the palm" is concentrated alkaline salt plus condensation. Wash your hands before touching your face, eating, or handling contact lenses. Better: don't bring contact lenses at all. Wear glasses.

### The dust is also caustic

The afternoon wind lifts salt and soda dust off the dry pans and flat roofs and drives it through the streets. Near the processing hall there will also be crushed trona and calcined soda ash dust. This dust is an irritant to eyes, nose, throat, and lungs, and it becomes an alkaline paste the moment it lands on wet skin, which here is all skin.

- **Sealed goggles** for the afternoon wind and for anywhere near the crusher hall or kilns. Ordinary sunglasses won't keep fine dust out.
- **A respirator** (a good particulate mask) for the same situations. In the heat and humidity it will be miserable to wear, so don't plan to wear it constantly. Put it on when the air goes tan.
- **Cover exposed skin in the afternoon**, then rinse with fresh water in the evening. Skin creases, the neck, behind the knees, and the groin will collect salt paste and begin to rash.

### The mud smells of eggs for a reason

The black ooze a few centimeters under the crust smells of eggs because of **hydrogen sulfide**, produced by bacteria in the oxygen-free mud. In open air, at the level you'll smell walking around, it's just unpleasant. But hydrogen sulfide is heavier than air and collects in low, enclosed spaces, and at higher concentrations it deadens your sense of smell before it knocks you unconscious. "The smell went away" is sometimes the last warning.

Places where it could collect:

- **drainage culverts** (where the bats roost)
- **pumphouse basements and sumps**
- **intake canal pits**
- **abandoned buildings whose floors have sunk into the brine**
- any cellar, pit, tank, or trench

**Do not enter confined or below-grade spaces.** If you have a serious interest in exploring them, bring a personal H₂S gas monitor and never go alone. The ordinary rule is simpler: if it's underground or enclosed and damp, stay out.

### The water will poison you slowly

Every drop of groundwater in the basin is brine. You cannot drink it, cook with it, or wash with it. Fresh water will come from one of three sources: rain collection, water brought in by rail, or a treatment system at the plant. Find out which before you arrive.

In rift-valley settings like this, even "fresh" local water often carries high levels of fluoride. You may notice the older residents have mottled or brown-stained teeth. Short-term drinking won't harm you, but if you have a choice, drink sealed or brought-in water.

## Part Three: Your Body in the Basin

### Water and salt

You will sweat constantly, profusely, and pointlessly, losing a liter or more an hour with exertion. You must replace both water and salt. Plain water alone, in large volumes, can dilute your blood sodium dangerously.

- Carry **oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets** in quantity. Plan on several liters of fluid a day, with electrolytes in at least half of it.
- Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Your thirst will lag.
- Check your urine. Dark and scanty means you're behind.

Salt is literally in the air around you, and you'll taste it on your lips all day. You will still need to eat it. The salt on your skin does nothing for your blood.

### How to actually stay cool

Here's the cruel trick of humid heat: most of the usual cooling strategies stop working.

- **Wet cloths and misting barely help**, because the water can't evaporate.
- **Fans help only if the air is cooler than your skin**, and at midday it may not be. A fan blowing 38°C air on you can actually heat you up.
- **What works:** shade, stillness, drinking cool water, cold or ice water on the wrists, neck, and armpits if you can get it (the plant may make ice, or have a cold room), and simply not generating heat.

The practical answer is to **live on the basin's rhythm**, as the description lays it out:

- **Dawn to mid-morning:** your working time. Walk, explore, photograph. Everything is dripping but the heat hasn't peaked.
- **Midday:** stop. Get into the deepest shade you can find (thick concrete buildings hold coolness better than anything with a metal roof) and do nothing. This is when the flies peak and the birds feed. You can watch from shelter.
- **Afternoon:** the wind comes up and the dust moves. Goggles, respirator, or stay indoors.
- **Evening:** the second good window. Long, dim, orange light, the red pans glowing, the bats pouring out. This is probably the most beautiful time in the basin and the most tolerable.

Learn the early signs of heat exhaustion and **stop at them**: headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, cramps, a pounding heart at rest, irritability. Confusion, stopped sweating, or collapse are signs of **heat stroke**, a life-threatening emergency. Travel with a companion if at all possible, because heat stroke often takes your judgment first.

### Skin that never dries

In most climates, skin gets a break every day. Here it is wet all the time, with alkaline salt ground into it. Within days you can expect:

- **chafing** where anything rubs
- **heat rash** (prickly heat) where sweat ducts block
- **fungal infections** in feet, groin, armpits, and under straps
- **cracked, macerated feet** from wet socks and boots

Bring **antifungal powder and cream**, a **chafe balm**, and plenty of **changes of socks and underwear**. Every evening: rinse with fresh water, dry thoroughly (a small towel in a well-sealed bag, since nothing air-dries here), powder the creases, and put on dry clothes to sleep if you have any left.

### Feet: the priority

You will walk a lot on hard, warm, polished slag, on uneven berms, and occasionally, by accident, through crust into warm mud. Your feet are your most valuable and most vulnerable asset.

- **Boots:** leather will stiffen and rot in alkaline brine. Choose synthetic or rubber-soled boots with good drainage, or high rubber boots for pan work (terrible in the heat, but excellent protection against brine). Many visitors might settle on synthetic boots with gaiters for general use and rubber boots for the edges of the pans.
- **Socks:** synthetic or wool blends, several changes a day. Cotton socks will be sodden within an hour.
- **Air your feet** at midday rest and every evening.
- **Rinse your boots** in fresh water when you can. Salt crust eats seams.

### Sleep

The nights barely cool, and the condensation means your bedding will likely be damp by morning. Don't expect good sleep for the first several nights. A mosquito net (useful mainly for the flies and anything crawling in the barracks), a lightweight sheet rather than a blanket, and a sleeping spot with some air movement will help. Earplugs may help too, not for noise (it's quiet) but for the pump thudding, which some people will feel through the floor all night. Others will find it strangely soothing, like a slow heartbeat.

## Part Four: Clothing and Gear

### What to wear

- **Loose, light-colored, quick-drying synthetic clothing** with long sleeves and long legs. Long sleeves may seem counterintuitive in heat, but they protect against dust, brine splash, UV, and flies landing on you, and they don't stop evaporation that isn't happening anyway.
- **A wide-brimmed hat.** The haze hides the sun, but it does not stop ultraviolet. Diffuse light plus reflection off white crust and pale water can burn you without your ever seeing a shadow.
- **Sunscreen**, water-resistant, reapplied often, since sweat will strip it off.
- **A head net** for fly peaks. The flies don't bite, but a cloud of them bumping your lips, nose, and eyes for hours is its own ordeal.

### Protecting your equipment

Everything metal in this town is weeping and rusting. Your equipment will join it.

- **Electronics:** keep phones, cameras, and batteries in sealed waterproof bags or cases with desiccant (which will saturate fast; bring lots and rotate it). Assume moisture will get into connectors and ports. Consider devices rated for water resistance.
- **Camera lenses** will fog every time you move from a shaded interior to the outside, or vice versa. Let them acclimate in a sealed bag. A lens hood and UV filter will protect the front element from salt spray. Wipe salt off with fresh water and a soft cloth, never dry, or you'll grind crystals into the coating.
- **Metal tools, knives, zippers, buckles:** anything steel will rust within days. Stainless, plastic, or coated gear will last longer. A tiny bottle of oil or corrosion inhibitor helps.
- **Rinse everything in fresh water** when you can, and dry it.

### A headlamp, and spares

At night, the grid beyond the few lit blocks is **completely dark**. You'll need a reliable headlamp with spare batteries, and a backup light. Red light mode will let you see without destroying your night vision or disturbing roosting birds.

## Part Five: Moving Through the Town

### Navigation in a perfect grid

This sounds easy. It isn't, quite. Every street is straight, every block looks the same, every façade is the same salt-bleached color, the escarpments are hidden in haze, and the sun is a pale disc that casts almost no shadows. A perfect grid with no landmarks is surprisingly disorienting.

- Learn the system: **numbered streets run east toward the water; lettered avenues run north–south.** The plant is at the east end of Second Street. The inhabited blocks cluster around Second and C. The salt silo is on H Avenue.
- **Use the pumps for orientation.** The thudding comes from the lake edge, to the east. The plant's orange glow at night is also east.
- Carry a compass and a simple map. Note which blocks are inhabited.
- Street signs, if any survive, may be salt-crusted to illegibility.

### The ground

- **Stay on paved streets and berm tops.** The crust off-pavement looks solid and is not. You'll break through into warm, sulfurous, alkaline mud, and your footprint will fill with brine within a minute. At worst it's deep enough to lose a boot. More often it's just filthy and caustic.
- The pavement is **polished and uneven**, with salt-heave ridges at the joints. It'll be slick when wet in the morning. Watch your feet.
- The **pink-centered puddles** are concentrated brine with the same microbes as the pans. Step around them.

### The buildings

The town is "weathered into equilibrium," which is not the same as "safe."

- **Spalled concrete** and rusted rebar mean that ledges, balconies, and stair edges may fail under load. The worn, bowl-shaped stairs are slippery.
- **Corroded roofs** are lace. Never walk on them, and never stand directly under loose sheeting when the afternoon wind is blowing.
- **Rusted edges** cut easily, and cuts here will stay wet and invite infection. Wear gloves when touching anything structural, and keep a basic wound-care kit.
- **Floors in sunken buildings** may sit in brine or collect hydrogen sulfide.
- **The salt silo on H Avenue** is the great viewpoint over the flamingo colony. If you climb it, ask the locals about its condition first, climb in the cool morning, check each step, and never climb alone.

### Bird droppings and bats

Every ledge is white with droppings. Dried droppings and the dust from bat roosts can carry fungal spores and other pathogens that cause lung infections when inhaled. Avoid stirring up guano dust in enclosed spaces. Don't enter the culverts where the bats roost. Never touch a bat, alive or dead; bats anywhere can carry rabies. Wash your hands before eating. If you develop a fever, cough, or flu-like illness in the weeks after your visit, tell a doctor where you were.

Large bird colonies can also be sites of avian influenza. Don't handle dead or sick birds, and don't handle chicks.

## Part Six: The Pans, the Lake, and the Birds

### Walking the berms

The berms are narrow and topped with narrow-gauge track. They're the best way to see the pans, and they come with rules:

- **The trains move at walking pace, but they move.** On a narrow berm with brine on both sides, there may be nowhere to step aside. Before walking out, learn from the plant or the train crews when and where the trains run. Listen for the grinding and the horn.
- **Sluice gates and pumphouse intakes** have moving water, suction, and slippery concrete. Stay back.
- Berms may be soft or undercut in the wet season.

### The flamingo colony

Tens of thousands of nesting flamingos on the abandoned northern pans, a pink field a kilometer long. It will likely be the most extraordinary thing you see here.

**Watch it from a distance.** Flamingo colonies are sensitive. Disturbance can cause birds to flush off their nests, leaving eggs and chicks exposed to heat and to the marabou storks waiting on the barrack roofs. A single careless person walking toward the colony can cost hundreds of chicks. And the nests sit on a brine crust that probably wouldn't hold you anyway.

Use binoculars, a telephoto lens, and the silo or other high points. Move slowly and quietly near the working pans, where birds are feeding. If they lift, you're too close. The sound of a section lifting, "a sustained low rush… like wind through a gap," is something you'll want to hear, but let a train or a gust cause it, not you.

### The flies

They don't bite. They are also on everything: the walls, the windows, your hat, your arms, the rims of your water bottle, the corners of your eyes if you let them. During peaks they rise in a moving black film as you walk and settle behind you. In the empty barracks they sit in shifting clots.

Practical notes:

- A head net during peak emergence days.
- Keep food and drink covered. Drink from bottles with closable lids.
- Don't breathe through your mouth while walking through clouds of them.
- Expect the drifts of dead flies along the berms to smell, especially in the heat.

Mentally, the flies are a test. Some visitors will find them disgusting for the whole stay. Many will find that after a day or two they stop noticing them, and then, as the description says, notice only when the drone stops. It may help to think of them as the base of everything: the flies feed the swallows, the lizards, the bats, the shorebirds. They are why the basin is so alive.

### Other animals

- **Lizards** on the warm pavement are harmless and scatter.
- **Marabou storks** are huge, unafraid, and can be aggressive around food. Keep your distance and don't feed them.
- **Gulls** near the rail yard will steal food.
- Nothing here is large or predatory toward humans.

## Part Seven: The People of the Works

Six hundred people live in a town built for eleven thousand. They call it *the works*, not Kessel Flat, and you should too. Using the old map name will mark you as someone who read about the place rather than someone who knows it.

What to expect, intuitively:

- **A company town.** Everyone here is likely connected to the plant: workers, families of workers, retirees who never left, a few people who keep the essential services running. There may be one store, one canteen, one clinic, all run by the company or with its permission. Money may or may not work. Find out in advance whether there's company scrip, barter, or ordinary currency, and whether outsiders can buy food and water at all.
- **Few outsiders.** Visitors will be rare. People will notice you. Most will probably be reserved rather than hostile. Places this isolated tend to produce people who are self-contained, practical, dry-humored, and not especially interested in explaining themselves.
- **Ask permission.** Before entering the plant, walking the berms, climbing the silo, or photographing people, ask. The plant is an industrial site with kilns, crushers, and moving rail; you are a liability until someone vouches for you. The train crews and pump operators know the ground better than any map.
- **Bring something useful.** In a town that receives freight twice a week, small things from outside may be valued: good batteries, fresh fruit, medicines, news, tools that don't rust. A gift offered to whoever helps you find lodging is a reasonable courtesy.
- **Respect the contraction.** The inhabited blocks have been pulling back toward the plant street by street, over decades. For the people here, the empty grid isn't a spooky backdrop. It's where their neighbors used to live. Wandering it with delight in its eeriness is fine; talking about it as a ruin in front of residents may not be.

Lodging will probably be an empty room in one of the inhabited blocks, a company guesthouse, or a bunk in a barracks. Expect basic. Expect damp. Be grateful for thick walls.

## Part Eight: Night

When the sun goes down behind the western haze, the red pans glow for about an hour as if lit from below. Then the bats emerge from the culverts in the thousands. Then the few lights come on: sodium lamps on the plant, scattered windows near Second and C, red lamps atop the pylons.

The rest of the grid is black.

Walking out of the lit blocks at night is, in the description's words, "like walking out of a room into a field." The streets continue, perfectly regular, into total darkness. If you do it:

- Carry your headlamp and a backup. Tell someone where you're going.
- Stay on streets you walked in daylight.
- Remember that the pan edges, the sluices, and the crust are invisible at night.
- Stay off the berms after dark unless you're with someone from the works; you may not hear a train until it's close.
- Turn off your light for a while, somewhere safe. Let your eyes adjust. Watch the plant's orange glow reflected in the pans, the red pylon lamps, the faint movement of bats against the haze. Listen to the pumps and the colony's low gabble from the north.

The pylons deserve a separate warning. They carry live power across the basin, their insulators are crusted with salt, and in humid air they hum and crackle. Salt-crusted insulators can arc. **Don't touch the pylons, don't climb them, and don't linger directly under the lines during rain or heavy condensation.**

## Part Nine: The Mind in the Basin

Physically preparing for Kessel Flat is mostly a matter of discipline. Mentally preparing is harder to plan for, but worth thinking about.

**The heat will make you stupid.** Even well below the danger thresholds, sustained humid heat slows thinking, shortens temper, and erodes judgment. You'll make worse decisions at 2 p.m. than at 7 a.m. Make your plans in the morning and stick to them.

**The emptiness is structured, and it gets under your skin.** A ruined town is sad. An empty grid, perfectly intact, every block the same, all the doors dark, is something else, closer to uncanny. Expect to feel watched in the empty blocks, though you almost certainly aren't. Expect a mild, persistent unease, especially at dusk. That's normal.

**The sensory world is flattened.** Shadowless light, a sky the same color as the lake, a horizon that dissolves, a hush that absorbs your footsteps, a constant drone you stop hearing. Some people find this meditative. Others find it oppressive and get headaches or low moods. Having something to do — a project, a notebook, sketching, birdwatching, recording sounds — keeps the mind anchored.

**And then there is the life.** The thing to prepare for, emotionally, is the scale of it. A landscape with no plants, no trees, nothing growing, that is nonetheless packed with living things on every square meter: billions of flies, uncountable shrimp, tens of thousands of flamingos, thousands of bats, swallows through every door, lizards on every warm surface, pigment blooming pan by pan as microbes flourish and die. It runs against every instinct you have about what a living place looks like. The description ends with you standing on Fourth Street, "almost the only person anywhere you can see, in the middle of more life than you can count." That combination of loneliness and abundance is the real reason to come. Give yourself time to feel it. Stand still in the evening. Let the swallow cut past your ear.

## Packing List

**Health and body**
- Oral rehydration salts / electrolyte tablets (lots)
- Water bottles with closable lids, and a way to carry several liters
- Wet-bulb or temperature/humidity meter
- Eyewash bottles (at least two), kept filled with clean water
- Antifungal powder and cream
- Anti-chafe balm
- Water-resistant sunscreen, lip balm
- Wound-care kit: antiseptic, dressings, waterproof bandages
- Antihistamines, painkillers, anti-diarrheal, any personal medications (packed waterproof)
- Earplugs

**Clothing**
- Loose, light-colored, long-sleeved synthetic shirts and trousers (several)
- Many pairs of synthetic or wool-blend socks
- Many changes of quick-dry underwear
- Wide-brimmed hat
- Head net
- Synthetic boots with drainage, plus gaiters
- Rubber boots for pan edges
- Work gloves
- Small towels in sealed bags

**Protection**
- Sealed goggles
- Particulate respirator and spare filters
- Glasses rather than contact lenses
- Personal H₂S monitor (only if you plan to go near enclosed spaces, which you shouldn't)

**Gear**
- Headlamp with red mode, backup light, spare batteries in sealed packaging
- Compass and map of the grid
- Binoculars
- Waterproof bags and cases, lots of desiccant
- Lens cloths, a small bottle of fresh water for cleaning lenses
- Small bottle of corrosion inhibitor or oil
- Mosquito net and a light sheet

**Social and logistics**
- Confirmed knowledge of the freight train schedule, and permission to ride it
- Currency, or whatever the works accepts
- Small useful gifts from outside
- A notebook

## Last Word

Go slowly. Rest at midday. Keep your eyes clean and your feet dry, which is to say: try. Stay on the pavement and the berms. Stay out of anything enclosed and damp. Keep your distance from the colony. Ask the people of the works before you go anywhere they work. Catch your train.

And on one of those long, dim evenings, when the red pans are glowing and the pumps are thudding somewhere behind you, stop at the east end of Fourth Street and just stand there. That's what you came for.
