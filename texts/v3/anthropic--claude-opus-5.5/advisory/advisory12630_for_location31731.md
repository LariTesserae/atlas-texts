# Preparing for Low Hesk: Notes for the Traveler

## The Short Version

You are going into a cold, saturated, deafening, near-lightless building the size of a region, one that is still being built by something nobody has seen. It is not actively hostile. Nothing there wants you. That is the danger. It is indifferent and uniform, and it goes on further than you can walk. People don't die at Low Hesk from anything dramatic. They die from being wet at eight degrees for too long, from losing count, and from following something outward.

Pack for a cave, behave like a guest in a lighthouse, and navigate like a sailor. Plan to leave before you want to.

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before the gear, adjust your expectations, because the place has a feel that catches people off guard.

**It will not feel like a ruin.** Most vast, empty, underground places feel old, and your instincts will reach for that register: reverence, decay, dust. Low Hesk is raw and new. The edges bite your thumb and the formwork is still bolted on. It feels like a construction site that someone walked away from an hour ago, repeated for kilometers. Some people find this more unsettling than any ruin, because a ruin is finished and this is not. There is a faint, persistent sense of trespass, of being somewhere before it's ready.

**It will feel alive, and you'll resist that, and then you'll stop resisting.** The breathing is real: air, mist, and pressure moving on a fixed thirty-minute rhythm. Within a day your body will start keeping time to it. You'll find yourself bracing before the in-breath and relaxing into the out-breath without thinking. Let this happen. The rhythm is the most useful tool you have there.

**It is monotonous in a way that erodes you.** Pillar, lamp-cone, dark, pillar, gutter, pillar. The hall has almost no landmarks, and the ones it has repeat. Expect your sense of distance and time to fail. That is not a personal weakness. The place does it to everyone.

## Clothing: The Cold That Doesn't Leave

Eight degrees sounds mild. It isn't, down there, for three reasons. The air is fully saturated, so your sweat won't evaporate and your clothes will slowly wet through from inside and out. The concrete is a heat sink that pulls warmth from anything touching it. And the draft, which runs toward the pumps for eleven minutes and back for nineteen, never lets a warm layer of air settle around you.

- **Wool or synthetic, never cotton.** Merino base layer, a fleece or wool mid-layer, and a spare of each sealed in a dry bag. Cotton is dangerous here, because once it's wet it stays wet and pulls heat from you continuously.
- **A waterproof shell with a hood,** as breathable as you can get. Be honest with yourself, though: nothing breathes well at 100% humidity. The shell's real job is keeping off the weep, the ceiling rain that shakes loose during each in-breath, and the draft.
- **Move slowly to stay dry.** This is the counterintuitive one. If you walk hard, you sweat, the sweat never dries, and an hour later you're shivering. Walk at a pace where you're slightly cool. Put on your warm layer *before* you stop, not after you're cold.
- **Two pairs of gloves.** Thin liners for handling notebooks and gear, and waterproof insulated overgloves for the rail, the pillars, and anything metal. The iron is colder than the air, and it takes the warmth out of bare palms fast.
- **A wool hat, and a spare.**
- **Socks: at least three extra pairs,** sealed. Change them every few hours whether they feel wet or not. Wet, cold feet over a long visit lead to trench foot, and it's a real risk on a multi-day stay.

## Footwear

The floor is wet concrete with a thin film of water moving across it and, in the older sections, a calcite skin that is slicker than it looks.

- **Waterproof boots with deep, soft-rubber lug soles.** Hard soles skate on calcite. Rubber wellingtons are acceptable in the old hall but become miserable over distance. Waterproof hiking boots with gaiters are better.
- **Do not wear anything that exposes skin to the floor near the edge.** Fresh and green concrete is strongly alkaline. The water pooling on uncured slabs and running from new pours can cause chemical burns that don't hurt until they're already serious. If your boots leak in the new sections, get out of them, rinse your feet with clean water (not gutter water), and dry them.
- **Watch the grates.** The gutters every fifty meters are wide enough to lie down in, and the grating is iron and slick. Step across them deliberately. Don't jump them in the dark.

## Hearing Protection

For eleven minutes of every thirty, you will be inside a roar that speech cannot cross.

- **Foam earplugs plus over-ear muffs** for the in-breath. Bring many plugs, because they get wet and dirty. Muffs are worth the bulk. Over a day you'll sit through roughly fifty cycles, and unprotected, your hearing will pay for it.
- **You can't block the low end.** The pillars ring with a hum at the edge of hearing, and the pump grind comes up through the concrete into your feet and teeth. Some people get headaches, faint nausea, or a vague dread they can't explain. That is infrasound, not intuition. Knowing this helps. If it gets bad, move away from the pillars during the in-breath and stand in open floor between them.
- **Take the plugs out on the out-breath.** You need your ears for the out-breath: for other people, for the gutters, for the direction of the pumps. Get in the habit of plugs in at the grind, plugs out when it falls away.

## Light

The hall lights itself badly. About one sodium lamp in four works, so the floor is an archipelago of orange islands separated by stretches of brown-grey fog.

- **A good headlamp with a red mode, a strong handheld torch, and more batteries than you think.** Cold drains cells quickly. Keep the spares in an inside pocket against your body.
- **Expect your light to bounce back at you.** In the mist a strong white beam lights up the droplets and blinds you, like headlights in fog. Hold the torch low and angle it down at the floor, and use a warmer or dimmer setting. Often you'll see more by letting your eyes adjust to the lamp-glow than by blasting the fog.
- **Chemical lightsticks** are worth carrying to mark a turning point or your return path. Chalk marks and tape won't last on walls that weep constantly.
- **Be considerate with light.** The moss grows only where light falls, and the moths gather in the lamp cones. A traveler shining a torch into a drift of settled moths, or leaving a glowstick against a wall, is changing a small ecosystem that exists on a very narrow margin. Keep your light moving and don't leave it burning.
- **Glasses fog constantly.** Anti-fog treatment helps a little. If you can manage without glasses, consider it. If not, bring a soft cloth in a sealed bag and accept that you'll be wiping them all the time.

## Electronics and Paper

- Everything goes in **dry bags with silica gel packets.** Condensation forms on anything colder than the air, which in practice means everything you carry in from outside.
- **Don't rely on devices for navigation.** Whatever positioning your world uses probably won't reach you here. Some travelers report that compasses behave, and some say otherwise. Treat any compass as a second opinion.
- **Waterproof paper and a pencil.** Ink runs in this air. Pencil on waterproof paper survives. This notebook will be the most important object you carry, as the next section explains.

## Navigation: The Grid Will Try to Keep You

This is where visitors get into real trouble, so read this section twice.

The hall is a regular grid of identical square pillars, each stenciled with a yellow number on its **south face.** That gives you two tools: the numbers tell you where you are, and the side the stencil is on tells you which way you're facing. Beyond that, the grid is designed, or has happened, to be featureless.

**Rules:**

1. **Write down every pillar number you pass, in order.** Do this for every pillar, not every tenth one. It feels obsessive until the moment you need it. Your list of numbers is your thread back.
2. **Note which face you read the number from.** "Passed 1187 on my left, number facing me, so I'm walking north" is the kind of entry that saves you.
3. **Set a hard turnaround** in pillars or breath-cycles before you set out, and keep to it no matter how interesting the next lamp looks.
4. **Never leave the lamp-lit routes without a reason and a plan.** The routes between working lamps and pump houses are known to the tenders. The dark between them is not.
5. **If the numbers stop making sense** (a number repeats, a sequence jumps, or you find a number that "hasn't been cut yet"), **stop.** Don't press on hoping it will sort itself out, and don't improvise a new direction. Sit down, put on your warm layer, drink something hot, and go back along your written list exactly, pillar by pillar, in reverse. If your list stops matching what you see, stay put and listen.

**The place gives you three natural compasses. Learn them before you need them:**

- **The breath points to shelter.** During the in-breath, the draft flows toward the pumping stations, and the pumping stations have warm booths, telephones, and tenders. If you're lost, cold, and have lost your count, stand still through an in-breath, feel which way the mist is being pulled, and walk with it. You'll reach a pump house. This is the single most important thing to know about surviving Low Hesk.
- **Age points home.** The calcite curtains and stalactites are longer near the stair, because that's the oldest part of the hall, and the walls get barer and shinier as you go outward. The moss is thickest in the older, long-lit sections (even where it has died back to brown lace). **Longer stone and older growth mean you're going toward the stair. Bare, sharp, raw surfaces mean you're going toward the edge.**
- **Blue lamps mean turn back.** Sodium orange is the settled hall. Cold blue-white means new sections and the advancing margin. If blue lamps are getting more common around you, you are heading outward.

One compass does **not** reliably help you: **the water.** It always runs to the gutters, and the gutters always run lower, down into a branching network under the floor. Water leads down, not out. Don't follow it.

## Keeping Time

The breath is eleven minutes in, nineteen minutes out, and it has never varied. That makes it a clock.

- Carry a watch anyway, but also **count cycles.** Keep a tally in your notebook. Ten cycles is five hours. Two full cycles is an hour.
- **Plan movements around the out-breath.** Talk, shout, listen for tenders, and make decisions in the quiet. Walk steady, familiar stretches in the roar.
- **Expect time to slip.** Visitors routinely underestimate how long they've been down. Trust the tally over your feeling.

## Communication

- **During the in-breath, speech carries about an arm's length.** Agree on **hand signals** with any companions before descending: stop, come to me, turn back, I'm cold, I'm hurt, follow me. Make them simple and big enough to read in fog by headlamp.
- **Carry a whistle.** Tenders whistle across the hall during out-breaths, and sound carries astonishingly far then. Agree on patterns: one long blast for "where are you," three for "help."
- **Stay within sight of each other.** In the mist, a companion who is two lamp-cones away is effectively gone.
- **The booth telephones** connect the pump houses. If you reach a booth and a tender is willing, a call ahead to the next station along your route is a courtesy and a safety net.

## Food, Water, and Warmth

- **A good vacuum flask of something hot and sweet,** and the means to refill it. The best thing you can do for your body every few cycles is put warmth into it from the inside.
- **More calories than seem necessary.** Cold and wet burn energy quickly. Fatty, dense food is best, eaten little and often.
- **Bring your own water.** The weep is mineral-heavy and, near newer concrete, alkaline. The gutter water is fast and cold, comes from somewhere unknown, and carries eels. If you must drink local water, filter it *and* treat it, and never take it from the fresh sections.
- **Know the signs of hypothermia** in yourself and in others: persistent shivering, then fumbling hands, stumbling, slurred or oddly cheerful speech, and poor decisions. The last one is the dangerous one, because it's the moment people decide to "just check the next few pillars." If you notice it, head for a booth.

## The Tenders: How to Be a Guest

The tenders are few, a kilometer or more apart, and they live alone in warm amber rooms with a kettle on a coil and a wall of trembling gauges. They're not guards or guides. They are maintenance staff for something vast, working for an authority no one can find. Treat them as you would a lighthouse keeper whose door you knocked on in a storm.

- **Knock during the out-breath.** Nobody hears a knock in the roar, and standing at a steel door for eleven minutes is a poor way to arrive.
- **Bring gifts, and make them practical.** Tea, coffee, sugar, dried fruit, chocolate. Dry socks are welcome, because everyone there understands what dry socks are worth. Batteries. Something to read from wherever you came from. News of any world.
- **Bring the one gift that's native to the place: a list of dead lamps.** The tenders trade pillar numbers of failed lamps between booths. If you've been recording every pillar anyway (and you should have), mark which lamps were dead, flickering, or glowing rose, and offer the list. It's genuinely useful to them, and it shows you understand how the place works.
- **Don't touch the valves or gauges.** Everything in those booths is connected to the breath, and the breath is keeping more than the tenders going.
- **Ask about the routes, not the mysteries.** Tenders will tell you which stretches are lit, where the last good lamp is, and how far the next station is. Questions about who built the place, where the cutters are, or what the three-toed tracks belong to tend to get shrugs or careful silence. They have lived with those questions longer than you have. Don't press.
- **Look at the drawings if they're offered,** but handle them gently. The linen blueprints are soft with damp and irreplaceable. Notice how the later sheets outgrow the earlier ones, and how the last show only the grid. Many visitors find this the moment the place gets under their skin.
- **Don't overstay.** The booth is someone's whole world, and a small one. A cup of tea and an hour's warmth is a gift. A day of hosting is a burden.

## The Living Things: Leave Them Be

Everything alive at Low Hesk lives on a very narrow margin of light, breath, and water. The rule for all of it is the same: look, and don't interfere.

- **The moss** grows in combed stripes along the weep lines, only where lamps have shone. Don't scrape it, lean on it, or take samples. It is slow, and it's the hall's memory of its own lighting.
- **The moths** hang in the in-breath and settle on the pillars in drifts during the out-breath. Don't swat them, collect them, or shine lights directly into the settled drifts. The tenders' belief that the moths would simply fall if the pumps stopped is worth respecting, whether it's literally true or not.
- **The eels** move under the grates. You may glimpse one: long, colorless, with the spine a dark thread through translucent skin. It's a lovely thing to see. **Do not lift the grates.** What lies under the floor is a branching network going down and out, and the one person who looked found a knot of eels as thick as a body, lying still. Some things are meant to stay under the floor.
- **The three-toed tracks** in the green concrete at the edge lead outward, toward the cutters. **Don't follow them.** Nobody knows what made them, and nobody knows what they were walking toward, or whether they arrived.

## If You Go to the Edge

The honest advice is **don't, on a first visit.** If you must, go with a tender's blessing, a clear turnaround point, and extra equipment:

- **A respirator (P100 or equivalent).** The air there smells of cut stone and cement dust, and freshly cut rock dust can scar your lungs. The smell of hot oil means working machinery somewhere close.
- **Sealed eye protection.** Dust and caustic splashes.
- **Fully waterproof, alkali-resistant boots and gloves.** The green concrete is soft enough to take a fingernail and caustic enough to burn.
- **Stay out of the unpoured bay.** The excavated cavern of broken rock beyond the last pour is unstable, unlit, and between you and whatever is making the sound.
- **Remember the parallel tracks, a meter wide and too far apart for any vehicle.** Whatever made them comes back through there.
- **Check your pillar numbers against your list constantly.** The reports of numbers that haven't been cut yet come mostly from people who went far out.
- **Watch the lamps.** Blue-white everywhere around you means you are at the front. Stand for one out-breath, press your ear to a pillar, feel the steady, patient, directional tremor of the cutting, and then go back.

## The Inner Preparation

Bring something to hold your mind steady against the uniformity. A tune you know by heart, a poem, a set of questions for yourself. Some visitors take to whistling back at the tenders across the out-breath. That thin tune between pillars, carrying absurdly far, is one of the most human things in the hall, and joining it helps.

Be ready for the scale to affect you. Standing on that first landing, with the far side a rumour of lamps and the drip falling longer than it should, many people feel a lurch that is part vertigo and part grief, hard to name. It passes. What replaces it is stranger: a kind of calm, set to the rhythm of eleven minutes in and nineteen out, in a place whose every new edge is the youngest thing in the world.

Enjoy that calm, but don't trust it too far. The calm is what makes people stop counting.
