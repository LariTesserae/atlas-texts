# Preparing for the Blanched Steps

The Blanched Steps won't try to kill you the way a storm or a predator might. The danger is subtler. Almost everything that makes the valley beautiful is also what wears a visitor down: the light, the thin air, the wet, the silence, the broken ground. And a great deal of what you could do there by accident would harm a place that is being carefully and patiently kept. Prepare with both in mind: protecting yourself from the valley, and protecting the valley from you.

---

## 1. Understand what kind of place this is

Before packing anything, fix a few facts in your mind, because they shape every other decision.

- **It is very high.** The small hard sun, the violet zenith, the black shadows and the sharp stars all point to an altitude comparable to the high Andes or the Tibetan Plateau, probably 4,500 meters or more. Your body will notice this before your eyes finish adjusting.
- **It is wet and dry at once.** Every surface drips at dawn, yet there are no streams, and you can't count on drinkable water. The air above the moss dries you out as fast as any desert.
- **It is fragile and slow.** Some cushion plants are centuries old. A square yard can hold forty species. Nothing you damage will recover within your lifetime, however lush the place looks.
- **It is tended.** The machines are clean, maintained, and apparently integrated into how the valley lives. You are a guest in someone's garden, or something's. You won't meet the gardener. Behave as though you are being watched by a patient, careful host, because in some sense you are.
- **You will be alone.** No settlement, no trail, no rescue. Plan as you would for a remote wilderness expedition where you are the only medical help.

---

## 2. Your body: altitude

This is the preparation most likely to decide whether your visit is a wonder or a misery.

**Acclimatize before you go.** Arriving from sea level straight into the Steps risks acute mountain sickness at best and high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema at worst. If you can, spend a week or two beforehand somewhere high: 2,500–3,500 meters, then higher. If your mode of travel drops you in with no gradual approach, treat your first two days as rest days no matter how good you feel.

**On arrival:**
- Move slowly. The ledges are knee-high to head-high, and every step up is a small climb in thin air. You'll be breathless within minutes.
- Expect headache, poor sleep, loss of appetite, and a strange heaviness. Expect especially the periodic breathing at night, when you wake gasping for no reason. In the Steps' silence this is unsettling, because you hear every irregular breath.
- Know the warning signs of danger: a headache that doesn't respond to painkillers, confusion, clumsiness, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, pink froth. The only real cure is descent. Know which direction is down: east, toward and into the cloud sea, the way you came in.

**Medical kit additions:** acetazolamide for prevention, dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies (get proper medical advice before you go), a pulse oximeter, and plenty of electrolytes.

---

## 3. Your eyes and skin: the light

The Steps are, in effect, a snowfield made of mirrors. The description's warning is not exaggerated: after an hour your eyes ache even behind smoked glass. Here the light comes from below as much as above, and that defeats most ordinary sun protection.

**Eyes:**
- Bring **glacier glasses**, category 4 lenses, with side shields or a wraparound fit. Ordinary sunglasses let reflected light in at the edges and bottom.
- Bring a **second pair.** Losing or breaking your only pair here could mean photokeratitis (snow blindness) within a day: gritty, agonizing eyes, temporary blindness, days of recovery with nobody to lead you.
- Bring an **emergency backup**: a strip of dark cloth or a card with a thin horizontal slit, as Arctic peoples used.
- Pack lubricating eye drops, and learn what snow blindness feels like. It often arrives hours after exposure, at night.
- **On arrival, do nothing until you can see.** You come up from the grey cloud into total white. Stand still and close your eyes. Put your glasses on before you emerge, if you can. Don't take a step until the ledges have "developed." A sheer head-high drop can hide in that first white minute.

**Skin:**
- UV here is ferocious, and the reflected component burns places you never think to protect: under your chin, under your nose, the inside of your nostrils, the backs of your ears, under your jaw, your eyelids.
- Use high-SPF, water-resistant sunscreen and reapply often. Morning drip and afternoon rain will wash it off.
- Wear a wide-brimmed hat with a neck cape, a buff or face cover pulled up to the cheekbones, long sleeves, and light gloves. Covering up works better than any cream.
- Bring lip balm with SPF, and apply it constantly.

**Heat and cold at once:** In thin air, sunlight is intense but the air holds little heat. Standing in sun you may feel hot. Step into one of those razor-edged black shadows and you may be cold within a minute. Dress in layers you can open and close quickly.

## 4. Your feet and hands: the ground

The terrain is endless steps, blades, organ-pipe columns, and shard scree, with a spongy, water-logged mattress of moss over cool, slick rock.

**The moss is the real hazard.** It hides edges. It hides cracks. It hides the fault lines where water vanishes downward. It makes a ledge look like a slope. It's slippery underneath. Walk as though every soft surface might be concealing a drop or a hole.

**Footwear:**
- Boots with good ankle support, since you'll be stepping down onto uneven surfaces thousands of times a day.
- Soles that grip wet rock. A lugged, sticky-rubber sole is best. Stiff mountaineering boots are clumsy on the constant scrambling.
- Waterproofing, and gaiters. The moss seeps water into every footprint, and everything is soaked until noon.
- Spare dry socks in a sealed bag. Wet feet at altitude and in cold nights lead to blisters, maceration, and worse.

**Trekking poles.** Use them to probe the moss before trusting it, and to take load off your knees on the countless step-downs. One important exception appears in the section on the cables below.

**Hands:**
- Wear light gloves with leather palms. The scree shards are glassy, long as a forearm, and sharp. Freshly fractured edges of andesite can cut like a knife. You'll be putting your hands down to scramble constantly.
- Bring a real wound-care kit: irrigation, closures, dressings, tape.

**Your knees** will hurt. Hundreds of step-downs a day is punishing. Strengthen your quads and knees beforehand with step-ups, step-downs, and lunges, carrying weight.

**The rock is rising.** New fractures open every year. Don't camp directly beneath a fresh, bright face or at the foot of a scree fan. Don't lean on snapped-off column tops or trust a column's stability for a climb.

## 5. Water

Here is the paradox: you are surrounded by water you can't easily drink, and you'll dehydrate faster than almost anywhere else.

- **Carry more than you think you need,** and treat everything.
- **Bring your own fog-catcher.** A square of fine mesh or a smooth tarp, strung or propped at night so it drains into a container, can collect real water from the nightly cloud. This is the single most useful and least harmful water strategy available, and it copies what the valley itself does.
- **The column-top pools** are tiny, perfectly clear, and there are thousands of them. They are also microhabitats, and possibly part of what the birds and insects depend on. Take from them only in real need, take little, and never disturb the plants around them.
- **The pipe spouts** will be tempting, since they drip steadily. But that water belongs to the valley. It passed through nets that grow green threads each morning and through old iron pipes, and it is on its way to a purpose. If you must use it, take a little and treat it thoroughly. Better to leave it alone.
- **Never squeeze the moss for water.** It works, and it's destructive.
- **Filtration:** use a filter plus chemical or UV treatment. It's unknown water from an unknown world.

## 6. Shelter and the cloud

Each night the cloud sea rises and fills the basin a few hundred feet deep. Where you sleep relative to that line is your biggest camp decision.

**Option A: sleep inside the cloud (low camp).**
- Everything gets soaked. Condensation soaks your tent, bag, and clothes by morning.
- You'll be blind in white darkness if you have to move.
- The cloud may be somewhat warmer than the open sky above it.
- You'll wake to the dripping, glittering dawn in the middle of it.

**Option B: sleep above the cloud (high camp).**
- Choose a high step or terrace well above the valley floor. The top of the tower and the highest pylons stand clear, so there's a known clearance height. Estimate it on your first evening by watching how high the cloud climbs.
- Under a thin black sky you'll get clear, radiant cold, possibly well below freezing. It will be very cold.
- You'll have the view the description saves for last: a white sea, the tower's upper fog-sails, the faint blue corona along the cables, and more stars than you've ever seen.
- It's drier, but frost is likely.

Either way, bring:
- A tent or bivy built for condensation and cold. Something waterproof and breathable, and sturdy enough for the occasional wind.
- A sleeping bag rated well below freezing, with a waterproof shell or synthetic fill. Down that gets soaked by cloud is useless.
- A good insulated pad. The rock underneath is always cool and slick. Pitch on bare rock where possible, never on cushion plants, and try not to flatten large areas of moss.
- Dry bags for everything.

**Rules:**
- **Be in camp before the cloud climbs.** Dusk is beautiful and long, and it is your warning. Never be caught traveling in the rising cloud. Visibility drops to nothing, the moss hides the edges, and you won't hear anything.
- **Don't move at night.** The valley is "drinking." Stay put.
- **Don't camp beneath the power cables.** The plants there are warm, lush, and inviting. Don't.

## 7. The cables, the pylons, and the machines

This is where the Steps stop being merely wild.

**The cables carry real current.** Your hair lifting and a metallic taste in your mouth are signs of a strong electric field. The blue corona at dusk means high voltage.
- Don't stand under the lines for long.
- **Don't raise trekking poles, tent poles, or anything metal toward them.**
- Don't touch the pylons.
- Don't touch the warm, iridescent blue-bronze leaves directly beneath the cables. You don't know what they are doing, or what they're conducting.
- If you have a pacemaker or other implanted electronic device, think very carefully about coming at all, and keep well away from the line.
- During the afternoon rain, the ground under the cables is wet. Stay well away.

**Navigation near metal:** A tower of riveted iron, miles of iron pipe, iron trestles, and live cables will all deflect a compass. Don't trust magnetic bearings near any of them. There's probably no GPS here either. Navigate by landmarks:
- The **smoking peaks** to the west.
- The **cloud sea** to the east and downslope.
- The **tower** at the center of the basin, visible from almost everywhere and standing above the night cloud.
- The **pylon line**, which runs perfectly straight. It's a superb handrail as long as you walk *beside* it, not under it.
- The **pipes**, which lead across the valley and end in the densest thickets.

Bring a notebook to sketch your own map. Everything looks alike from inside a thicket of polished cutlery.

**The machines:**
- **Touch nothing.** Don't climb the tower. Don't open the generator shed. Don't try the ore car, don't push it, don't ride it, don't unload it. Don't adjust the sliding joints, or scratch the trestle posts, or add your own date.
- It will be tempting to test them: to see if the car really moves, or whether you can speed a generator up. Don't. They appear to be answering the valley itself. You don't know what you would be interrupting.
- Remember that nothing here is abandoned. Every piece of metal is cared for. Treat each machine as you would a stranger's house with the door unlocked.

## 8. The silence

Most people underestimate this one. It can be the most disorienting part of the trip.

**Practical consequences:**
- **A shout carries about twenty yards.** If you travel with companions, you can't call to each other across the valley. Stay within sight at all times. Agree on hand signals beforehand.
- **Signal mirrors are less useful than usual,** since the whole valley already flashes. Bright non-silver colors are more visible: orange, red. Brightly colored clothing, a colored flag on a pack.
- **Radios** may work, but test them early, and away from the cables, which may cause interference.
- **Whistles** will do better than your voice, but the moss swallows even them. Don't count on being heard.
- **You won't hear falling rock, a companion's slip, or your own footing.** You lose a sense most people don't know they rely on for balance and warning. Watch your feet.

**Psychological consequences:**
- After a few days you'll hear your pulse, your jaw, your breath. Some people find this deeply peaceful. Others find it claustrophobic, even frightening, especially at night with altitude-disturbed breathing.
- Consider bringing a small familiar sound: a short piece of music, a recording of a voice you love, a tiny bell, to use sparingly and quietly. Mostly you won't need it. When you do, you'll be glad of it.
- The ring of two shards knocked together will feel shocking. Enjoy it, once or twice. Don't make a habit of it.
- The silent waves of light when wind crosses the leaves are one of the great sights of the multiverse. Plan to sit for an hour and just watch.

## 9. The living things

**The plants:**
- **Walk on rock whenever you can,** on bare fracture faces and fresh scree, rather than on moss.
- **Never step on a cushion plant.** They are hard as stone and centuries old, as big as sleeping cattle, and they'll look like the perfect step. They are not steps.
- Don't pick, collect, or press anything. Don't take a single thumbnail orchid.
- **Don't eat anything.** The silvered, waxed, and metallic leaves suggest chemistry you don't want in your body, and the plants near the cables may concentrate who-knows-what.
- The lushness near the pipe ends is almost jungle. Don't bushwhack into it. Go around.

**The bird:** It's small, dun, silent, and hops between the thickets, feeding in the bromeliad cups. Don't disturb the bromeliads, which also means leaving their water alone.

**The insects:** Nothing bites. **Leave insect repellent at home.** You don't need it, and it can harm the pale moths and the polished beetles.

**The grazer:**
- If you see it, count yourself lucky. Many visitors never do.
- **Stay still.** Let it come to you. Let it look at you with those dark eyes. Look back.
- Don't feed it, don't touch it, don't photograph it with a flash.
- **Don't follow it when the cloud rises.** Nobody knows where it goes. Its path may lead nowhere a human can survive, and following anything into rising cloud on this ground is how people disappear.

## 10. Leaving no trace, and bringing nothing in

This deserves real rigor, more than most wilderness areas.

**Biosecurity before arrival:**
- This is a dense, closed, finely balanced system, and you are coming from another world.
- Scrub your boots, gaiters, poles, and pack, especially seams, treads, and velcro. Check pockets and cuffs for seeds, soil, and spores.
- Wash or freeze your clothing if you can.
- Bring no fresh food that could carry seeds: no whole fruit with pits, no raw grains.

**While there:**
- Pack out everything, including food scraps, tea bags, and toilet paper.
- **Human waste:** carry it out in waste bags. Digging "catholes" in moss over rock is impossible and destructive anyway.
- Make no fires. There's no deadwood
