# Preparing to Visit the Stair

## The short version

You are going to a place that is very high, very bright, very cold, very wet, and very quiet, and where the ground moves under you all the time. Each of these alone is manageable. Together they will wear you down faster than you expect. The people there have built a whole way of life around constant, patient effort. As a visitor you will be useful to them or a burden to them, and nothing in between. Prepare your body, your eyes, your feet, and your manners, and learn to talk with light before you go.

---

## 1. Anchor your return point to bedrock

This matters more than anything else, so it comes first.

If your method of travel fixes an arrival or return point to a location, **do not fix it anywhere on the tongue.** Every stone in the town is travelling toward the lip at about a body-length a day. A return anchor set on a boulder in a lane will be several houses downhill in a week and in the tarn within a season or two. You could come back and find your door somewhere under milk-green water.

Anchor to the **headwall**, the living rock at the top of the town, where the cushion plants grow. The cirque rim, the headwall, and the sky are the only fixed things there. Everything else is a conveyor belt.

The same applies to anything you set down. Don't cache supplies on the rubble. Don't leave a pack on a wall overnight and expect to find it where you left it. At that pace it won't have gone far, but it will have gone, and it may have tipped into a gap full of water.

---

## 2. Altitude

The cirque sits well above the treeline, with permanent snowfields, near-violet skies, and air thin enough to swallow sound. It is probably somewhere around 4,000–5,000 metres. Most people arriving there suddenly, without adjustment, will get sick.

**Acclimatize before you go.** If you travel by instant transfer rather than by walking up, you skip the gradual ascent that protects climbers. Spend two to three weeks beforehand at real altitude in your own world, climbing progressively and sleeping as high as you safely can. If you can arrive by walking up from a lower valley in the Stair's world, do that instead.

**Know the signs of altitude sickness:**
- Headache, nausea, poor sleep, and loss of appetite are ordinary acute mountain sickness. Rest, drink, and don't go higher.
- Breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, or pink froth means fluid on the lungs (HAPE). This is an emergency.
- Confusion, stumbling, or unusual behaviour means brain swelling (HACE). This is an emergency.

The only real cure is **descent**, and descent from the Stair is not simple. The low end of the town is a rubble lip dropping into an ice-cold lake. Before you go, know your emergency exit: either your return method, or a walked route out of the cirque that you have scouted. Carry acetazolamide and dexamethasone if you can get them, and learn how to use them.

**Expect reduced capacity even when acclimatized.** You'll be slower, clumsier, and short of breath doing things the locals do without thinking. Carrying stone uphill will leave you gasping after a few trips while a grandmother passes you with a larger load.

## 3. Your eyes

This is the second most dangerous thing about the Stair, and the easiest to underestimate.

The light comes from **every direction**: from the sun, off the snowfields all around, off the wet stones, and in flashes off mica in the rock and off thousands of signal mirrors. The locals squint permanently, wear slit visors, and many of the elderly have cataracts. That is decades of exposure. You can get snow blindness (sunburn of the cornea) in a single afternoon. It feels like sand ground into your eyes and can blind you for a day or two.

**Bring:**
- **Glacier glasses with full side shields**, category 4 lenses, wraparound. Ordinary sunglasses are not enough, because light comes up from below and in from the sides.
- **A second pair.** Lose or break your only pair and you are in serious trouble.
- Possibly a local **bone slit visor** as a backup. They work, and wearing one is a small courtesy.
- Lubricating eye drops. The air is thin and the glare constant.

**Expect visual fatigue.** The whole bowl glitters and switches. Stones tilt and flash, mirrors stab light at you, and shadows are faint and bluish, which makes depth hard to judge. If you are prone to migraines or light-triggered seizures, think seriously before going. Flashes are the town's language, and you cannot avoid them.

**Sunscreen everywhere, including places you'd never think of.** Because light reflects up off snow and wet stone, you'll burn under your chin, inside your nostrils, behind your ears, and on your eyelids. Use high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm with SPF, and a hat with a brim. Something to cover your neck is useful too.

## 4. Cold and wet, both at once

The Stair is not just cold. It is **cold and constantly wet**, and that combination is what causes hypothermia.

Every stone weeps meltwater. At noon water runs beneath everything. Fog climbs out of the tarn in the afternoon and beads on your hair and eyelashes. Then night comes, everything freezes, and the town glazes over. The cycle can repeat several times a day when clouds pass over the sun.

**Clothing principles:**
- **No cotton.** It soaks, stays wet, and chills you.
- **Wool or synthetic base layers**, which stay warm when damp. Bring more than one set so something dry is always waiting.
- A **waterproof, breathable shell**, jacket and trousers, worn nearly all the time. Squalls come over the rim without warning: violet sky, then snow, then violet again.
- A warm **insulated layer** for evenings and when you stop moving.
- **Gaiters** to keep meltwater and slush out of your boots.
- A warm hat, plus a buff or balaclava for fog and snow.
- **Two kinds of gloves.** You need tough, grippy, waterproof work gloves for handling wet, abrasive, freezing stone. You also need warm dry gloves for everything else. Bring spares of both. Wet stone will shred ordinary gloves in days.

**Feet** deserve their own section.

## 5. Walking on ground that walks

Nothing in the Stair is solid underfoot except the headwall. Every step lands on a stone that may be sliding, tilting, or glazed.

**The dangers:**
- **Dawn ice.** Every lane is a sheet of clear glaze until the carriers knock it loose and the sun softens it. Slipping on wet, uneven rock is the most likely way you'll hurt yourself.
- **Noon slop.** Water runs everywhere, stones settle, and blocks shift.
- **Sudden holes.** Ice melting out under a stone can make it collapse into a void full of meltwater cold enough to ache your teeth. Frost heave can also push stones up without warning.
- **Pinch points.** This is the one people don't think about. The stones grind past each other constantly. **Never put a hand, foot, or limb into a gap between stones**, and don't sit with your legs wedged in a crack. The movement is slow, but it is the movement of a glacier. It doesn't stop for flesh. A trapped ankle could be slowly crushed, and in a place where shouts die within ten paces, no one might hear you.
- **Leaning walls.** The lower quarter is full of unmaintained, disintegrating houses. Walls lean, courses split, and stones fall. Don't wander there casually, and never shelter against a wall there.
- **The lip.** A two-metre front of loose rubble above a lake at near-freezing temperature. Falling into water that cold causes an involuntary gasp and then rapid incapacity. **Stay well back from the lip, especially in fog**, when you can't see where the town ends.

**Bring:**
- **Stiff-soled, waterproof, insulated mountaineering boots** with good ankle support.
- **Microspikes or light crampons** for dawn ice. Ask your hosts whether metal spikes on their walls are acceptable. They may damage lichen-stones that families have kept for twenty generations. Be ready to take them off in lanes and near houses.
- **Trekking poles** with rubber or carbide tips, to feel out stones before you trust them.
- The habit of **testing every step.** Watch how the locals move. Copy their pace and where they put their feet. Watch the goats too. They seem to enjoy it, which you won't at first.

**Timing your movement:** late morning, once the ice has softened and before the heavy melt, is probably the safest time to get around. Dawn is glass, noon is running water, afternoon is fog.

## 6. Silence and not being able to call for help

This is the part of the Stair that will feel most alien.

Sound dies within ten paces. Two people at arm's length have to lean in to hear each other. Think about what that means for you:

- **You cannot shout for help.** A whistle, the standard emergency signal, may be almost useless.
- **You won't hear things coming.** Avalanches off the snowfields are silent. Shifting stones, a falling wall, a goat, a person with a load behind you: you'll hear none of them in time. You have to keep looking.
- **You'll lose each other easily.** If you travel with companions, a few lanes' distance is enough to lose contact completely.

**Learn to flash before you go.** This is the most important social and safety preparation you can do. Everyone in the Stair carries a mirror, and children learn to flash before they can talk. If a multiverse guide or earlier visitor can teach you even the basics of their code (short and long, doubled and tripled, angled off snow or sky), learn it. At the very least, agree a simple code with your companions: help, come here, all well, danger.

**Bring a proper signal mirror.** A heliograph-style one with a sighting hole lets you aim flashes accurately. Practice aiming in bright sun before you go. It is harder than it looks.

**At night the town goes mute.** There's no sun for mirrors. You can't flash and you can't shout. Be indoors, with people, before dusk. A headlamp is still worth carrying for your own safety. Ask whether light in the dark lanes is acceptable, and don't flash it at houses. It may mean something to them, and a stranger's light in the dark could alarm people.

**The psychological effect.** Many people find heavy silence unsettling. Expect some disorientation and a sense of pressure in your ears. You may feel watched or startled, because things appear beside you without warning. It helps to know this in advance. The silence is not a sign that anything is wrong.

## 7. Talking to people

**Close conversation is a murmur, faces nearly touching, often with hands on each other's forearms to feel the voice.** This is normal and polite. It is not intimacy and not aggression. If you are uncomfortable with close physical contact from strangers, prepare yourself mentally. Leaning away may be read as refusing to talk.

**Never shout.** It won't carry anyway. It will just look strange and possibly frightening, like someone making huge gestures in a quiet room.

**Talk to the elders by touch.** Many old people are nearly blind and can't read flashes. They navigate by counting steps and by the feel of the lane. Approach them slowly, let them know you're there, and speak close. Don't grab their arm to guide them unless they ask. They know the way better than you do.

**Language.** You probably won't know theirs. Learn a few murmured phrases if you can. More useful still, learn their flash conventions for basic needs.

## 8. Your hosts, and the work

The town's whole life is the work of carrying stone uphill. Every house has to climb as fast as the ground carries it down, or it drifts.

**As a guest you add a mouth and a body to someone's house.** Your hosts' food, fuel, and water are finite. Their position on the slope depends on labour. If they are hosting you instead of carrying stone, their house may be drifting a little on your account. Take that seriously.

**Offer your hands.** This is probably the most meaningful gift you can give. You'll be clumsy and slow, and you'll tire quickly at altitude, but carrying is what the town means by being a good member of a household.

**Train for it before you go:**
- Climb stairs and hills with a heavy pack.
- The locals carry with **tumplines**, braided goat-hair straps across the forehead. That puts the load on the neck and spine. If you plan to use one, strengthen your neck gradually beforehand, or bring a proper pack frame and accept that you'll look odd.
- Prepare your hands. Wet, cold, rough stone is hard on skin. Work gloves are essential.

**Don't move stones on your own initiative.** Every stone belongs to a house, a course, or a family's history. Some carry lichens older than the houses and are known by face like grandmothers. Taking a stone from the "wrong" wall could damage someone's position on the slope. Ask, watch, and carry only what you are given.

**Don't take stones as souvenirs.** Not even little ones. Especially not lichen-stones.

**Where you're hosted means something.** Height is wealth. The honoured hearths are at the headwall and the drifting ones near the lip. Your hosts' position will shape how others see you and how you are received in other quarters. Be aware of this without playing into it.

## 9. Drifting, the lip, and not pitying people

*They're drifting* is how the town speaks of decline: illness, old age, or a household that has stopped. Houses near the lip hold the dead and the departed and come apart stone by stone into the tarn.

People speak of this **without sorrow, the way elsewhere people speak of leaves.** *My grandmother's hearthstone went over the lip in the thaw.*

**Don't bring heavy condolence to these moments.** Grief is real everywhere, but a loud show of sympathy, or pity for a drifting household, may come across as rude, strange, or insulting. Follow your hosts' tone. Accept the statement quietly. Maybe touch a forearm if you've learned how.

**Don't go and watch family stones fall unless you are invited.** Some people go down to watch; most don't. It is a family matter.

**Don't try to "help" a drifting house** by carrying stones for it on your own initiative. It might be welcome, or it might interfere with something you don't understand: a family's choice, a death, a slow and dignified letting go. Ask first.

## 10. Water, food, and fuel

**Water.** People drink from the stones. Each house has a soapstone drip-trough under its north wall, and the wall fills it. This water is probably clean. It is meltwater from buried ice, filtered through rock. Your gut isn't adapted to local water, though, so bring a filter or purification tablets for the first days.

- **Don't drink the tarn water.** It is milky with rock flour, and suspended grit can irritate your gut.
- **Don't contaminate a drip-trough.** Don't wash your hands or dip your bottle directly in it without asking. It is a household's drinking supply.
- **Drink a lot.** Altitude dehydrates you, and the cold hides how thirsty you are.

**Food.** The town has goats for milk, and pikas are sometimes eaten. Very little grows. Food is likely scarce and precious.

- **Bring your own calorie-dense food** for your whole stay: nuts, fats, dried meat, chocolate, grains. Altitude kills appetite, so bring things you genuinely like to eat.
- Accept small portions of what you are offered, with gratitude, but don't eat into your hosts' stores. Your food is a good gift to share. Fats and sugars are probably treasures in a place like this.
- Goat milk may upset your stomach at first. Go gently.

**Fuel.** Goat dung, burned for warmth and in fat lamps. Expect smoky rooms at night. Dry-stone walls are drafty, which probably helps with ventilation. Still, if you get a pounding headache indoors, step outside for air.

**Toileting.** Ask. In a town where water runs beneath everything and people drink from the walls, where waste goes is certainly a serious and settled matter. Don't improvise.

## 11. Sleeping on a moving floor

At night families lie on felts and feel the floor slide slowly toward the lip. You'll hear the grinding of stone, the voice of a pika in the walls, and every so often a faint *plock* from far below.

**Expect this to disturb your sleep at first.** The steady sense of motion is like sleeping on a boat. Some people find it soothing, and others get vestibular unease or mild nausea. Earplugs won't help much, since the motion is felt as much as heard, but an eye mask might help against the brightness at dawn.

**Don't camp on the tongue.** A tent pitched on the rubble would drift, sit over water and ice, and take the full nightly freeze. Sleep in a house.

**Expect visitors in the night.** Pikas come and go through the walls freely. **Dippers** may surface through a hole in the floor, dripping, calm, and entirely unconcerned. Don't panic, don't strike at them, and don't leave food out.

**After you leave**, you may keep feeling the ground move for days. This is called disembarkment syndrome. It usually passes.
