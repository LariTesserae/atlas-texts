# Preparing for the Rising Country

## A Traveler's Briefing

The Rising Country is not hostile the way a battlefield or a predator's territory is hostile. Nothing here wants you dead. The trouble is that nothing here has settled into any shape that accounts for you. The air is not yet air you can live in. The ground is not yet ground in the usual sense. The darkness is not night. You are visiting a place in the middle of being made, and the process has not yet produced anywhere for a human to stand.

You can visit, but only briefly and deliberately, and you will spend the whole time working to stay alive. The visit is still worth making. Very few places let you watch a mountain grow, or hold a stone that is older than the ground beneath it.

This briefing covers the body, then the equipment, then the hazards and how to move through them, then the living film and the built stones, and finally the mind, which will be the part of you most at risk.

---

## Part One: The Body

### Altitude

Treat this as death-zone altitude or worse. The description gives you the clues: a sky that "should be" blue-black, stars that would be hard and many, breath that feels "half-taken." That is the altitude of the highest peaks of our own world, and the plateau is still rising while you stand on it. Your arrival point will be higher when you leave than when you came.

Unaided acclimatization will not be enough. Even very well-adapted mountaineers lose judgment, coordination, and memory at these heights, and this terrain punishes lost judgment faster than any mountain on Earth.

**Before you go:**
- Spend at least three to four weeks at real high altitude in a stable, ordinary place. Climb high during the day and sleep lower. This does not make you fit for the Rising Country. It makes you less fragile once your oxygen system fails, and at some point it will.
- Get a full medical evaluation, including cardiac and pulmonary screening. If you have any history of altitude illness, migraine with aura, clotting disorders, or cardiac irregularity, reconsider going at all.
- Train your legs and core for slow, loaded, careful movement over broken rock. Speed does not matter here. Balance while carrying weight does.
- Train yourself to follow checklists, until checking your oxygen, gas monitor, and tether becomes a reflex you perform even when you feel fine. At altitude you will feel fine right up until you are not.

**Supplemental oxygen is mandatory.** A closed-circuit rebreather is ideal because it stretches your supply and warms the gas. Bring redundant supplies and a spare regulator, and set a hard rule: when your reserve reaches a pre-set level, you leave, whatever you are looking at.

A pulse oximeter should be on your finger or wrist at all times, with an alarm. Check it on a schedule, not only when you think to.

### Temperature: The Split Body

This is one of the strangest physical experiences the place offers, and it can hurt you in quiet ways. **The air is freezing and the ground is warm.** Your feet will feel comfortable, even pleasant, while wind strips heat from your face and hands. Your body receives contradictory reports and does not know how to respond. Frostbite can develop on exposed skin while you feel as though you are standing beside a hearth.

At the same time, the ground's warmth is not uniform. The rock is "warm as a hand" in most places, but near the larger fissures it becomes kiln-hot, and new fissures open without warning. The surface temperature beneath you can change quickly and a great deal.

Dress for Arctic air above and volcanic ground below.

### Water and Food

There is no water. There has never been water here. There is not even snow, because the air holds nothing to fall. Every drop you drink you must carry in.

At altitude, in dry, fast wind, you lose water very quickly through breath alone. Plan for four to six liters per day, which is heavy. The weight of water is the main reason visits here should last hours, or a day or two at most, rather than weeks.

Carry calorie-dense food that you can eat through a mask port or during brief, controlled moments with the mask off. You will not feel hungry, because altitude suppresses appetite. Eat on a schedule.

Keep water bottles against your body. The air will freeze them and the ground will not keep them warm unless you set them down, which you should not do.

## Part Two: Equipment

### The Suit

You want something between a high-altitude mountaineering system and a volcanologist's field kit. Build it in layers:

- **Base and mid layers:** Standard high-altitude insulation for the upper body and arms. The air is the enemy there.
- **Outer shell:** Heat-reflective, abrasion-resistant, and sealed at the wrists, ankles, and neck against ash. Aluminized or ceramic-coated fabrics, of the kind used near lava flows, are worth the weight on the lower legs.
- **Boots:** Thick, heat-rated soles, the kind made for walking on recently cooled lava. Soft rubber soles can soften or glaze on hot rock. The uppers need insulation against the cold. Add knee-high gaiters.
- **Gloves:** Two properties matter, cut resistance and heat resistance. Obsidian edges are among the sharpest natural edges that exist, and many fracture planes here are "split so recently that the faces still hold a faint mineral sheen." Every surface you grab could cut you. Wear a cut-resistant liner under an insulated, heat-rated outer glove.
- **Face:** A full-face mask that integrates your oxygen supply, gas filtration, and sealed eye protection. Exposed skin will freeze, ash will reach your eyes, and gas will reach your lungs. Cover everything.

### Respiratory and Eye Protection

**Volcanic ash is not soot.** It is pulverized rock and glass: tiny, jagged, abrasive shards. It scratches eyes, lacerates lungs, gums up zippers and seals, and grinds its way into electronics.

- Use combined filter cartridges for particulates (P100 or equivalent) and acid gases, since sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide are near-certain here.
- Carry spare cartridges and swap them on a schedule. An ash-front can clog a filter in minutes.
- Bring sealed goggles as a backup to your mask. Bring sterile saline for eye irrigation, because you will get grit in your eyes eventually and there is no water to rinse with.

### Gas Detection

**This may be the single most important piece of equipment you carry.**

The description notes that "in the hollows between boulders the heat pools." Where heat pools, gas pools too. Carbon dioxide from volcanic vents is heavier than air and settles into depressions, low chambers, and sheltered pockets, exactly the places a cold, tired traveler will be drawn to rest. It is invisible and odorless and can kill in the time it takes to sit down.

Hydrogen sulfide is worse in one respect. At low concentrations it smells of rotten eggs, but at dangerous concentrations it deadens your sense of smell. The smell vanishing is the warning.

- Carry a multi-gas monitor for CO₂, H₂S, SO₂, CO, and O₂, with loud and vibrating alarms.
- Mount it low, at ankle or knee height, not at your chest. The danger rises from the ground. A second unit on a pole, which you can lower into a hollow or a doorway before you enter it, is worth the extra weight.
- The rule is absolute: **never sit, crouch, or rest in a hollow without testing it first.** When in doubt, stay on higher, open, wind-swept ground.

### Light

"You see by the ground. The sky gives you nothing." The underglow is real and useful, but it is uneven, it pulses, and it vanishes entirely inside ash-fronts.

- Carry at least two headlamps with red-light modes. Red light preserves your night vision, and you will rely on it heavily to read the underglow.
- Bring a handheld high-output lamp for scanning ahead across broken ground.
- Carry strobes or beacons you can set down or clip to your partner.
- Battery chemistry suffers in cold. Keep spares inside your suit against your body.

The underglow tells you things. Orange means a fissure. A brightening orange means one that is widening. A darkening line may mean one that is sealing, or that ash is passing over it. Learn to read it the way sailors read water.

### Navigation

Maps are useless here. As the description warns, "Nothing here is where it was." A map made on Monday is fiction by Thursday.

Instead:
- Carry an inertial navigation unit or step-counting system that tracks where you have moved, since you cannot trust what you see.
- Bring your own **placed beacons**: small, bright, battery-powered markers you set down along your route. Accept that the ground will carry them upward, tip them over, or swallow them.
- Keep a written or recorded log of patterns rather than places. Note which direction the ground rises, which ridges shed, the rhythm of the nearest vents (time their pulses), and which fissures have stayed open and which have wandered. That is what the land itself advises: *learn it as a pattern rather than a map.*
- Use the built structures as landmarks. They are the only things on the plateau that keep a recognizable shape, and they move only as the ground moves. More on them below.

### Ropes and Probes

- Travel roped to a partner, with enough slack for normal movement and a solid anchor habit when you stop.
- Carry a **probe pole**, a long, heat-resistant rod, to test ground ahead of you, sweep across hair-thin fissures, and check whether a slab is solid before you commit weight to it.
- Bring a small anchor kit, though fresh rock is fractured and unreliable, so treat every anchor as suspect.

### Medical Kit

Beyond ordinary expedition supplies:
- **Altitude medications** (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine), with training in when and how to use each.
- **A portable hyperbaric bag** if you can manage the weight. It can save a life when high-altitude cerebral or pulmonary edema develops.
- **A burn kit**: non-adherent dressings and burn gel. You will touch something hotter than you expected.
- **A laceration kit**: closure strips, sterile dressings, and tourniquets. Obsidian cuts are clean and deep.
- **Eye irrigation** (saline) and an eye patch.
- Wound irrigation fluid. There is no water here to spare.

## Part Three: Moving Through the Land

### The Principle

Everything moves, but not constantly, and not uniformly. The skill is to recognize which movements you can predict and which you cannot, and to stay out of the path of the ones you cannot.

### Fissures

- **The big ones stay open. The small ones wander.** Paradoxically, the small cracks are more dangerous, because you ignore them and they change.
- Never step across a glowing crack without probing both sides. A hair-thin line can be the surface trace of something wider below.
- Don't linger at the edge of a chasm. The heat rising from it, "like the open door of a kiln," carries gas with it. Look quickly if you must, while your partner holds the rope, then step back.
- Avoid areas crossed by many parallel small cracks. They mark ground that is actively splitting.
- Listen for the sound of tearing cloth. It is sharp but small, and it means a new fissure is opening close by. Stop, look down, and look around. Move toward ground that looks solid and unbroken.

### Rockfall and Unstable Boulders

"Everywhere, boulders balance in positions that gravity has not yet had time to argue with."

- **Do not touch balanced boulders. Do not lean on them, climb them, or pass directly downslope of them.**
- Stay off the flanks of the northern ridges that "shed." When you cross beneath a shedding line, do so quickly, one person at a time, with the other watching.
- Avalanches here are "slow, grey, rolling" and arrive as a "soft, crumbling hiss." The quietness of this place means **you will not hear danger coming the way you would elsewhere.**

### Listening with Your Hands and Feet

Because the thin air carries so little sound, you will have to feel for danger:

- Stop regularly, go still, and pay attention through your feet. Shudders travel through rock better than through air.
- Occasionally put a gloved palm or your ear to a large, stable boulder and listen for the "slow grinding groan, very far down." Learn what normal sounds like. Changes in it may mean changes in the ground.
- If you can, carry a small seismometer or vibration sensor. Some phones and tablets can serve if protected from ash.
- The "ticking of rock as it heats and cools" is constant and harmless. Learn to tune it out so you notice when it stops or changes.

### Ash-Fronts

"Inside an ash-front the world goes fully black."

Rehearse this protocol before you leave, until it is automatic:

1. **Stop at the first sign of an approaching plume.** Do not try to outrun it across unknown ground.
2. **Close the distance to your partner** and check the rope.
3. **Crouch or kneel on solid ground** if you are on open ground. Do not retreat into a hollow, which could hold gas.
4. **Check your mask seal and filters.**
5. **Turn on a strobe**, not to see by, but so your partner can see you.
6. **Wait.** Do not move until it passes. The ground may shift while you cannot see it.
7. **Afterward, re-orient from scratch.** Assume the terrain has changed. Look for your beacons, check your inertial log, and find the nearest structure or known feature.

### Volcanic Lightning

Ash plumes can generate lightning, sometimes intense, and on high ridges in thin air you are exposed. If you see flashes inside a plume or feel your hair lift, get off the crest. Find a spot that is neither a sharp high point nor a gas-filled low point: a broad, gentle slope a little below a ridgeline. Crouch low with your feet together.

### Where Not to Be

- Hollows (gas)
- Beneath balanced stones or shedding ridges (rockfall)
- Beside chasms (heat and gas)
- On ground crossed by many small cracks (active splitting)
- Near a vent during its bright phase (heat and gas surges)
- On the exposed crest of a ridge during an ash storm (lightning)

What remains are broad, open slopes of solid-looking rock, away from vents and away from loose stone. They are few. Learn to find them.

### Resting and Sleeping

Ideally, don't. Keep visits short enough that you never need to sleep here.

If you must rest, choose a broad, stable spur of rock, test the gas, make sure nothing looms overhead, anchor yourselves, and set alarms on both your gas monitor and your oximeter. Sleep in shifts, with one person awake at all times.

### Time

There is no day. The ash ceiling blocks the sun, and the underglow gives no cue for morning. Your circadian rhythm will drift and then fail.

Wear a watch set to the time you keep at home, and work to a strict written schedule for eating, drinking, rest, oxygen checks, and turnaround. The place will not tell you what time it is, and at altitude your sense of how long you have been here will be badly wrong.

## Part Four: The Film

Near the vents, where warm air meets cold, you'll find the mineral dew in "pale fans and feathers" and the dark crust growing in slow rings, rust-red or bruise-violet. It is the only life here.

**Leave it alone.** This matters more than it might seem, for several reasons:

1. **For your safety.** It grows at vents and gas boundaries, which are exactly the places you should avoid. It is also "slick," with "a faint wet sheen." If you step on it on a slope, you may slide.
2. **For its safety.** A boot print could wipe out a colony that has been advancing, a fraction of a finger's width at a time, since the last fissure opened.
3. **For the whole world.** This is "a world that has not yet been introduced to anything that moves on its own." You would be the first. The bacteria, fungal spores, and microorganisms on your skin, in your breath, and in the treads of your boots could be the first foreign life ever to arrive here. Nobody can predict what they would do to the film, or to the future of whatever might come after it.

**Contamination protocols:**
- Decontaminate the exterior of your boots, suit, and gear before arrival.
- **Pack out everything.** That includes food waste, packaging, and human waste. Bring sealed waste bags and use them.
- Don't breathe directly onto the colonies. Keep your mask on near them.
- Don't sample the film unless you're equipped for sterile collection and have a very good reason. If you must, take the smallest amount possible, from the edge, and seal it immediately.

Watching it is permitted, and worth the time. If you stand beside a patch long enough, you can see its edge move. It is "the only thing on the plateau that seems to have kept pace with the mountain." There is patience in it that is worth learning from.

## Part Five: The Stones Someone Placed

The cairn, the curving wall, the ring of slabs, the hut with the corbelled roof: these are the heart of the place, and they deserve care.

### Practical Considerations

- **Use them as landmarks.** They are the most stable reference points on the plateau. They move, but they keep their shape longer than anything else here.
- **Don't lean on them or climb them.** Several are already tilting, cracked, or being pulled apart. The wall on the eastern ridge is split by a fissure. The ring "leans outward like petals." Your weight could be what finally brings one down.
- **The stone ring surrounds a vent that no longer breathes.** Dead vents can wake up. Before you enter the ring, test the gas at its center with your pole-mounted monitor, since CO₂ may still be seeping. Don't stand in the middle.
- **The hut is a gas trap.** A low, enclosed stone chamber with a doorway you must crawl through is the textbook place for heavy gases to collect. The "smell of old smoke" could be a memory preserved by the stone, or it could mean something is still seeping in. **Lower your monitor through the door and wait for a reading before you put your head inside.** Crawling puts your face exactly where the gas would be. Don't go in alone, and keep your partner outside with the rope.

### Respect

- **Don't take stones.** Not even a pebble from the cairn. These are the only things on the plateau that remember another world.
- **Don't try to repair or restore them.** The urge will be strong, especially with the cracked wall. But you don't know how they were meant to stand, and the mountain will keep taking them apart whatever you do. What you would add is a false history.
- **Don't add to them.** Placing a stone on the cairn is a common human instinct, but it would mix your moment with theirs.
- **Photograph, measure, sketch, and record.** Note how they're tilting, where the cracks are, and how far one half of the wall has risen above the other. If you or anyone else returns, those records will show how fast the country is taking them apart.

The swept floor of the hut is worth a moment of attention. Someone once swept it, a person who kept this small space clean on lower, gentler ground, where the air was thick enough to breathe. You'll likely feel the weight of that. Let yourself.

## Part Six: The Mind

The Rising Country will be hard on your body. It may be harder on your mind, and your mind is what keeps your body alive.

### Hypoxia's Traps

Oxygen deprivation doesn't feel like suffering. It often feels like confidence, calm, even euphoria. You may decide that a gas reading is probably fine, that a boulder is probably stable, that the turnaround time can stretch a little because you're so close to the next structure. **This is why every critical decision must be made before you arrive, and written down.** Your turnaround time, oxygen reserve limits, and gas rules should not be negotiable once you're there. Agree with your partner in advance that either of you can call a turnaround, and that the other will not argue.

### Sensory Disorientation

Everything here contradicts ordinary expectations:
- Warm feet, freezing face.
- Violence without sound.
- Darkness that is not night.
- Mountains that look ancient and are days old.
- Stones that look like they belong and don't.

Expect to feel uneasy, and possibly nauseated or dizzy, from these contradictions alone. Your brain will struggle to build a stable picture of where you are. This is normal. Rely on instruments and on your partner's observations as well as your own senses.

### The Silence and the Singing

The hush of the place can be oppressive. The constant clicking of rock, "like a thousand small clocks," may become hypnotic. And on the worst nights, the obsidian curtains "sing together, a pale chord without melody."

Hearing it may unsettle you. You may feel it is addressed to you, or feel something close to religious awe, or dread. None of this is dangerous in itself. But the singing usually means strong ash-wind is hitting the glass, which means an ash-front may be close. **Treat the chord as a weather warning first and a wonder second.**

### Loneliness

There are no animals, no birds, no insects, nothing that moves on its own except you and whoever you came with. The structures suggest people, but the people are gone, perhaps for ages beyond counting.

**Never visit alone.** This is a safety rule, and it is also a matter of sanity. Having another human voice here, another body keeping the same schedule, is an anchor in a place where nothing else holds still.

### Grief

Many visitors will likely feel a particular sadness at the structures: the thought of a lowland people, their shrines and walls and one small swept house, lifted over unknown ages to the roof of the world and set among fire in the dark, now slowly being broken. This isn't a hazard, but it can be heavy. Give yourself time to feel it when you're safely home. Don't let it distract you on the ridge.

## Part Seven: Leaving

Plan your exit before you arrive, and plan it with this in mind: **your arrival point will not stay where it was.**

The ground rises "a hand's width in a day, sometimes more, sometimes in lurches." Fissures open and close. A spot that was stable when you came may be split, tilted, or buried when you return to it.

- If your means of traveling between worlds depends on a fixed physical location, it is at risk here. Choose a method that doesn't depend on the terrain, or one that can be triggered from anywhere, or one tied to something portable you carry.
- If your exit must be tied to a place, choose the most stable ground you can find: broad, open, uncracked rock, well away from vents, ridges that shed, and balanced stones. Mark it with multiple beacons and record its relationship to the nearest structure.
- Have a contingency plan, and a second one, in case your exit point is destroyed while you're away from it.
- Leave with a real margin of oxygen, water, and energy. Don't spend your reserves reaching one more structure.

When you get home, decontaminate thoroughly, carefully, and away from other people. Ash in fabric and gear is abrasive and harmful to breathe. Clean your equipment outdoors. See a doctor for a check of your lungs and eyes, even if you feel fine.

## What It Will Actually Be Like

Your intuition should prepare you for this more than any checklist.

You will arrive in dim orange darkness, breathing through a mask, feeling the strange warmth under your feet. For the first hour you'll be almost entirely occupied with not dying: checking gas, probing ground, reading the underglow, keeping the rope right. It will be exhausting and frightening, and it will not feel like sightseeing.

Then, slowly, you'll start to see the patterns. You'll notice the vent to the east breathing on a rhythm. You'll notice that the northern ridge sheds and the southern ground sags. You'll feel the shudder come through your boots before the hiss reaches your ears. You'll catch yourself thinking *that ridge is taller than it was*, and you'll be right. The land will stop looking like chaos and start to look like a process, like something being made.

And at some point, crossing a saddle in the pulsing glow, you'll see a line of fitted stones on a ridge crest. Something small and careful, set there by hands. Its edges will be softened by weather that this place has never had. You'll stand beside it in the thin, hot, freezing, silent dark, and you'll understand that you are looking at the oldest thing for miles: a memory of lower ground, of thicker air and a sky with a sun in it, carried up here by the mountain and not yet forgotten.

That moment is the reason to come. Prepare well enough that you are still clear-headed and alive when it happens, and prepare to leave soon after, before the place has had time to argue with you.
