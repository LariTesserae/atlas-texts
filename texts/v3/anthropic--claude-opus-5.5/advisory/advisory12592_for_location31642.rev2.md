# Preparing to Visit the Combing Stones

## What You're Actually Walking Into

The description is lovely, and underneath it is a demanding environment: a high, cold, wet, labyrinthine landscape the size of a small sea. It has no signage, no food, and almost no people. Its structure is slowly and constantly falling down. Its weather runs on a daily cycle driven by the city itself.

It is not dangerous the way a war zone or a volcano is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a bog, a slot canyon, or an alpine moor is dangerous. Small discomforts accumulate quietly, and the place never raises its voice to warn you.

The best mental model is an alpine wilderness trek through a stone maze inside a cloud. Prepare for that, and add the courtesy you'd bring to a sacred site that is also someone's home.

---

## Part One: Reading the Clues About Conditions

The description never gives numbers, but it tells you a lot.

**Altitude.** Pikas, cushion plants, knee-high hundred-year-old junipers, clear radiative nights, and hard noon light all point to real altitude. It is probably alpine, possibly 2,500 to 4,000 meters or the local equivalent. If you arrive from sea level, you may feel it: headache, poor sleep, breathlessness on the stepped lanes. If you can, spend a few days below the escarpment first, or climb the escarpment slowly on foot rather than stepping out onto the plateau at the top.

**Temperature.** Nights are cold enough for rime on the capstones before dawn, apparently even outside winter. Mornings are cold and saturated. Noon may feel genuinely warm in the sun. Afternoons bring cold rain. In a single day you may cycle through near-freezing damp, bright warmth, and a chilling downpour, then a clear, cold night.

**Wetness.** This is the defining condition. From dawn to about noon in the western districts, you are inside a cloud that condenses on every surface, including you. You will not get rained on so much as slowly saturated. Then, most afternoons, it actually rains.

**The gradient.** The west (windward, near the escarpment) is the wettest, slickest, darkest, and foggiest. The east (lee) is drier, paler, and lichen-covered, and gets morning sun while the west is still in cloud. This east-west gradient is the most useful piece of geography you have.

## Part Two: Clothing — The Hypothermia Problem

The classic killer in places like this is not dramatic cold. It is being wet and slightly cold for many hours in a row. The combing fog will find its way into everything.

**Absolutely no cotton.** Not socks, not underwear, not a T-shirt. Cotton holds water and cools you. Wool or synthetics only.

**Layer system:**
- **Base layer:** merino wool, top and bottom. It stays warm when damp and doesn't stink after days of wear.
- **Mid layer:** a fleece or light synthetic insulated jacket. Synthetic insulation is better than down here, because down collapses when wet and you will not get it dry.
- **Shell:** a genuinely waterproof, breathable jacket with a good hood, plus waterproof trousers. You'll wear the shell most mornings and every afternoon.
- **Warm layer for camp and night:** a heavier synthetic puffy, kept in a dry bag and only worn when you've stopped moving.

**Hands.** You'll be touching wet stone constantly: bracing against lane walls, steadying yourself on steps, feeling your way through dim tunnels. Wet stone pulls heat out of bare hands fast. Bring thin liner gloves for dexterity, waterproof overmitts for rain, and a spare pair of liners. You'll also want bare hands sometimes, to cup water or to seat a stone properly, so keep gloves easy to remove.

**Head.** A warm hat for nights and mornings, a brimmed hat or cap for the noon sun, and a buff or neck gaiter.

**Feet: the most important section.** You will have wet feet. Plan for it rather than trying to prevent it entirely.
- Boots or approach shoes with **soft, sticky rubber soles and good tread**. The black, tar-like lichen films in the channels and the worn, saddle-shaped steps will be as slick as wet ice. Hard or smooth soles are an injury waiting to happen.
- Good ankle support. The ground is all cobbles, uneven slabs, and dished steps.
- Several pairs of wool socks. Rotate them, and dry the used pair against your body or in the noon sun.
- Foot powder or an anti-maceration balm. Days of wet feet can lead to trench foot, even in mild temperatures. **Take your boots off at noon every day, let your feet air and dry, and change socks.** This matters more than almost anything else on this list.

## Part Three: Gear — Built for Narrowness

The lanes are rarely wider than one person. Many are roofed into stone tunnels, lit only by gaps where capstones have slipped. Plan your kit for this.

**Pack:** Choose a narrow, low-profile pack with nothing lashed to the outside. A wide expedition frame or dangling gear will snag on walls, scrape lichen, and possibly dislodge stones. A tall pack will hit tunnel roofs. Picture yourself turning sideways and stooping through a damp stone throat, and pack accordingly. Put everything in dry bags inside the pack.

**Light:** A headlamp with a red-light mode, plus spare batteries or a charge source. The tunnels and beehive cells are dim. Red light is also more courteous around inhabitants and animals, and it preserves your night vision under what are probably spectacular clear skies.

**Helmet:** A lightweight climbing helmet is worth considering, especially in the old western quarters, and essential in spring thaw, after autumn storms, and in winter. Capstones drop into cells. Courses slump. Ice feathers an arm's length long break off. Be aware that a helmet also makes low tunnels more awkward.

**Water treatment:** More on this below. A filter, purification tablets, or both.

**Shelter and sleep:**
- A sleeping bag rated below freezing, a sleeping pad (stone floors pull heat out of you all night), and a waterproof bivy bag or small tarp.
- You may be tempted to sleep in an abandoned beehive cell. Read the section on cells before you do.

**Navigation:** A compass (test it: some multiverse locations have odd magnetism), an altimeter or barometer if you have one (useful for reading weather), and a **notebook and pencil**. Your notebook is your breadcrumb trail, because you shouldn't mark the walls.

**Other essentials:**
- A small sit pad. Sitting on wet, cold stone saps your warmth.
- A whistle, with caveats explained below.
- Sunglasses and sunscreen. The noon light is "hard and clean" at altitude, often after hours of fog have lowered your guard.
- Bags for packing out waste, including human waste. See below; this is not optional.
- A basic first-aid kit weighted toward sprains, abrasions, blisters, and foot care.

## Part Four: Food and Fuel

**Nothing edible grows in the city.** There are no fields, no fruit, no game you should hunt. The inhabitants eat what comes up from below the escarpment or from the peat meadows at the far northern edge. Food here is carried in, which means it is precious.

- **Bring all your food for the whole visit, plus a reserve of several days.** Getting lost is a real possibility, and your stay may run long.
- Choose calorie-dense food. Cold and wet burn energy fast, and warm food raises morale enormously.
- **Don't expect to buy or be given food.** If you're offered some, understand what the gesture costs them.
- Food you've carried up may be the most meaningful thing you can offer the inhabitants. Offer it; don't press it.

**Fuel:** Bring your own stove and fuel. The cells smell of old smoke, so people do burn something, perhaps peat from the north or brought-up wood. **Do not burn juniper.** Those knee-high trees are a century old, deliberately left alone, and bound up in an unresolved question about whether they hold the walls together or pull them apart. Burning one would be both ecological vandalism and a serious breach of local feeling.

## Part Five: Water — Abundant, but Treat It with Care

It's tempting to think a city that drinks cloud has perfectly pure water. Mostly it probably does. Freshly condensed fog is close to distilled. But it runs down stone covered in lichen, moss, bird droppings (those orange lichen rosettes mark where birds perched), snail tracks, and centuries of residue, and then it stands in cisterns.

**Best practice:**
- The freshest water is what's beading and threading down a clean joint in the morning, especially from a high point. The locals cup their hands under a weeping joint, and it's reasonable to do the same with a bottle.
- Treat or filter anything from channels, gutters, or cisterns.
- Avoid water that has run through the black lichen films in the channels or pooled near animal areas.

**The critical rule: the entire city is a watershed, and you are standing in it.** Every wall feeds a channel, every channel a gutter, and every gutter a cistern that someone may drink from. So:
- **Never wash, rinse dishes, spit toothpaste, or pee into any channel, gutter, or cistern.** Not even a little.
- **Don't cup your hands under a weeping joint if they're coated in sunscreen, insect repellent, or stove fuel.**
- **Pack out solid human waste.** Bring waste bags. There is no safe place to dig a cat-hole in a city made of drainage. If there is local practice for this, and there must be, ask about it or watch for it, and follow it.
- Grey water (from cooking or washing) goes into your waste container, or is scattered thinly well away from any drainage path, if you can even find such a place. In the wetter districts, there isn't one.

A careless visitor could genuinely contaminate a household's water supply. This is the single most serious way you could harm the place or its people.

## Part Six: Living by the City's Day

The daily cycle is so strong and so regular that you should plan every day around it.

**Before dawn — cold and still.** Rime whitens the capstones. Stay in your bag until you need to move. If you're up, the stars and the silence may be the most beautiful thing you experience here. Watch out for rime on steps: it's slippery.

**First light — the cloud arrives.** If you're in the western districts, the fog will come over the rim like a slow grey wall and swallow everything within an hour. Visibility drops to a few walls' length and sound goes flat.
- **Don't start a long or unfamiliar journey as the fog arrives.** Know where you are before it does.
- Morning in the west is good for short, known routes, for staying put, or for simply experiencing it: standing in a lane inside the cloud, listening to the walls weep. That is the heart of this place, and it deserves an hour of stillness.
- If you want sun in the morning, be in the eastern districts. You can watch the fog front come toward you across miles of domes, which must be an extraordinary sight.

**Mid-morning — the weeping.** The western walls run with water. Everything is slick. Drink fresh water now, and move carefully if you move at all.

**Noon — the window.** The cloud lifts, the light comes back hard, and the city steams. **This is your travel and orientation window.**
- Find a high tower if you can, and look at the roofline to see where you are.
- Take your sun bearing.
- Dry your socks, air your feet, and eat a warm meal.
- Put on sunscreen and sunglasses. The contrast after a morning of fog will be startling.

**Afternoon — the city makes its weather.** Cumulus build over the districts in lines following the walls. By three or four they're dark-bottomed.
- **Plan to be under cover, or at least settled for the night, by four or five.**
- Keep an eye on the sky and, if you have one, the barometer. A dropping pressure reading and towering cloud mean rain is coming.
- **Stay off towers and other high points in the afternoon.** Convective storms over a plateau can mean lightning, even if the description only mentions rain. In autumn the storms become violent enough to bring down walls. Don't shelter against old, leaning walls then.

**Dusk — clearing.** The rain passes east, the sky clears, and everything drips. You might hear the pika's single whistle.

**Night — deep quiet.** You'll hear stone ticking as it cools and water finding its joints. It will get cold fast under clear skies. Get into dry sleep clothes and stay warm.

## Part Seven: Navigation — Getting Lost Is the Main Hazard

The city covers roughly the area of a small sea. It has no signs, no inscriptions, and no maps you can buy. It is dense, repetitive, and often foggy, and it echoes. Whole districts are empty. It is easy to imagine a visitor wandering for days in a maze of identical grey domes.

**Take this seriously.** Before you go, find out whether you can get a local guide, or at least instructions from someone who knows the city. Arriving through the drier, thinner edges (the lee side in the east, or the northern peat meadows where the city trails off) is likely safer than walking straight into the dense western quarters from the escarpment.

**Learn the city's own clues.** The inhabitants navigate without signs, and you can learn some of what they use:
- **Wall orientation.** The main walls run crosswise to the fog's drift, like teeth of a comb. Lanes running along the walls go roughly across the wind. Travel with the wind (east-west) means crossing walls, which means seeking gaps, courtyards, and tunnels. Your compass plus this rule gives you a grid.
- **Lichen and moisture.** Black, slick stone with mossy joints means you're toward the windward west. Pale, lichen-crusted walls mean you're toward the lee east. Even within one lane, windward faces are wetter and mossier.
- **Water sound.** Channels get louder toward cisterns and courtyards, which are where people are likely to live.
- **Sun and fog.** At noon, the sun gives you direction. In the morning, the fog comes from the west.
- **Escarpment.** The western edge is a cliff. **In fog, the rim is a genuine fall hazard.** If the stone is getting wetter and blacker and the fog thicker, you're heading toward the drop.

**Don't mark the walls.** The city has no inscriptions, and this is clearly deliberate or at least deeply habitual. No chalk arrows, no scratched lines, no little cairns (stacking stones in a city of stacked stones would be absurd at best and disrespectful at worst). Instead, keep a written route log: turns, compass bearings, distinctive features (a juniper growing from a wall, a collapsed dome, a courtyard with cushion plants, a slipped capstone letting in light). Record it as you go, not afterwards.

**If you're lost:**
1. **Stop.** Especially in fog. Wandering in a cloud inside a labyrinth only makes it worse.
2. **Wait for noon.** The fog lifts every day. It is the most reliable rescue you have.
3. **Get height.** Find a tower or a sound vantage point, and look for the escarpment, the eastern dry districts, or the northern meadows.
4. **Follow the water.** Channels lead to gutters, gutters to cisterns, cisterns eventually overflow into the old outwash channels that drain the plateau. Water is ultimately a path out, though it may be a long one.
5. **Look for signs of life:** cisterns in use, freshly relaid stones, smoke smell.

**On whistles and shouting.** Voices double and die against the stone, so sound is unreliable for finding people or being found. A whistle is still worth carrying for emergencies. Know that it may be hard to locate, may be confused with the pika's whistle, and would be startling in a place so quiet. Save it for real emergencies.

## Part Eight: The Physical Hazards

**Slipping.** Probably your most likely injury. Dished steps, cobbles, slate, wet moss, black lichen films, and dawn rime. Move slowly, keep your weight over your feet, and use your hands on the walls, gently.

**Falling stone.** The city is in balance, not at rest. Capstones drop. Courses slump. It happens most in the spring thaw, after autumn storms, and in winter under the weight of ice.
- **Don't climb on domes or walls.** A roof that looks like solid ground may be a corbelled vault waiting for an excuse.
- **Don't lean heavily on walls,** particularly the leaning old ones in the western districts. A light touch for balance is fine; don't put your weight into them.
- **Don't pull on protruding stones** as handholds.
- If you hear a grinding or clicking in a wall, move away from it.

**Beehive cells: a special caution.** They're tempting shelter, dim and domed, out of the rain. But:
- Look at the capstone and the upper courses. If anything looks slipped or cracked, sleep elsewhere.
- **Never run a stove or burn anything inside a cell with poor ventilation.** Corbelled domes with small openings are a carbon monoxide trap. The smell of old smoke shows people have made fires in them, and they may know how to vent them. You probably don't.
- **Some abandoned cells are pika larders,** full of harvested cushion plants. Don't sleep in those, and don't disturb the stores. They're an animal's winter survival.
- Ask whether a cell is lived in or kept by someone before entering. An empty-looking district may still be "maintained by habit."

**Weather exposure.** This covers hypothermia (wet plus cold plus wind), sunburn and snow-blindness at noon (especially in winter on ice), and lightning in the afternoon storms.

**Altitude.** Watch for headache, nausea, confusion, and severe breathlessness. If they get worse, descend.

**Claustrophobia and disorientation.** Some visitors may find the narrow, roofed lanes and the fog-walls genuinely oppressive. If you're prone to claustrophobia, stay in districts with more open courtyards, and spend your fog hours in places you already know.

## Part Nine: Choosing Your Season

**Summer:** The wettest and foggiest season, with the cushion plants in flower for two weeks and the cisterns full. Temperatures are least severe. This is probably the best season for a first visit, as long as you're ready to be damp.

**Autumn:** The finches pass through for a week, which is a lovely thing to witness. But the afternoon storms grow violent enough to bring walls down. This is for experienced visitors, who stay away from old, leaning walls in the afternoon.

**Winter:** Spectacular and serious. Fog freezes on contact. The windward districts are glazed in inches of rime, and ice feathers an arm's length long grow from every edge into the wind. You would need full winter mountaineering gear: insulated boots, crampons or microspikes, a helmet, a four-season sleeping system, and experience. The ice breaks walls, and falling ice is a real hazard. Late winter's thaw drips for days and adds to the risk of collapse. Only for the well-prepared.

**Spring:** The season of rebuilding. Fallen walls are gathered and relaid, so there's more activity and more people to see. It is also the season just after the thaw collapses, so structural hazards are high. If you want to see the inhabitants doing their work, and perhaps be invited to help, this is the time.

## Part Ten: The People and How to Behave

They're real, even if you don't see anyone the first day. Expect not to, and don't take it as a sign that something is wrong.

**Be quiet.** They speak softly because the lanes turn voices into strange doublings. Talk in low voices, and don't shout to each other through the lanes. Silence may be the most natural state here; become comfortable with it.

**Don't mark anything.** No graffiti, no carved initials, no chalk, no cairns, no flags. This is a place with no inscriptions at all.

**Don't take stones.** Not even one pebble as a souvenir. The city is built entirely from stones that were already there, and it rebuilds from its own collapses. Every stone is part of a cycle that has lasted centuries. Taking one, however small, removes it from that cycle.

**If you dislodge a stone, reseat it.** Watch how the inhabitants do it: the flat of the palm, tapping it into its seat. A good stone is one that sits. Try to put it back where it wants to sit, and if you can't, find someone who can, or leave it gently where it fell rather than forcing it.

**Leave the living things alone.** Don't pick the cushion plants, which grow extremely slowly. Don't peel lichen or crush moss cushions; they are part of the water system. Don't touch the snails, and certainly don't eat them. Don't harass or feed the pikas. Don't damage junipers.

**Enter nothing without an invitation.** Empty districts and lived-in ones may be hard to tell apart. Treat courtyards and cells as possibly someone's home.

**Offer help, don't impose it.** Spring rebuilding is work they have done for a very long time and are unlikely to need a stranger's guidance on. But an offer to carry stones, made quietly and without expectation, might be welcome. If you're allowed to help, watch closely and learn. How a stone is chosen for a place is presumably the deepest skill this culture has.

**Gifts.** Food from below might be valued. Useful, durable things might be too, but don't assume they want anything. A culture this quiet and this unmarked may not want much at all. Ask, or watch what they value, before you give.

## Part Eleven: Preparing Your Body and Mind

**Physically:**
- Hike on uneven ground with a loaded pack. Stepping, scrambling, stooping.
- Work on ankle and balance strength. Much of your time will be spent on slippery, rounded surfaces.
- If you can, practice moving through tight spaces: turning sideways, crouching, moving slowly in low light.
- Acclimatize to altitude if possible.

**Mentally:**
- **Slow down before you arrive.** This place runs on geological and meteorological time. Travelers with fixed schedules and long checklists will fight it and be frustrated. Build rest days and buffer days into your trip.
- **Expect sensory narrowing.** Mornings in the fog mean a world of a few walls' length, almost no sound, and grey in every direction. Some people find this deeply peaceful; others find it frightening. Know yourself.
- **Expect vastness.** At noon, from a tower, the grey knuckles of the roofline go to the horizon. The scale may be dizzying, humbling, even lonely.
- **Expect solitude.** Days without seeing a person, in a place clearly made by people. It has an eerie quality, like walking through a living ruin.

---

## Part Twelve: A Suggested First Visit

A reasonable shape for a first trip of a week or two:

1. **Arrive from the east or north,** where the city is thinner and drier. Spend a day or two on the edge acclimatizing, learning to read wall-grain and lichen, and practicing your route log.
2. **Move west gradually,** in the noon windows, settling each afternoon before the rain.
3. **Spend at least one morning simply standing inside the cloud** in a western lane, listening to the water running down the walls. Don't go anywhere. That's what the place is.
4. **Find a tower at noon** at least once, and watch the city steam and the cumulus begin to line up over the walls.
5. **Watch the afternoon rain from shelter** as the whole city runs at once with that soft, diffuse hiss.
6. **Spend a clear night** listening for the pika and for the cooling stone.
7. **Leave by the water.** If you can follow the drainage out, from wall to channel to gutter to cistern to the old outwash courses, you'll have traced the city's logic from start to finish.

---

## A Final Note

The Combing Stones won't threaten you. They'll simply be indifferent to you, like a mountain or a tide. The city doesn't need visitors, and the people who keep it don't need to be seen. The best visit is one where you move through carefully, drink lightly, take nothing, mark nothing, and leave the stones exactly where they want to sit.

Come prepared to be cold, wet, slow, and quiet. If you can manage those four things, you'll likely remember the sound of the weeping walls for the rest of your life.
