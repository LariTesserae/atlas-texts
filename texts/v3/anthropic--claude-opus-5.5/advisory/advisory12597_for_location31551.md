# Preparing to Visit the Holding Room

The Holding Room is not dangerous the way a cliff or a storm is dangerous. It won't hurt you if you respect it. The difficulty runs the other way: in a place where nothing has changed in a very long time, **you are the only variable**. Every preparation below comes down to one goal: arrive, add one stone, and leave without being the first thing that ever went wrong there.

---

## 1. Finding It

**Don't expect to see it.** Nothing on the plain stands higher than your knee, so landmarks are useless and the entrance is only a fold in the ground. If your world has coordinates or a guide, get them as precisely as you can. Even so, plan to spend time on foot searching.

**Search with your feet, not your eyes.** The hum arrives through the soles of your feet before it reaches your ears. This changes your footwear choice (see below). When you think you're close, stop, stand still, and pay attention to your feet and chest. Walk in the direction the pressure strengthens. It helps to take off your shoes briefly, or to lie flat with an ear or palm to the limestone.

**Go early in the day.** The room is bright only while the sky is bright. At night it holds a faint, directionless glow, which is enough to feel its presence and not enough to walk the path safely. Aim to arrive with several hours of daylight left.

**Mark the entrance once you find it.** Put a small flag or cloth on the plain *beside* the crease, not in it. From even a short distance, the crease "looks like nothing." Coming out dazed and deafened, you'll be glad of an obvious sign pointing back to where you left your gear or companion. Take the marker with you when you leave.

---

## 2. Surviving the Plain

The room is gentle. The plain is not.

- **Glare:** Gypsum and pale limestone reflect sunlight like snow. Bring good sunglasses, a brimmed hat, and sunscreen. Snow blindness is a real risk on white ground.
- **Temperature:** The plain "may bake or freeze." Check the season. You'll pass between the plain's extremes and the room's steady cool, so dress in layers.
- **Water:** There is none anywhere. Carry everything you'll drink for the round trip, plus a margin. Drink heavily on the plain *before* you go down, for reasons explained below.
- **Wind:** The plain is wind-scoured. Grit gets into eyes and gear, so wrap sensitive equipment.

Leave most of your kit on the plain, at the marker, with a companion if you have one.

---

## 3. What to Wear

You'll be walking a foot-wide path of stepping slabs, turned sideways, arms held in, between ancient stacks that touch the path on both sides. Your clothing is either a hazard or it isn't.

- **Close-fitting clothing, no loose ends.** No scarves, dangling straps, drawstrings, or open jackets. Anything that swings can catch a cairn. Tuck in or tie down everything.
- **No backpack.** A pack sticks out behind you, and you won't feel it brush a stack over the noise. Carry what you need in close pockets, or better, carry almost nothing.
- **Thin, flexible, grippy shoes.** Heavy boots are clumsy on a foot-wide path, and you'll want to feel the slab through the sole. Think climbing approach shoes, soft-soled moccasins, or clean socks over thin soles. Gypsum can be slick where polished.
- **A warm mid-layer.** The room is a permanent cool spring morning, probably somewhere around 8–14°C. If you arrive sweating from the plain, you'll chill quickly as the dry draft pulls the heat off you.
- **Clean everything first.** This matters more than it sounds. Brush your clothes, pick seeds out of your socks and laces, and wash your shoes. The room has no animals, no insects, and no seedlings. A single burr or beetle carried in would be the first of its kind. There are no living things there by design or by long equilibrium, so don't be the one who changes that.

---

## 4. The Sound

This is the part people underestimate.

**It's loud enough to feel in your chest.** Speech is useless and shouts vanish. Plan on total verbal isolation from the moment you step into the crease.

**Bring hearing protection and decide how to use it.** Foam earplugs, earmuffs, or both. Be aware, though:
- Much of the sound is low, possibly below hearing. Earplugs cut the whistles and moans but won't stop what comes through your bones, chest, and feet.
- Many visitors say the experience is the sound: the moment it stops being noise and becomes "a condition." Full protection may keep you from that.

A sensible approach is to go in with plugs, and once you're settled and stationary, consider removing them for a few measured minutes. Put them back before you walk out.

**Know the physical effects of sustained low-frequency sound:** unease, a sense of presence, mild nausea, pressure in the sinuses or ears, a feeling of dread that has no source, and blurred vision from the eyes themselves vibrating slightly. None of these mean something is wrong with you. They're what a body does in that kind of chord. Knowing this beforehand keeps you from panicking on the path.

**People who should think carefully before going:** anyone with inner-ear or balance disorders, a history of panic attacks or strong claustrophobia, migraines triggered by pressure, recent ear surgery, or heart conditions sensitive to vibration. The space is small, crowded, loud, and you can't call for help.

**Afterward:** expect muffled hearing, possible ringing, and a hum "in your bones for hours." Don't drive or do anything requiring fine balance straight after. Sit on the plain until the world stops feeling too quiet.

---

## 5. The Light

The shadowless light is the most practically dangerous feature of the room, because it quietly disables your depth perception.

With no shadows, your brain loses one of its main cues for distance and shape. The far wall seems close and the near wall seems far. On a foot-wide path lined with irreplaceable stacks, that's a serious problem.

**How to prepare:**
- **Practice balance beforehand.** Walk a narrow beam or a line of bricks sideways, slowly, eyes half-closed, placing each foot deliberately. Train your body to trust contact over sight.
- **Move by touch and count.** Feel each slab fully under your foot before moving weight onto it. Don't step on the basis of what you think you see.
- **Keep your gaze low and near.** Look at the next slab, not the room. Look at the room when you're standing still.
- **Consider sunglasses inside.** The room is "far brighter than a hole in the ground has any right to be." Light tinting can reduce the flattening, though it can also make things stranger.
- **Think before using a flashlight.** A small handheld light would restore shadows and could help you judge a tricky step. It would also be the first directional light the room has held in ages. Use it only if you need it for safety, and keep it pointed at your feet.

If you feel vertigo, **stop where you are**. Crouch low on your slab if you have to, with your arms tucked in. Close your eyes. Wait. Nothing in the room will change while you wait.

---

## 6. The Dryness

"The whole room is a single dry breath." There has never been water there.

- **Hydrate on the plain, not in the room.** Drink well before descending and plan to drink again after. Don't bring an open bottle down the path.
- **Protect your lips and eyes.** Apply lip balm before going down. Bring eye drops if your eyes dry out easily, and use them before entering.
- **Don't spill, sweat, or spit onto the stone.** Gypsum is slightly water-soluble, and a drop of water on a waxy polished surface centuries in the making could leave a mark. A cough or sneeze onto a lichen patch older than the stack it sits on is worth avoiding. If you need to cough, turn toward your elbow.
- **Don't eat inside.** Crumbs would be the first food the room has ever held.

---

## 7. Moving Through the Room

- **Walk slowly. Slower than that.** There's no reason to hurry, and hurry is how accidents happen.
- **Touch only what you must.** Many people want to put a palm on a stone and feel it vibrate. If you do, choose a wall stone at the path's edge, press lightly and briefly, and never touch lichen or cushion plants. Skin oil and pressure can damage lichen that grows millimeters per century.
- **Don't lean on the stacks.** They look fused and stable, and they are. They're finished. But no stack has ever fallen, and you don't want to test whether yours would be the first.
- **Keep your hands out of the wall apertures.** Blocking a hag stone's hole even briefly might alter the chord, the one sound that has held steady forever. It's probably harmless, but in this room "probably harmless" is not a good enough standard.
- **Leave the grass alone.** The straw-coloured blades in the path seams are stiff, dry, and unchanging. Step around them if you can.

---

## 8. The Stone

This is the reason people come. Give it real thought, because what you bring will stay.

**Choosing your stone:**
- **Small.** It needs to fit a ledge, a gap, or the top of a cairn without much searching.
- **Stable in shape.** Choose something with a flat face or a rounded belly that will sit. You don't want to fiddle for balance and you can't force a fit. A stone that rolls is a stone that might knock another.
- **Plain and unmarked.** No paint, no carving, no inscription, no glue. The builders never cut or dressed a stone. Everything there was found and chosen.
- **Probably not a holed stone.** Hag stones in the walls are what make the sound. A holed stone set in the wall's line of airflow could, in theory, add a note to a chord that hasn't changed in unknown time. Leave the voice as it is.
- **Local or carried: your choice.** Gypsum or quartz from the plain would match what's there. A stone carried from another place, or another world, follows the example of the grey flint, which "must have been carried from somewhere else." Some travelers bring a pebble from home, a small piece of their own world left to be absorbed. That seems in keeping with the place, as long as the stone is clean and holds no soil or seeds.

**Deciding where:** You may want to decide on your approach, not on arrival. You'll be sideways on a narrow path in shadowless light with a roar in your chest, and that's no place for long deliberation. A ledge within arm's reach of the path is easiest. The top of a cairn is more intimate. Reaching toward the dense old knot at the center means extending your arm over other stacks, so only do that if you're confident and steady.

**Placing it:** Hold it lightly. Lower it until it touches. Feel for the point where it stops wanting to move. Let go slowly. Then take your hand back the same way it came. Nothing else is required. No words, since none could be heard, and no gesture unless one feels right to you.

**Don't take anything.** Not a pebble, not a flake of selenite, not a pinch of the draft-dust. Everything in the room is either holding something or held by something.

---

## 9. Timekeeping

"Time seems to pool here instead of passing." Morning and afternoon look identical. The sound never shifts. The cold doesn't change. You will lose track of time.

- **Set a vibrating alarm** on a watch or device worn against your skin. You won't hear an audible alarm.
- **Decide your duration beforehand.** Twenty minutes to an hour is plenty for most visitors. Some who want the voice, or the silence that equals the roar, may stay longer. Choose deliberately, not by drifting.
- **If you have a companion, leave them on the plain.** Two people on the path are twice the risk to the stacks, and you can't communicate inside anyway. Agree on a return time. Agree that if you're late, they will *not* rush in after you, but will come carefully and wait at the entrance first. Settle hand signals in advance: *I'm fine*, *I need out*, *help me steady*.

---

## 10. Recording and Photography

Photographs will probably disappoint. With no shadows, images of the room will look flat, overexposed, and nearly abstract. Audio recordings will clip or register mostly as distorted rumble, since much of the chord is felt rather than heard. If you bring a camera, keep it small, strap it to your wrist, and don't use a flash. Many visitors decide to leave recording devices on the plain. What the room gives is not easily carried out.

---

## 11. Mental Preparation

Expect disorientation. The evenness of the light and the steadiness of the sound are uncanny in a way nature usually isn't. Everything in ordinary life flickers, drifts, and changes. Here nothing does. Some people find this peaceful. Others find it profoundly unsettling, as though the room has already finished and is simply waiting.

The absence of life can feel pointed. There are no insects and nothing in the corners. It can feel "deliberately cleared." Nothing indicates the room is hostile. It's simply complete. You're a visitor, not an intruder, as long as you behave like one.

You may hear the voice. You may reach the full silence. You may simply hear a very loud chord for a while and then leave. None of these outcomes is the wrong one.

Being briefly the only moving, warm, breathing thing in a place that has settled so completely is itself the experience. Let it be strange.

---

## 12. Leaving

- Walk out exactly as carefully as you walked in. Many accidents happen on the way out, when people feel they've "done it" and relax.
- On the plain, sit down. Take out your earplugs. The open air's silence will feel "the loudest thing you have ever heard." Let your hearing and balance come back.
- Drink water. Eat something. Put your warm layer on or off as the plain requires.
- Collect your entrance marker.
- Look back once. The crease will look like nothing. Somewhere below, a stone you carried is resting in a room that will absorb it the way the chord absorbs a shout.

---

## A Short Checklist

**Bring:** precise location information · ground marker · water (much) · sunglasses, hat, sunscreen · layered clothing, close-fitting · thin grippy shoes · earplugs/earmuffs · lip balm, eye drops · vibrating timer · one small, clean, stable, unmarked stone · (optional) small flashlight for emergencies only

**Leave on the plain:** backpack, food, open water, loose gear, most electronics, your companion

**Clean off before entering:** seeds, burrs, soil, insects, anything alive

**Never:** lean on stacks, touch lichen, block wall holes, spill liquid, eat, take anything, hurry

Go in carrying one stone. Come out carrying only the hum.
