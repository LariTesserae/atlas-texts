# Preparing for the Warm Throat

This place is gentle, but it is not safe. Nothing in it wants to hurt you. It is simply a hot, wet, sealed room full of bats, reached through a hole barely wider than your body, and it will punish carelessness the way any such room would. Most of the preparation is about two things: keeping your body working in air that won't let you cool down, and keeping yourself from damaging something that took ten thousand years to settle into its shape.

---

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Your instinct from the description may be that this is a sanctuary, somewhere to sit, breathe, and be soothed. It is that. But translate it into physical facts:

- **The air is at or near skin temperature and close to 100% humidity.** Sweat will not evaporate. Your body's main cooling system stops working the moment you arrive. This is the single most important fact about the room.
- **It is a bat roost.** Hundreds or thousands of bats have been dropping guano into warm, damp organic litter for centuries. In our world that is a textbook environment for *Histoplasma* fungus, and for elevated ammonia and carbon dioxide.
- **The only exit is a shoulder-width, twice-bent, slick chute.** It is also the bats' only exit, and they use it for about an hour at dusk and again before dawn.
- **Sound doesn't carry.** If you get into trouble, nobody above you will hear it. Your companion across the room may barely hear it.
- **It is a closed system with endemic life.** Eyeless fish, glass shrimp, and a luminous film that exists nowhere else. You are the only real threat in the room.

Keep all of that in mind and the rest follows.

---

## Before You Leave: Medical Preparation

**Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** Get the full series before you go. You are unlikely to be bitten if you behave well, but you'll be spending hours beneath a ceiling that is "more fur than stone," and bats sometimes fall. Pre-exposure vaccination turns a possible bat contact from an emergency into a manageable follow-up.

**Know about histoplasmosis.** There's no vaccine. Your protections are a respirator and not disturbing the floor. Tell whatever doctor you see afterward where you were. The flu-like illness it causes shows up three to seventeen days later and is easily misdiagnosed.

**Tetanus booster**, if you're not current. You'll be handling roots, stone, and soil with scraped hands.

**Leptospirosis awareness.** There are rats, and there is warm standing water. Don't drink the pool water or let it into cuts, and don't eat with unwashed hands.

**Heat tolerance.** If you have any heart condition, take medications that affect thermoregulation (some antihistamines, beta-blockers, diuretics, stimulants), or have ever had heat illness, think hard about whether to go. Spend a week or two before the trip acclimatizing to heat: exercising in warm conditions, saunas, anything that trains your body.

**Claustrophobia test.** The throat bends twice and is barely wider than your shoulders, and it's slick. If you've never done a tight squeeze, find a safe one in a familiar cave or a crawlspace first. Panic in a bent chute is dangerous in its own right.

**Multiverse-specific caution.** The glowing film on the root-tips, the parasitic bell-flowers, and the local fungi are unknown organisms. Treat all of it as potentially allergenic or toxic, and treat yourself as a potential contaminant to it. Decontaminate your gear before arrival (more on that below), and consider a brief self-observation period after leaving before you travel on to another world.

## The Timing Problem: Plan Around the Bats

This is the most important logistical decision, and the description buries it: **the bats pour through the throat for nearly an hour each way.** At dusk that shoulder-wide chute becomes a river of bats flowing up. Before dawn it becomes a river flowing down.

You must **never be in the throat during either flight.** You'd block the only exit for hundreds of animals, cause a pile-up and panic, and likely be bitten and scratched in an enclosed space while climbing on slick stone. It would be a disaster for them and for you.

That leaves two workable plans.

### Option A: The Afternoon Visit (recommended for most people)
- Descend around **midday or early afternoon**, when the throat-light is strongest and the surface is hottest.
- Watch the light move across the wall of hands.
- Stay through the **late-afternoon rain** (about half an hour) and the heavy steaming afterward.
- **Leave well before dusk**, and I mean well before. Give yourself at least an hour of margin before the first bats stir. Bats sometimes begin leaving early, and you don't want to be halfway up when the first ones come.

You will miss the glow, the moths' orbits, the rats, and the exodus itself. You'll also stay only three or four hours in the heat, which your body will thank you for.

### Option B: The Overnight (for the experienced and heat-hardened)
- Descend early afternoon.
- Rain, then dusk. **Settle yourself on the sitting stone before the bats leave**, stay still, lights off, and let them go. You'll feel the pressure change and the warm stir of air, and hear the papery shiver.
- Night: dark-adapt fully and see the glow.
- Stay put through the pre-dawn return. The throat is closed to you again.
- Dawn: the faint "sweating."
- Climb out mid-morning, once the bats are settled and there's light in the throat.

This means **eighteen or more hours** in air that won't let you cool. It is a serious physical undertaking, not a relaxing night. Do it only if you've done Option A first and know how your body responds.

## Getting In and Out

**Do not use the fig roots as your anchor.** They look like rope let down a well, and they'll probably hold, but a harness load or a fall-arrest jerk on living roots can tear bark and injure the tree that the whole room depends on. Anchor to rock at the surface: a bolt-free sling around a solid limestone block, or a stake-and-deadman if there's no suitable boulder. Keep your rope from abrading the roots where it enters the gap; pad it with a rope protector.

**Rope and harness.** Even if the throat is climbable by hand, a hand line or a proper rope with an ascender makes the climb out much safer. That climb happens when you're overheated, dehydrated, soaked, and tired. Most cave accidents happen on the way out, not the way in.

**Helmet.** Yes. The throat is uneven, and the ceiling is full of hanging roots you'll want to avoid hitting anyway.

**Gloves.** Thin, grippy, fitted work gloves. The walls are slick with warm condensation. Gloves protect your grip, protect your hands from scraping, and protect the roots and stone from your skin oils.

**Pack small.** You cannot wear a normal backpack through a shoulder-width, twice-bent passage. Use a narrow cave bag or duffel on a tether: lower it ahead of you going down, and haul it after you or clip it below your feet coming up. Everything you bring has to fit in it.

**Topside partner.** Strongly recommended. They watch the rope and anchor, hold your emergency information, and know your turnaround time. Since sound won't carry up the throat, agree on **rope-tug signals**: one tug for "okay," two for "coming up," three for "help / pull." Radios will probably not work through the rock. If your partner comes down with you instead, agree on hand-touch signals for inside the room, because a normal voice won't reach across twenty paces.

**A hard turnaround time.** Set it before you go down and keep it no matter how beautiful things are.

## Surviving the Heat

This is the part people underestimate. A "warm, gentle, breathing" room at body temperature and saturated humidity is, physiologically, a slow sauna you can't step out of.

- **Water: far more than you think.** At least a liter per hour of your stay, plus extra. For an overnight, that's a heavy bag. Plan for it.
- **Electrolytes.** Plain water isn't enough over many hours of sweating. Bring salts, tablets, or mix.
- **Know the warning signs** of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, dizziness, cramps, heavy fatigue, a racing heart, the sense that your thinking is getting "soft." In a place designed to lull you, these can feel like relaxation. If you notice them, **leave while you can still climb.** Heat stroke, meaning confusion, stopped sweating, or collapse, at the bottom of that throat would very likely be fatal, because no one could get you out.
- **Stay still.** The room's own rule applies to you too: nothing moves fast. Every movement generates heat you can't shed.
- **Do not get in the pool.** It's almost too hot for a hand. Full immersion would raise your core temperature quickly. It also isn't yours.
- **Consider a cooling aid**, such as a thermos of ice water, or a phase-change cooling vest if your world makes them. Ice held against your wrists and neck can buy a lot of comfort.

## Breathing

**Bring a respirator.** A P100 or N95-equivalent, well fitted. It protects against *Histoplasma* spores in disturbed litter and guano dust.

Be honest about the tradeoff: a respirator in saturated heat is miserable. It will soak through, and it will make you feel hotter and more short of breath. The damp air does keep dust down, which helps. A reasonable approach:
- **Wear it for the descent, and whenever you're walking or disturbing the floor.**
- Once you're settled and motionless on the sitting stone, far from the densest guano and not stirring anything, you can make an informed choice. Know that you're accepting some risk.
- Bring a spare, since the first will get wet.

**Air quality.** The rising warm air suggests the room ventilates, but decomposing litter and bats can raise CO₂ and ammonia. A small gas monitor for CO₂ and O₂ is worth carrying. If you get a headache, find yourself breathing fast, or smell sharp ammonia, go up.

---

## Clothing

- **Lightweight, loose, long-sleeved, full-length.** It will be soaked within minutes whatever you wear, so choose thin quick-dry synthetic, not cotton. Long sleeves and legs matter because you'll be brushing roots with crickets, centipedes, and spiders on them, and because guano on skin is worth avoiding.
- **Closed, grippy footwear** that you don't mind getting soaked and filthy. The floor is a deep mattress of litter; the flowstone is smooth and wet.
- **A change of clothes, sealed in a dry bag, left at the surface.** When you emerge soaked into dry scrub air, evaporation will hit you all at once. You may feel suddenly chilled, strangely, even in hot sun, and you'll start dehydrating fast.
- **Nothing scented.** No sunscreen, insect repellent, perfume, or scented soap, at least not on the day and definitely not reapplied at the surface before you go down. Every chemical on your skin will drip into that closed system with your sweat, and some of it will end up in a pool full of eyeless fish.

---

## Light

- **A headlamp with a red-light mode**, plus a dim white mode and a backup light. That's two independent light sources minimum, a caving rule for good reason.
- **Use red light around the bats**, and keep it pointed down. Never shine white light into the roost.
- **Plan for real darkness.** The glow is so faint that you need full dark adaptation: 30 to 45 minutes with no light at all, not even a glance at a screen. If you're doing the overnight, decide beforehand that you'll turn everything off and sit. One flash of white light resets your eyes and costs you the sky on strings.
- Bring lens wipes. The condensation will fog your headlamp lens.

## Moving Through the Room Without Harming It

The room has kept its shape for a long time. Your job is to leave it exactly as you found it.

**Walk one path, and walk it softly.** The floor is centuries of litter and a working beetle-soil factory. Every step compresses it. Take the shortest route from the base of the throat to the sitting stone and use only that route, both ways. Walking around to "see everything" is not worth it. The description tells you the stone already shows you everything.

**Don't touch the hanging root-tips.** The swollen, wet, searching ones are where the glow lives. Brushing them could strip the luminous film, bruise the growing tip, and divert a root that has been choosing where to land for years. Duck. Move slowly. This is where the helmet helps you in a way you might not expect: you'll feel a root touching it before you've damaged it.

**Don't touch the handprints.** Not even to "see how your hand compares." Skin oils stop flowstone from growing and discolor it. The one with only four fingertips still showing is being slowly buried in its own time, and that is what it is. Look. Don't reach.

**Don't step on or sit on the old wall.** It's one ridge of cemented rock now, with roots and moss on it. It's an artifact and a garden at once.

**Don't touch the pots.** The whole one is being held together by the roots that cracked it. The balance is delicate.

**The sitting stone is yours.** It was made for sitting, and people sat there long before you. Sit on it. Consider a thin pad under you, not for comfort but to avoid soaking your body oils into a surface that has been worn smooth by other people's.

**The pool is not yours.** No hands, no feet, no rinsing, no dipping a water bottle. The fish drift toward anything that falls in. Anything means your sweat drips, your crumbs, your sunscreen. Sit where you won't drip into it.

**Decontaminate before you come.** If your boots, gloves, rope, or clothes have been in any other cave, in any world, clean and disinfect them thoroughly. In our world, white-nose fungus traveling on caver's gear has killed millions of bats. A room this isolated could be wiped out by something you carried in on a bootlace.

**Pack everything out.** That includes human waste. On an overnight stay you will need a sealed waste kit. Do not urinate in the room: not in the litter, not near the colonnade, and certainly not near the pool.

**Eat little and carefully.** Crumbs feed rats and alter the floor. Eat compact food over a bag, if you eat at all.

## Living With the Inhabitants

- **Bats:** Don't disturb the roost. Stay out of the upper reaches, make no sudden light, and be still during the exodus. If one falls or lands near you, don't touch it with bare hands. A downed bat is a rabies consideration.
- **Centipedes:** The blood-colored ones hunting crickets will give you a genuinely painful, venomous bite. Watch where you put your hands. That's another reason for gloves.
- **Snakes:** The thin brown ones in the colonnade eat geckos and bats. Assume they may be venomous. Don't reach into the colonnade.
- **Spiders:** They live in crevices. Don't put fingers where you can't see.
- **Rats:** They come out at night. Keep your food sealed and your bag closed.
- **Geckos, frogs, crickets, snails, moths:** Harmless. Just watch. Watch for a long time. The description is right that once you see one gecko you'll see twenty, and that's part of the pleasure.

## Things Worth Bringing for Their Own Sake

- **A bat detector** (an ultrasonic receiver that makes echolocation audible). This is the one gadget that transforms the visit. You'll be sitting in "the noisiest place for miles," and with a bat detector you can finally hear it: the roar and clicking of hundreds of voices mapping every root around you. Keep it low. Earbuds are better than a speaker.
- **A small notebook in a waterproof bag**, if you want to record anything. Cameras will fog, and electronics will struggle in saturated air. If you bring a camera, let it warm up in a sealed bag at the surface first so the lens doesn't fog instantly, and accept that most photos will fail. It may be better not to bring one.
- **A watch.** Not for the room, since the room is a slow clock and the light on the wall will tell you the time, but for your turnaround.

## What It Will Actually Feel Like

Expect the first few minutes to be disorienting. The heat hits as you come out of the throat, a wet wall against your face. It's dark and your eyes can't cope. It's silent, but the silence is wrong: thick, muffled, as though you've gone slightly deaf. You'll feel your own heartbeat and hear your own breath inside the respirator more loudly than anything else.

Then your eyes open to it slowly, the ceiling first, and you realize it's alive. It will take you a long time to take it all in, and you shouldn't try to hurry.

Expect your sense of time to slide. The room runs on light across stone, the pool's finger-depth rise, and the rain. There are no sharp events. People who visit places like this often report that hours pass like minutes. That's part of why the turnaround time and the heat warning signs matter: the room will not remind you to leave.

The rain, when it comes, is the heart of the visit. Be sitting still for it. Warm drops everywhere, the soft hush of it, the bell-flowers already open, the snails climbing toward it. Afterward the steam thickens and the light turns to visible curls of moving air. If you're going to leave before dusk, this is your moment, but give yourself a few minutes in the steam first.

Expect a strange emotional weight from the hands, especially the small ones, and the one that's sinking. People came here more than once, over a long time, and made something like a shrine. You're doing what they did: climbing down, sitting on their stone, looking at the room. It's hard not to feel you're keeping some kind of appointment.

And expect the climb out to be hard. You'll be heavier with soaked clothes, weaker from heat, and slower than you think. Rest before you start. Drink. Go slowly. The walls are still slick.

When you come out, the surface will be as the description promises: thin, harsh, loud, bright. Sit in shade. Change into your dry clothes. Drink more than you want to. Let your body remember how to cool itself.

---

## Afterward

- Keep drinking and replacing salts for the rest of the day.
- Wash all clothing and gear thoroughly, and disinfect anything that touched the floor or guano, before you go into any other cave, anywhere.
- **Watch your health for two to three weeks.** Fever, cough, chest pain, or unusual fatigue in that window should send you to a doctor with the words "bat cave, warm, guano," for histoplasmosis. Any bat scratch or contact you weren't sure about needs follow-up even with pre-exposure vaccination.
- Watch for any skin reaction where roots, film, or bell-flowers brushed you.

And yes, for a while afterward, when the afternoon cools, you'll find yourself waiting for rain. Nothing needs to be done about that. It's the part of the room you get to keep.
