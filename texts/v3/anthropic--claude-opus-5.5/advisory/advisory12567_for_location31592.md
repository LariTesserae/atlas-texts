# Preparing to Visit the Held Chord

The Held Chord is not dangerous the way a volcano or a war zone is dangerous. Nothing there wants to hurt you, and nothing there will change while you visit. Its danger is the danger of a place that is completely indifferent and completely constant. It is cold, wet, dark, and loud, and it has been so for so long that it has no margin for you. You have to bring your own margin. You also have to bring the discipline to leave it exactly as you found it, because almost anything you damage there cannot be undone.

What follows covers your body first, then your mind, then your conduct, then leaving.

---

## Part One: Before You Go

### Health screening

Be honest with yourself about these conditions, because the chamber will test all of them.

- **Heart conditions and implanted devices.** The chord is felt in the sternum before it is heard. Low-frequency vibration strong enough to set standing ripples on wet stone is strong enough to be felt in the chest cavity for hours. If you have a pacemaker, arrhythmia, or any cardiac condition, consult someone who understands infrasound exposure, or do not go.
- **Vestibular problems, migraine, panic disorder.** Sustained low-frequency sound is associated with nausea, pressure headaches, unease, and a sense of dread that has no object. People prone to vertigo or panic may find the chamber overwhelming. None of this means the place is hostile. It is simply what bodies do inside a sound this big.
- **Claustrophobia.** The entrance is a doorway-wide seam. The stair to the still room must be climbed sideways. The chamber is large, but the walls are crowded with dark openings, and the passage behind you is black.
- **Circulation problems, including Raynaud's.** The water is "cold enough to ache the fingertips," and you will be touching it constantly.
- **Hearing.** Get a baseline audiogram before you leave. You will want to know afterward whether anything changed.

### Training

- **Cold, wet endurance.** Nine degrees Celsius in saturated air is hypothermia weather. It won't come on fast. It will come on steadily and quietly, while you are distracted by wonder. Practice spending several hours in cold rain, in the right gear, while staying still. Much of the cold you'll meet comes from standing and looking, not from exertion.
- **Silent communication.** Learn a signal set with your companion before you go. See Part Three.
- **Dark adaptation.** Practice moving by very low light. Learn how long your eyes take to adjust, typically twenty to forty minutes for full adaptation, and how fast a single white light destroys that.
- **Slow movement.** Practice walking on wet, polished, rounded surfaces. The stairs are forgiving, since "the stone has spent a long time agreeing" with feet, but the rest of the city is not a trail.

### Company

Go with one other person, and no more than two or three. You will not be able to call for help, and a single fall on glazed flowstone can break an ankle a hundred wet steps below the surface. Someone should also stay at the surface or at the ravine, with a turnaround time. If you have not come out by then, they go for help. There is no signal down there. Nothing carries through the chord.

Keep the group small for the city's sake as well. The place holds almost no life and almost no change. A crowd would be an event, and the chamber has not had an event in a very long time.

## Part Two: The Body

### Cold and water: dress for being soaked, not for staying dry

You will not stay dry. The air "is already fog." Your clothes will be wet within the first hundred steps, from the walls, the drip, and the suspended mist, and they will never dry while you are inside. Dress for insulation that works when saturated.

- **Base layer:** Synthetic or merino wool, never cotton. Cotton that is wet at nine degrees is a slow way to die.
- **Mid layer:** Fleece or a wool sweater. Synthetic insulation keeps some warmth when wet. Down loses almost all of it.
- **Outer layer:** A waterproof caving suit or a heavy PVC oversuit. Serious cavers in wet systems sometimes wear a thin wetsuit or a neoprene layer under the oversuit. For a long stay, consider it. Expect condensation inside whatever you wear. The goal is to slow heat loss, not to stay dry.
- **Hands:** Thin neoprene or waterproof gloves, with a spare pair in a dry bag. Bare fingers will stop working properly within the hour.
- **Head:** A caving helmet, both to protect against low niches and ceilings and to carry lights. Wear a wool or synthetic cap beneath it. You lose a lot of heat through your head, and water drips on it constantly.
- **Feet:** Rubber boots or caving wellies with soft, grippy soles, plus wool socks and a spare pair. Avoid hard lug soles. They grip poorly on flowstone and can scratch it.

**Warmth management:**
- Bring a thermos of hot, sweet drink. Drink it in the still room or before the long climb out.
- Bring more high-calorie food than you think you need. Your body burns fuel simply by staying warm.
- Bring an emergency bivvy bag or space blanket and chemical heat packs. If someone is injured, you may have to wait hours for help, and waiting is where cold kills.
- **Learn the "umbles."** Stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. These are the signs of creeping hypothermia. You won't be able to hear mumbling in the chord, so watch for the others: clumsiness, slowness, and apathy. Pay particular attention to apathy, because the chamber naturally induces a calm, dissolved state, and that can mask the warning.

**Set a hard time limit.** For a first visit, two to three hours in the chamber is probably wise. The place will make you want to stay. The cold will not care.

### Sound: protecting yourself from a chord you cannot fully block

This is the part most visitors underestimate. "Loud enough to take conversation away" by the fortieth step means you are already in the range where hearing damage becomes a question of hours. Deeper down it is louder.

- **Double hearing protection.** Wear foam earplugs pushed in properly, with over-ear defenders on top. Use helmet-compatible muffs if you can find them. They do not keep out cold or damp well, so bring spares or dry them between visits.
- **Know what protection cannot do.** Earplugs block the upper tones: the "thin shimmer" from the whistling slots, the ringing of the basins. The deepest note, from the great bowl "so large three people could lie in it," is felt through the bones and body cavity. You will not block it. You will live inside it.
- **Bring a noise dosimeter, or a simple sound level meter,** if your world has one. It will tell you your dose. Without it, count time.
- **Take breaks.** Walk partway back up the passage, where the sound thins. Or use the still room, which is the one true rest.
- **After exposure**, expect your ears to ring. If they still ring a day or two later, or sounds seem muffled, get checked.

**What the sound will do to your body:**
- Pressure in your chest, "like a hand laid flat."
- Possibly a subtle vibration in your vision, especially if you stare at one spot. Some low frequencies can resonate faintly in the eyeball.
- A sense of fullness in your sinuses and head.
- Nausea for some people.
- Deep physical tiredness, even when you haven't done much. The body spends effort just being shaken all the time.

If nausea, headache, or unease becomes strong, leave. It is not a test of will. It is a place where some bodies simply do not settle.

**On taking protection off:** You may be tempted to remove your hearing protection to hear the chord "properly." If you do, do it briefly, a minute or two, near the entrance of the chamber rather than beneath the falling sheet. Then put it back. The chord will still be there. It has been there all along, and it will be there when you are gone.

### Light: bringing your own without killing theirs

The chamber has its own light, the blue-green glow of the living film. It is faint, colorless to your eyes, and constant. You will be tempted to flood it with headlamp light. Resist.

- **Primary light:** A good caving headlamp with both white and red modes, and ideally a dimmable output.
- **Backup lights:** At least two more independent sources. Cavers' rule: three sources of light per person. The passage out is "pitch dark." If your lamp dies, the glow of the chamber will not guide you up a hundred steps of black stone.
- **Spare batteries,** kept warm, inside your clothes. Cold drains them, and damp corrodes them. Keep lights and batteries in sealed bags.
- **Red light for moving within the city.** Red preserves your dark adaptation. Use white light only on the stairs and for safety checks.

**Practice this sequence:**
1. Descend with white light. Watch your footing on the flowstone.
2. At the chamber's edge, stop. Switch to red, then off. Stand still for twenty to thirty minutes while your eyes open.
3. The city will "seem almost lit." Move slowly by the glow, using red light for tricky steps.
4. When you turn to leave, turn your white light back on *before* you enter the passage. Your adapted eyes will tell you the chamber is bright. The passage will prove it is not.

**Why restraint matters:** The glowing film has lived in constant, unbroken darkness forever. You don't know how it responds to bright light. It may respond not at all, or it may be harmed. The light-writing in the carved channels exists only as long as that film thrives. Do not shine bright lights into the channels to "read" them. Do not use camera flash anywhere.

**Glasses and lenses:** Glasses will fog constantly in saturated air. Anti-fog treatments help, but not much. Consider contact lenses, with a backup pair of glasses in a hard case.

### Footing and movement

- **The stairs** are gentle but glazed. Walk flat-footed, weight centered, one hand on the wall when possible. The wall is wet and cold. Wear your gloves.
- **The bridges.** Do not walk the bridges. They are "thin as ribs," uncut rock left behind as the stone around them was carved away, spanning the chamber "from one side to the other." You do not know their strength, and a fall from one would be fatal. The only bridge you must use is the thickest, the one holding the still room, and even there, move slowly and stay centered.
- **The upper tiers.** The lips that shape the chord are near the vault, jutting into the falling sheet. Stay away from them. Your body cannot improve anything there. It can only fall, or strike stone that has taken ages to reach its shape.
- **The floor channels.** Do not step in them. That is where the salamanders are, where the glow is richest, where the makers' water still runs in its "channeled and stepped and lipped" course.
- **The dwellings.** Cells and niches are small, and their inner walls are "a hand's width thick, sometimes less." Do not climb into them, lean on them, or brace against them. Look in from the doorway.
- **The still room stair.** It is so narrow you must turn sideways. Remove your pack before climbing. Go slowly, and have your companion wait at the bottom.

## Part Three: Communication

You will not talk down there. Plan for that.

**Before you go, agree on:**

- **Hand signals,** visible by red light or by the glow:
  - Okay / Are you okay? (Circle of thumb and forefinger, as in diving.)
  - Problem / Not okay. (Flat hand, rocking.)
  - Cold. (Arms crossed, hands rubbing shoulders.)
  - Stop / Wait.
  - Look here. (Point, then touch your own eye.)
  - Time to go. (Tap wrist, then point up the passage.)
  - I'm going to the still room.
  - Emergency / Leave now. (Something unambiguous and big, such as both arms crossed overhead or a repeated fist to chest.)

- **Touch signals,** for when light is too dim to see hands:
  - One squeeze of the arm: okay.
  - Two squeezes: follow me.
  - Three squeezes: stop.
  - Repeated tapping: urgent, leave.

- **Light signals:** Headlamp circles for "okay," side-to-side waves for "come here," rapid flashing for emergency. Use these sparingly, because every flash disturbs your own dark adaptation and the chamber's.

- **A slate or waterproof notebook** with a grease pencil, for anything more complex. Paper will turn to pulp. Waterproof paper or a plastic slate is needed.

**Buddy checks:** Every fifteen to twenty minutes, find your companion and check in. Look at their face and hands. The chord will make each of you vanish into your own experience. That is part of its gift, and part of its danger.

## Part Four: The Mind

This is where the Held Chord will surprise most visitors. Prepare for it.

### The loss of the inner voice

The description says the chord "has taken thought's inner voice as well, or seems to." Take this seriously. For many people, the inner monologue is so constant that they don't notice it until it's gone. When it goes, you may feel:

- Peace. A vast, unfamiliar quiet in the mind, even as the ears are full.
- Disorientation. A strange loss of your usual self-narration, as if you were watching yourself from slightly outside.
- Unease. For some, the absence of the inner voice is frightening, like losing a handrail.
- A sense of dissolving, of becoming part of the sound.

None of this is harmful in itself. But it has practical consequences:

- **Your judgment of time will collapse.** No day, no night, no change, no voice counting minutes. Set an alarm you can *feel*, like a vibrating watch strapped against your skin. You will not hear anything else.
- **Decision-making will slow.** Make your important decisions before you enter: how long you'll stay, where you'll go, where you won't. Write them on your slate. Inside, simply follow the plan.
- **You may not notice creeping cold, hunger, or fatigue.** This is why the buddy checks exist.

### Constancy

Most places change as you watch them. Light moves, wind shifts, animals come and go. Here nothing changes. The standing ripples on the walls "never move." The light "does not flicker." The chord "does not change." The tools "lie where they were set down."

Some people find this deeply calming. Others find it oppressive, even eerie, as if time had stopped. Know that it will feel strange, and know that it is simply what the place is. It isn't frozen. It is finished, "the stillness of something that has finished becoming what it is."

### Absence

The city is crowded with the traces of people. Doorways shoulder to shoulder, walls worn through, ledges that once held everything, tool marks everywhere, the tools themselves laid down as if someone had just stepped away. There are hundreds of dwellings. There are no people and no bones.

It may affect you more than you expect, less as grief than as the particular feeling of an inhabited place with no one in it. Let yourself feel it. Don't try to solve it. No one knows where the makers went.

### Scale

It is small for a cave, the size of a church, yet it holds a city carved by subtraction, "generations taking the mountain away one flake at a time." Every scalloped surface is "ten thousand strikes." Standing in it, you may be overwhelmed by the sheer accumulated labor of it: stone against stone, in cold and dark and roaring sound, for generations. Allow time simply to look. The place was made to be lived in slowly, and it rewards slow attention.

## Part Five: Conduct: What You Must Not Do

The Held Chord is a place where "erosion is finished." The lips have worn to exactly the shape the water gives them and stopped wearing. The water and stone "came to an agreement." You are not part of that agreement. Almost any change you make will be permanent.

### Do not touch the lips or the ringing surfaces

This is the most important rule. Every lip was cut to a particular angle and depth, and then refined over ages by the water. Every basin, shell, and slot has its pitch set by its size and the thickness of its stone. Chip one, and you change a note forever. Add something to one, even a hand pressed against it or a stone set inside it, and the chord shifts. The chord is "the chord the last maker heard, and the first." Do not be the one who changes it.

### Do not disturb the tools

The flint blades, antler picks, pounding stones, and copper pins lie exactly where they were left. Many are fused to their ledges under calcite and verdigris. Picking them up will break them off, break them, or break the ledge. Moving them breaks the record of where they were set down. Look. Photograph if you can do so without flash. Leave them.

### Do not touch the walls more than you must

The flowstone glaze builds up by "a hair in a lifetime." The oils and bacteria on your skin can stain it and change how it grows. Touch the walls only for safety, and with gloves.

### Protect the living film

The glowing film is the base of the chamber's entire food web. The salamanders, shrimp, and presumably the crickets all depend on it, directly or indirectly. It also writes the makers' light-patterns in the carved channels.

- **Do not touch the water** in channels, basins, or the light-writing grooves.
- **Do not drink the water.** It may be pure deep aquifer water, but it carries an unknown microbial community. Treat it as a biological unknown, both for your safety and theirs.
- **Clean your gear before you arrive.** Scrub your boots, gloves, and suit, and disinfect them if possible. You can carry microbes, spores, and fungi from other caves and other worlds. In a place this sealed, a new organism could take over.
- **Clean your gear after you leave,** for the same reason in reverse. You don't know what the film is, or what it might do elsewhere. Across a multiverse, this is basic hygiene.

### Protect the animals

There are maybe a few dozen shrimp in the great bowl. The salamanders may live a century and barely move. These populations are tiny, slow-growing, and irreplaceable.

- Do not handle them.
- Do not shine lights directly on them for long. They are eyeless, but they may still sense light through their skin.
- Do not step in their water.
- Watch the salamander for an hour, if you like. That may be the finest thing you can do there.

### Leave nothing

- Pack out all waste, including human waste. Bring waste bags designed for this purpose. In a sealed, sterile-ish ecosystem, your waste is a massive nutrient pollution event.
- Do not leave food crumbs. Eat over a bag.
- Do not leave marks, cairns, arrows, or tape. If you need to mark your route (you probably won't, since there is one stair down and one stair up), use something you'll remove on the way out.
- Do not leave offerings. The impulse may be strong. Resist it. Anything you leave will rot in the damp, or it will stay forever as something that doesn't belong.

### Do not shout, sing, or make sound into the chamber

You couldn't be heard anyway, but some visitors try. Singing into resonant spaces is a human instinct. Here it is pointless and a little rude. The chamber is already singing.

### Photography and recording

- No flash. Use long exposures with a tripod set on bare floor, not on any ledge or carved surface.
- Protect your camera from saturated air with a waterproof housing or bag. Expect lens fogging.
- Audio recording will likely clip and distort. If you want to try, use a microphone that can handle very high sound pressure levels, with the gain set very low. Record briefly.
- Be aware that no recording will convey it. The chord is not only heard, it's felt as weather. You have to be there.

## Part Six: The Still Room

Plan your visit to the still room deliberately. For many visitors it will be the centre of the whole journey.

### Practicalities

- It is reached by a narrow stair, sideways, in the thickest bridge near the chamber's center. Take off your pack. Your companion should wait at the bottom. Agree on a time or a signal. Three knocks on the rock, perhaps, or a tug on a light line, since you *will* be able to hear inside.
- It holds one person. Go one at a time.
- The floor is a smooth dish, polished by generations of sitting. Sit gently. Do not stand in the dish if you can avoid it. Don't bring anything that could scratch it: no metal-soled boots, no hard buckles against the stone.
- It will still be cold. Bring your thermos. Here, and only here, you can pour and drink in silence.

### What to expect

The chord stops. You will have been inside it for an hour or more, perhaps long enough that you stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing weather. When it stops, the absence will hit you physically. Many people will gasp. Some may weep. Your body will suddenly relax in ways you didn't know it was clenched.

Then you will hear your blood, your breath, "the faint tick of a single drop somewhere." If you have tinnitus, it will announce itself. If you've been exposed for long, your ears will ring. That ringing is not the chamber. It is you.

Remove your hearing protection here, carefully. This is the only place in the city where your ears can rest completely.
