# Preparing for The Pans

## The Short Version

You are going to a hot, humid, blindingly bright plain of ancient rock where water pools but never drains, life crowds around that water, and the space between pools is empty and exposed. The main dangers are ordinary ones: **heat, sun, glare, disorientation, slippery rock, soft mud, storms, snakes, and bad water.** None of them is dramatic. Several can kill you anyway.

The place is also fragile in ways that aren't obvious. Anything you leave behind (soap, sunscreen, urine, a crushed moss cushion, a bootprint in a pan) stays there, because nothing here washes away. Go gently.

---

## 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

It helps to have an honest picture before you arrive.

- **It will feel flat, and it isn't.** The ground tilts in rises no taller than you across kilometers. Your inner ear will register slopes your eyes can't find. Expect mild unease, like standing on a very slow ship.
- **Distance is broken.** There are almost no vertical objects to measure against. Trees and termite mounds sit at unknowable intervals, and midday mirages stack false water on real water. Something that looks like a twenty-minute walk may be two hours away.
- **Life is concentrated, and emptiness is the default.** You will walk through long stretches of bare, ringing laterite where almost nothing moves. Then you'll reach a pan margin and walk into a crowd of birds, frogs, shrimp, lizards, and insects. The animals that hunt those crowds, snakes and monitors among them, gather there too.
- **You're arriving on a clock.** It rained three days ago. The land is at its most alive and will slowly dry for weeks. Each day the pans shrink, the sedge rings yellow, and the pools grow more crowded. A storm can reset everything in an afternoon.

The feeling of the place is a loud interruption in a very long silence. Plan to be quiet inside it.

---

## 2. Timing Your Visit

- **Early in the wet (where you are now):** Maximum water, maximum noise, the most insects, the softest ground, the most slippery rock. Water is easy to find, but mosquito- and midge-borne illness is at its peak.
- **Mid dry-down:** The pans have retreated and left salt crust and tidemarks. Walking is easier, the rosette plants may be flowering in the gnammas, and wildlife is more concentrated and easier to watch.
- **Late dry-down:** The last pools are crowded, warm, and increasingly foul. Drinkable water becomes scarce, heat is brutal, and the frogs go quiet as they bury themselves.

If you can choose, **one to three weeks after rain** is the sweet spot. If you arrive now, three days in, expect wet feet, a lot of biting insects, and a spectacle you won't forget.

**Plan your days around the land's rhythm, not your own:**

| Time | What's happening | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-dawn to sunrise | Frogs fading, mist on the pans, birds arriving | Wake, pack, watch the water. Coolest walking. |
| Sunrise to ~2 hours after | Mist burns off, birds at the water | Main travel window. Cover distance now. |
| Late morning to mid-afternoon | Heat, glare, mirages, lizards, cicadas | Stop. Rest in checked shade. Hydrate. Don't navigate. |
| Afternoon | Cloud buildup, possible storms | Watch the sky. Stay off high points if cells build. |
| Late afternoon to dusk | Low light reveals the terrain, wallabies and bats emerge | Second travel window. Best light for route-finding. |
| Night | Frog chorus, snakes hunting | Stay put. Headlamp for camp only. |

## 3. Heat, Sun, and Humidity

This is the thing most likely to hurt you.

The air is **hot and thick**. Water off the pans keeps humidity high, so sweat doesn't evaporate well and your body sheds heat poorly. The rock absorbs heat all day and radiates it back all night. The pan water is "warm as blood," so wading won't cool you.

**Prepare:**
- **Acclimatize before you go** if at all possible. Spend a week or two exercising in hot, humid conditions. Your body will learn to sweat earlier and lose less salt.
- **Water:** Budget **6–10 liters per person per day** if you're moving, more if you're working hard. Carry enough capacity to reach the next reliable water with a large margin, because in heat you don't want to depend on gnammas you haven't verified.
- **Electrolytes:** Bring plenty of oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets. Drinking lots of plain water in heavy sweating conditions can dangerously dilute your blood sodium.
- **Know the signs:** Heat exhaustion (heavy sweat, nausea, headache, dizziness, weakness) means stop now, get into shade, drink, and cool yourself. Heat stroke (confusion, stumbling, strange behavior, hot skin, sometimes no sweating) is a medical emergency. Cool the person aggressively by any means available, especially wet cloth and fanning. Travel with someone who knows to watch you, since heat confusion is hard to notice in yourself.

**Sun and glare:**
- The light is described as coming from above and below. **Every pan is a mirror.** Sunburn will come up under your hat brim, under your chin, and into your nostrils.
- **Polarized sunglasses with side protection are mandatory,** not optional. Hours of reflected glare off water and wet rock can cause photokeratitis, a sunburn of the cornea that feels like sand in your eyes and can leave you temporarily unable to see. Bring a spare pair.
- Wear a **wide-brimmed hat** with a chin strap for the storm winds, ideally with a neck flap.
- Use sunscreen, but read section 9 about where you put it.

## 4. Clothing and Footwear

**Clothing:**
- **Long sleeves and long trousers** in light-colored, lightweight, quick-drying fabric. They protect you from sun and insects better than sunscreen and repellent alone. Loose fits let air move.
- A **light buff or scarf** to wet and wear around your neck for evaporative cooling.
- A **headnet** for the midge columns and mosquitoes at dusk. Midges get through ordinary insect mesh, so look for fine no-see-um netting.
- A **lightweight rain shell** for storms. The rain will be warm, but wind-driven wet can still chill you at night when you're exhausted.
- **Light gloves** (thin leather or synthetic) for scrambling on rock and lifting anything. Overhangs and crevices are occupied.

**Footwear:** this is a real dilemma, because the ground has three moods.
1. **Hard laterite crust:** rough, abrasive, ringing. Wants sturdy soles.
2. **Wet granite and algae mats:** extremely slippery, especially the "slick and living" blue-black algae along seep lines. Wants soft, grippy rubber.
3. **Pan clay:** soft as custard. It will suck a boot off your foot and hold it.

**Recommendation:** sturdy, **well-draining hiking shoes** with soft sticky rubber soles (the kind used for rock scrambling), worn with **snake gaiters** over the lower leg. Your feet will be wet much of the time, so:
- Bring **multiple pairs of quick-dry socks**, and dry your feet fully every night.
- Carry **foot powder, blister care, and a small tube of barrier balm**. Wet, hot feet macerate and blister fast, and in this climate small wounds infect quickly.
- **Don't plan to walk through pans.** If you must cross soft ground, tighten your laces hard, move slowly, and test each step.

## 5. Moving Across the Land

### Navigation
This is a place where people get lost in plain sight.

- **Don't trust your compass blindly.** The ground is ironstone and laterite, which is iron-rich rock. It can deflect a magnetic needle, sometimes badly. Cross-check often, and hold the compass away from the ground and from any exposed ironstone.
- **Don't assume satellite navigation exists.** You're crossing universes. Bring a device if you like, but plan as if it won't work.
- **The termite mounds are your friends.** They're aligned **north–south**, with their broad faces east–west and edge-on to the midday sun. A line of termite spires is a compass the land built for you. Learn to read them before you arrive, and confirm the alignment on your first day against the sun's path.
- **Use the sun and stars.** Know how to find direction by the sun's position, by shadow-stick, and by the night sky. Before you go, find out which hemisphere-equivalent you're in, or be ready to work it out.
- **Navigate from high points in the evening.** From the top of a larger whaleback in low light, the plain "shows itself" as swells and hollows, each hollow holding sky. That's when you can see where the pans are and plan tomorrow's route. **Don't navigate at midday.** Mirages make real water and false water indistinguishable, and you can walk toward a pan that doesn't exist.
- **Mark your arrival point meticulously.** Note the shape of the nearest rock domes, the positions of distinctive trees and termite lines, and bearings to the far bluish "herd" of domes on the horizon. Sketch it. The pans will move and shrink, but the rock domes and ancient trees won't. They're your permanent landmarks, and your way home.
- **Estimate distance conservatively.** Pace-count on a known stretch early on, then trust the count over your eyes.

### Footing
- **Exfoliation sheets:** the thin curved plates around boulders "rock and give a hollow clack." Some are whole, some are rotted. Don't trust them on slopes. Step on the solid dome beneath, not on the loose plates. The clack also announces you to every animal nearby.
- **Seep lines:** the dark wet stripes down the whaleback flanks are the most slippery surfaces on the plain. Cross them low and flat, never on a descent if you can avoid it. A fall on smooth granite has nothing to catch it.
- **Salt crusts on drying pans** can look solid over soft clay, like crust over custard. Stay off them.
- **Sheet water:** a glaze only ankle-deep can still hide a gnamma or a soft patch. Probe with a trekking pole.

**Trekking poles** are worth their weight here: for balance on wet rock, for probing water and mud, and for tapping ahead as a gentle snake warning in the sedge.

## 6. Water: Finding It, Treating It, Respecting It

You'll be surrounded by water that is mostly not safe to drink as-is.

**Best sources, in order:**
1. **Rainwater you collect yourself.** When a storm comes, catch it on a tarp. This is the cleanest water on the plain.
2. **Seep lines,** caught where they drip off rock before pooling. Clearer, though still to be treated.
3. **Gnammas,** especially deep ones with clear water. Take from the upper layer without stirring the silt of grit and dead insects at the bottom. The water is tea-colored from plant tannins, which aren't harmful.
4. **Pans:** last resort. They're full of animals, the water is warm, and as they dry they concentrate salt, waste, and whatever else. Late dry-down pan water may be too salty to drink.

**Treatment:**
- **Filter AND disinfect.** Use a good filter for particles and protozoa, and add chemical treatment or boiling for viruses. Warm, animal-dense water is a microbial soup.
- Bring **pre-filters or cloth** to strain out insects and grit first, or your filter will clog within a day.
- Carry a **backup method.** If your filter cracks, your trip becomes dangerous.

**Do not submerge your head in warm pools.** In warm, still freshwater there's a small but real risk of brain-infecting amoebae entering through the nose. Wade if you must. Don't dunk, dive, or snort water.

**Gnammas are not bathtubs,** even the ones "wide enough to lie down in." They're closed systems. Anything you add stays until evaporation concentrates it. They're also drinking water for every wallaby, parrot, and frog in the area. See section 9.

## 7. Health Hazards: Things That Bite, Sting, and Infect

### Snakes
"Snakes follow the frogs." This is the line to take most seriously. Assume venomous snakes are present, especially around pan margins, sedge rings, and anywhere frogs gather at night.

- **Wear gaiters,** especially in sedge and near water.
- **Watch where you put your hands and feet.** Don't reach blindly into crevices, under rock sheets, or into overhangs.
- **At night, use a headlamp for every step** around camp. Snakes hunt frogs in the dark.
- **Give every snake space.** Most bites happen when people try to move, kill, or handle them. Stop, back away slowly, and go around widely.
- **Know first aid for elapid-type bites before you go,** and carry at least two wide elastic pressure-immobilization bandages. The usual approach: keep the victim calm and still, apply firm pressure bandaging over the bite and up the whole limb, splint it, and don't cut, suck, tourniquet, or wash the bite. Get evacuation. Take a real wilderness first-aid course that covers this, and don't rely on a paragraph.
- **Have an evacuation plan.** Out here, "get to help" may mean crossing back through the multiverse. Know how long that takes.

### Overhangs: "cool, damp, and occupied"
The shade will call to you at noon. **Check it first.** Overhangs may hold:
- Bats. Avoid disturbing roosts, and don't camp in or stir up guano, which can carry fungal spores that infect the lungs.
- Wallabies resting. They're harmless, but they'll bolt.
- Snakes, which also want cool shade.
- Wasps, hornets, spiders, scorpions, and centipedes in the cracks.
- Colonies of mud nests. Don't disturb them.

Approach slowly, look before entering, use your pole to check the back, and take the edge rather than the deep interior. It's often cool enough.

### Insects
Midges rise in "columns that hum audibly." Mosquitoes will breed explosively in every pool.
- **Before you go:** find out what insect-borne diseases exist in comparable environments, get any vaccinations or preventive medication available to you, and ask a travel-medicine doctor. Assume arboviruses such as encephalitis and arthritic fevers are possible.
- **Repellent** on skin and clothing, a **permethrin-treated** clothing kit if you can, a headnet at dusk, and a **fully enclosed insect-proof tent or net** for sleeping.
- **Antihistamines** and sting-relief cream. Midge bites can drive you mad.

### Soil and mud
Wet tropical clay can harbor bacteria that infect through cuts, and occasionally through inhaled dust or spray during storms.
- **Cover all cuts** with waterproof dressings before handling mud or wading.
- **Clean wounds promptly and thoroughly.** Carry antiseptic and plenty of dressings.
- Avoid standing in wet clay barefoot.
- Any wound that turns red, hot, or swollen, or any fever after your trip, needs medical attention. Tell the doctor where you've been, in terms they'll understand: "tropical wet season, clay soil, stagnant freshwater."
- Leptospirosis can come from water contaminated by animal urine. That's another reason to treat water and keep cuts sealed.

### Monitor lizards
They're generally not aggressive, but they're large, strong, and have a nasty bite and claws. Don't corner them, don't feed them, and don't leave food out. Give them the path.

### Crocodiles
The description doesn't mention them, and shallow ephemeral pans are unlikely habitat. But if you encounter any deep, permanent-looking water body, treat it with caution until you know otherwise.

## 8. Weather: The Afternoon Storms

Clouds build in the afternoons, "tall and white," and sometimes bring "a short hard storm that drums on the rock."

**On this plain, you are tall.** The only vertical things are lone trees and termite mounds.

- **Lightning:** When towering clouds build and darken, get off the tops of the whalebacks. **Don't shelter under a lone tree.** That's the most dangerous place to be. Keep away from termite spires, and spread a group out so one strike can't take everyone. With nowhere good to go, crouch low on your insulated sleeping pad in a low, not-waterlogged spot, feet together, and wait it out. Start counting from flash to thunder, and treat anything under about 30 seconds as close.
- **Sheet flow:** After a storm, water starts "moving again" across the rock. It won't be a flash flood in the canyon sense, since there are no canyons, but hollows fill and your camp may become a pond. **Never camp in a hollow or on a pan margin.**
- **Temperature drop:** Storms can bring a brief chill and wind. Being soaked and exhausted at dusk can make you colder than you'd expect in a hot place.
- **Opportunity:** Storms are your best clean-water source. Keep a tarp and containers ready to catch runoff off rock, ideally clean granite rather than the algae-covered seeps.

## 9. Conduct: How to Not Damage a Place Where Nothing Washes Away

The most important rule of The Pans is **"Nothing drains."** Everything you introduce stays and concentrates. That changes how ordinary travel habits play out.

- **No soap, sunscreen, or repellent in any water.** A film of sunscreen on a gnamma is poison for fairy shrimp and tadpoles in a sealed pool, and it stays there. Apply products well away from water, let them absorb, and don't wade or wash in natural pools while wearing them. Wash yourself from a bucket, with biodegradable soap if any, far from pans, and scatter the gray water thinly on bare laterite.
- **Human waste: pack it out.** There's barely any soil to dig a cat hole in, and in the next rain everything on the rock surface washes into the pans. Bring waste-bag kits. Urinate on bare rock far from water and seep lines, and ideally on a rise that doesn't drain toward a pan. Of course, on this land nothing drains clearly anywhere, which is exactly why you should pick well-separated spots.
- **Don't walk in the pans or on the pan floors.** The clay holds dormant eggs of shield shrimp and fairy shrimp, some decades old, and frogs sealed in their cocoons. A bootprint can crush them and compact the clay. On dry pan floors, the salt crust and scrapes may be bird nests lined "with shell and grit." They're nearly invisible. Stay on the rock.
- **Don't step on the moss and ferns.** The resurrection mosses are velvet green now and will be burnt paper later. They grow painfully slowly in rock cracks and are easily torn away. Step on bare rock.
- **Stay off the algae mats.** It's for your safety and theirs.
- **Leave the trees alone.** Each is ancient and lives on water from a single deep crack. Don't cut branches, strip the peeling bark, or burn deadwood from around them. Bring a **stove and fuel,** and make no fires. The dry sedge rings would burn fast, and there's almost nothing to burn anyway.
- **Don't break or climb termite mounds.** Many are centuries old and occupied.
- **Keep your distance at drinking sites.** Parrots at the big gnammas and wallabies at dusk are drinking to survive. Watch from a distance, downwind, and still. If they won't approach, you're too close. **In the late dry-down, don't camp near the last pools at all.** You may be the thing keeping animals from the only water left.
- **Don't handle frogs** with sunscreen or repellent on your hands. Their skin absorbs everything.
- **Don't collect anything.** No shrimp in jars, no rosette plants, no rock sheets. Some things here have waited longer than you've been alive.
- **Take everything out.** Food scraps don't decompose fast in this nutrient-starved land, and they'll draw monitors and ants.

## 10. Camping and Sleep

**Choosing a site:**
- A **slight rise of bare rock**, away from any pan, seep, or hollow, ideally a few hundred meters from the nearest chorus.
- Not under a lone tree (lightning, falling limbs), not on a whaleback summit (lightning, wind), not in an overhang (occupied).
- Level enough not to roll, but you'll be sleeping on rock, which means:

**Gear for sleeping:**
- A **thick sleeping pad.** The rock is hard, and the laterite is rough enough to puncture thin inflatables. Bring a closed-cell foam layer underneath, or at least a patch kit.
- A **free-standing tent** with full insect mesh. You can't drive stakes into granite, so plan to weight the corners with rocks. Use loose stones already lying around, and put them back.
- A light **sheet or quilt** rather than a heavy bag. The rock radiates heat all night.

**The frog chorus:**
It "drowns out your own footsteps and your own voice" and "goes on until nearly dawn." It's one of the most extraordinary sounds you'll ever hear, and on your third night it may make you want to cry for other reasons.
- Bring **high-quality earplugs,** foam ones rated for a strong noise reduction. Consider **over-ear sleep headphones** as well.
- **Camp farther from pans** than you think you need to. The sound carries kilometers over flat water, but it drops with distance.
- Plan for **fragmented sleep** and a **midday nap** in the heat. The land's schedule wants you to rest at noon anyway.
- **Communication:** Your group won't be able to hear each other near the pans at night. Agree on hand and light signals before dark. Carry a whistle, though you may not be heard over the frogs either, so stay close.

---

## 11. Food

There's nothing for you to forage. The land is starved of nutrients so badly that its plants eat insects. Bring all your food.

- Choose **heat-stable, lightweight, salty foods**: dried meats, crackers, nuts, salted foods, hard cheeses (they'll sweat), and dehydrated meals.
- Your appetite will drop in the heat. Eat anyway, especially salt and carbohydrate.
- Store food sealed and inside your tent or in hard containers. Ants will find it, and monitors can smell it.

## 13. Mental Preparation

This part matters more than it sounds.

**The scale will get to you.** The sky is enormous, the land is low, and you're often the tallest thing for a long way. Some people find it exhilarating. Others find it quietly frightening, with a sense of exposure and smallness that nags at them all day. Know which you might be, and give yourself time on day one just to sit on a whaleback and look.

**The rhythm will overwhelm you, then settle.** Blinding silver noons, then copper evenings, then a roaring night. The first night of the frog chorus feels like weather. By the fifth night you'll start picking out voices: the *bonk*, the ticking, the machine-trill, the sheep. Let yourself learn it.

**The silences will be strange.** On still noons, when birds and frogs are gone, you may hear the clay ticking as it cracks. Don't fill that silence. It's rare.

**Deep time sneaks up on you.** You're standing on the stumps of mountains that were worn to their roots, and then the roots were worn too. The shrimp in the water belong to a lineage older than almost anything. The trees may have been growing for centuries in one crack. Many visitors feel an odd grief or calm here, a sense that the land is nearly finished and has no hurry left in it. Let it happen. It's part of the visit.

**Slow down.** The land sorts itself by millimeters. The animals know every small rise and hollow. You'll only see that if you stop moving and look: at evening from a high dome, at a gnamma edge at dawn, or by lying on warm rock watching the sheet water decide which way to go.

## 14. A Suggested First Three Days

**Day 1: Arrival and orientation**
- Arrive in the morning if you can. Climb a moderate whaleback carefully and away from seeps, and record your landmarks: rock domes, trees, termite lines, distant blue domes.
- Confirm north using the sun and a termite line. Check your compass against it to see how much the ironstone deflects it.
- Rest through the noon heat in checked shade. Drink.
- Evening: walk the edge of one pan at a distance, then climb back up to watch the low light reveal the washboard of the plain. Map what you see.
- Camp on a rise well away from water. Earplugs in once you've listened for a while.

**Day 2: Water and life**
- Dawn: watch birds at a pan margin from a distance with binoculars. Watch the mist burn off.
- Morning: find and assess gnammas and seep lines. Collect and treat water, and top up fully.
- Midday: rest.
- Evening: watch the parrots come to a large gnamma, and then the wallabies. Stay still and downwind. At dusk, watch the bats stream out over the midge columns.
- Night: from camp, with a headlamp, walk a few careful meters toward the chorus with gaiters on, then retreat.

**Day 3: Traveling**
- Plan a route from high ground the evening before. At dawn, travel toward the next cluster of domes or the next distinctive tree, pace-counting and checking your bearings against the termite lines.
- Stay on rock. Skirt the pans.
- Watch the afternoon sky, and be off the heights and set up before any storm.
- If it rains, catch water. Then walk out afterward and watch the whole land start moving again.

---

## Final Word

The Pans won't hurt you out of malice. The heat, the glare, the slick rock, and the snakes are just the conditions of a place that has been doing the same slow thing for longer than you can imagine. Prepare for them plainly and carefully.

Then, once your body is looked after, give the place your attention. Arrive during the interruption, while it's wet and alive and roaring. Walk softly and take nothing. Leave before the last pools dry and everything goes back under the clay.
