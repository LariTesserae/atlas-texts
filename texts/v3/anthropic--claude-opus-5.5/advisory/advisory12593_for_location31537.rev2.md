# Preparing to Visit the Cut Cistern

## The Short Version

This place is a small, hidden, freshly cut water chamber that is almost entirely flooded with warm spring water, reached only by a narrow vertical shaft. It is beautiful and quiet in every way except sound. It is also:

- **Someone's water supply.** The channel runs downslope toward a field or village.
- **Biologically untouched.** Nothing living has reached the water yet.
- **Physically awkward.** You descend a shaft, drop into water with almost no air space, and must climb out again while wet and heat-softened.

Prepare for it the way you would prepare for a cave dive, a hot spring, and a visit to a sacred site all at once. The hardest part won't be the swimming. It will be getting out, and leaving no trace.

---

## 1. Understand What You're Walking Into

**The site is hidden on purpose, or at least effectively.** The shaft sits flush with the grass, rimmed with fitted blocks and half-covered by a thornbush. You could walk right into it. Approach any hillside here slowly, and watch for a square of darkness under thorns.

**The builders are recent.** The stone dust hasn't washed out of the tool marks. The mortar is still white. The scaffold sockets are empty, which means the scaffolding was only just removed. The people who made this could come back tomorrow to check their work, and they may consider it private, sacred, or strategically important. A hidden water source always matters to someone. Don't assume you're alone in the landscape. If you can, find out who built it before you go. If you can't, act as though you're trespassing in a well, because you probably are.

**The water leaves and goes somewhere people use it.** Anything you put in the water goes downstream: sweat, sunscreen, skin oils, urine, microbes from other worlds, a dropped buckle. Keep that in mind throughout this guide.

---

## 2. Physical Hazards and How to Prepare

### The Shaft
- **Size:** A little over a body's width across, and about four body-lengths deep (roughly 6–8 meters). That's too narrow to climb comfortably with bulky gear on your back, and too deep to jump.
- **Anchoring:** Don't trust the thornbush. The fitted rim blocks are the only solid structure, and they belong to someone else. Use a removable anchor, such as a stake-and-sling system set well back from the rim, a tripod over the opening, or a partner holding a belay. Pad the rope where it runs over the rim edge, both to protect the rope and to avoid scarring the new stonework.
- **Hazards inside the shaft:** You'll pass the soil layer with its fern, moss, and the dangling thornbush root. Brush past them gently. The root is described as reaching toward the water and not quite reaching. Don't be the one who snaps it off or pushes it down into the pool.
- **The bottom:** The shaft opens into the crown of the vault. You'll come out into a thin band of air and then drop almost immediately into water. There is no ledge, no floor, and no place to stand.

### Getting Out (Plan This First)
This is the most dangerous part of the visit. You'll need to go from floating in body-temperature water, with nothing solid underfoot, into a vertical shaft, and climb about 7 meters while wet, tired, and heat-flushed.

- **Rig a rope ladder, etrier, or ascender system before you enter,** and test it from the water side if you can. Getting a foot into the first rung while treading water under a low vault is harder than it sounds.
- **Have a surface partner** who can haul or assist. A mechanical advantage system (a simple 3:1 pulley) and a chest harness would let them lift you if you fade.
- **Don't count on the scaffold sockets as footholds.** They're at shoulder height (underwater, relative to the old floor), they're shallow, and they belong to the structure's history. At most, use them as a gentle momentary touch-point.

### The Heat
The water is close to body temperature and a little above. It feels wonderful, and that is exactly the problem.

- **You cannot shed heat in it.** Every bit of effort, whether swimming, climbing gear, or holding your breath repeatedly, warms you with nowhere for the heat to go. Hyperthermia sneaks up on you in water like this.
- **Hot-tub syncope is real.** Long immersion in warm water dilates your blood vessels and lowers your blood pressure. When you haul yourself out and climb, you may get dizzy or faint, in a narrow shaft, above water.
- **Preparation:**
  - Limit immersion to about 15–20 minutes per session, then rest in the cool air up top.
  - Drink plenty before going in. Bring more water for after, and don't drink the spring water.
  - **Don't wear a wetsuit.** You don't need insulation. A thin rash guard or lycra skin gives cut protection without trapping heat.
  - Go in well rested and fed, and not after drinking alcohol.
  - Climb out slowly, and pause at the waterline with your head up before committing to the climb.

### The Air Lens
The air space is a thin band pressed against the vault: a hand's breadth at the walls, somewhat more under the curve of the crown. And gas is collecting there. Bubbles from the spring rise, pool under the vault in a silvery skin, and periodically release.

- **Assume the gas isn't just air.** Deep warm springs commonly carry carbon dioxide, and sometimes hydrogen sulfide or radon. CO₂ is heavier than air, but in a sealed pocket fed from below it can still build up. The shaft provides some ventilation, but the far corners of the vault may be stagnant.
- **Bring a portable multi-gas detector** (O₂, CO₂, H₂S) and lower it down before you descend. Then check again at the waterline.
- **Breathe near the shaft, not in the corners.** Don't do breath-hold recoveries in pockets far from the opening.
- **If you get a headache, feel sudden breathlessness, or feel an odd euphoria, get out.** Raised CO₂ can cause panic and poor judgment, which is exactly what you don't want in a flooded room.

### Breathing Equipment Choice
- **Snorkel and breath-hold:** The lightest and simplest option, and the least disruptive. But repeated breath-holds in warm water drain you faster than you'd expect, and the only surface is that thin lens under the vault. Freedive only with a partner watching you, and use a mask and snorkel for most of your time.
- **Small scuba cylinder (pony bottle) or surface-supplied air:** This gives you longer, calmer time underwater. You'll need to lower the tank separately down the shaft on a line. Keep your exhaust bubbles in mind: they'll collect under the vault and churn the delicate surface film.
- Whatever you choose, you don't need to go deep. The room is small and shallow enough that you'll be moving within a few meters of the surface.

### Sharp Stone
The vault ribs are sharp enough to cut a palm. The tool-marked walls are basically rasps. The chisel gouges along the soft north-wall band are rough and ragged.

- Wear **thin gloves**. Cut-resistant dive gloves are ideal.
- Wear a **rash guard and full-length leggings or a skin suit**.
- **Watch your head.** When surfacing into the air lens, raise one hand first to feel for the vault. Rising blind into a sharp rib is the easiest injury to get here.
- Carry a small waterproof first-aid kit for the surface. Small cuts can bleed freely in warm water.

### The Rough Spring Opening (East End)
- **Don't enter it, reach into it, or explore it.** It's the only undressed hole in the room, and it leads into fissures no one has seen. You can hear knocking from deeper in the rock, and the spring pulses harder on some days.
- Keep a respectful distance. The shimmering blur of warmer water marks the outflow. It's lovely to watch from a meter or two away.
- If the knocking grows stronger or more frequent while you're there, or the roar climbs noticeably, **treat it as a sign to leave.** A pulsing geothermal source is not something to be near during a surge.

### The Outflow Channel (West Wall)
- A trough two hands wide, running full, at the waterline, dropping over a carved step. It isn't big enough to take a body, but it's easily big enough to catch a hand, a fin tip, a loose strap, or a dangling hose. And anything it takes is gone downhill for good.
- **Keep clear of the west wall at the waterline.** Secure all loose gear and clip everything off. Tuck your hair away.

### Visibility
- **At noon the room is almost too bright to look at.** Bring a mask with a lightly tinted or anti-glare lens, and sunglasses for the dim air zone.
- **At dawn and dusk it's gray-blue dark.** Bring a waterproof dive light, and consider a backup.
- **After heavy rain, the water goes milky for about a day.** The tool marks vanish and the room turns into cloud. It's beautiful, but you lose all visual reference to walls, the shaft, the exit, and the channel. **Don't enter during milky conditions** unless you're on a fixed guideline running from the shaft down into the room, and even then, keep it brief.
- **On high-pulse days, fine grit drifts through the water.** Visibility drops and the water becomes more abrasive. It's safe enough, but less comfortable.

### Sound
The roar is constant: heavy rain inside a drum, amplified by the vault. Underwater it becomes a pressure you feel in your chest and jaw.

- **Hearing protection** (moldable wax or silicone plugs, or vented dive earplugs) will help in the air lens and on the descent. Prolonged exposure in that vault could be genuinely fatiguing.
- **You won't be able to talk to your partner, even shouting.** Agree on hand signals before you go down, including "okay," "problem," "air," "out now," and "gas/bad air." Also agree on rope-tug signals with the surface partner: for example, one tug for okay, two for more slack, three for haul me up.
- Some people find the underwater throb disorienting or nauseating. If you're prone to motion sickness or sensitive to low-frequency vibration, try it for a short first session before committing to a longer one.

### Water Quality and Health
- **Don't drink the spring water.** Deep mineral springs can carry high levels of dissolved minerals, sometimes arsenic or other elements, and nothing guarantees this one is potable, even if the village uses it.
- **Keep water out of your nose.** Warm freshwater anywhere can in principle host dangerous amoebae. This spring is described as lifeless, but "nothing living has found its way in" refers to visible life. Use a nose clip, or keep your mask sealed and your head controlled.
- Rinse off with fresh water after your visit.

## 3. Protecting the Place

Leaving the place as you found it is the part of this visit that matters most.

### You Might Be the First Living Thing to Arrive
The description is explicit: no fish, no snails, no insects, no frogs. "Something will eventually come down the shaft or up the channel and live here." If you go in without care, that something is you, and whatever you carry: skin bacteria, spores from other worlds, fungal fragments in your gear, algae from your last swim, larvae in your boot treads.

**Biosecurity checklist:**
- **Use brand-new or fully sterilized gear.** Clean everything that touches the water or the shaft walls with a proper disinfectant and let it dry completely. Pay special attention to rope, fins, gloves, masks, and snorkels. If your gear has been in other bodies of water, in this world or any other, assume it's contaminated.
- **Shower thoroughly before entering.** Use no soap residue, no lotion, no sunscreen, no insect repellent, no hair products, no perfume. The water is someone's drinking supply, and the algae patch is the only ecosystem in the room.
- **Don't urinate in the water.** In body-temperature water the urge comes easily. Go before you descend.
- **Wear clean clothing** that has never been worn in soil or other water.
- **Don't bring food down.** Eat up top, away from the shaft.

### Things You Must Not Touch
- **The algae patch** beneath the shaft. It's the size of a sleeping mat, it's the only life in the water, and its shape is a record of a year's sunlight. Don't touch it, fin near it, or stir up sediment over it. Hover well above it.
- **The travertine crust** at the spring mouth and along the outflow path. It's fingernail-thin, fragile, and the beginning of centuries of growth. A careless hand would wipe it away.
- **The fresh lime mortar and plug patches** in the north wall. Fresh lime can still be mildly caustic, and it's the most vulnerable part of the construction.
- **The fern, the moss, and the hanging root** in the shaft.
- **The bubble skin under the vault.** Touching it won't harm anything permanently, but it's one of the room's quiet rhythms. Let it gather and release on its own.
- **The stone dust in the tool marks.** It's evidence of how recently this was made. Don't scrub it out with your curiosity.

### Things You Must Not Leave
- **Nothing.** Anything dropped goes one of two places: to the floor, where it becomes an unexplained artifact in a newly made room, or out through the channel into someone's field or water jar. Tether every piece of gear. Count your items before and after.
- **No marks.** Don't scratch, chalk, or bolt anything into the stone.

### Minimize Disturbance
- Move slowly. Fin gently, or better, pull yourself along with light fingertip touches. The current already sweeps grit west, and you don't want to add your own trail to the map the water is drawing.
- Go with at most one or two people in the water. It's a small room.

## 4. When to Go

- **For the full experience: a clear day, arriving an hour before noon.** You'll see the column of light slide down the western lip, down the wall, into the water, and down to the floor. Then the room goes incandescent for an hour or two. Plan your water sessions around that window, with rest breaks up top in between.
- **For stillness and sound: dawn or dusk.** The room is dim and the roar seems louder. It's eerie and meditative, and more demanding. Bring lights.
- **At night:** Mist curls up out of the shaft. Watch it from above, but don't go down. The descent and exit are hard enough in daylight.
- **Avoid:** the day after heavy rain (milky water, zero visibility), and days when the spring is pulsing hard (more grit, more heat, more noise, and that knocking). If you arrive and the roar seems much louder than you expected, sit at the rim for a while, and consider coming back another day.
- **Season matters.** The sun's angle shapes the algae patch, so light will reach deeper or shallower depending on the time of year. Midsummer gives the longest, most vertical column of light.

## 5. Gear List

**Access and rescue**
- Static rope, rope protector for the rim edge, removable anchor or tripod
- Harness, descender, ascenders, or a rope ladder/etriers extending into the water
- Haul system (pulleys) for the surface partner
- Tether lines for all gear

**In the water**
- Low-volume mask (anti-glare or lightly tinted), snorkel, nose clip
- Short fins, or none. The room is small, and fins stir sediment.
- Thin rash guard and skin leggings (no wetsuit)
- Cut-resistant thin gloves
- Optional: pony bottle or surface-supplied air, lowered separately
- Waterproof dive light and backup
- Slate or pre-agreed hand signals

**Safety**
- Portable gas detector (O₂, CO₂, H₂S)
- Earplugs
- Waterproof first-aid kit (at the surface)
- Drinking water, plenty of it, and electrolytes
- A towel and a cool, shaded rest spot up top
- A way to call for help, if your multiverse travel allows

**Biosecurity**
- Disinfectant for gear, fresh water for rinsing
- Clean, dedicated clothing
- Bags for any waste

## 6. What It Will Actually Be Like

The descent will be hard, with rope burn, thorns, and the tight square of stone around your shoulders. Then the air thickens and warms, and the sound hits you before anything else. You don't expect a hidden underground room to be that loud.

Then you're in the water, and it's body temperature. For many people this is the most disorienting part. Water at skin temperature doesn't register as cold or hot, so you lose the sense of where your body ends and the water begins. Combine that with the shadowless, glowing light, the roar you feel in your jaw, and a room so small and so bright that there's no dark corner for your eyes to rest in, and some people will feel profoundly calm. It's womblike. Others may feel a creeping panic. **Know which kind of person you are before you go**, or find out with a short first session.

The first time you surface into the air lens with a vault inches from your face, it will feel close. Surface near the shaft, where you can see the square of sky, until you trust the space.

If you go at noon, expect to be stunned. The water doesn't stop the light. The floor glows. The ceiling is covered in moving nets of brilliance. Every chisel mark throws a tiny, trembling shadow. You'll see the heat shimmer of the spring rising like a ghost at the east end, and bubble threads flashing. It may be one of the most beautiful rooms you ever enter.

You may also feel something harder to name. Every surface shows the decisions of workers who aren't there: where one person handed the chisel to the next, where they fought with the soft stone and patched it, where they set the waterline to the width of a hand. The room is so new that it hasn't started forgetting them. Take time just to look.

And you'll be aware, if you've prepared properly, that you're a guest in a place that hasn't been discovered yet, not even by the snails. The kindest thing you can do is leave it undiscovered, with the mortar still whitening slowly, the fern uncurling, and the root still reaching and not quite reaching the water. Let whatever eventually comes to live there find it on its own.

Climb out slowly. Rest in the grass. Drink your water. Watch the mist rise from the shaft at dusk. Then go.
