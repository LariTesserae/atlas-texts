# Preparing for the Underleaf

## What You Are Actually Preparing For

Most travelers prepare for the Underleaf as if it were a jungle. It isn't one. A jungle is full of things that bite, rot, grow, and fall. The Underleaf has almost none of that.

The real dangers are quieter:

- **Heat you cannot shed.**
- **Light you cannot escape.**
- **Space you cannot navigate by ordinary means.**
- **A stillness that works on the mind the way warm water works on a tired body.** It is pleasant, and then you have been in it too long.

Pack for the body, but plan for the mind. The second matters more.

---

## Part One: The Approach

The Underleaf sits in a salt plain, and the plain will try to kill you before you ever reach the green.

**Water for the crossing.** Carry more than you think you need. The cup-pools are drinkable, but you cannot count on reaching them if something goes wrong on the crust. Carry enough for the walk out as well. On the return you will be tired, possibly disoriented, and reluctant to be where you are.

**Eyes.** A white salt plain under full sun is a glare field. Bring glacier goggles or high-rated sunglasses with side shields. Snow blindness happens on salt too. You will also want them at the lip, where the green roof shines at midday like a sheet of water.

**Feet.** Salt crust can be sharp and can break through into brine or soft sediment underneath. Wear sturdy boots with gaiters. You will change into different footwear below (see Part Four).

**Skin.** Wear loose, full-coverage clothing and a wide-brimmed hat, and use sunscreen. Salt dust gets into everything and chafes badly once you start sweating. Bring something to rinse it off before you descend, or you will carry that grit into a place where you will sweat constantly for days.

**Timing.** Cross in the early morning or evening if you can. Remember that desert nights can be cold. This matters more on the way out than on the way in.

**Mark your entry point from above.** This is critical. From the plain, the Underleaf is a crescent a few kilometers across with a uniform surface. Once you are inside, one part of the lip looks like every other part. Before you descend:

- Build a cairn, plant a pole with a bright flag, or leave a cache.
- Take a compass bearing to distant landmarks on the plain, if there are any.
- Note where your entry point sits along the crescent: near a horn, near the belly of the curve, near the east wall.

You will want this information badly later.

## Part Two: The Descent

The roof is a mass of thin, layered leaves, and you push through it into a 150-meter drop to the floor. It closes behind you. Treat this as a vertical descent into unknown structure.

**Climbing equipment.** Bring:

- A harness
- Rope long enough for the full drop plus anchoring
- Slings for wrapping stems
- A descender, and ascenders for the return

The upper structure is lattice and lateral stem, so you can probably climb down through it in stages. But the stems are smooth, faintly translucent, and possibly beaded with condensation. Do not assume they will grip like bark.

**Anchoring without damage.** Wrap slings around stems rather than driving anything into them. You may not be able to damage the plant in any lasting way. Still, a cut you make "never having happened" might also mean an anchor that suddenly never held. Do not test what the place does with wounds while your weight is on the result. Use girth hitches and friction wraps around living columns, and back everything up.

**Plan the climb back up now.** Ascending 150 meters in glasshouse heat is brutal. Leave your rope fixed if you can. Whether the plant tolerates a foreign rope over time is unknown. Tied knots of the plant's own tendril are known to persist, so consider tying tendril markers along your descent line as a backup.

**The light transition.** Coming down through the roof, you pass from harsh, directional desert sun into shadowless, even, all-around glow. It may be disorienting, even briefly nauseating, the way flat light on a snowfield can be. Pause at a gallery once you are through and let your eyes and your sense of balance adjust before you continue down.

## Part Three: The Body

### Heat and Humidity: The Main Physical Danger

The air is hot, saturated, and still. That combination is far more dangerous than dry heat, because your body cools itself by evaporating sweat, and here the sweat will not evaporate. It will run off you and do nothing.

- **Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke,** and make sure your companions know them: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, and eventually the frightening stage where you stop sweating. Confusion is the dangerous one, because the Underleaf already encourages a dreamy, unhurried state of mind. You may not notice you are becoming impaired.
- **Move slowly.** The place practically demands it anyway. Exertion generates heat you cannot dump. Climbing galleries is exertion.
- **Do not count on cooling.** The cup-pool water is blood-warm and will not chill you. The mist pockets might be cooler, but no one reports that they are. Consider bringing chemical cold packs or a small insulated store of ice or frozen gel packs for emergencies only. They will not last long, so save them for someone who is overheating, not for comfort.
- **Acclimatize before you go.** Spend time in hot, humid conditions in the weeks beforehand: saunas, a greenhouse, a tropical climate. Heat acclimatization is real and trainable.
- **Carry electrolytes.** You will sweat constantly. Drinking sweet cup-water without replacing salt risks hyponatremia. Bring electrolyte tablets or salt packets, and use them. There is an irony here: you just crossed a salt plain, and down here you will need salt.

### Water

The cup-pools are described as clear, drinkable, and faintly sweet. That is good news, but be careful.

- **Filter or treat anyway, at least at first.** Nothing here seems to rot, which suggests the place may be close to sterile. That is not the same as safe. Watch how your body responds.
- **Choose pools without the watching shrimp,** at least until you know more. Nothing suggests the shrimp is harmful. Still, drinking from the only thing here that looks back at you seems unwise.
- **Take water gently.** The water in the cups does not evaporate or rise. It is unknown whether a pool you empty will refill. Take a little from many pools rather than draining one.
- **Leave the leaf-bowl by the hammock alone.** Do not drink from it.

### Food

**Bring all of it.** Nothing here fruits. There is no game. The plant's internal fluid is unknown. Do not eat plant tissue, and do not assume that a plant which "forgets" being cut will be forgiving to your gut.

- Pack calorie-dense, heat-stable food that will not melt into a mess or spoil in humidity: hard biscuits, nut butters in tubes, dried meats, hard cheeses, rice or oat packets that can be rehydrated with warm water.
- Your food may behave strangely. In a place where nothing decays, you may find it keeps better than expected. Do not rely on that.
- **Appetite may fade.** Heat suppresses hunger, and stillness may suppress it further. Eat on a schedule regardless.

### Skin and Feet

Constant wetness is hard on skin. Even if the environment turns out to be sterile, you are bringing your own microbes with you.

- Bring antifungal powder or cream, and use it on feet, groin, and underarms.
- Pack several pairs of thin socks and rotate them, drying the used pair against your body or in a mesh bag.
- Take care of blisters and chafing immediately. Friction plus moisture turns small problems into large ones quickly.
- **Watch your wounds.** This matters for its own reason. The plant forgets its injuries. Nothing indicates the place does the same for you. Your cuts will probably heal the ordinary way: slowly, and with some risk of infection in the damp. Keep a proper first-aid kit and keep wounds clean. If a cut on your own body ever vanishes when you look away, leave.

### Light and Sleep

There are no shadows. It is unclear whether the Underleaf ever goes dark. The roof is lit by the sun, so night above may mean dimming below, or the glow may persist. The traveler who "wakes in the upper world in the dark" and misses the Underleaf suggests that the dark is something the place does not have, or does not have much of.

Prepare for permanent light:

- **An excellent sleep mask.** Make it contoured, blackout, and comfortable in heat, and bring a spare, because one will get soaked.
- **Earplugs, possibly.** The high ticking is faint. For some sleepers it will be soothing. For others it will become the only thing they can hear.
- **A watch with the date,** analog if possible. Electronics may suffer in the humidity (see below).
- **A strict schedule.** Decide in advance when you sleep, eat, explore, and rest, and keep to it. Your circadian rhythm will have nothing to anchor it except what you give it. Without a schedule, days blur.

Going without shadows is also subtly tiring. We read depth, distance, and form partly from shadow. Expect some visual fatigue and mild disorientation, especially judging distances on the galleries. Test every step before you trust it.

## Part Four: Moving Through It

### Footwear

Change out of your desert boots at the bottom. The root-felt floor is springy and warm, and the galleries are smooth stem. You want:

- Soft, flexible shoes with sticky rubber soles, like approach shoes or climbing slippers, for the galleries.
- Something breathable for the floor. Some travelers go barefoot on the felt. If you do, check your feet often.

### Navigation: The Second Great Danger

The Underleaf is built to get you lost. Consider:

- **No shadows,** so no sun direction and no sense of time from light.
- **Light that does not fade with distance,** so near and far look strangely alike.
- **Repetition.** Galleries recede in identical layers, column behind column.
- **No wear,** so there are no paths, no trampled routes, and no signs of where you have been.
- **Erased marks.** Scratches vanish when you look away. Blazes, chalk marks, and cut signs cannot be trusted.

Plan around all of it:

1. **Bring a compass.** Someone described a hammock "near the eastern wall," so direction appears to be knowable here. Check your compass against known bearings before you rely on it.
2. **Bring a way to mark your route with your own materials.** Brightly colored flagging tape tied around stems, cord, or small reflective markers may persist better than any mark on the plant. Test this early: tie a marker, walk away, come back an hour later, and see if it is still there. If markers vanish, stop exploring and reassess.
3. **Learn the tendril knot.** The plant keeps knots tied from its own braided tendril. They are the only human-made things known to last. Before you go, practice tying secure knots with thin, flexible, fibrous material. Tie a marker knot of tendril at each junction you pass. This appears to be what earlier travelers did. The arches and doorways they bound "from one part of the gallery to another identical part" may have been waymarkers, attempts to make one place different from another.
4. **Keep a written route log.** Record compass bearings, distances in paces, and levels (floor, low gallery, high gallery), plus any distinctive features: a mist pocket, an unusually thick column, a cup-pool with a shrimp.
5. **Use the fixed features that already exist.** The hammock, the seed ring, and the bound arches are landmarks. Find them, record their positions, and use them to orient yourself.
6. **Never explore alone.** If you go in a group, set rules for separation: how long to wait, and where to regroup. Voices do not echo, and sound soaks into the tendrils. A lost companion twenty meters away may not hear you.
7. **Bring a whistle** anyway, and agree on signals. High, sharp sounds may carry better than voices. Test this early too.

### Climbing the Galleries

The galleries are walkable, but they are high, smooth, possibly damp, and hard to judge in shadowless light.

- Use a harness and a short tether above floor level.
- Test lateral stems before putting your full weight on them. They look solid, but you do not know how they bear load.
- Remember that the root-felt floor, springy as it is, will not catch you gracefully from forty meters.

### Parting the Curtains

Filament curtains hang everywhere, dense enough to part with both arms. Wear long sleeves anyway. Fine tendrils can irritate skin, get into eyes, or tangle in gear. Tie back long hair. Keep straps, cords, and loose ends stowed so you do not become snarled.

## Part Five: Gear That Suffers Here

**Electronics.** Saturated humidity condenses on anything cooler than the air and gets into seams. Bring dry bags, silica gel packs, and waterproof cases. Expect battery performance to behave oddly in the heat. Do not depend on anything electronic for your life. Carry paper maps, a compass, and a notebook.

**Paper and ink.** Use waterproof notebooks and pencils or waterproof pens. Your journal is important (see Part Seven). Protect it.

**Fabric.** Nothing will dry. Bring clothing that is tolerable when wet, avoid heavy cotton, and keep one dry set sealed in a waterproof bag for the exit.

**Metal.** Condensation means corrosion over time, even if the air itself seems clean. Oil your tools and knife.

## Part Six: Conduct

The Underleaf does not resist you. It forgets you. That is not permission to do whatever you like.

- **Do not cut, carve, or deliberately damage the plant.** Your cuts vanish. Whether something else happens alongside the vanishing, no one knows.
- **Leave the traces alone.** Do not untie the knotted arches. Do not disturb the seed ring. Do not step inside it without thinking carefully about why you want to. Do not take the leaf-bowl. Do not sleep in the hammock by the eastern wall. It may be empty, or it may not be. Leave it as you found it.
- **Do not collect the moths.** They settle, they do not die, and they have no apparent reason to exist except to be there. Disturbing them is unnecessary.
- **Be gentle with the pools.** Do not bathe in them. Do not drain them. Do not wash dishes in them.
- **Carry out your waste.** All of it: food wrappers, human waste, spent supplies. The plant may forget a scratch, but you do not want to learn what it does with a latrine. It is also worth knowing whether your leavings stay, because that tells you something about whether you stay.
- **Speak softly.** Loud speech feels like shouting in a library, and it probably is. It is also the courteous thing in a place this quiet.

### The Watchers and the Walker

**The shrimp** turns to face you. Let it. Don't prod it, don't scoop it out, and don't treat it as a curiosity to catch. It is the only thing here that appears aware of you. Whatever it is, it deserves not to be poked.

**The large, pale, many-limbed walker** has been seen perhaps a dozen times. If you see it:

- **Do not follow it.** It moves without hurry along the high galleries, and following it means climbing, forgetting your route, and going deeper than you planned. Everything about that scenario is how people get lost.
- **Do not approach it.** Nothing suggests it is dangerous. Nothing suggests it is not.
- **Note where you saw it,** at what height, and in which direction it was moving. Then let it go.

### The Note

Sometimes a deep, soft sound, like a finger drawn across the rim of a glass, rises from somewhere across the hollow. You may feel an urge to find its source. Consider that no one has, and consider how many people have tried. Write down when you heard it and which way it seemed to come from. That is enough.

## Part Seven: The Mind

This is the part most travelers neglect, and it is the part that decides whether you come home.

### The Danger of Nothing Happening

The Underleaf is warm, bright, gentle, and changeless. There is water at arm's length. You are never cold. Nothing threatens you in any obvious way. For a tired person, a grieving person, or anyone who has been waiting a long time for things to stop, this can feel like rest. It isn't quite rest. People who stay long say the changelessness is exactly the problem.

Prepare for it:

**Set a hard departure date before you arrive,** and do not renegotiate it while you are inside. Write it down. Give it to someone above. Make the decision in the upper world, where things change, not in the Underleaf, where it will always feel as though there is no hurry.

**Travel with a companion who agrees to hold you to it.** Choose someone you trust to drag you out if you start saying "one more day." Agree beforehand that "I'd like to stay a little longer" is a warning sign, not a request.

**Keep a daily journal, in your own hand.** Record the date, what you did, what you ate, how you feel, and one thing that was different from yesterday. That last item may be very hard to find. That is the point. If you go several days without finding anything different, it is time to leave.

**Bring something that changes.** A candle that burns down. A small hourglass. A seed in a sealed pot of soil from home, which will sprout or won't. A book you have not read yet. Your body needs evidence that time is still moving, and the Underleaf will not supply any.

**Watch your own record-keeping.** If you find your journal entries getting shorter, then vaguer, then missing, if you stop marking your route, if you stop checking the date, notice it. The place does not resist damage. It forgets it. Earlier visitors "left, or were forgotten along with every scratch they made." It may be possible to be forgotten slowly, and the first thing to go may be your own attention.

**Check yourself in a mirror.** Bring a small one. Look at your face each day. You may learn whether you are changing in here. It is not obvious which answer would be more reassuring.

### The Emotional Pull

Expect beauty. The omnidirectional gold, the thousand hanging droplets like lenses, the far galleries fading into green-white haze: it will move you, possibly more than you expect. Let it. But know that the pull of the Underleaf is not a reason to trust it. A place can be lovely and still not be good for you to remain in.

---

## Part Eight: Leaving

**Leave before you are exhausted.** The climb out is the hardest physical part of the whole trip: 150 meters up through heat and lattice, then out through the roof into desert. Budget strength and water for it.

**Change into dry clothes at the lip.** You will emerge soaked. If it is day, the desert sun will be brutal after the soft light, so have your goggles ready before you break through. If it is night, the desert may be cold, and cold on wet skin causes hypothermia quickly. Get dry and layered at once.

**Expect the shadows to be shocking.** After days without them, the hard-edged shadows of the upper world may look almost violent. Your depth perception may be off for a while. Walk carefully on the crust.

**Expect the dark to be worse.** The first real night after the Underleaf will be very dark. Keep a headlamp within reach. You may want a small nightlight for some time after you get home.
