# Preparing for the Drowning Shelf

## First, understand what you're walking into

The description makes this sound like weather. It is closer to diving, canyoneering, and high-altitude mountaineering at the same time, inside a waterfall that doesn't obey gravity reliably. Most visitors will get three things wrong:

1. **They'll prepare for cold.** The water is blood-warm. The danger is overheating, not hypothermia.
2. **They'll prepare for falling water.** The water that kills you here is more likely the water going *up*.
3. **They'll expect to hear danger coming.** You won't hear anything useful. You'll *feel* it, and sometimes you'll feel it before it happens.

This place isn't hostile. Nothing here wants you. You are simply standing in the middle of an argument between a mountain and an ocean, and neither side will make room for you. Your whole job is to be small, heavy, low, and brief.

---

## Physical conditioning

Start months ahead. You need to be comfortable in water that is actively trying to move you.

**Breath-hold training.** You shouldn't plan to rely on breath-holding (more on that below), but you need the calm it teaches. Learn static apnea and CO₂ tolerance tables with a certified freediving instructor, never alone. The goal isn't a big number. The goal is that when your regulator gets knocked loose and your mask floods in a hammering layer, your body doesn't panic. Panic uses oxygen, and panic is what drowns people here.

**Surge and whitewater work.** Spend time in surge channels, under small waterfalls, and in whitewater swimming courses. Learn how moving water feels on your body, how it finds the gap between your mask and your face, and how it pulls limbs away from the torso. Learn to make yourself compact. Swiftwater rescue courses teach this well.

**Disorientation training.** Helicopter underwater egress ("dunker") training, or anything that flips you upside down underwater and makes you find your way out, is valuable. On the Shelf the sea's surface will sometimes be above you and sometimes below you, and it will pass *through* you. Your eyes will lie about which way is up. You need practice trusting your hands and feet on stone over what you see.

**Neck and core strength.** The hammering layers push down hard. A helmet and mask catch that force and put it into your neck. Train for it.

**Heat tolerance.** Train in warm conditions. More importantly, learn what heat exhaustion feels like in *your* body: the headache, the slight stupidity, the irritability. On the Shelf you'll be working hard in 37°C water with no way to shed heat, and the first symptom of heat exhaustion is poor judgment, in a place where one bad judgment is enough.

## Breathing: the central problem

"For most of any hour there is no air at all." Take that literally.

**Bring a gas supply.** A closed-circuit rebreather is ideal, since it's quiet and efficient and you'll be exerting yourself. Standard scuba with a pony bottle as backup also works. Air may appear in brief gaps, but you shouldn't count on it.

**Use a full-face mask, not a mouthpiece regulator.** A hammering layer will tear a regulator out of your mouth. A full-face mask with a good hood and a secure spider strap keeps your airway sealed when the water hits you from above, below, and sideways within a few seconds. Practice clearing a flooded full-face mask until you can do it without thinking.

**The most important breathing rule: exhale when you rise.** This is where the Shelf can kill a trained diver. If you are breathing compressed gas and an upward layer lifts you, or the membrane-surface drops past you and you are suddenly "above" the sea, the pressure around your lungs may change fast. Holding your breath during a rapid ascent can rupture your lungs. Any time you feel lifted, breathe out slowly and continuously. Drill this until it's reflex.

**Pressure is unreliable.** The Shelf sits near a summit, thousands of meters above the sea's real surface, so the ambient air (when there is any) is thin. Yet sometimes there is a sea surface *above* you with real water depth between you and it. Your ears will feel pressure that comes and goes. Equalize often and gently. Bring a dive computer with altitude settings, but don't trust any single reading, because the water column may not follow ordinary hydrostatics. Keep your visit shallow in time, stay conservative, and treat it as a high-altitude dive for decompression purposes afterward.

**Sulfur.** The fissures breathe hydrogen sulfide. Underwater it's mostly dissolved, but in the rare moments when air opens up, the gas can pool in low spots near the vents. If you smell rotten eggs, move away and keep your mask sealed. If you *stop* smelling it while still near a vent, move away faster, because at dangerous concentrations H₂S deadens your sense of smell.

## Gear

**Exposure protection.** A thin dive skin or 1–2 mm suit, for abrasion protection only. You don't need insulation. You need armor against glass-rinded basalt, barnacle scars, and the constant grit lifted by upward rain. Full coverage, including gloves (reinforced palms) and a hood.

**Helmet.** A climbing or whitewater helmet over the hood. Pebbles and glass flakes are lifted and dropped constantly, and the hammering layers can throw you.

**Footwear.** Canyoneering boots with sticky rubber soles. Pillow basalt is bulbous and uneven, the weed mats are slick, and you'll spend much of your time bracing. Fins are useless and dangerous here. Don't bring them.

**Weight.** This matters more than it seems. Normally divers aim for neutral buoyancy. Here you want to be **heavy**, noticeably negative, so the upward rain has a harder time lifting you. Wear more lead than you normally would, distributed in a harness or integrated system rather than a single belt, so it can't slide off and so it keeps you upright. Don't ditch weight when lifted. Ditching it will make you rise faster and farther. Keep your weight and get your hands on stone.

**Tether.** Go with a partner and tether together with a short line, 2–3 meters. You can't talk, so agree on tug signals before you arrive (for example: one tug = "OK?"/"OK"; two = "stay put, brace"; three = "go to the doorway now"; continuous = "help"). Don't anchor the tether to the Shelf by drilling bolts or hammering pitons. More on why under *Conduct*. If you must anchor, a sling around a boulder at the cliff base is acceptable. Don't anchor to the built structures.

**Hearing protection.** Vented diving earplugs, the kind that allow equalization. The roar is "a condition you are inside," and sustained intense sound, especially underwater where it couples straight into your skull, will damage hearing. You won't lose anything useful by muffling it. Voices are meaningless here anyway.

**Light.** One modest dive light, clipped off, mostly unused. The bottle-green dimness is enough to move by once your eyes adjust, and a bright beam in turbulent, particle-filled water just creates a white wall in front of your face. The light is for signaling your partner and for emergencies.

**Water.** A hydration bladder with a line that feeds into your full-face mask, if your model supports it, or a way to drink during air-gaps or in the doorway. You'll be sweating inside your suit in blood-warm water while breathing dry compressed gas. You'll dehydrate faster than you'd guess, and you're surrounded by seawater you can't drink. Consider mild electrolyte solution rather than plain water.

**Anti-nausea medication.** Test it at home first. The combination of infrasound-level roar, shifting light, and vestibular chaos from the layers and the membrane will make many people sick. Vomiting into a full-face mask is survivable if you've practiced it, but avoid it if you can. Choose a non-drowsy option. You can't afford sedation here.

**What not to bring:** anything loose, anything you'd mind losing, and anything metal meant for cutting or striking stone. Clip or pocket everything. The upward rain lifts whatever isn't held down and carries it off, and whatever goes up doesn't come back.

## Arrival and departure

If your method of crossing lets you choose where you arrive, **arrive in or beside the doorway**: the two leaning slabs near the center of the Shelf. It's the one place where the water falls gently and evenly. It will be your base, your refuge, and your exit.

Your second choice is the cliff base, away from the open edge, near the reed stand if you can locate it. The cliffs give you something to hold, and the reeds' thin wail is one of the few sounds you can use to orient yourself.

**Never arrive near the open fourth side.** The drop into cloud is the single greatest danger on the Shelf. An upward layer near the edge doesn't need to lift you far. It only needs to lift you a little, and the next shear can carry you sideways into nothing.

Plan to leave from the doorway too. Give yourself a firm time limit before you arrive and keep it, however beautiful or strange things become. An hour is a lot. Thirty minutes of actual movement on the Shelf, with rests in the doorway, is a reasonable first visit.

## Moving on the Shelf

**Stay low.** Crawl or move in a deep crouch, with three points of contact on stone at all times. Standing upright makes you a sail in every layer.

**Read the layers by feel.** You'll learn fast that the column is not uniform. A hammering layer pushes you down and flat. A slow layer feels like drifting snow against your suit. A still layer is quiet, and you can press your mask into it and see clearly. The rising layer is the one to fear. You'll feel it first as a lightness around your ankles and knees, a tugging *upward* on your boots and the hem of your suit. **The moment you feel your feet lighten, get your hands on stone and make yourself heavy.** Don't wait to see whether it strengthens.

**Watch the ground.** Pebbles and glass flakes begin to stir before a strong upward surge arrives. When grit starts to drift, grab stone.

**Trust the boom before the fold.** This place has a quirk that works in your favor: the echo off the cliffs sometimes arrives *before* the collapse that caused it. You'll feel a boom in your chest a heartbeat before the water folds. Most visitors are unsettled by this. Use it instead. Treat any deep chest-boom as a warning and brace immediately. On the Shelf, effect-before-cause is your early-warning system.

**Watch the fish.** The long, transparent fish with the blue spinal line seems to know where the layers will shear before they shear. If you see it, watch where it goes and when. If it suddenly leaves a band of water you're in, leave too, or brace. It has survived here far longer than you will. Don't try to touch it or follow it into the column, though. It swims up into the rising layers to climb, and it's built for that. You aren't.

**Don't follow the bird either.** The web-footed hunter flies into the column from the cloud beyond the edge. It has somewhere to come from and somewhere to go back to that you can't reach. Its path leads toward the edge.

**The membrane.** At some point the silvery ceiling will descend and pass through you, cold and brief, and you'll find yourself looking *down* at the Shelf through a skin of water with your feet on the other side. This will be the most disorienting moment of your visit. Nothing physically changes. Your feet are still on stone. **Don't move while it's happening.** Freeze, keep your hands on the rock, exhale, and wait for your vestibular system to catch up. Remember that "which side of the sea you're on" is something the place does *to* you, not something you have to fix.

**Near the fissures.** The sulfur vents warm the rock pleasantly, but the water right at the fissure mouths may be hotter and more acidic than the rest. Don't put bare skin into a crack, and don't linger over one.

**The weed mats.** They are slick when flat and cling like hair when upright. Don't use them as handholds, since their root-threads are fine and won't hold you, and you'd tear them out. Step between them where you can.

**The low wall at the edge.** When the upward rain comes, the wall's stones stay down. In an emergency, if you're being carried toward the drop, that wall is the last thing between you and the cloud. Grab it. It will probably hold. But don't treat it as a railing for sightseeing, and don't lean over it to look into the cloud. There's nothing to see that justifies the risk.

## The doorway

Go there first. Rest there between every foray. Go there if anything goes wrong.

Inside the gap between the slabs, the water falls at one gentle, even speed, like ordinary rain. The roar is still enormous, but it comes from *outside*. For the first time you'll be able to think in complete sentences. Check your gas. Check your partner. Drink. Let your heart rate come down. Notice how much you were bracing without knowing it.

A few practical points:

- It's narrow. It may only fit one of you at a time. Agree in advance how you'll take turns.
- Don't wedge gear into the gap, lean heavily on the slabs, or try to make the space more comfortable. The slabs are leaned together, not fixed.
- The doorway is where you'll feel the purpose of this place most strongly. Many visitors will want to stay. Don't overstay your time limit because the doorway feels safe. It is safe, but your gas and your body's heat still run down in there.

## The heat and the salt

**Heat.** Every time you're in the doorway, ask yourself honestly: *Am I thinking clearly? Do I have a headache? Am I annoyed at my partner for no reason?* Those are early signs of overheating. The water is blood-warm, so your body can't dump heat into it. If you feel them, end the visit. Don't push through.

**Salt.** It's in your mask seals, your ears, and every seam of your suit. It will chafe wherever something rubs: under straps, at the wrists, the back of the neck. Apply an anti-chafe balm before you go in.

**Cuts.** You will almost certainly get small cuts from the glassy basalt and barnacle scars, even through gloves. Warm salt water from a living (or once-living) reef can carry bacteria that cause fast, nasty wound infections. Don't dismiss small cuts. Clean every one with fresh water and antiseptic immediately after the visit, and seek medical care if one becomes red, hot, swollen, or painful out of proportion within the next day or two. Tell the doctor you were exposed to warm seawater.

## The sound

Nothing fully prepares you for this, but it helps to know what it will do.

The roar is felt more than heard: in your ribs, your teeth, the long bones of your thighs. Low-frequency sound at this intensity can produce anxiety, dread, a sense of being watched, nausea, and pressure behind the eyes, all without any external cause. **Some of what you feel on the Shelf will be the sound acting on your body, not a real threat.** Knowing that helps. When dread rises, check your actual situation: hands on stone, gas OK, partner OK. If all is well, the dread is the note, not a warning.

When a still layer passes over your head, the roar becomes a thick throbbing hum, like standing inside a struck bell. Many people find this the most beautiful part of the visit. Let yourself have it.

The reeds' thin wail is a useful landmark. It's one of the few high sounds, so it's one of the few with direction. If you're lost on the Shelf, move toward it to find the cliff base.

Expect tinnitus for a day or two afterward even with earplugs. If it lasts longer than a week, see an audiologist.

## Conduct: how to behave in someone else's argument

Someone built here. They used no tools, cut no stone, and left no metal. They placed stones by hand, built a wall that keeps nothing in or out, set up a doorway where the sea is allowed to fall gently, and put one shell on each of a dozen cairns, facing up toward where the water comes from.

Whatever that was (an offering, a reminder, a plea), it's still in progress, and you aren't part of it.

- **Don't touch the cairns.** Don't straighten a shell, pick one up to look at it, or photograph one by moving it. If a surge threatens one while you're watching, leave it. The place and its builders have been managing that for a long time.
- **Don't take anything.** No shells, no pillow-basalt fragments, no weed, no reed segments. It's tempting to bring home a glass flake from a rock that was seafloor a few human lifetimes ago. Don't. Everything loose here is already being argued over.
- **Don't mark the rock.** No bolts, no scratched initials, no chalk, no cairns of your own. The builders chose to use no tools. Respect that choice even if it makes your anchoring harder.
