# Preparing for the Knot

## A note before the list

Most places you visit ask you to adapt to them. The Knot asks you to be absorbed into its routine. You cannot observe it from the outside, because there is no outside inside it: no light to watch by, no loud voice to ask questions with, no place to sit still for long. You will know the city only by joining the one thing everyone there does, which is keeping it open. If you go expecting to be a tourist, you will spend your time cold, disoriented, and crawling with small animals. If you go expecting to be a guest who works, you may come out with one of the strangest and most tender experiences a traveler can have.

Most of what follows is practical. Some of it is not.

---

## Part One: Should you go at all?

Answer these honestly before anything else.

**Can you hold your breath and swim blind through cold black water under a root mass?** The entrance is about three body-lengths underwater, beneath the hummock's edge, in fen water near freezing much of the year. It is not a long swim. It is a hard one. You will see nothing, the passage is made of root, and the cold will try to force a gasp out of you the moment you go under. If you have never done a cold-water breath-hold, you cannot yet do this one.

**How do you do in total darkness?** This is not the darkness of a bedroom at night, where your eyes eventually find something. It is complete. The only exception is a foxfire in the lowest rooms, which you will not see for days. Some people find this peaceful. Others find that the mind fills the empty visual field with phantom lights, faces, and movement, sometimes called "prisoner's cinema." That is normal and passes. Genuine panic in the dark is different, and the Knot is a very bad place to discover you have it.

**Can you tolerate being covered in invertebrates, continuously?** Not occasionally. All the time. Amphipods on the walls, springtails rising when you touch anything, mites as a moving grain under your fingertips, harvestmen dropping from the ceiling in clusters, cave crickets brushing you with antennae longer than your forearm, leeches in the water. A strong phobia of insects, spiders, or crawling sensations will not survive this. Don't go to cure one.

**Are you comfortable being touched by strangers?** The tenants speak mostly by touch: a hand flat on your back, pressed in rhythms. They sleep in heaps for warmth. If touch without warning distresses you, you will be distressed nearly all the time. That is a reasonable thing to be, and a good reason to stay home.

**Are you healthy?** This matters more than it usually does, for a grim reason. The tenants give the very old and the very ill, the ones who cannot move, to the root. Nobody will do that to a visitor lightly. But understand the structure of the place: there is no fire, no light, no medicine beyond what they know, and the only exit is a breath-hold swim. An injured person effectively cannot be evacuated. A broken ankle down there is not an inconvenience. It could change what you are to the city. Do not go with a condition that might flare, and do not go reckless.

If you have answered all of these and still want to go, read on.

---

## Part Two: When to go

Time in the Knot runs on cold and water, and your timing decides what kind of visit you have.

**Deep winter: don't, unless you intend to stay.** The fen freezes and the flooded entrance skins over with ice. The tenants are sealed in until the thaw. Arriving in early winter could mean you cannot leave for months. It is also the gentlest season inside: the cutting slows, the animals go still, everyone sleeps in warm heaps. If you go deliberately to winter over, you would see the Knot at its most intimate. But choose that with open eyes and with supplies.

**Spring thaw: the most dangerous time to arrive.** The water rises, the lowest tier floods, the eels come in by the thousand, and the root wakes hungry. Everyone cuts day and night because a passage can close in a week. This is when rooms are lost. You would be an untrained pair of hands and a new body to keep track of during an emergency. A strong, experienced visitor might be useful. A first-timer will be in the way.

**Summer: the easiest arrival.** The water falls, the bottom channels drain to mud, the tenants harvest mussels, snails, and water-plant roots. The root still grows but less desperately, so there is more time to teach you. The walls teem with new life, so expect more animals, not fewer.

**Late summer into autumn: recommended.** Arrive when the water is low and plan to stay through the autumn exodus, the three nights when the eels leave all at once through the flooded entrance. The tenants sit at the edge of the water in the dark and feel the current of the leaving pull at their ankles. If you can be there for that, be there. Leave well before the first hard freeze. Once the eels are gone, you have weeks at most, and you will not see the ice forming.

One practical warning: do not swim out *during* the eel exodus. The entrance will be full of eels heading for the fen.

## Part Three: Physical preparation

Start months ahead.

**Cold-water acclimatization.** Train regularly in cold water, gradually and with a partner, until the cold-shock gasp reflex is something you can ride through rather than obey. Learn to put your face into near-freezing water and stay calm. That reflex is the single most likely thing to kill you at the entrance.

**Breath-hold training.** With a qualified instructor and never alone, build a comfortable static breath-hold well beyond what the swim should need. Aim for a large margin. Three body-lengths should take only seconds, but blind, cold, and possibly snagged, seconds stretch. Practice swimming through confined spaces with your eyes closed, following a line by touch.

**Hand conditioning.** You will cut root daily with a sharpened mussel shell and chert scrapers, on wet wood, in the cold. Your hands will blister, split, and get small cuts that stay wet. Toughen them beforehand. Practice fine work, whittling, knot-tying, with cold hands and eyes closed.

**Dark practice.** Spend long stretches blindfolded in your own home, then in unfamiliar places with a guide. Learn to move with one hand on a wall, to feel draughts on your face, to smell rooms. Tie knots blindfolded. Untie them. Learn to tell different knots apart by feel. The Knot's ropes are its map, and you will be reading them.

**Quiet practice.** Learn to speak in a murmur that carries only a short distance. Practice suppressing coughs, sneezes, and startled cries, or at least muffling them into your elbow. Practice staying silent when something crawls into your collar. In the Knot, a scream is not just embarrassing. It makes the hiss of the city rise into a roar, drops harvestmen from the ceilings, and for a while nobody can feel anything but legs.

**General fitness.** The spiral is a long stair of wet, slightly spongy root. You will climb it, crouch in doorways, and wade waist-deep in water full of eels. Stamina matters more than strength.

---

## Part Four: Crossing the fen and finding the hummock

The fen is its own hazard before you ever reach the Knot.

- **Walk with a pole**, and probe ahead. Every step sinks a little before it stops, until one step doesn't stop. Bog pools can be deeper than they look, and sphagnum can float over water like a carpet.
- **Tell someone where you've gone** and when you should be back. Better, bring someone who will wait above. See Part Twelve for why.
- **Wear warm, windproof layers** for the crossing, and keep your swimming clothes separate and dry until you need them.
- **Finding the right hummock is genuinely hard.** It looks like a hundred others: knee-high, red and green sphagnum, a few stunted alders leaning off one shoulder. Some possible signs:
  - Water moving strangely at one edge, perhaps a faint draught or current where the flooded passage runs under.
  - Eels concentrated in the pools near it at the right times of year.
  - A tenant arriving or leaving. People do come and go. If you can watch patiently from a distance, you may see someone slip into the water and not come up.
  - Frost that forms differently on its cap. It is a hollow, breathing thing, and that may show in the morning.

The best approach, if at all possible, is to be guided by someone who has been. A tenant who has come up, a fen-dweller who knows, a traveler who returned. Arriving by accident at the wrong hummock and diving into an ordinary bog pool would be a stupid way to die.

---

## Part Five: The swim in

This is the threshold. Treat it as such.

- Stop on the hummock's edge. Get your breathing slow and even. Don't hyperventilate beforehand; it makes blackout more likely, not less.
- Your pack should be small, sealed, waterproof, and tethered to you so it cannot snag or drift. Anything loose is lost.
- **Tie back or braid your hair** tightly, or crop it before you go. Loose hair and root passages are a bad combination.
- Feel along the passage for any guide-line the tenants may keep. If there is one, follow it with your hand.
- Go under, face first. Pull yourself along the root with your hands rather than kicking wildly, because the passage is not smooth and you will not see its turns.
- **If you get stuck, do not thrash.** Back out slowly the way you came. You have more air than your panic tells you.
- You will come up gasping in total darkness, into still air. Stay where you surface. Hold onto something. Breathe. Do not call out loudly. Someone will likely have felt the change in the draught and will come.

What you will notice first is probably not the dark or the cold. It is the sense of being inside something alive and attentive: the walls sweating under your palms, the grain of tiny bodies moving over your fingers, and the air holding still around you and then, very slightly, beginning to lean toward your breath.

Then, somewhere up the spiral, you may hear a shell blade cutting root.

---

## Part Six: Staying warm without fire

This is the most important physical problem you will have.

The Knot is cold: never frozen, but chilled and saturated, every surface beading water. You arrive soaking wet. **There will never be a fire to dry you.** Fire would kill the root, and the root is the city. Do not bring matches, lighters, candles, a stove, tobacco, or anything that burns. Do not even ask.

Plan accordingly.

- **Wool, wool, wool.** Wool keeps some warmth when wet. Cotton becomes a cold compress and will make you ill. Close-fitting merino base layers and a thicker wool middle layer are the best.
- **Carry a full dry change in a sealed waterproof bag.** Get out of your swimming clothes immediately on arrival and into the dry ones. Your wet set will never truly dry, but it may become less wet over time, pressed into a heap of bodies.
- **Accept the heap.** The tenants share warmth. When they sleep, especially in the colder months, they sleep curled together. This is not intimacy in the way you might mean it. It is how warm-blooded creatures survive in wet cold. Join it when invited.
- **Keep moving.** Movement makes heat, and movement also keeps the root off you (see below).
- **Chemical hand warmers:** these make heat without flame, but the root grows toward warmth. Ask the tenants before using one. They may not want a concentrated heat source on you that the city will lean toward. Do not assume.
- **Watch yourself for hypothermia**: shivering that stops, clumsy hands, confusion, a strange calm. In the dark, no one can see your lips turn blue. Tell someone by touch, and learn how to say it before you need to.

## Part Seven: Sleep and the root

The root grows toward breath: warmth, moisture, carbon. It grows slowly enough that you cannot see it and fast enough that you can feel it. A doorway narrows by a finger's width in a month. A person who sleeps too long in one place wakes with rootlets in their clothes and hair, and very gently along the lines of their skin.

This is not an attack. It is what the root does. But you must live by the tenants' rules about it.

- **Sleep in shifts.** Someone should always be awake near you.
- **Sleep curled**, as they do.
- **Never sleep twice in a row in the same room.**
- **Pull rootlets away early.** When you wake, run your hands over your scalp, face, neck, wrists, ankles, anywhere clothing meets skin. Rootlets pulled early are harmless. Don't wait to see what happens if you leave them.
- **Carry a silent, vibrating timer** if it helps you respect the rhythm, but the tenants' touch will usually wake you. Let it.
- **Consider shorter hair and closer-fitting clothes.** Loose weave and long hair give the root more to find.
- **If you feel ill, tell someone at once.** Stillness is dangerous here, and illness is the likeliest cause of stillness.

You may find this deeply unsettling at first: waking to something soft threaded along your wrist, which was reaching toward you in the dark while you slept. Many visitors describe a strange tenderness about it later. You don't have to feel that. You just have to pull the rootlets away.

## Part Eight: The animals

The Knot is a clearing inside a swarm. You cannot avoid the animals. You can only learn to live alongside them.

**Amphipods, springtails, mites.** Harmless, and everywhere. They graze the film on the wet wood. You will feel them on your hands constantly. The mites will get into your clothing. Accept it. Bring a spare set of underclothes sealed away for leaving, and plan to wash thoroughly when you return. Consider an antihistamine if you react to mites.

**Harvestmen.** They hang from the ceilings in clusters the size of a head, thousands of legs interlaced, and shift when the air changes. They are not spiders and do not bite. If you startle the city with noise, they drop. They will land on you. Stay calm.

**Cave crickets.** Long antennae sweeping endlessly through the rooms. You will be touched by them a thousand times. Harmless.

**Leeches.** The tenants tolerate them and value them. They may have medical uses you don't know. When one attaches, **don't rip it off**. Let it finish and drop, or ask a tenant to help. Keep the wound clean. Leech bites bleed for a long time, and in the Knot you can't see how much.

**Shrews.** The chief predators of the upper tiers: tiny, fierce, constantly hunting. They are unlikely to bother you unless you corner one or put your hand on a nest. Some shrews have mildly venomous saliva, so a bite can be painful and swell. Clean any bite and watch it.

**Centipedes.** As long as a hand. They hunt young shrews. A bite hurts badly but is rarely dangerous to a healthy adult. Move your hands slowly into unfamiliar crevices. The tenants likely know where centipedes nest. Ask.

**Eels.** Black, slow, cold, and in season so thick that the water becomes more eel than water. You may wade among them to the waist. They part and close around you like thick cloth. They are not aggressive. The experience is overwhelming, but it is not dangerous in itself.

**Frogs in the walls.** Frozen almost solid in winter, hearts stopped. If you find one while cutting, **leave it where it is.** It will thaw in spring and walk out as if nothing happened.

**Dead moths** drift in the empty rooms. You will walk through them. They crunch softly.

A general rule: **move slowly and touch lightly.** Almost everything that could hurt you in the Knot is only dangerous if startled or pressed.

---

## Part Nine: Silence and touch

The city is quiet and teeming at once. There are no voices of any size, only a continuous faint hiss like snow falling on snow, which is the sound of a million small bodies on wet wood. Under that comes dripping water, the occasional needle-thin squeak of a shrew, and once in a long while a sound like a knuckle cracking, which is the root growing.

**Loud speech is a kind of violence here.** There is no rule against it. It simply sets the whole city moving. Speak in murmurs, close to someone's ear, and only when you must.

Most talk is touch: a hand flat on the back, pressed in rhythms. One rhythm means *follow*. Another means *the east stair is closing*. Before you go, if you have any way of learning a little of this language, learn it. Otherwise, learn it on arrival, and make these your first priorities:

1. *Follow*, and how to recognize it.
2. *Stop* or *wait*.
3. *Danger*, or *closing*.
4. *I am cold* or *I am unwell*.
5. *I don't understand.*

Your own gestures matter too. A hand grabbed suddenly in the dark is alarming anywhere. Be gentle. Be predictable. Let people feel your hand approaching through the air before it lands.

You will learn a great deal about attention here. You will learn to feel a person's mood through their palm on your shoulder, and to feel a room shrinking because it has stopped breathing the way it used to. That knowledge is some of what you will carry out.

---

## Part Ten: Finding your way

You cannot see, and the city changes shape. Your map is the ropes.

Every rope along the spiral is knotted in a code giving the quarter, the tier, and the distance to water. Ask the tenants to teach you. They may be glad to, since a visitor who can read the ropes is a visitor who won't get lost. Early on:

- **Never wander alone.** A passage you came through yesterday may be narrower today and gone by spring.
- **Keep one hand on a rope** or a wall whenever you're moving.
- **Learn the air.** Cold draughts come from the flooded entrance, and colder ones rise from the water at the bottom. A cut passage breathes differently from a closing one. This is your compass and your exit sign.
- **Learn the smells.**
  - Fresh cuts: green and bitter.
  - Old cuts: vinegar.
  - **Warm sweetness: something is wrong.** That smell means the root has begun to take something. If you smell it, tell someone and don't go further in alone.

The ropes are retied every spring because the city changes underneath them. The map is never finished, and neither is your learning of it.

---

## Part Eleven: The work

Everyone who can hold a shell blade walks the spiral every day and cuts. They cut rootlets across the doorways, clear the stairs, reopen rooms no one has lived in for generations, and pare the vaults. It is the only true labor of the Knot, and it is how you belong there.

**Expect to cut.** Offer to cut. This is the price of your welcome and the best way to understand the place.

- You'll be given, or taught to make, a sharpened mussel-shell blade or a chert scraper. Where a single root runs alone, it is soft enough to carve with a shell. Where two roots cross, it is as hard as horn. Learn the difference by feel, and cut along the ribs the root has made, not against them.
- **Don't ask to use the bronze adzes.** There are three, green-crusted and passed hand to hand along the spiral. They are the city's one precious thing. If one is offered to you, understand that it is an honor.
- **Protect your hands.** Small cuts on wet, cold hands in an environment full of invertebrates are an infection risk. Clean every wound with purified water and keep a small antiseptic kit sealed in your pack. Thin gloves may help with blisters but will blunt the sense of touch you depend on. Decide carefully, and perhaps use them only on your weaker hand.
- **Walk through the empty rooms.** The tenants believe the root holds back from complexity. They think the empty rooms are argument, part of the shape that keeps the whole hollow alive, and that every one of them has to be cut, named, and walked through even if no one ever sleeps there. You may not believe this. Walk through them anyway. The Knot has never closed. That is the only evidence, and it is not nothing.

There is something to be learned from cutting open a room so that no one will live in it, and doing it carefully, and doing it again next month. You don't have to put that lesson into words. Just do the work.

---

## Part Twelve: Food, water, and waste

**There is no fire, so nothing is cooked.** The tenants eat eels, mussels, snails, and the swollen roots of water plants. Their food is stored in birch-bark-lined pits.

- **Be careful with eel.** Raw eel blood is toxic to humans. Cooking normally destroys the toxin, and here there is no cooking. The tenants must have their own ways of preparing eel. Watch them, ask them, and don't improvise. If in doubt, don't.
- **Raw shellfish from still, warm-season mud carries real risk** of illness. Stomach illness here is dangerous, because it weakens and stills you. Eat what the tenants eat only in the way they prepare it, and in small amounts at first.
- **Bring your own food** for at least the first part of your stay, and ideally for all of it. Choose calorie-dense, low-odor, sealed foods. Smell is navigation here, and strong smells may disturb the swarm. Nuts, dense bars, dried meat in sealed pouches, and fats all work well. You'll need more calories than usual just to stay warm.
- **Offer to share**, as a courtesy, but understand that unfamiliar food may be refused.
- **Water:** the tenants carry it in bladders. Bring a filter and purification tablets. Do not bring a UV purifier, since it emits light. Drink regularly; cold suppresses thirst.
- **Waste:** ask, early and plainly, where and how. There will be a way, probably involving the water or a room the root is allowed to take. Pack out all your packaging. Leave nothing but footprints, and those will be cut away within the season.

---

## Part Thirteen: Light

You will be tempted to bring a light. Think hard about this.

The Knot has never been lit. The tenants live by touch, air, smell, and animals. A torch beam would:

- expose the city in a way no tenant has ever seen it, which is a kind of violation;
- disturb every animal on every wall at once;
- destroy your own dark-adaptation, and theirs, for the foxfire in the lowest rooms;
- tell everyone around you that you don't trust their way of knowing their home.

**Recommendation:** carry one small, dim, red emergency light, sealed deep in your pack. Tell no one it is there. Use it only in a true, life-threatening emergency. Better still, never use it at all. Consider not bringing it.

If you stay long enough, several days in the dark, ask to be taken to the old rooms at the bottom of the spiral, where the wood has been dead a long time. You may see the foxfire. It is not light so much as a thinning of the black, a place where the eye stops insisting. It is one of the few things in the Knot you cannot feel with your hands. Don't rush it. It only appears to people who have waited.

---

## Part Fourteen: The mind in the dark

Beyond the physical, there are a few psychological things to prepare for.

- **You will lose track of time.** There is no day and night. A tactile watch, one you read by touch, can help anchor you, if you want an anchor. Some visitors choose to let it go entirely. Decide which you are before you go.
- **You may see things that aren't there.** Colors, shapes, movement in the dark. This is your visual system talking to itself. It is not madness. It will ease.
- **You may feel watched.** Not by the tenants, but by the place. The root leans toward your breath. The walls respond to your touch. The hiss rises when you move. Most visitors feel this. Some find it frightening, others comforting, and many find it both.
- **You will feel the fragility of the place.** Everything is freshly cut and nearly gone. Nothing is old. There's a particular emotional note to living inside something that exists only because people keep refusing to let it close. It may make you think of your own life. Let it.
- **There is a long rope in the lowest occupied tier, heavy with knots.** Every knot is a room the root has taken. Tenants run it through their hands in the dark sometimes, the way people elsewhere might look at stars. If you are offered the chance, take it. Don't ask what each knot was called unless someone offers to tell you.
- **Language will be strange.** The tenants have a word that means both *home* and *the work of keeping home open*, and another that means both *room* and *reason*. You may find these words working their way into how you think. That is not a problem.

---

## Part Fifteen: Etiquette in brief

- Be quiet.
- Touch gently, and let people feel your hand before it lands.
- Cut every day you can.
- Walk through empty rooms.
- Don't bring fire.
- Don't bring light, or don't use it.
- Don't wear scent: no perfume, no scented soap, no deodorant. Smell is navigation.
- Don't claim a room.
- Don't argue with the idea that the empty rooms matter.
- Ask before bringing new tools. A steel knife would be a wonder here and possibly a disruption. If you want to give one as a gift, raise it with the tenants and accept it if they decline.
- If you are shown the rope of lost rooms, handle it as you would something sacred.
- Leave frogs in the walls.
- If a withy screen crosses a door in a lower room, do not go in. That room is not waiting to be reopened. Not yet, and not by you.

## Part Sixteen: What to bring

**Wear, or carry sealed:**
- Merino wool base layers, top and bottom (two sets)
- Wool mid-layer
- Close-fitting wool hat, or none, with hair cropped or braided
- Wool socks (several pairs)
- Thin soft-soled shoes or wading socks that grip wet root; nothing clumsy
- A full dry change of clothes in a waterproof dry bag
- A separate sealed set of clean clothes for leaving

**Pack (small, sealed, tethered):**
- Calorie-dense, low-odor food in sealed packaging
- Water filter and purification tablets
- A small bladder or soft bottle
- Antiseptic, bandages, and tape, sealed
- Antihistamines
- Any personal medication, double-sealed
- A tactile watch, if you want one
- A silent vibrating timer, optional
- One dim red emergency light, deep in the pack, optional and ideally unused
- Gifts: small and useful, and ask before giving. Good cord, a few pieces of good chert, birch bark, or simply your labor.

**Leave behind:**
- Anything that burns
- Tobacco
- Headlamps, torches, phones with screens or cameras. There's nothing to photograph, and a lit screen is a light.
- Scented products
- Cotton clothing
- Loose, flowing, trailing things
- Anything you cannot afford to lose in black water

**Above ground, cached near the hummock or held by a companion:**
- Dry warm clothes
- Insulated blanket and windproof shell
- A warm drink in a flask
- Sunglasses
- High-calorie food

## Part Seventeen: Leaving

Leaving is its own challenge.

- **Say goodbye by touch.** Ask a tenant how. There may be a way.
- **Have a tenant take you to the entrance.** Find it by the draught.
- Change into your swimming clothes. Seal your pack. Breathe slowly.
- **Swim out through the black water.** It may feel harder going this way. You are leaving something, and the cold is waiting on the other side.
- **You will surface into light.** Even the grey light of a winter fen will be blinding after days in the dark. Keep your eyes closed at first and open them slowly. Put on sunglasses.
- **The world will be deafening.** Wind, birds, your own feet squelching. After the hiss, all of it will sound like shouting. Give yourself time.
- **Warm up immediately and carefully.** Get out of wet clothes and into your cache. Beware "afterdrop," where your core temperature keeps falling after you leave the water as cold blood returns from your limbs. Move gently, drink something warm, and don't walk the fen alone until you've stopped shivering and can think clearly.
- **Wash thoroughly** when you get somewhere you can. Launder or freeze your clothes to deal with the mites.

---

## Part Eighteen: Afterward

Some visitors report that for weeks after returning, they sleep lightly and wake to run their hands over their wrists and scalp, checking for rootlets. Some find they can no longer stand noisy rooms. Some find themselves thinking of the work they do, whatever it is, as cutting: the daily, unfinished, unglamorous effort of keeping something open that would otherwise quietly close.

Some think about the empty rooms and the tenants' claim that the shape itself is what holds the root back, and that a single room would close in a year while a city stays open. They wonder whether that holds for other things.

And some, not many, find that they cannot stop hearing, somewhere at the edge of their attention, the soft patient sound of a shell blade on root. They go back. They find the swim, and they do not come up again.

Prepare for that too. Decide before you go which kind of visitor you intend to be. Then be gentle with yourself if, once you are there in the dark with the walls breathing against your palms, you find you were wrong.
