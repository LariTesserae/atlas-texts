# Preparing to Visit the Seam

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

The Seam is not a destination in the tourist sense. It is a working industrial site built inside an active geological wound, and people happen to live there. Everything about it is about maintenance: of the machines, of the space, and of the people's own continued ability to exist in it. You will be a guest inside a machine that is also a town that is also a slow-motion collapse.

The single most useful mental adjustment is this: **in the Seam, you are one more thing that has to be managed.** You take up room in a place that only ever gets smaller. You make sound in a place where sound means alarm. You carry objects into a place where objects left alone get swallowed by stone. Your goal is to be as little burden as possible while you're there, and to leave nothing behind.

---

## Physical Preparation (Weeks Before)

### Heat acclimatization
This is the most important thing you can do, and it has to start early. The Seam's heat is humid heat. Your skin goes slick within a minute and the sweat doesn't evaporate, so it doesn't cool you. Your core temperature will climb steadily whether you're working or not.

- Spend ten to fourteen days before your trip gradually exposing yourself to hot, humid conditions for an hour or two a day: saunas, steam rooms, or a hot climate if you can get to one. Your body will learn to sweat earlier, lose less salt, and hold more blood volume. It genuinely helps.
- Get comfortable with the feeling of being *unrelievedly* warm. There is no cool corner in the Seam, no breeze, and no shade, because shade doesn't matter when the heat comes from the air and the metal and the rock itself.
- If you have heart conditions, blood pressure problems, or take medications that affect sweating or fluid balance (diuretics, some antihistamines, some antidepressants), talk to a doctor who is willing to take the question seriously.

### Claustrophobia rehearsal
You will never be able to stretch both arms out without touching something. The sky, where you can see it, is a ragged ribbon of darkness nearly closed over. Visibility dissolves into amber murk past twenty paces. If you have any tendency toward claustrophobia, this place will find it.

- Practice spending long periods in tight, dim spaces: a closet, a crawlspace, a small room with the lights low.
- Learn a slow breathing pattern you can fall back on. As it happens, the engines will give you one: an eleven-second cycle, sigh down and exhale up. Many visitors find themselves breathing to it without deciding to.

### Hand and grip strength
You'll be climbing stairs and ladders, moving along grated walkways, and gripping railings (with gloves, always) that are slick with condensation. Forty iron stairs to get in, forty to get out, and six or seven levels in between. Be fit enough to climb steadily while overheated.

## Air: Take This Seriously

The air smells of struck flint, wet slate, and "faintly of eggs." That egg smell is almost certainly hydrogen sulphide, or something behaving like it.

At low concentrations it's merely unpleasant. At higher concentrations it deadens your sense of smell, so the danger stops announcing itself right as it becomes dangerous. Beyond that it causes headaches, eye irritation, nausea, and worse.

Then there is the absence. **No animal life comes down the forty stairs.** No rats, though the town has everything rats want. No insects at the lamps. Dogs refuse the last flight. Birds turn away above the quarry lip.

Maybe it's only the heat. But animals are often better gas detectors than we are, and dogs carry their noses low, where heavy gases pool. The residents have clearly adapted to whatever it is, possibly over years, possibly at some cost they don't discuss. You haven't adapted.

**Bring:**
- **A personal multi-gas monitor** that reads hydrogen sulphide, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and oxygen, with an audible alarm and a vibrate alert. Set it to vibrate, since a shrill alarm in the Seam will panic everyone around you (see "Sound"). Clip it near your collar, in your breathing zone.
- **A half-face respirator** with combination cartridges: P100 particulate filters (for reasons given below) plus an acid-gas cartridge. Carry spares. Heat and humidity shorten cartridge life.
- **A plan for when the monitor alerts:** go up, not down, calmly and in rhythm with foot traffic. Know the route to the forty stairs from wherever you are at all times.

If you start getting headaches, sore or streaming eyes, or a strange indifference to the smell, leave. Don't wait to see if it passes.

### The lagging
The pipes are "lagged in cracked asbestos board." Cracked. In a place with constant vibration from three beam engines and constant chipping and cutting work. There will be fibers in the air, and more near any crew doing work on the pipes.

- Wear your P100 respirator whenever you're near pipe bundles, near active crew work, or on lower levels.
- Don't brush against the lagging. Don't touch it. Don't sit near where it's crumbling.
- Plan to **bag your outer clothing** when you leave, and wash it separately or discard it (more under "Leaving").

## Clothing

You need to be protected from sharp rock, hot metal, and constant drip, while wearing as little as possible because of the heat. These goals conflict, so accept some discomfort.

**Body:** Long sleeves and long trousers in a tough, tightly woven, *natural* fiber: heavy cotton canvas, linen-cotton blends, or wool (lightweight wool handles damp heat surprisingly well). The basalt and glass edges "cut a sleeve," so better the sleeve than your arm. Avoid synthetics. Some will soften or melt against hot brass, and all of them hold stink and bacteria in that endless damp. Choose light colors so you can see mineral buildup, and loose cuts so air moves at all.

**Hands:** Gloves with **leather palms** are how the locals do it and how you should too. Every piece of iron is warm and brass is hot. Bring two or three pairs, because they will get damp, stiff with mineral salts, and eventually crusted. Rotate and dry them.

**Feet:** Sturdy boots with **heat-resistant soles** and excellent grip on wet metal grating. Everything is filmed with condensation. Ankle support matters on stairs and ladders. Steel toes are worth considering in a place full of hanging chains and heavy iron. Bring **many pairs of socks**. Your feet will be wet with sweat constantly, and trench-foot-style problems and fungal infections are real risks over a stay of more than a day or two.

**Head:** A close-fitting cap or hard hat. The walkways are stacked, pipes loop overhead, chains hang, and you'll be ducking constantly without reliable depth perception in the amber haze. A hard hat also keeps the steady mineral drip off your scalp.

**Eyes:** Clear or lightly amber-tinted safety glasses with anti-fog coating (bring a cloth and anti-fog solution anyway). Drips fall from above, chisels throw stone chips, and sulphur gases irritate. Don't bring dark lenses; there's hardly any light.

**Underneath:** Moisture-wicking natural-fiber underlayers, changed often. Bring **anti-chafe balm and a drying powder** (plain cornstarch or zinc oxide). Heat rash (prickly heat) is almost guaranteed on a longer stay.

## Hydration and Food

- **Water:** Assume none of the Seam's water is drinkable. The condensation is full of dissolved stone and the brine is "oily between the fingers." Bring your own, and much more than you think you need. In sustained humid heat you can lose over a liter an hour. Ask your hosts what they drink and how they source it. It's a meaningful question in a place like this, and they'll respect it.
- **Electrolytes:** Oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets, taken regularly. Pure water without salt replacement will eventually make you sick in a different way.
- **Food:** Simple, salty, calorie-dense, and sealed: tinned, foil-packed, or waxed. Nothing that spoils quickly in heat.
- **Seam-salt:** Do not taste it, whatever anyone says. It's "not really salt," it's mostly sulphates, and it contains "something else the assay offices have never agreed on." Sulphates alone are purgatives. The unknown fraction is unknown.

## Your Belongings: The Sinter Problem

This is where the Seam differs from any ordinary hot, dirty place. **Anything left still will be claimed by stone.** A railing gets a knuckle of sinter within a season, doors seal, and bolt threads fill. On a short visit you won't see whole objects entombed, but you will see a white film building up on everything, zippers stiffening, and buckles gritting.

**The Seam's first rule is also yours: if you want to keep a thing, touch it, turn it, use it.**

- **Paper curls here. Books are kept in tins.** Bring notebooks, maps, and documents in sealed metal tins or heavy zip bags. Write in pencil, since ink runs in the damp.
- **Electronics:** Sealed waterproof cases, conformally coated if you can arrange it. Condensation will form inside anything that moves between temperatures. Bring desiccant packs and assume they'll saturate quickly. Keep devices in cases except when in use.
- **Cameras:** Your lens will fog instantly on arrival and whenever you move between the red-lit lower levels and the cooler upper ones. Let the camera acclimate in its bag before opening it. Flash photography is inadvisable anyway (see "Light").
- **Zippers, buckles, clasps:** Work them every day. Bring a small brush or old toothbrush to clear mineral grit. Prefer buttons, ties, and toggles over fine mechanisms.
- **Nothing hangs, nothing rests.** Don't hang your jacket on a railing. Don't set your pack down on a walkway and wander off. Beyond the sinter, you'd be adding clutter to a space that has none to spare, and you might be setting something down on hot metal.
- **Glasses and jewelry:** Metal rings and watches get hot against pipes. Consider leaving them behind.

## Arrival

You'll find the entrance at the bottom of a quarry pit: a lip of black rock, iron railing, a signal lamp gone amber with mineral scale. It looks like nothing. Take a moment at the lip.

Before you descend:
- Put on your gloves.
- Turn on your gas monitor and confirm it's reading.
- Have your respirator ready, hanging at your neck or clipped on.
- Take off any outer layer you can spare and pack it. You won't want it below.
- Drink water.

Then go down the forty iron stairs slowly, gloved hand on the rail. Around the time you pass the last daylight, the air will "close over you like bathwater." That's the moment to stop for a few breaths and let your body register what's happening. The heat, the humidity, and the smell hit all at once. Dizziness, a sense of pressure, or a pulse of panic are common. Stand still, breathe, and let it settle.

Notice, if you can, how the last flight feels. That's where the dogs stop. Some visitors say they feel nothing. Others describe a faint, wordless reluctance, as if their bodies know something. Pay attention to yours.

## Moving Through the Seam

### Keep time with the engines
This is the most practical rule of all. The three beam engines nod once every eleven seconds, and the whole town moves in their rhythm: gantries, chains, pumps, people. Someone out of rhythm is someone about to be caught by a swinging chain or a lurching gantry.

- Spend your first quarter-hour standing somewhere safe and **just listening**: the long sigh of the downstroke, the iron exhale of the return. Feel it through your boots.
- Watch how people move. They step, reach, and pass each other on the beat. Copy them.
- When crossing near machinery, wait for the beat that locals use. If unsure, wait and follow someone.

### Never lean on anything
It's hot, it's crusted with sinter, it may be sharp, or it may be lagged in asbestos. Stand on your own two feet. Grip railings with gloves only when needed.

### Watch the edges
The rock is freshly fractured, basalt and volcanic glass with conchoidal ripples, sharp as knapped flint. Keep your elbows in. Don't trail your fingers along walls, however tempting the frozen ripples look.

### Don't touch the copper lines
The narrow copper lines "carry nothing anyone can name anymore." No one removes them because no one is sure. Take the hint and give them the same respect you'd give a live wire.

### Mind the heat gradient
It gets hotter as you descend. The lowest level has the furnace mouths, where steam is superheated for the engines. Don't go down there without a guide and a reason. Spend most of your time on upper levels, and step back up to cooler air whenever you feel your head going thick.

## Light

The Seam is lit by rationed, caged sodium lamps whose orange light is diffused into haloed globes, by red furnace glow coming up through the grating, and by the green shimmer of glass veins in the walls.

- **Bring a dim headlamp with a red-light mode** for emergencies and close work. Don't flood the walkways with white LED light. Lamps are rationed here for a reason, light has meaning (the ferns grow toward it), and blazing brightness would feel as intrusive as shouting. Ask your hosts whether and when personal lights are acceptable.
- **Your eyes will need time to adjust.** Sodium light is monochromatic, so colors will look strange and flattened, and everything will seem amber, rust, or black. Skin looks jaundiced, blood looks black. Know that going in so it doesn't unsettle you.
- **Shadows fall upward** as often as down, because of the red light from below. Your depth perception will be off. Move more slowly than feels necessary.
- **The glass veins:** You will probably find yourself standing in front of one at some point, watching it glow green with light that "isn't really" coming from inside. Everyone does it. It's fine, and it may be the most beautiful thing you see there. Just be aware of your surroundings while you look. Don't stand in a walkway, and keep track of the engine beat.

## Sound and Speech

This is the social rule you most need to internalize before you arrive.

**Speak low. Shouting is not rude; it is alarming.** In the Seam, the only reason to raise your voice is a burst line or a jammed jack. If you shout, even with laughter or surprise or to call a companion, people will stop what they're doing and look for the emergency.

- **Practice speaking softly before you go.** Seriously. Many people raise their voices unconsciously when surprised, when not heard, or when excited. Rehearse catching yourself.
- **Agree on hand signals** with any travel companions for "come here," "stop," "I'm unwell," and "go up."
- **Silence your devices entirely.** No ringtones, no notification chimes. Set your gas monitor to vibrate.
- **The deep crack:** Once in a great while you'll hear a single low grinding crack from deep in the rock. That's the walls closing another fraction of their yearly thumbnail. **Do what the locals do: go still.** Don't run and don't shout. Wait. The drip resumes, the engine takes its next stroke, and someone writes the time in a ledger. Then carry on. It will frighten you, and that's normal. It won't hurt you.

The Seam's sound has been described as "a library built inside a boiler." Once you've adjusted, that dense quiet is oddly soothing: drips ringing different notes on iron, stone, and fern leaves, pipes ticking, a valve hissing somewhere, the dry chink of a chisel. Let yourself hear it.

## The Silence of Absent Life

You may not notice what's missing until your first sleep-shift. Then you'll lie awake and realize there's no scratching, no fluttering, no whine of a mosquito, no mouse in the walls. Only drips, pipes, and the engines.

For some people this is peaceful. For many it's unsettling in a way that's hard to name, like a room where someone has just stopped talking. Prepare for it:
- Bring earplugs if you want them, or conversely a small familiar sound (quiet music, recorded at very low volume, used privately and discreetly).
- Remind yourself that it's a known feature of the place, not a sign that something is wrong right now. Keep your gas monitor on while you sleep regardless.

## Etiquette and Being a Good Guest

### You are taking up room
The town can only get smaller. Nothing new is ever built. Every body is a body in a shrinking space.
- **Travel light.** One pack, compact, nothing dangling.
- **Don't linger in walkways.** Step into alcoves to let crews pass.
- **Stay only as long as you have reason to.**

### Gifts
Anything bulky or decorative is a burden in a town that has to cut its own rooms down every autumn. The best gifts are **consumed or used**, because use is what keeps things alive here:
- Good tea (people pour tea on the downstroke)
- Leather work gloves
- High-quality cold chisels, files, or stiff brushes (the town spends its life chipping itself free)
- Tinned delicacies
- Lamp parts or supplies, if you know what they use (ask first)

Don't bring anything that would need a shelf.

### Offer help, but don't insist
The crews do two kinds of work: cutting the town narrower as the walls close, and chipping it free as stone grows over it. If invited to help chip a bolt free or scrape a pan, do so, in rhythm and carefully, and treat it as a privilege. Don't take up tools uninvited.

### Listen to the old residents
They "describe rooms that no longer exist." If someone tells you about a workshop that used to be wider, a gallery that was cut away, or a door that sealed, listen. This is how the Seam remembers itself. Don't treat it as quaint. It's grief and history and craft all at once.

### The ledger
Someone notes the time of each crack. You might ask about the ledgers if the moment is right. Don't touch them.

### Don't pick the ferns or scrape the algae
They're the only green things in the town, "clinging to the edges of the human systems." The ferns map every lamp in the dark. The copper-green film greys when the engines stop and returns when they restart, "as if it had been waiting." People are attached to them, whether or not they say so.

## Health Watch: Signs to Leave

Go up the forty stairs, calmly, if you notice any of these:
- Headache that doesn't ease with water and rest
- Nausea, confusion, clumsiness, slurred thoughts
- Burning or streaming eyes, or cough
- **Stopping sweating** while still hot (a heat stroke warning sign)
- Racing heart at rest
- Your gas monitor alerting
- A sudden sense that you *need* to leave. In a place where dogs refuse the stairs, trust that.

Tell someone you're going. In the Seam a person disappearing without word is worrying.
