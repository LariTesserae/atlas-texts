# Preparing to Visit the Blister

## First, the honest question: can you get in?

Before gear, training or vaccinations, settle one thing. **The only entrance is a split "no wider than a forearm."**

If that means the thickness of a forearm, about 8–10 cm, no human body will pass, and your visit is an exterior one. You can put your face to the crack, feel the hot breath, hear the roar, and watch the evening flight. That is still extraordinary, and you should prepare for it properly.

If it means something closer to a forearm's length, about 25–30 cm, a slim adult might squeeze through sideways, exhaling, with nothing bulky on their body. This is the scenario the rest of this guide addresses. Even then, understand what you'd be doing:

- You'd be forcing yourself through a crack of **unweathered volcanic glass**, whose edges are described as sharp as obsidian blades.
- You'd be entering a chamber at 45–60°C, full of ammonia and volcanic gas.
- The way out is the same squeeze, done while hot, dehydrated, possibly panicked, and wearing a respirator.
- While you're in the split, **you are plugging the only exit and entrance for a quarter to half a million animals.**

Getting stuck in the split is the single most likely way to die here. Test the fit in daylight, fully equipped, before you commit. If there's any doubt, the answer is no.

If you arrive by multiverse transit, **arrive outside, on the flank, never inside the chamber.** Materializing into a dark, 45°C room packed with bats, over a metre of loose guano, with poorly known air, is a bad start.

## The mountain before the room

People fixate on the chamber and forget they first have to reach 4,100 m on a young volcano.

**Altitude.** 4,100 m is high enough for acute mountain sickness, and it will compound everything else. The heat inside will raise your heart rate, the respirator will increase breathing resistance, and the thin air will already be taxing you. Acclimatize for several days at progressively higher camps. Know the signs of high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema, and treat descent as the cure.

**The flow itself.** The lava tongue is 41 years old and not finished cooling. The Blister proves the flow contains sealed voids, and there may be others without an open seam. Crusts over hollows, loose clinker, and sharp glassy rock make it bad terrain. Scout a route in daylight, mark it, and use only that route.

**Weather.** The summit freezes most nights, and the wind is constant and carries sulphur and pumice dust. You'll be moving between well below freezing and a desert afternoon within a few steps. Bring:
- A full cold-weather outer system you can take off quickly at the entrance and weigh down against the wind.
- Goggles for the pumice grit on the approach.
- A small windproof shelter near the split to change in and to recover in afterward.

**The volcano.** It is active enough to still be feeding gas through the rock. Before going, check whatever monitoring exists: seismicity, gas flux, recent changes. The sulphur and geothermal works on the other side of the ridge are your best local resource. They'll have a road, staff who know the mountain, gas detectors, first aid, and possibly a view of the bat column. Contact them if your world allows it. They are also the nearest rescue.

## When to go in

This choice matters most, for your safety and for the colony.

**Don't enter by day.** The colony is home, the chamber is at its hottest, and you'd be in the split as the pre-dusk surge crawls toward the exit.

**Don't enter at dusk.** An hour-long river of bodies pours through the only opening. Being in its path is dangerous for you and harmful to them.

**Don't enter in the pre-dawn hours.** The adults are returning, and blocking the split then leaves exhausted, full-bellied mothers circling outside in freezing air while their pups call.

**The window is the middle of the night:**
1. Wait until the outflow has completely finished, a good hour and a half after dusk.
2. Watch the split for ten more minutes to make sure stragglers are gone.
3. Enter.
4. Be out well before the return begins, with a margin of at least an hour or two.

At night the chamber is at its coolest, emptiest and quietest. That still means around 44°C, many thousands of pups, and considerable noise.

**Seasonally,** the peak weeks after birth, when numbers approach half a million and the crèche is densest, are the worst possible time. Pups that fall are lost, and the dermestids on the floor are proof of how many already do. A disturbance that makes the crèche shift or startle will kill young bats. If you can, go early in the season before pupping, or late when the young can fly. Better still, recognize that the most respectful visit may be the exterior one.

In many worlds, entering a maternity roost like this would be illegal. Find out.

## The air

The hazards stack. The ammonia you'll smell immediately. You may not detect the others at all.

- **Ammonia** from the guano, strong enough to burn the eyes.
- **Volcanic gases** seeping from the fissure: sulphur dioxide, hydrogen sulphide, and possibly carbon dioxide. The dangerous ones are heavier than air and will collect exactly where you'd stand, in the bowl of the floor near the hot fissure. The bats are fine because they live in the dome, not at the bottom.
- **Guano dust**, kept airborne by wingbeats: pulverized chitin, mites, bat hair, and whatever microbes survive desiccation. The dryness probably suppresses the fungi that make damp caves notorious for histoplasmosis, but don't bet your lungs on it.
- **Arsenic.** The orange realgar in the hot corners is arsenic sulphide, and light slowly turns it to powder. That powder is in the dust.
- **Sulphur dust**, which is combustible. Combined with the chamber's strong static, that's a reason to bring no flames and nothing that sparks.

**What to bring:**
- A **full-face respirator**, which protects your eyes as well as your lungs, with **combination cartridges** rated for ammonia/methylamine, acid gases (SO₂, H₂S), and P100 particulates. Put in fresh cartridges for the visit. Ammonia at these levels exhausts them faster than you'd expect.
- Test the fit before the trip, and practice working hard in it in heat. It will feel suffocating at altitude.
- A **multi-gas monitor** for O₂, H₂S, SO₂, NH₃ and ideally CO₂, clipped low, near your knee, not at your collar. The low pocket is where the danger is. If the oxygen reading drops or the CO₂ climbs, leave at once. Cartridges filter contaminants. They do not add oxygen.
- A **one-way valve for drinking through the mask**, if your model supports it.

A supplied-air system would be safer, but it won't fit through the split. You are accepting a real respiratory risk. Keep the visit short.

## The heat and the dryness

44°C in the dome, several degrees warmer when the colony is home, and about 60°C at the rock near the fissure. Humidity near zero.

Very dry heat is deceptive. Sweat evaporates before it beads, so you won't feel soaked, and you won't notice how much water you're losing. At the same time, the respirator and protective clothing will trap heat against your body. Plan your stay in **minutes, not hours.** Twenty to thirty minutes is a sensible first limit. Stay in contact with someone outside, and let them call time.

**Before entering:**
- Drink steadily for hours beforehand, with electrolytes.
- Drink again right at the split.

**Inside:**
- Carry a small sealed water bladder if your mask allows drinking.
- Put heavy lip balm on beforehand. Lips crack within the hour.
- Treat these as signals to leave immediately: headache, nausea, confusion, clumsiness, stopping sweating, or a racing heart that won't settle.

**Afterward:** the split dumps you straight into freezing wind while you're hot and possibly damp at the skin. Get into the shelter and insulation quickly. Treat the transition itself as a hazard, because the combination of heat collapse and hypothermia is real.

**Electronics and materials:** batteries and some plastics dislike 60°C. Keep devices close to your body rather than setting them on hot rock. Anything soft and synthetic is a static generator, and paper will curl.

## Clothing and contact

Everything in the Blister either cuts, burns, stings, bites or crawls.

- **Coverall.** Choose one that's full-coverage, tightly closed at wrists, ankles and neck, and not highly static-generating. Standard disposable polyethylene suits build static fiercely. Cotton-rich or anti-static fabric is better here. It will be ruined, so plan to bag and discard it.
- **Gloves** in two layers: cut-resistant inner gloves for the glass, and leather outer gloves for heat. Never brace yourself on the dome or walls with a bare hand.
- **Boots** with thick soles, since the floor is warm and the fissure edge is hot, and with tops sealed by **gaiters** or taped cuffs. The guano holds dermestids, roaches, mites, pseudoscorpions and solifugids. The solifugids are fast, alarming and essentially harmless, but you don't want any of them up your trouser leg.
- **Hood** sealed to the respirator. Things will fall from the ceiling: guano, dust, mites, and occasionally a pup.

**Where to walk:**
- **Stay off the central mound.** It's over a metre of dry, crunching, uncompacted guano over irregular lava lobes. You'll sink, kick up clouds of dust, and bury your gas monitor in the worst air in the room. Worse, you'd be trampling the floor's whole living population. Keep to the thin margins where the basalt rises.
- **Don't break the sulphur needles.** They're growing at a millimetre a decade, and a careless boot destroys a century.
- **Never approach the fissure.** It's the hottest point, the gas source, and it sits directly under the crèche.

**Medical preparation:**
- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination** is essential. If a bat touches bare skin, bites, or scratches you, or you wake to find one on you, treat it as an exposure and get post-exposure care.
- Tetanus should be current.
- Carry a kit that will close deep, clean cuts from volcanic glass. They're likely.
- Afterward, check yourself for mites and hitchhikers.

## Light

The instinct is to flood the room with a bright torch. Don't.

Bright white light will startle the crèche, adding to the pup fall, and it will begin, invisibly, destroying the realgar. It also shows you everything at once: the seething ceiling, the glittering eyes, the crawling shimmer on every surface. It is genuinely hard on the mind.

**Recommended instead:**
- A **dim red headlamp** at its lowest setting, for your own footing.
- A **low-power infrared illuminator with night vision** if you want to see the dome. It's gentler on the colony, and it gives you some visual distance from the mass.
- A **thermal camera** will be close to useless. The rock, the air and the bats are all near body temperature.

**Before you go in,** stand at the split and look at the single blade of daylight dying on the violet glass. That, and the darkness beyond it, is the room as it actually is. Consider a stretch of time inside with no light at all, just listening.

Static is real. In full darkness, sweeping a gloved hand may produce a faint blue crackle. Enjoy it, but keep sparks away from sulphur dust.

## Sound, and your mind

You will be inside three layers of noise.

1. **The colony.** A constant high chittering like frying fat, loudest in the late afternoon.
2. **Ultrasound** you can't hear but will feel: a pressure, an ache in the ears, a low unease. It overloads recorders, so bring equipment rated for high sound pressure if you want to capture it.
3. **The industry**, arriving through four kilometres of unfractured rock. The once-per-second compressor throb, the rising moan of a drill rig starting up, and every few hours the deep exhalation of a pressure-relief vent, felt in the chest.

**Earplugs** will blunt the audible layer. They won't do much about the ultrasonic or the infrasonic.

**Prepare mentally.** Even at night, the Blister combines:
- extreme heat,
- a respirator's claustrophobia,
- total darkness,
- a ceiling of living bodies,
- a floor that crawls,
- sensory overload from every direction,
- and an exit narrow enough that you have to think about it.

If you have claustrophobia, entomophobia, or a history of panic attacks, be very honest with yourself. Panic here can kill you, both in the split and at the gas-heavy bottom of the bowl. Rehearse the exit in your head and in practice, and know exactly where the split is at every moment. A faint line of starlight or cold air is your guide.

**Know what to watch for.** When the generators change shift, the throb cuts out, and the whole colony falls briefly silent. That silence can be unnerving if you don't expect it. When it comes, stand still and listen.

## Team and communication

**Never go alone.** One person enters, and one stays outside at the split in full cold-weather gear, timing the visit, watching the gas readout if you can relay it, and watching the sky and the hour.

Radio may be unreliable through the rock and the split. Agree on simple signals: a light tug line or a sequence of light flashes.

**If you run a line,** keep it thin and at one edge of the split, and **pull it out when you leave.** Nothing should remain in the entrance.

**Agree on a hard turnaround time**, well before any possible pre-dawn return, and keep to it.

---

## Leaving

**The exit squeeze is harder than the entry.** You'll be hot, dry and tired. Go slowly, protect your hands and face from the glass edges, and exhale to pass.

**Decontaminate in the shelter:**
1. Bag the coverall and outer gloves.
2. Wipe down your mask and gear.
3. Wash your face and hands before eating or drinking.
4. Change into dry, warm layers immediately.

**Take nothing out:** no crystals, no guano, no realgar. The realgar is arsenic, and everything else is either alive or growing too slowly to replace.

**Rehydrate and warm up** before descending. If you develop a fever, cough, or breathing trouble in the following weeks, tell a doctor you were in a bat roost.

---

## The visit worth making even if you never go in

Arrive on the flank before dusk. Settle down upwind of the split, dressed for the freezing wind, far enough back that you're not in the flight path.

Feel the mountain breathing hot air out of a crack in the rock. Then, over an hour, hear the roar rise inside the stone, and watch the first bats drop out. The flow thickens into a river, then a twisting dark column climbing off the black tongue of lava into the cold, visible to the valley below and to the night shift at the sulphur works across the ridge.

Stay a while after it passes. Put your ear near the rock and listen for the compressors. Somewhere inside, in a room no water has ever touched, thousands of pups are calling, a few more atoms of sulphur are settling onto a crystal, and the rock is ticking as it loses half a degree.

For most people, this is the right way to meet the Blister. It is the way the Blister survives the meeting best.
