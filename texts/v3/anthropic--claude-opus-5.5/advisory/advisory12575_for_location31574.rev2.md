# Preparing for Hesk

## A Traveler's Briefing

Hesk will probably not kill you through violence. Its dangers are attrition, disorientation, and a slow loss of certainty about where and when you are. Most of what hurts people here happens gradually: they run short of water, misjudge a step in the backward light, walk one more hour down an avenue that doesn't end, or stand too long at a terrace edge on an evening when the sky has a floor.

The good news is that Hesk is quiet, slow, and mostly indifferent to you. Nothing here is in a hurry. You shouldn't be either.

---

## Part One: Your Body

### Altitude is the first and largest problem

The description of the air as "pressed between two sheets of glass" tells you this is extreme altitude, well above the cloud tops and above where anything grows. Treat it as a high Himalayan environment, possibly higher.

**Before you go:**
- **See a physician** who understands high-altitude medicine. Discuss acetazolamide for acclimatization, and carry dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies. Learn exactly when to use them.
- **Train your cardiovascular base** for months: long, loaded uphill walks and stair work with a pack. You will carry heavy water loads on a days-long slope of broken slate.
- **Learn to recognize the signs** of altitude illness in yourself and your companions:
  - *Acute mountain sickness:* headache, nausea, poor sleep.
  - *High-altitude pulmonary edema:* breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, gurgling in the chest, blue lips.
  - *High-altitude cerebral edema:* confusion, stumbling, strange behavior, crushing lethargy.

**Use the Approach.** The shoulder ramp takes days, and that is a gift. Ascend slowly, rest days if you need them, and if possible sleep lower than the highest point you reached that day. Arrive at the plateau acclimatized, not merely arrived.

**Carry supplemental oxygen** if at all possible, at minimum an emergency supply. The main reason is HACE: confusion is the symptom that stops you from realizing you're sick. In a city where your sense of direction is already unreliable, a mind dulled by hypoxia is a serious danger.

**Bring a pulse oximeter** and use it morning and evening. Record the numbers. Your subjective sense of how you feel will not be trustworthy here.

**The descent is the cure.** If someone develops pulmonary or cerebral edema, the answer is to go down, immediately. Know your route off the plateau before you need it, and understand that "down" may be harder to reach than it looks (see *Distance is unreliable*, below).

### Water is the second problem, and the logistical heart of the trip

There is effectively no water in Hesk. The rime lasts twenty minutes, it isn't yours (see *Etiquette*), and it wouldn't be enough anyway. You also lose water fast here: every breath of thin, dry air carries moisture out of you.

- **Budget at least 4 liters per person per day**, more if you exert yourself. This will feel like too much. It isn't.
- **Multiply your planned stay by three** when calculating supplies. Hesk's streets can hold you far longer than you intended (see *Navigation*).
- **Cache water on the ramp** in stages on the way up, for the way down. Mark the caches with something that doesn't depend on the city behaving normally, since the ramp is outside the city and should be ordinary. Record GPS coordinates if your devices work there, cairn heights, and distinctive bone-colored boulders.
- **Insulate your water.** The cold is cellar-cold, not killing-cold, but water left out overnight at altitude can still freeze. Sleep with a bottle in your bag.
- **Watch your urine color.** Dark means you're behind. At altitude, dehydration and altitude sickness feel alike and worsen each other.

The weight of your water will be the single greatest constraint on how long you can stay. Accept this. A short, well-supplied visit is far better than a long, desperate one.

### The light will burn you from below

The sky is nearly black and the sun is "a hard white coin," which means very little atmosphere is filtering ultraviolet. Meanwhile the cloud sea reflects that light up at you, the way snow does, but from directly beneath.

- **Glacier glasses or goggles with full side and bottom coverage** are essential. Ordinary sunglasses shade you from above, which is the wrong direction here. Snow blindness from reflected light is intensely painful and will leave you blind for a day or more in a city you can't navigate even with sight.
- **Apply sunscreen to the undersides of everything:** under your chin, under your jaw, inside your nostrils, beneath your earlobes, the underside of your nose, the backs of your wrists. Burns will appear in places you've never burned before.
- **Wear a brimless or soft hat, or a neck gaiter pulled high.** A brim does little when the light comes up at you.
- **Bring a good lip balm** with high SPF. Your lips will crack in the dry air and burn from below.

### The dust

The dust in Hesk moves like slow water through the streets, pools in the lee of walls, and hangs suspended at the seam. It is fine, stony, and everywhere.

- **Carry a quality respirator** (N95 or better, ideally with an exhalation valve so it doesn't ice up or fog your goggles). You won't need it all the time, but you'll want it in the dust-rivers when they run fast, and at the seam.
- **Protect your eyes** with sealed goggles when the dust moves.
- **Seal your gear.** Stove parts, zippers, camera mechanisms, and watch crowns will grind and fail. Use dry bags and keep things closed.
- **Nasal saline and a small tub of ointment** for your nostrils will keep dry air and dust from making them bleed.

### Cold

It is steady, dry, cellar cold, not an Arctic emergency. That makes it easy to underestimate.

- **Dress in layers:** a wicking base, an insulating mid-layer, a windproof and dust-proof shell. The wind is slow but constant.
- **Stone draws heat.** You'll be sitting, sleeping, and standing on laminated rock that stays cool; the dark threshold stone stays cold even at midday. Bring a thick closed-cell foam pad to sit on during the day, plus a proper insulated sleeping pad.
- **Keep batteries warm,** in inner pockets or in your sleeping bag at night. Cold and altitude drain them fast.

### Food and cooking

- **Pack dense, dry, high-calorie food** that needs no water to prepare: nut butters, dried meat, chocolate, hard cheese, oily crackers, dense bars. Altitude kills appetite. Bring things you'll *want* to eat, not just things that are efficient.
- If you bring a stove, remember that **water boils at a low temperature** here, so cooking takes longer and "boiled" food may not be fully cooked. Freeze-dried meals may stay crunchy. A stove suited to low-pressure, cold conditions (remote-canister or liquid fuel) is best.
- Every liter you boil for food is a liter you can't drink. Consider leaving the stove behind and eating cold.

## Part Two: Gear

### Essentials

| Category | Items |
|---|---|
| **Breathing** | Supplemental oxygen, pulse oximeter, respirator masks (several) |
| **Eyes & skin** | Glacier goggles with bottom shielding (plus a spare pair), high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm |
| **Water** | Insulated bottles and bladders, more capacity than seems reasonable |
| **Shelter** | Freestanding tent or bivy (no stakes; you can't drive pegs into laminated stone, and you shouldn't try), insulated sleeping pad, sit pad, warm sleeping bag |
| **Light** | Headlamp with red mode, spare headlamp, lithium batteries kept warm, chemical lightsticks |
| **Navigation** | See Part Three; this is a whole kit of its own |
| **Communication** | Hand signals (learned in advance), whistles, signal mirror, small writing slates or notebooks |
| **Safety** | Climbing harness, 30m of rope, a few slings; first aid kit with altitude medications |
| **Records** | Mechanical watch, digital watch, notebook, pencils (ink may fail in cold), a camera |
| **Waste** | Sealable waste bags; pack out *everything* |

### A note on light

When the cloud sea parts, "the city goes suddenly black, its light cut off from underneath." These blackouts last minutes. Keep your headlamp *on your body*, not in your pack, at all times. When the light fails, **stop where you are.** Do not walk. Wait for it to return. If you must move, use your headlamp.

Your headlamp also has a quieter use. Hesk's light comes from below, and your eyes will keep failing to read depth and edges correctly. Stairs worn into shallow bowls, drifts of rubble, and troughs in the pavement are all hard to see when their tops are in shadow. A headlamp throws light from *above*, restoring the cues your brain expects. On difficult ground, even in "daylight," switch it on and look at your footing. Keep it on low or red; there's no need to flood the city with white light.

### What to wear, color-wise

The masons vanish against the stone. You shouldn't. Wear at least one bright, saturated layer, orange or red, something that looks foreign to Hesk, so your companions can find you in the grey dimness. It is also a small psychological anchor. A patch of color in a place that has none helps more than you'd expect.

## Part Three: Navigation and Time

This is where Hesk stops being merely a hard high-altitude trip and becomes something else.

### Distance is unreliable

The plateau is maybe twenty miles across, but an avenue can take three days without ending. Towers approach or recede without regard for your steps. The city is "larger on the inside of its streets than on the outside of its rim."

**Principles:**

1. **The rim is your friend.** The outside of the city seems to behave more honestly than its inner streets. The edges, where the terraces drop into the cloud sea, give you a constant reference: the bright white horizon, below and all around. When in doubt, move toward the light below, *carefully*, to find the edge. Travel along the rim rather than through the heart of the city when you need to get somewhere reliably.

2. **Navigate by time and supplies, not by distance.** Set strict turnaround rules: "When we've used 40% of our water, we head back, no matter where we are." Don't reason, "It's only a little further." In Hesk, it may not be.

3. **Keep the Approach in mind.** Know exactly where you came onto the plateau, and note the shape of the ramp's shoulder against the cloud sea. That's your door out.

4. **Test your instruments early.** Try your compass and GPS on the ramp and on the first day in the city. They may work, drift, or disagree with each other. Don't rely on them until you've confirmed them, and don't trust them if they start contradicting what the rim and the light tell you.

5. **Distrust markers.** Chalk, cairns, and ribbons may be blown away by the dust, or they may be aged or un-aged by the city itself. A cairn you built yesterday might be worn smooth, or not yet built. If you use markers, use them as *information*, not as a lifeline. Better still, bring a long, thin, bright cord and run it from a fixed point when exploring a district you intend to return from. Even so, don't be shocked if it behaves oddly.

### Streets repeat, and they repeat across time

You will pass the same doorway twice, and the second time it may be older, a crack widened and a carving worn away, or it may be *younger*.

- **Photograph distinctive features.** A lintel crack, the drift of dust on a step, a particular carving. When you encounter them again, compare. This will tell you *something*, even if you can't be sure what: perhaps that you've looped, and perhaps which way you've drifted in time.
- **Keep a written log:** time, landmark, direction, and what you observed. Your memory will blur in the thin air and the grey light. The log won't.
- **When you notice repetition, stop.** Sit down. Drink water. Check your oximeter. Talk it through with your companions. Decide deliberately which way to go instead of pushing on in a daze. People lost in ordinary wilderness tend to walk faster when they start to panic. In Hesk, that is the worst thing you can do.

### Keeping track of time

- **Carry two watches,** one mechanical and one digital, and have every companion carry at least one. Compare them daily and write the times down.
- **Track days by the dawn rime and the dusk chord.** These rhythms are the city's own clock, and they may be more reliable than your watch.
- **Watch your food, beard growth, fingernails, and batteries.** If these drift from what your watch says, something has happened to you. Don't panic, but write it down and take it seriously.
- **Separate from companions as little as possible.** If you're apart for a stretch and reunite, compare watches immediately. A significant discrepancy is a sign that one of you has been somewhere the other hasn't.

### Buildings that exist only from some angles

A dome is solid from one spot and gone a step to the left.

- **Don't lean on walls you haven't tested.** Before you rest your weight against anything, look at it from a few steps to either side.
- **Never enter an angle-dependent structure.** You have no idea what happens to someone inside a dome when they step to where the dome isn't. Admire them from outside, and from the spot where they are visible.
- **Don't stand beneath** anything that flickers in and out of existence.
- **Don't cross open plazas blindly** in districts where these structures are common. Walk slowly, looking around you. The empty space you're about to step into may be a wall from your next footfall.

### The sky with a floor

On certain evenings, the black above turns solid, shadows pool against it like smoke on a ceiling, and "you feel the pull to fall toward the light." The light is below you, beyond the terrace edges, in the cloud sea.

Take this seriously. It is the most straightforwardly lethal thing in Hesk.

- **Learn the signs:** shadows from the towers flattening rather than fading, the sky seeming near, a sensation of vertigo, a creeping doubt about which way is up.
- **When it begins, get away from the rim.** Move to the center of a plaza or a broad street, far from any drop.
- **Sit or lie down.** Put your hands flat on the stone. Feel it under you. Close your eyes if that helps. Contact with the ground grounds the vestibular system.
- **If you are near an edge and can't move away, clip in.** Carry your harness and a sling, and anchor to a solid, *confirmed* structure (checked from several angles) or to a companion who is farther from the edge.
- **Talk to each other.** Say out loud, "The ground is down, we're sitting on it." It sounds silly. Do it anyway.
- **Consider motion sickness medication** for these evenings, after testing it at home first; some cause drowsiness.
- **Never sleep near the rim.** You don't want to wake up during one of these evenings with the light calling you.

### The seam

The seam is a quarter-mile band running north to south, where buildings flicker between old and new, the dust hangs undecided in the air, and the masons gather most densely.

Visit it if you must. It is almost certainly the most remarkable thing in Hesk. But understand that it is the place where time is least sure of itself, and you are made of time too.

- **Go in with a partner and a tether.** Link yourselves with a short cord.
- **Compare watches before entering and after leaving.**
- **Wear your respirator.** The suspended dust is fine and constant.
- **Don't linger.** Set a strict time limit and keep to it.
- **Don't touch the flickering walls.** A wall that is both assembling and crumbling is not a wall you want your hand inside.
- **Watch the masons.** They know the seam better than anyone. If they move away from a place, you move away from it too. If they stop, you stop.
- **Stay out of the masons' way** above all here, where their work matters most.

### East and west

- **East (ancient, eroding):** Leaning towers, collapsed arcades, rubble drifts. Treat it like any ruin: don't climb unstable structures, don't stand beneath overhangs, and stay alert for falling stone. Cracks here widen.
- **West (healing, assembling):** Rubble rising into walls, cracks closing, dust blowing *into* the stone. This is less obviously dangerous, but more strange. **Don't put your hands or tools in cracks.** Don't leave gear against walls or in rubble, or anywhere the city might be growing. Don't sleep against western walls. A thing that knits itself back together may not distinguish between its own stone and your sleeping bag.

## Part Four: The Living Things

### The masons

The masons are the stitching that holds Hesk together, and you should behave around them as you would around any work that cannot be interrupted: quietly, respectfully, from a distance.

- **Never block a mason's path.** They move slowly and deliberately. Give way. Don't stand still in front of them.
- **Never touch fresh paste.** A single bead represents a mason's full day. Damaging it destroys that day's work, and possibly more, if it matters at the seam.
- **Never touch a mason.** There's no indication they're dangerous, but there's also no reason to test it.
- **Don't stand on a line a mason is following.** They "follow something," filling in lines already there. You don't want to discover what happens when a mason arrives at a line and finds you occupying it. If one approaches as though you were in the way of something invisible, move.
- **Watch them for cues.** They are the only local experts. When the stone chimes and they stand still with necks lifted, you should stand still too. When they descend to the damp lower terraces in the high months, understand that this is their most important work, and give them room.
- **Be patient.** They may seem like animals, but the work they're doing is closer to the most important job in the world, at least in this world. Treat them accordingly.

### The rime is theirs

The dawn rime on the northern faces is the only moisture in Hesk, and the masons need it to build. The dust-moths feed on it too.

- **Don't lick, wipe, or collect the rime.** Don't brush against north-facing walls before dawn. Stay out of the way of masons gathering it.
- **Don't let your own breath and moisture disrupt it.** Don't camp against north faces, and keep your distance at dawn.
- **Treat the twenty minutes of rime as sacred.** It is the fuel for the whole city.

### Dust-moths

- **They weigh nothing and make no sound.** You'll only notice one when it lands on you. When it does, don't swat it. Let it rest, then let it go.
- **Don't collect them.** They are fragile and part of an ecosystem with almost no margin.
- **Take care at dusk** when they're drifting in the dust-rivers. Walk gently.

### Stilt-ravens

- **Don't feed them.** They live on dead moths and flakes of paste. Your crumbs could change their behavior or harm them.
- **Listen for the click.** It is the only deliberate sound in Hesk, and its spacing is a strange comfort. You'll come to love it.
- **Keep your food secured** anyway. Scavengers are scavengers.

### The thing that hunts the stilt-ravens

Nobody has seen it. It leaves black feathers in perfect spirals on high balconies, with nothing else nearby.

You don't know what it is, how large it is, or whether it has any interest in you. You should assume nothing, and act conservatively.

- **Avoid high balconies,** especially at dusk, at night, and on evenings when the sky has a floor.
- **Never sleep in high places.** Sleep at street level, in open, wide spaces, well away from the rim.
- **Stay with companions,** particularly after dark.
- **If you find a spiral of feathers, don't touch it, and leave the area.** Note where it was in your log.
- **Watch for spirals in other forms:** in the dust, in patterns of debris, in the way the air moves. You don't know what they mean. Treat them as a warning.
- **Take turns keeping watch at night** if you're in a group.

The unknown predator is probably not your biggest risk here. Altitude and dehydration are. But it is the risk you understand least, so give it respect.

## Part Five: Etiquette and Leave No Trace

In Hesk, anything you leave behind might become permanent. It might be built into a wall, preserved forever in the cold dry air, or drawn into the city's slow tides of assembly and ruin. You would be leaving a mark on something that is trying to finish itself.

- **Pack out everything.** All waste, including human waste, in sealed bags. Nothing decomposes here; there's no water and no life to break it down.
- **Don't take stone.** Not a flake, not a chip, not a pebble of rubble. Anything you remove is part of a city that is still being built and unbuilt. There's also no telling what a piece of Hesk's stone might do in your pocket over time, as it tries to age or un-age. Take photographs, sketches, and notes instead.
- **Don't carve, mark, or chalk anything permanent.**
- **Don't strike the stone to hear it ring.** Let the city chime on its own schedule.
- **Leave the dust.** Before you descend from the plateau, shake out your clothes and gear at the rim. The dust belongs to the masons; they build the city from it. Don't carry it home.
- **Don't try to "help" the masons.** Don't lay down paste, don't carry dust to them, don't clear their paths of rubble. You don't know the lines they're following.

## Part Six: Your Mind

Hesk is almost entirely silent, almost entirely empty, dim, grey, lit from the wrong direction, and full of things that don't agree with themselves. It is an extremely demanding environment psychologically, even for experienced wilderness travelers.

### Silence

You'll hear your own footsteps as though through a wall. Voices won't carry far in the thin air. Silence this complete can be disorienting: your ears start producing their own sounds, and your thoughts get very loud.

- **Learn hand signals** with your companions before you go. Agree on a few essential ones: *stop*, *come here*, *danger*, *I'm sick*, *go back*, *look*.
- **Use touch** to get attention; shouting may not work.
- **Carry small slates or notebooks** for anything complicated.
- **Stay within sight of each other.** Assume that if you can't see a companion, you can't reach them.
- **Bring something quiet for yourself:** a song you hum under your breath, a recording you can listen to with one earbud at night. Don't fill the city with noise, but don't let the silence take you over completely.

### Light

The constant upward light makes the world look wrong, and "the eye keeps failing to make sense of it." Expect fatigue, headaches, and a lingering sense of unease. Rest your eyes. Close them sometimes. Look down at the cloud sea for a while; it is the one thing that is *meant* to be lit.

### Emptiness

The city is enormous and no one lives in it, or ever will, or everyone already does. You'll find yourself inventing inhabitants to fill the doors cut for creatures you'll never meet. This is normal. It may also be unnerving.

- **Travel with people you trust,** people whose steadiness you've seen under pressure.
- **Have a daily ritual:** a shared meal, a moment at dusk to watch the chord rise and fade, a check-in where everyone says how they're doing.
- **Watch each other for signs of dissociation:** someone growing quiet, drifting away from the group, staring at the stone, forgetting to drink.

### Uncertainty

Hesk refuses to be fully understood, and it will not be solved. Go with the mindset of a witness, not a surveyor. Accept that you'll leave with more questions than you arrived with. The travelers who struggle most here are the ones who insist on making it make sense.

### Beauty

The city is also one of the most beautiful places you will ever see. Give yourself time to simply stand in the great plaza at dusk, with the dark sky pressing down, the glowing sea below, the shadows streaming upward, a single mason at work, a moth passing, a raven clicking, a chord rising out of the stone. Let yourself feel it.

## Part Seven: When to Go, How Long to Stay

### Season

- **High months (cloud sea rises):** The lower terraces fill with damp and the masons work furiously there. The city is at its brightest, lit strongly from below. This is probably the best time to visit, **but stay out of the lower terraces.** The cloud there means whiteout at the edges of long drops, the stone may be slick, and the masons are doing the most important work of their year. Watch from above.
- **Low months (cloud sea falls away):** The whole city is exposed to the black sky and only faintly lit, and the dust-rivers run fast. It is darker, dustier, harder to navigate, and more dangerous. Avoid this season unless you have a specific reason to see it.

### Length of stay

- **A first visit should be short:** a few days on the plateau, at most, after a careful approach. Stay near the rim. See the great plaza at dusk. Watch a mason. Listen to the chord. Go home.
- **Longer stays** of weeks, enough to see the two tides, require a serious expedition: a supply chain up the ramp, a base camp at a fixed, confirmed location near the rim, careful logs, and people willing to rotate up and down for rest and resupply at lower altitude.
- **Don't try to walk to the end of an avenue.** It may not have one.

### A sample first itinerary

1. **Days 1–5 or more:** Climb the Approach slowly, caching water, acclimatizing, testing instruments, and learning your hand signals until they're second nature.
2. **Day 6:** Arrive on the plateau. Camp just inside the rim, at street level, in an open space. Watch your first dusk chord. Rest.
3. **Day 7:** Explore the district near your arrival point, in daylight, staying in sight of the rim. Photograph landmarks. Watch a mason at work. Return to camp well before dusk.
4. **Day 8:** If everyone is healthy, make a careful day trip toward the great plaza, with a strict turnaround time based on water. Stand in the plaza at dusk if you can safely return; otherwise, go at midday and come back another time.
5. **Day 9:** Descend. Shake out the dust at the rim. Collect your caches on the way down. Compare watches and logs at the bottom.

## Part Eight: Leaving

- **Shake out the dust** at the rim and leave it behind.
- **Check your belongings** for anything that has changed: aged, un-aged, worn, sharpened. Note it in your log.
- **Compare watches, logs, and memories** with your companions, both at the rim and at the bottom of the ramp. If there are discrepancies, write them down carefully, and don't argue about whose version is right. You may both be right.
- **Descend slowly and watch your health.** Altitude illness can still strike on the way down, and fatigue causes accidents on broken slate.
- **Expect an adjustment period afterward.** Ordinary light, lit from above, may look strange for a few days. Sound may feel loud. You may find yourself watching the undersides of arches, or waiting for a chord at dusk. This is normal. It will fade, although you may not want it to.

---

## A Last Word

Hesk is not hostile. It simply doesn't have room for you. There's no water, no air to spare, no one to meet, and not even a stable sense of where or when you are. You'll be the only warm, wet, noisy, short-lived thing in a city built of dust and rime and something older than time.

Go carefully, and carry more water than you think you need. Stay near the light below, but never too near the edge. Be kind to the masons, and don't take anything home but what you saw.
