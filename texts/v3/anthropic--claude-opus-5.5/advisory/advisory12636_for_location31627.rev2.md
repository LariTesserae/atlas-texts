# Preparing for the Casting Grounds of the Upper Reach

*Notes for an ordinary human traveler*

---

## The Short Version

The Upper Reach will not try to kill you. Most places that are dangerous to humans are dangerous because they are indifferent, and the Reach is something slightly different. It is *busy*. It is occupied with making itself. The risks come from being inside a workshop that has no concept of a visitor: the light, the altitude, the edges, the tooling, and a quieter danger underneath all of them. The place smooths over irregularities, and after long enough, you may start to count as one.

Prepare for the physical hazards the way you would for a high glacier crossing mixed with an industrial site. Prepare for the rest the way you would for solitude, sensory deprivation, and a landscape that lies about where things are. Go in with a fixed departure date, and keep it.

---

## 1. The Light

This is the first thing that will hurt you, and it will do it quickly.

You are above the cloud, the sky is thin, and every surface is new, pale, and reflective. The light comes from everywhere, including from below. It is snow-blindness conditions without the snow, and without any sun you could turn your back on.

**Bring:**
- **Glacier glasses with full side shields**, category 4 lenses. Ordinary sunglasses are not enough, because light will come in around the edges, off the ground, and up under the frames. Bring two spare pairs. Fine dust will scratch lenses and you will lose one.
- **A backup that can't break**: slit goggles made of leather or wood. They are old technology, and they cannot shatter.
- **High-SPF sunscreen, and a lot of it.** Put it under your chin, under your nose, and inside your ears, because the light is reflected upward. Bring SPF lip balm too.
- **A wide-brimmed hat with a dark underside**, plus a neck gaiter or buff to cover your lower face.
- **Lubricating eye drops.** Photokeratitis feels like sand under the eyelids and shows up hours after the damage is done. If your eyes start to feel gritty in the evening, you have already overdone it.

The passage says that after an hour "your eyes forget how to stop" squinting. Take that literally. Chronic squinting causes headaches, jaw tension, and fatigue. Plan rest periods in shade, the dim clefts behind mold-halves or under pipe-bundles, just to let your face relax.

### There may be no night

"Moths come out at dusk, if it is ever dusk." Assume the light may be continuous, or close to it.

- Bring a **proper blackout sleep mask**. Bring a **dark-interior tent** or a dark tarp to hang inside your tent.
- Keep time on a **mechanical watch and a digital one**. Wind the first and check them against each other. You need a clock that belongs to you and not to the place.
- Keep a strict schedule for sleeping and eating. Melatonin may help. Without dusk your body will lose track of time within days, and a disoriented mind is the one the Reach finds easiest to work on.

---

## 2. Altitude

You come up *through* cloud. The Reach sits above a cloud layer, and the sky is described as "scrubbed thin." Assume serious altitude.

- **Acclimatize beforehand** if you can, at real altitude somewhere on your home world, over a period of weeks.
- Carry a **pulse oximeter**. Your judgment will be affected by the altitude, so take a daily reading and trust the number over how you feel.
- Talk to a doctor about **acetazolamide**. Learn the signs of AMS, HAPE, and HACE: headache, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, confusion, and loss of coordination.
- **Your descent route is down through the cloud.** Know which way is down at all times (see Navigation below). For altitude sickness, descent is the cure. In a place where the valley is sometimes also above you, finding true down is a skill you need to practice.

The air is "cool, never cold." That is a mercy, since you won't face frostbite. But thin cool air with constant damp still pulls heat out of a tired body. Dress in layers.

---

## 3. Edges, Flashing, and Surfaces

Nothing here has been softened. Treat the whole landscape as freshly cast industrial material that nobody has deburred.

**Flashing** is the most important physical hazard to understand. These are thin translucent fins that squeezed out along the mold seams. Some stand a hand's width proud and catch the light "like frost." Expect them to be as sharp as broken glass, or sharper. Whole ridgelines are crested with them.

- **Cut-resistant gloves** (Kevlar or Dyneema), worn over thin liners. Bring several pairs. Never put a bare hand on a seam.
- **Stiff-soled, cut-resistant boots** in the style of a mountaineering boot. Trail runners will be shredded.
- **Gaiters and heavy trousers.** Brushing past flashing at shin height will open your leg.
- **A serious laceration kit**: wound closure strips, a suture or skin-stapler kit if you're trained, hemostatic gauze, irrigation syringes, and plenty of antiseptic. Every cut will be full of fine white dust.
- **Don't use flashing as a handhold.** It is thin and brittle. It will either cut you or snap. Or it may hold, which is its own kind of lesson.

**Cliffs break in clean planar fractures.** They are sheer, featureless, and seamed. Rock climbing technique relies on irregularity, and there is little of it here, just the same crescent pit every forty paces. Where you can, don't climb the castings. Climb the infrastructure instead.

**The infrastructure is dense.** Gantries, catwalks, stairways that go nowhere, and pipe-bundles taller than a person, layered three and four deep. This is your road network, and it is also where you are most likely to fall.
- **Wear a helmet, all the time.** There are always things above you.
- Carry a **climbing harness, a short rope, slings, and locking carabiners**. Clip in on exposed catwalks. You have no way of knowing how old any given gantry is, or whether it is scheduled for re-casting.
- Carry a **headlamp**. The spaces between castings, under the bundles and behind the molds, will be dim after the glare outside, and your eyes will be slow to adjust.

**Heat:** the surfaces are "faintly warm," and the newest castings steam. Stay away from sprues, from freshly released peaks, and from anything still misting at the seams. Assume the newest material can burn you.

---

## 4. Dust

The broad faces are "powdery with the fine white dust of fresh castings." You don't know what it is made of. On Earth, fine pale dust from fresh castings and industrial process material means silica, lime, or worse. Treat it as hazardous until you know otherwise.

- **Respirator with P100 filters**, plus spare cartridges. Wear it whenever you're on the dry faces, and especially when wind or swifts kick the dust up.
- **Sealed goggles** for dusty stretches, as a supplement to your glacier glasses.
- **Dry bags for everything.** Dust will get into zippers, lenses, and electronics.
- **Nasal saline rinse.** You will be breathing it in anyway.
- Wet dust becomes paste. Clean your gear before the morning sweat turns the dust into something that sets.

---

## 5. Water

The good news is that water arrives every morning without fail. The Reach condenses. The bad news is that most of it has run over fresh castings and dissolved some of whatever they are made of.

**Best practice: make your own condensation surface.** Bring a large, clean, non-white sheet of plastic or silnylon. Rig it on a frame overnight, or drape it over a cool pipe with your own material between the pipe and the water. The same sweat that beads on the iron will bead on your sheet, and it will be close to distilled. That is your cleanest source.

**If you drink from the gutters:**
- **Filter, then treat chemically**, and ideally use an activated-carbon stage as well. You're concerned less about microbes than about dissolved minerals.
- Only take water from the top of the system: fresh beads, shallow gutters. Avoid the collecting tanks and anything that has been sitting still.
- The standing water is "dark and very still." **Do not wade into the deep collecting-tanks.** Their depth is unknown and their sides are probably sheer cast walls. You could not climb out.

**Do not follow the gutters expecting them to lead downhill.** Water sometimes runs uphill "briefly, then thinks better of it." The gutters lead into the works, not toward safety.

Mornings will be wet. Carry a **waterproof shell** and waterproof bags. Expect your tent to drip with condensation inside, so ventilate it.

---

## 6. Food

The passage says the ibex "can feed you." Be careful with that. The ibex are cast. They are seamed, grained like the rock, and some carry flashing. Nobody knows whether they were made on purpose or by accident. Nobody knows what their flesh is made of.

**Bring all your own food.** Calculate your trip length and add at least 50%. The terrain is exhausting, and hunger, cold, and fatigue weaken judgment.

- Do not eat the lichen, the moss, or the ferns. The ferns look real, and they "seem real." Leave them alone.
- If you are truly desperate, the hares in the pipe-bundles may be the least strange option. The passage doesn't say they are cast. Still, check every animal for a seam before you even think about it.
- **Do not eat anything with a seam.** This is a firm rule. You don't want to take in whatever makes up the Reach.

## 7. Navigation

This is the hardest part of the trip, and it is why ordinary travelers get lost here.

**Everything that normally works, fails:**
- **Landmarks repeat.** The peak behind you turns up ahead of you. "Either there are many of that peak, or there is one and it has moved."
- **Textures repeat every forty paces.** Your eye will insist it recognizes places it has never seen.
- **No sun direction.** The light comes from everywhere, so you have nothing to steer by.
- **Shadows lag and lie.** Never use them for orientation.
- **Compasses are unreliable.** There are hillside-sized iron molds, current-carrying pylons, and pipework everywhere. Expect local magnetic chaos.
- **Towns are false.** The smoking settlement you see across the valley is never the one you reach.
- **Up and down are not always what they seem.** There is an inverted range overhead.
- **Your marks get erased.** The Reach repairs damage with scaffold, a small mold, and a fresh pour. Scratches, cairns, and chipped blazes may all be smoothed away.

**What to do instead:**

1. **Establish your arrival point obsessively.** Wherever you enter, mark it with something large, brightly colored, non-white, and non-damaging: a flag on a pole, lashed to a gantry. Photograph it from every direction. Record its altitude.

2. **Use gravity and pressure, not sight.** Carry a **barometric altimeter** and a **small plumb bob or spirit level**. Down is where the plumb bob points. Lower altitude means toward the cloud, the warmth, and the exit. Check them against each other. If they disagree, sit still until they agree.

3. **Leave removable, non-cast markers.** Use brightly colored flagging tape tied (not stuck) to scaffold every few hundred paces. Color-code by day. Write the date and a number on each one. The repair process targets damage to the castings, so tape on a gantry is less likely to be "fixed" than a scratch on rock. Check whether your markers are still there on your way back.

4. **Dead reckoning with discipline.** Count paces with a tally counter or ranger beads. Keep a written log of every turn, every stairway, and every catwalk. Go by your notes, not by what you think you recognize.

5. **Use photographs to tell repeats apart.** Photograph peaks with timestamps, and focus on *differences*: notches in the flashing, the state of the lichen, whether a mold is open or closed. If two peaks look identical right down to the notch, record that, and don't assume you know which one you're looking at.

6. **Consider a physical line** for your first excursions: a thin, bright cord unspooled from your base. It sounds absurd across a county-sized plateau, but for the first day, while you learn how the place deceives you, it isn't absurd.

7. **Stay out of the towns.** If you do go into one to see it, spend no more than an hour there, and mark your route in and out as if it were a cave. There is nothing inside for you: no people, no hearths, no doors that open, no shelter.

8. **Do not climb toward the inverted range.** Don't try to work out where it begins.

## 8. Sound and Communication

Your voice dies at about fifty paces. This changes how you travel with other people.

- **Travel in pairs, and stay within sight.** In terrain this dense, "never more than an arm's length of empty air," losing sight of someone means losing them.
- **Radios**, if they work, but expect interference from the pylons. Test them on arrival.
- **Whistles** will carry farther than voices, but not much farther. Agree on simple codes.
- Consider a **tether** between companions in the densest stretches.
- Remember the quiet works both ways. You won't hear anything coming. A falling piece of scaffold or a shifting press may make no sound until it reaches you.

**The mold opening.** A few times a season, a mold opens with "a single deep, cracking report that seems to come from inside your chest." It is survivable, but it may knock you off your feet, and it may shake loose things overhead. That is one more reason to keep your helmet on.

- **Never camp near a clamped mold.** Stay well clear of the swing of the halves and the hinges "thick as towers."
- Look at a mold's condition before you settle near it. Sweating, darkening iron and mist along the clamps mean it is close to opening.

**The quiet itself.** You will begin to hear your own heartbeat, your blood, the tick of cooling iron from far off. For some people this is peaceful. Others find it deeply unsettling. Bring earbuds and a little recorded music or voices from home, but use them only in camp, never while moving.

## 9. Wildlife

- **Swifts.** Their nesting areas in the vents and flues will be thick with droppings. Avoid breathing near them, as there is a fungal-spore risk, and wear the respirator. Their clouds pass silently. Don't be startled.
- **Crows.** They are identical, the same crow repeated. They are harmless as far as anyone knows. Don't feed them. Don't try to catch one to compare it against another. When they all turn their heads at once toward nothing, don't look where they're looking for long.
- **Ibex.** They are cast, alive, skittish, and heavy-horned, and their hooves ring. Give them space, as you would any large, horned mountain animal on a narrow ridge. **If you see an ibex lie down on warm flashing and go still, leave it alone.** Do not touch it or try to rouse it. Watch it, if you must, and then move on.
- **Lizards.** Small, white, seamed, basking on warm new castings. Harmless. Don't take any home.
- **Hares.** They live in the pipe-bundles. You may hear them before you see them.
- **The doorless buildings.** You will hear scratching inside some of them. **Do not open them. Do not try to make a door.** Don't press your ear to the wall for long. There may be molds in the works that "no one meant to fill." Whatever is inside came out of one of them, and it has no door for a reason.

---

## 10. Shelter

- **A freestanding tent in a strong, non-white color.** Stakes will not go into cast rock. Anchor the tent with webbing to scaffold, pipes, or gantry legs. Color matters: in a world of white and iron-black, you need to be able to spot your camp, and to tell your own things from the Reach's.
- **An insulated sleeping pad, always.** This matters for more than comfort (see below).
- **Choose your campsite carefully:**
  - Not near a clamped mold.
  - Not on the newest, warmest castings.
  - Not in a cracked or damaged place. Damage attracts repair, and repair means scaffold, then a mold, then a pour.
  - Ideally on an old, stable gantry platform, or in a sheltered cleft of mature casting, with your own material between you and the rock.
- **Solar charging works well here.** Light from everywhere, all day, is ideal for flexible panels. This is one of the few ways the Reach is accommodating.

## 11. The Real Danger: Becoming Part of It

This is the section to read twice.

The Reach keeps itself young. It repairs damage. It repeats. It takes things that go still and seals them in: the ibex lies down on warm flashing and "over some days its seam closes. After that you cannot tell it from the rock." The repair molds clamp over worn places. Everything irregular is eventually made regular.

Nobody says this will happen to a human. But you are a warm, still-ish, irregular object on its surface, and the Reach has not been told you are different.

**Rules:**

1. **Never sleep directly on cast rock**, especially warm or new casting. Always keep your pad, tent floor, or a layer of your own material under you.
2. **Don't rest motionless on new flashing.** If you feel tempted to lie down on a warm new ridge in that clean, calm light, get up and walk.
3. **Keep moving through the days.** Don't settle into long stillness in one spot. Move camp regularly.
4. **Watch for a seam.** Every day, check your companion and let them check you: along the spine, the crown of the head, the midline of the body. Carry a small mirror if you're alone. It will almost certainly be nothing. Check anyway, because the habit keeps you alert.
5. **Watch your shadow.** Lag in the world's shadows is part of the place. If your own shadow's lag begins to *grow*, if it keeps "shifting its weight" longer each time you stop, take that as a sign you have been there too long.
6. **Watch your mind.** These are the signs to leave:
   - You stop noticing the repetitions.
   - The identical crows stop bothering you.
   - The towns start to seem reachable.
   - You find the white hush more comforting than you should.
   - You start believing the theory about the inverted range.
   - You stop writing in your log because it seems unnecessary.
   - Your sense of yourself as distinct from the place starts to thin. You feel like "one more pale seamed animal walking the fresh flashing."
7. **Set a hard departure date before you arrive**, and give it to someone at home. Don't extend it while you're there. Your judgment in the Reach is not your judgment at home.

## 12. Anchors to Yourself

Bring things that don't repeat.

- **Something handmade and irregular**: a hand-knit hat, a carved wooden token, a stone from a riverbed at home, something shaped by erosion and by accident. When the forty-pace pattern starts to feel like the only pattern there is, hold it and look at how nothing on it repeats.
- **Handwriting.** Keep a paper journal and write in it daily. Your handwriting is irregular in a way the Reach's surfaces never are.
- **Photographs of faces and weathered places.** Look at them each evening.
- **Speak out loud** now and then, even alone. Your voice only carries fifty paces, but that is enough for you to hear it.
- **Mark time.** Count days. The Reach runs on seasons of pour and release. You run on days. Don't lose that.

---

## 13. Leaving

- **Find true down** with the plumb bob, altimeter, and your logs. Don't rely on the views, the shadows, or which way the water seems to run.
- Follow your flagging back. Collect it as you go and leave nothing.
- **Decontaminate before you cross.** Brush and wash the dust off your gear, clothes, and body. Clean out your respirator. You don't know what that dust would do in your home world.
- **Take nothing.** No flashing, no lizards, no crows, no fern fronds. No pieces of casting, however small, because you don't know which small things are molds. Nothing seamed goes home with you.
- Descend through the cloud. **It will feel warm, heavy, and loud.** Coming back into weather, wind, soft ground, and uneven light will be overwhelming at first. Some travelers cry. Let it happen.
- When you're home, **check your spine once more**. Then stop checking.

## Packing List Summary

**Eyes and skin:** glacier glasses (×3), slit goggles, sealed dust goggles, SPF 50+ sunscreen (lots), SPF lip balm, wide-brim hat, buff, eye drops, blackout sleep mask

**Body and edges:** helmet, cut-resistant gloves (several pairs) and liners, stiff mountaineering boots, gaiters, heavy trousers, layered clothing, waterproof shell

**Climbing and safety:** harness, short rope, slings, locking carabiners, headlamp and spare batteries, companion tether

**Health:** P100 respirator and spare cartridges, pulse oximeter, altitude medication, laceration and wound-closure kit, antiseptic, saline nasal rinse, general first aid, melatonin

**Water:** condensation-collection sheet and frame, filter, chemical treatment, activated carbon, water bladders

**Food:** all of it, plus 50% extra

**Navigation:** barometric altimeter, plumb bob or spirit level, tally counter, colored flagging tape (several colors), large marker flag and pole, notebook and pencils, camera, bright cord spool, mechanical watch, digital watch

**Shelter:** freestanding brightly colored tent, dark liner, anchoring webbing, insulated sleeping pad, flexible solar panels, dry bags

**Communication:** radios, whistles, agreed codes

**Mind:** handmade irregular token, paper journal, photographs, earbuds and recordings from home, small mirror

**Before leaving home:** a fixed departure date, given to someone who will expect you back

## A Last Note

People who come back from the Reach tend to describe it the same way: it wasn't cruel. It was clean, and bright, and so quiet that they could hear themselves thinking. Many say it was beautiful. Some say it was the most peaceful place they have ever been.

Go and see it. Watch a mold open. Watch the swifts spill across a white face like ink. Sit beside a fern that has grown in a spiral because it doesn't know where the light is coming from.

Then come home while you still notice that the crows are all the same crow.
