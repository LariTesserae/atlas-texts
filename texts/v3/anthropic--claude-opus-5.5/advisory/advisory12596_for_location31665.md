# Preparing for the Uncovered Shelf

## Understand what kind of place this is

Everything about this location points to the high Himalaya or its equivalent in some parallel world. Bar-headed geese, lammergeiers, snow leopards, blue sheep, pikas, red-billed and yellow-billed choughs, a monsoon carrying lowland soot upward: this is the roof of a continent. The indigo sky at midday, the pulse audible in your ears, and bar-headed geese passing *low enough to hear* all suggest an altitude somewhere between 5,000 and 6,000 meters. Plan for the high end.

That makes this, above all, a **high-altitude environment**. The polished rock, the soot ledger, and the ice worms are what you come for. The air is what can kill you. Everything else in this guide is secondary to that.

The second thing to understand is that this place is **new and unfinished in a physical sense**. The ground has no soil, no channels, no paths, no trails, and no weathering to give your boots grip. You will be one of the first large animals to walk on much of it. That affects how you move, how you camp, and what you owe the place.

---

## 1. The altitude problem, and why multiverse travel makes it worse

A normal mountaineer reaches 5,500 meters over one to two weeks of walking uphill, and the body adjusts along the way. A multiverse traveler may step through a threshold and arrive there instantly from sea level. **Do not do this.** Arriving unacclimatized at this height can bring on serious altitude sickness within hours:

- **High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE)**, which is fluid in the lungs
- **High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE)**, which is swelling of the brain

Either can be fatal within a day. There is no road down. There is a cloud floor, a drop, and unfamiliar terrain.

**How to prepare:**

- **Acclimatize elsewhere first.** Spend two to three weeks at progressively higher altitudes before transit, in this world or a friendlier one. Sleep at 3,000 m, then 3,800 m, then 4,500 m, with rest days, and climb higher by day than you sleep. If you can choose your arrival point, arrive at the far lip of the shelf, on the oldest ground, not at the ice face.
- **Carry medication and know how to use it.** Acetazolamide helps prevention and eases sleep. Dexamethasone is for HACE. Nifedipine is for HAPE. Consult a physician who knows high-altitude medicine.
- **Carry a pulse oximeter** and check yourself morning and evening. Expect readings in the high 70s to mid 80s even when acclimatized. Watch the trend, not just the number.
- **Consider a small supplemental oxygen supply** and, if your transit allows the weight, a portable hyperbaric bag (a Gamow bag). Treat either as emergency equipment, not comfort.
- **Know the warning signs.** A headache that pain medication doesn't touch, loss of coordination (you can't walk heel-to-toe in a straight line), confusion, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, or gurgling in the chest all mean the same thing: leave. If your transit method permits an emergency exit, rehearse it before you arrive. **Descent, or its multiverse equivalent, is the only real cure.**
- **Expect the normal discomforts anyway.** Sleep will be broken. You may wake gasping as your breathing slows and stalls in the night (periodic breathing), which is common and usually harmless. Appetite fades. Small exertions leave you panting. The "tightness behind the eyes" in the description is real, and you will feel it.

Travel with at least one companion if possible. Altitude impairs judgment, and the person with HACE is usually the last to know.

## 2. Cold, and specifically *wet* cold

This place is not merely cold. It is cold and constantly damp. It drips, it condenses, it rimes. Cloud swallows the shelf every afternoon, and for hours everything is soaked. Then night comes and everything freezes. Wet cold drains body heat far faster than dry cold.

**Clothing:**

- **Use synthetic insulation, not down, for your active layers.** Down collapses when damp, and it will get damp here. Keep a down parka for camp if you can keep it inside a waterproof shell and sleep with it protected.
- **Bring a fully waterproof, breathable shell jacket and trousers.** You will wear them most of every afternoon.
- **Base layers should be merino or synthetic.** No cotton, anywhere, including socks and underwear.
- **Hands are your main vulnerability.** You will want to touch the rock, feel the glass-smooth polish, and trace the striations. Bring thin liner gloves for handling things, insulated gloves, waterproof mittens, and at least one spare pair of each. Wet gloves will not dry here, so keep spares inside your jacket where body heat helps.
- **Feet:** bring insulated mountaineering boots, several changes of socks, and vapor-barrier liners if you're experienced with them. Days of wet feet can cause immersion foot, and freezing nights raise the risk of frostbite.
- **Bring a balaclava or buff, a warm hat, and a hood** that fits over the hat.

**Sleeping:** use a sleeping bag rated to at least −20°C, with a waterproof bivy cover or a very reliable tent. Bring two sleeping pads, one closed-cell foam and one inflatable, because you will be lying directly on stone that was under ice a few years ago. Sleep with your water bottles, batteries, and the next day's inner gloves inside the bag.

## 3. The ground itself: glass, ice, and falling stone

### The polish is a hazard

The description says the rock feels "almost like glass, cold and faintly slick." Take that literally. Glacially polished gneiss, wet with a film of meltwater, is one of the most treacherous surfaces you can walk on. At dawn it is worse: every weeping crack has frozen into a glaze of verglas (a thin sheet of clear ice) over rock that was already slippery. The whole slope tilts downhill, toward the lip and the drop into the cloud.

- **Wear boots with sticky, soft rubber soles.** Carry **microspikes or light crampons** for the icy mornings. Understand that metal points on bare polished rock skate as often as they bite.
- **Your crampons will scratch the stone,** adding your own marks among the glacier's striations. Walk on rock in spikes only when necessary.
- **Use trekking poles with carbide tips.** They are almost essential for balance on the smooth humps.
- **Take the humps from their smooth upslope side, not the downslope side.** The glacier plucked the downslope faces into angular blocks and small drop-offs. These make better handholds but worse footing, and loose plates are common.
- **Move in the late morning,** after the verglas has thawed but before the afternoon cloud and rockfall.

### The ice face

The glacier's snout is a wall about forty meters high, and it is retreating, which means it is shedding. The "rifle cracks" and "groans" are real structural events.

- **Stay well back from the ice face,** at least a distance equal to its height, and farther in afternoon warmth. Blocks of ice fall from retreating snouts without warning.
- **Don't go into or under ice caves or meltwater portals,** however beautiful they look. They are the most unstable features on any glacier.
- **Don't go onto the glacier surface** (to see the cryoconite honeycomb or the ice worms up close) without glacier training, a rope team, a harness, and crevasse rescue gear. The cracking you hear is crevasses opening. The cryoconite holes themselves are small ankle-breakers, and their dark sludge is slick.
- **If you want to see the ice worms,** the edge of old snow where it meets stone at dusk is safer and just as good. Bring a hand lens and kneel.

### Rockfall

The freeze-thaw cycle that is starting erosion here also means that loosened stones fall every afternoon.

- **Wear a climbing helmet** whenever you are below any slope, cliff, or ice.
- **Never camp below slopes or boulders that show fresh scars** or piles of new, sharp-edged debris.
- **Plan to be somewhere safe and open by early afternoon.**
- **Watch for bone-drop sites.** Lammergeiers drop bones onto slabs to crack them, and they favor exactly the kind of fresh, flat stone found here. A bone shattered on a slab makes an obvious spot. Don't sit or camp there.

## 4. Water: everywhere, and nearly undrinkable

All the water here is glacial meltwater loaded with rock flour, "the color of dishwater or weak tea." Glacial flour is not especially toxic, but it causes digestive upset, and it **clogs water filters within minutes.** The ice also holds a century and a half of black carbon and the pollutants that travel with it. Meltwater from the soot bands and cryoconite holes may carry concentrated contaminants, including metals and combustion byproducts.

**How to get drinkable water:**

- **Melting clean snow is your main option.** Gather it from fresh, white, wind-packed snow, not the dark-banded glacier ice, not the pink algae snow, and not snow speckled with springtails. You will need a stove, a lot of fuel, and time.
- **Stoves work poorly here.** Thin air and cold reduce their efficiency, and water boils at roughly 80°C, which is fine for melting and purifying but slow for cooking. Bring a stove proven at altitude, such as a pressurized liquid-fuel stove or a canister stove with an inverted-canister design. Bring a windscreen and much more fuel than you think you need.
- **If you must use meltwater,** let it settle in a container for hours to drop the flour. Pour off the clearer top, pre-filter it through cloth or a coffee filter, then purify it by boiling, chemical treatment, or UV. Don't rely on a standard pump filter alone.
- **Drink far more than you feel like drinking.** The air is extremely dry despite the damp surfaces (the rime sublimates without melting, which tells you how dry it is), and at altitude you lose large amounts of water with every breath. Aim for four liters a day. Dark urine is a warning.
- **The tarns freeze at night.** Keep your water in insulated bottles, stored upside down so ice forms at the base and not in the lid, and keep them in your sleeping bag.

## 5. Light: dim but not gentle

The light is described as dim, gray, and "pewter." Don't let that fool you. At 5,500 meters about half the atmosphere is beneath you, and ultraviolet radiation is intense even through cloud. It reflects off wet stone, snow, and ice from every angle.

- **Wear glacier glasses or goggles with side protection.** Snow blindness (photokeratitis), a sunburn of the cornea, can develop in overcast conditions and is excruciating. Bring a spare pair.
- **Use high-SPF sunscreen and SPF lip balm,** and reapply often. The undersides of your nose and chin burn from reflected light.
- **Bring a headlamp with plenty of spare batteries.** Nights are long, and the most interesting things happen at dusk and dawn: the ice worms rising, the copper light, the geese.
- **Carry navigation for zero visibility.** Every afternoon the cloud climbs up and swallows the shelf. In cloud over pale gray stone next to a white glacier, you can easily lose all sense of slope and distance (a whiteout). Bring GPS with the shelf's lip marked as a hazard, a compass, an altimeter, and a clear plan: when the cloud comes, stop moving unless you know exactly where you are. The lip of the shelf drops toward the cloud floor, and you do not want to find it by accident.

## 6. Camping on ground with no soil

You can't drive tent stakes into polished gneiss. There's no dirt.

- **Bring a freestanding tent** built for high winds.
- **Anchor it with rock.** Use stuff sacks or sand-bag anchors filled with loose stones, guy lines tied to boulders, or the natural plucked blocks on the downslope side of humps.
- **Pitch on a slight rise.** Sheet water spreads across the shelf by day and follows every hollow. A tent in a hollow will sit in a slurry pool that freezes solid by morning.
- **Choose already-bare rock on the middle-aged ground,** away from the ice face, below no loose slopes, and not on any plant, moss cushion, or lichen.
- **Cook in the vestibule only with ventilation.** Carbon monoxide poisoning is a real risk in cramped high-altitude tents, and your blood is already short of oxygen.

---

## 7. Food and body

- **Bring calorie-dense food you actually like.** At altitude, appetite collapses and you'll only eat what tempts you. Fats, sugars, salty snacks, chocolate, nut butter, and hot drinks all help.
- **Bring hot drink mixes.** A thermos of sweet tea at dawn, watching the eastern peaks turn rose, will be one of your best memories.
- **Expect weight loss** and slower wound healing.
- **Keep batteries warm.** Cold and damp kill phones, cameras, GPS units, and headlamp batteries quickly. Carry them against your body.

---

## 8. Wildlife: small, scattered, and mostly harmless

- **Snow leopards** are extremely shy and almost never dangerous to people. If you see one, you are lucky. Keep still and let it pass.
- **Choughs and snow finches** are bold and will raid unattended food. Don't feed them, and keep your food sealed.
- **Lammergeiers** are harmless to you, apart from the bone drops mentioned above.
- **Pikas** will investigate your camp. Don't disturb their hay piles. Those stacks of cut sedge are their winter survival.
- **Blue sheep** graze the far slopes and may knock down stones if they move above you.
- **Don't touch the spiders or the ice worms,** and don't collect them. The whole aeolian food web runs on a thin daily delivery from the wind, and it has very little margin.

## 9. Your responsibilities to a place with almost no life

This is one of the few places in any world where a single careless visitor can do lasting damage.

- **A lichen the size of your palm is older than anyone alive,** and a boot sole can destroy it in a second. Watch where you step. Walk on bare rock, which is most of the surface.
- **Never step on the cushion plants, mosses, saxifrages, or sedges.** They cover only one or two percent of the ground and represent decades to centuries of growth.
- **Pack out everything, including human waste.** Use waste bags, such as WAG bags. On most ground you'd bury waste, but there is no soil to bury it in. On this sterile, nutrient-starved stone, your waste would become a sudden, unnatural fertilizer patch and a lasting contaminant in a soil being built grain by grain. Also pack out food scraps and urine-soaked materials. Choose a urine spot on bare rock well away from the tarns and plants.
- **Don't build cairns or move stones.** You would be the first maker of anything on ground where "nothing has been built." Let it stay that way.
- **Don't sample the ice,** chip at the soot bands, or disturb cryoconite holes.
- **Think about your own footprint.** The whole shelf is the consequence of distant engines. It's worth considering how your own travel and gear were powered, and whether your visit adds to the ledger.

## 10. Timing your visit

- **Midsummer, just after the solstice,** is the easiest time. The cushion plants and saxifrages flower, the pikas cut hay, the ice worms and wind-fall insects are at their peak, and the shelf is mostly clear of snow. It's also when meltwater, cloud, and rockfall are at their worst.
- **Late spring or early autumn** brings the bar-headed geese: lines of them, passing at night and in the pre-dawn, the creak of wing feathers and soft honking overhead. The weather is colder and more changeable, and snow may still cover or already be burying the shelf.
- **Winter:** don't go. The shelf is buried in wind-packed snow, almost everything has left or gone dormant, and conditions are life-threatening.

**Plan your days around the daily cycle:**

| Time | What's happening | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-dawn | Everything frozen, verglas everywhere, geese may pass | Be awake, dressed, with a hot drink; watch the east |
| Dawn (~10 min) | Copper light on wet stone, meltwater lines glowing | Watch; the best light of the day |
| Late morning | Thaw, grip improves, visibility good | Main time for walking and exploring |
| Early afternoon | Wind rises, cloud climbs, rockfall peaks | Be settled somewhere safe and open |
| Afternoon cloud | Whiteout, dripping, rime | Shelter, rest, drink, stay put |
| Dusk | Wind drops, ice worms rise | Kneel at the snow's edge with a hand lens |
| Night | Hard freeze, orange glow on the cloud below | Stay warm; look southwest |

## 11. The mental preparation

Practical danger aside, this is a psychologically strange place, and it helps to arrive expecting that.

**Expect emptiness.** The description is honest: you can stand for an hour and see only a chough, a fallen moth, a single spider. If you come expecting spectacular wildlife, you'll be disappointed. The richness here is **small, close, and patient**. Bring a hand lens or a small macro lens. Plan to spend long periods kneeling or lying on your stomach on the stone, watching the snow crawl.

**Expect the quiet.** When the wind stops, the silence has a pressure you can feel, and you will hear your own heart. Some people find this peaceful. Others find it unnerving, especially when the ice suddenly cracks like a rifle. Neither reaction is wrong.

**Expect a sense of trespass.** You will walk on stone that has never been touched by any living thing, and the feeling of being the first is powerful. Walking from the ice edge outward to the lip is walking through time, from weeks-old stone to eighty-year-old stone. It's worth doing slowly, once, in the late-morning light.

**Expect a quiet grief, or at least unease.** The place is beautiful, but it is also plainly a consequence. The soot bands, the cryoconite holes boring downward, the orange stain on the clouds at night, the passing airliner: nothing here lets you forget that the ground exists because the ice is dying, and that the ice is dying because of machines far away. Travelers from a world with its own retreating glaciers may find this hits close to home. Give yourself time with it. You might write in a notebook, or simply sit at the lip at night and watch the glow of the cities you can't see.

**Expect to feel diminished.** At this altitude you'll think a little slower, tire faster, and feel strangely fragile. That's normal. Keep your plans simple, your days short, and your decisions conservative.

**Survival and medical:** altitude medications (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine), pulse oximeter, emergency oxygen or hyperbaric bag if possible, first-aid kit with blister and frostbite care, emergency shelter, a rehearsed emergency exit or evacuation plan.
