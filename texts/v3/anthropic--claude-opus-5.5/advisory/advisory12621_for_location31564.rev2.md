# Preparing for Ossary: A Traveler's Guide

## First, Understand What You're Walking Into

Most places ask you to adjust to a culture. Ossary asks you to adjust to living inside something. Nearly everything about it wears down an unprepared body: the warm wet air that never cools, the walls that sweat bitter water, the constant scream above your head and the pulse of the fans beneath your feet, the fact that every surface is moving. It is not hostile in the way a war zone or a desert is. Nothing here wants you dead. It is simply built for other creatures, and the humans who live there have spent eight hundred years bending themselves around those creatures. You will have days, not centuries.

The best way to hold it in your mind is as a greenhouse, a kitchen, and a lung all at once. Pack as if you were doing fieldwork inside a bat cave that happens to be the size of a country and has a steam-age government. Then add a layer of social and spiritual care, because this place has rules that aren't written down, and one law that absolutely is.

Four facts about daily life will shape everything else:

1. **You will never be dry.**
2. **You will never stand on a flat surface.**
3. **You will never hear silence.**
4. **You will never see a surface that isn't alive.**

Every preparation below is really about one of these four.

---

## When to Go

Ossary's year turns on the swifts' breeding cycle, and your visit will feel very different depending on where in that cycle you arrive.

- **Breeding season (birds present, roosts full):** This is the full spectacle: the Turning at its most overwhelming, the screaming at maximum, the chimneys taking in millions at dusk. It is also the hardest time to acclimatize. The nest-gatherers and roost-miners are idle or waiting, so the city is loud with birds rather than with its workers.
- **Post-breeding (nest harvest):** This is arguably the richest time for a visitor who wants to understand the place. The nest-gatherers are working and the rendering houses are boiling. There is ceremony, labor, and conversation. The flocks are still large.
- **The birds' absence (roost-mining season):** The Guano Works open their deep beds, since roosts can only be dug from below while the birds are away. The city will be quieter than at any other time, though still far from quiet. This is the gentlest introduction, but you will miss what Ossary *is*. Consider it for a first scouting trip. Ossarans may also find a visitor who comes only when the birds are gone faintly insulting, like someone visiting a family only while the grandparents are out.

My instinct is to arrive in the late breeding season, stay through the start of the harvest, and budget at least two weeks. The first week will be spent simply learning to function.

## Physical Conditioning (Start Months Ahead)

**Stairs, ramps, and uneven angles.** There are no level streets in Ossary. Every path is a ramp or a staircase tilted toward a drain, and most of them are wet with thin running sheets of slick, faintly caustic water. Your calves, ankles, knees, and stabilizer muscles will be working every waking moment. Train on stairs, preferably wet ones, for at least two to three months. Add balance work: single-leg stands, stepping on uneven or angled surfaces, walking downhill slowly with control. Downhill on slick stone is where people get hurt.

**Heat and humidity acclimatization.** Ossary is warm, never cold, and saturated. If you can, spend time in a hot humid climate beforehand, or do repeated sessions in a steam room or heated space. Learn how your body sweats and how fast you dehydrate when sweat can't evaporate. You will not cool off by sweating in Ossary. The air is already full.

**Altitude.** The tableland sits about a mile above the brine flats. That is not extreme, but combined with humidity, stairs, and heat, it will tire you more than you expect in the first few days. The sun above the haze is hard and white, so plan for high-altitude UV even though the air feels like a kitchen.

**Grip and upper body.** If there is any chance you'll be allowed into a chimney with the nest-gatherers, you'll be climbing iron rungs in near darkness among millions of birds. Train your grip and your pull strength. Honestly, don't expect to be invited, and don't ask on your first visit.

## Medical Preparation

See a travel-medicine specialist and describe the environment honestly: *dense bird and bat roosts, deep guano deposits, standing and running water contaminated by animal waste, a large rat population, dense insect swarms, and mildly caustic mineral water on every surface.* Then discuss the following.

**Respiratory risk is the big one.** Dried and disturbed bird and bat guano is a classic source of fungal lung infections such as histoplasmosis, and a city built on guano beds dozens of feet thick is the concentrated version of that risk. Add fine organic dust constantly falling from the sky, midge fragments, chitin flour from the mills, lime and ash from the kilns, and coal smoke from the Fanworks. Plan to wear a respirator in many settings (see gear below). If you have asthma or any chronic lung condition, think very seriously about whether this trip is for you, and bring more medication than you think you need.

**Vaccinations and prophylaxis to discuss:**
- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** Bats are everywhere, especially at night, and you may be struck, clawed, or nipped without noticing. This one isn't optional in my view.
- **Tetanus** booster.
- **Leptospirosis** risk from rats plus constant water. Ask about prophylactic antibiotics.
- **Hepatitis A** and standard enteric protection, since you'll be eating food handled in a damp, guano-rich environment.
- Anything relevant to **psittacosis-type** bird-borne illness. Know the symptoms: fever, headache, dry cough.

**Allergies.** Insect-body allergens (the midges, the chitin dust) can trigger serious allergic reactions and asthma in sensitized people. If you've ever reacted to dust mites, cockroaches, or shellfish, get tested. Pack non-drowsy antihistamines, a steroid inhaler if you're prescribed one, and epinephrine if you have any history of severe reactions.

**Skin.** The water weeping from every wall is bitter, slick, and faintly caustic, saturated with lime and ancient guano. Over days, it will irritate skin, especially feet, hands, and any skin kept wet under clothing. Expect maceration, cracking, and fungal infection in skin folds and between toes.

**Mental health.** The text says it bluntly: *visitors go half-mad in the first week.* The shriek at the top of hearing never stops. If you are prone to anxiety, sensory overload, migraines, tinnitus, misophonia, insomnia, or a fear of insects, birds, or crowds, take that seriously. People with trypophobia should know that the snail-covered walls, the textured roost-brick, and the swarms may be genuinely distressing.

**Your personal medical kit:**
- Antifungal cream and powder (lots of it)
- Barrier cream or zinc-based ointment for hands and feet
- Eye wash and saline drops, since midges will get in your eyes
- Antihistamines, oral and eye drops
- Oral rehydration salts, a large supply
- Antiseptic, wound-closure strips, waterproof bandages
- Sleep aids you have tested at home
- Anti-diarrheal and a broad-spectrum antibiotic, prescribed in advance
- A small bottle of mild acid rinse (diluted vinegar works) to neutralize alkaline residue on skin. Lime-rich water is alkaline, and a slightly acidic rinse helps.

## Gear

### Respiratory
- **A half-face respirator with P100 filters**, plus plenty of spare filters. They will clog quickly with dust and moisture, so assume you'll change them far more often than the packaging suggests. Wear it near guano works, kilns, chitin mills, roost chimneys, and in the Cup.
- **Disposable N95s** for general street use on dusty days, when drifts of dead midges lie in stairwells like grey snow.
- Bring something to keep masks dry, like sealed pouches, and expect condensation inside them. You'll be breathing hot wet air through a filter, which is miserable. Practice at home in a hot shower room so you know you can tolerate it.

### Eyes
- **Wraparound safety glasses or sealed goggles** for the Column, the eastern rim, and dusk, when midges and swifts are thickest. Midges will fly into open eyes constantly.
- Anti-fog treatment is essential. Every lens you bring will fog the moment it meets the wet air.
- Contact lenses are a bad idea. Between dust, insect fragments, and bitter water on your fingers, you are asking for infection. Bring glasses.

### Ears
This is delicate. You need protection, but you also need to hear warnings, whistles, and the swelling roar that signals the Turning.
- **High-fidelity filtered earplugs**, the kind musicians use, that reduce volume evenly without muffling everything. These will take the edge off the swift-scream without isolating you.
- **Foam plugs or over-ear defenders** for sleeping and for industrial areas like the Fanworks and the Throats.
- A **white-noise or low-frequency sound source** for sleep, if you can bring one. Some visitors find that the fans' own forty-beat pulse eventually becomes soothing, like sleeping next to a heartbeat.

### Head
- A **brimmed, waterproof hat.** You are walking beneath hundreds of millions of birds. Things will fall on you: droppings, dust, midges, water threads from the edges of every stair, and occasionally a dead bird or bat. A brim protects your face and neck and keeps the sun off during the bright gaps.
- A hood on your outer layer, for the Guttering's rain.

### Clothing
You're dressing for a place that is warm, saturated, and constantly shedding organic matter onto you.
- **Lightweight, quick-drying synthetics or linen** that you won't mind ruining. Cotton will stay wet forever and grow things.
- **Long sleeves and long trousers** anyway, loose rather than tight. You want a barrier against guano, caustic drips, spider silk, and bat claws, and loose weave lets what little air movement there is reach your skin.
- **An oilcloth or waxed outer layer** if you'll be in the lower Guttering, where it rains year-round.
- **Many changes of socks and underwear**, and a way to wash them. Things will not dry. Plan to rotate: wear one, wash one, and let a third try to dry, probably without success.
- **Avoid flat, open pockets and wide cuffs.** Anything horizontal on your body collects dust and midges, just as flat roofs would. Ossarans probably dress with this in mind; watch what they wear and imitate it.
- Consider buying local clothing on arrival. Whatever Ossarans wear has been refined over eight centuries for exactly this environment.

### Feet (The Most Important Gear Decision)
- **Footwear with deep, soft, siped rubber soles**, the kind made for wet rock or boat decks. You will be on sheeted, slick, film-coated stone at an angle all day. Hard soles will kill you.
- **Test your soles against alkaline water beforehand.** Leather will degrade and crack in lime-rich water. Some synthetic materials handle it better. Waterproof boots trap sweat, and your feet will stew in the heat. My instinct is fast-draining, well-gripping water shoes or mesh approach shoes worn with thin synthetic socks, and accepting that your feet will be wet. Dry them every night, powder them with antifungal, and rinse off the caustic residue.
- A **second pair**, so one can air out.
- **Trekking poles with rubber tips.** Don't use metal carbide tips; scraping the city's stone would likely be read as disrespectful, the way scuffing a church floor would be. Ask a local.

### Hands
- **Thin, grippy, waterproof gloves** for handrails and walls. You'll want to touch walls for balance constantly, and those walls are coated in bacterial films, snails, and caustic water. Wear something nitrile-coated.

### Electronics and Paper
- **Everything must be sealed.** Humidity in the high nineties, condensation on every cool surface, and mildly caustic water will destroy unprotected devices fast. Use waterproof cases and silica gel packs, and replace the packs often.
- Cameras need anti-fog lenses and sealed housings. The light is extraordinary: the "sieving" of flock-shadow, the gleam of the wet towers at dusk. You'll want to photograph it. Get used to your lens fogging each time you move between the warm outside and any cooler interior.
- Paper will turn to pulp. Bring **waterproof notebooks** and pencils rather than ink. Ossaran bookbinding, glued with nest-glue, is famously water-resistant. A locally bound journal might be both the most practical and the most beautiful thing you bring home.

### Miscellaneous
- A **dry bag** for your sleeping clothes, so that at least one set of garments stays dry.
- **Headlamp** with a red mode for the undercity and the Cup. It's dim there, and you don't want to blind bats.
- **Water purification** with the capacity to handle mineral content, not just microbes (see below).
- A **small, packable towel**, knowing it will never truly dry.
- **A dust brush**, like a clothes brush, to sweep midges and dust from shoulders and hat.

## Water and Food

**Do not drink wall water.** It weeps from every surface, bitter and slick. It is laced with lime and guano residue and slightly caustic, and it kills seeds within a day. Don't let it sit in your mouth either. Watch Ossarans. They must have clean water from somewhere, likely condensation collected in specific ways, cistern water, or supply brought in through the Throats. Ask what visitors drink. Carry your own purification, ideally a system that can deal with dissolved minerals. A simple filter won't take out alkalinity.

**Hydrate aggressively.** You will sweat constantly and it won't evaporate. You'll lose salts faster than you realize. Rehydration salts every day, not just when you feel bad.

**Food.** Nothing grows in Ossary. Everything that isn't animal comes up the escarpment on cable-lifts, presumably traded against fertilizer. That means:
- Imported grains, vegetables, and fruit probably exist but may be expensive or preserved.
- Local foods are likely to involve midges (chitin flour, pressed cakes), rats, gulls, and whatever else Ossarans consider edible. Go in with an open mind and a strong stomach. Midge flour is probably protein-rich and faintly shrimp-like.
- **Be very careful about anything involving swifts.** Killing a swift is the city's oldest taboo. Don't ask to eat swift, swift eggs, or nest-based food unless a local offers it, and even then, ask carefully whether it's appropriate. In our world, swiftlet nests are a delicacy; in Ossary they are glue and industry. Don't assume.
- Pack a supply of familiar, sealed, calorie-dense food for days when your stomach rebels.

**Gifts.** A city that grows nothing may treasure fresh things: fruit, fragrant herbs, flowers, fine woods. Seeds will die, so don't bother, and don't try to "plant" anything as a gesture. Something green and alive, given as a short-lived gift, might be very moving to someone who has never seen a leaf grow. It might also be meaningless, or even strange to them. Ask before you bring anything organic in bulk. Hitchhiking pests in a closed ecology are a concern anywhere.

## Arrival: The Ascent

You'll likely approach across the brine flats: a hundred miles of hot, shallow, lifeless-looking water carpeted in microbial mats. It will be very hot and there will be midges. On warm afternoons they rise in a smoky brown haze. Cross in the morning if you can, and wear your goggles and a mask.

From the flats you'll see the escarpment, a wall of pale sweating limestone almost a mile high with its permanent cloud on top. Take a moment to look. It is one of the few times you'll see Ossary whole.

The way up is probably by cable-lift, the same system that sends fertilizer down. If you're afraid of heights, prepare yourself. You'll ride a mile up a sheer face, through a rising river of warm, midge-laden air, and likely into the edge of the cloud. The Column runs the eastern rim, so if you come up on that side you'll be ascending *through* the insects. Goggles, mask, and a hood sealed around your face. It is also a strange and spectacular way to arrive: carried up into the city by the same wind that feeds it.

When you crest the rim, expect the sound to hit you all at once.

## The First Days: What It Will Actually Feel Like

**Day one** will be overwhelming. The shriek of the swifts sits at the top edge of your hearing and doesn't stop. Beneath it runs the wingbeat roar, like surf or distant trains. Beneath that runs the fans' slow pulse at about forty beats a minute, felt in the stone through your feet. Over all of it come the kilns, the rail carts, and the steam whistles. Your eyes will keep trying to parse the dark haze overhead as smoke. It isn't. It's birds, moving.

The smell, which the text never states, I'd expect to be powerful: ammonia and guano, lime-burning, coal smoke, hot wet stone, something like roasted shellfish from the chitin mills, and the green-copper mineral smell of the films. You'll stop noticing it within a few days, which is a warning of its own: you'll also stop noticing how much dust you're breathing. Keep wearing the mask.

**Day two or three** is where the "half-mad" part starts. Sleep will be poor. The noise never lets you drop fully. You'll be damp everywhere, your feet will ache, and you'll feel faintly itchy. You may be irritable, weepy, or disoriented. This is normal. Plan nothing important for these days. Find one quiet room, which will be relatively quiet at best, perhaps somewhere under chitin windows where the light goes the color of weak tea, and rest there.

**By the end of the first week**, something may shift. Ossarans don't notice the screaming anymore. You won't reach that point, but you may begin to hear it the way you hear rain. The motion of every surface, the snails crackling like frying, the beetles in the gutters, the flickering shadow of the flocks, may stop feeling like infestation and start feeling like a city breathing. A lot of visitors either leave before this point or fall in love just after it.

**Practical habits for the first week:**
- Every night, rinse your skin, especially hands and feet, with clean water and a mild acidic rinse. Then dry, powder, and inspect for cracks or rashes.
- Change mask filters and wash your goggles.
- Brush out your clothes and hat before going indoors.
- Check your hair and ears for midges. You'll be surprised how many you find.
- Drink more water than feels reasonable.

## The Rules, and How to Honor Them

### Nothing Is Flat
Tables are tilted, and each has carved rings to hold cups. Use them. Set every cup, bowl, and bottle in a ring, or it will slide, and you'll look like a child. Don't put your belongings on the floor expecting them to stay put; floors slope toward drains. Hang your bag, don't set it down. If you're given a place to sleep, notice how the bed is angled and don't try to "fix" it.

### No One Kills a Swift
**This is the single most important thing to prepare for.** It is the city's oldest law, and a deliberate violation means exile down the escarpment.

The danger for a visitor is reflex. A swift will come at your face at the Turning, or blunder into you in a narrow duct, and your hand will come up to swat it. Practice *not* doing that before you go. Train yourself, when something flies at your face, to duck, turn your head, and cover your eyes with a forearm rather than striking out. Wear goggles precisely so you don't need to flail.

Watch your feet too. Swifts can't take off easily from the ground, and dead and dying birds fall every day. Look before you step, especially on stairs at dusk. If you find a grounded, living swift, don't step on it, don't pick it up without guidance, and ask a local what to do. There are designated collectors for the dead, and there may be a practice for the living. If you find a dead one, don't touch it. Leave it for the collectors, whose work is ceremonial.

Don't joke about killing birds, even in exasperation at the noise. Ossarans have heard every visitor complain about the screaming. Complaining is forgivable. Wishing them dead is not.

### The Turning Is Sacred
At dawn and dusk, the city stops. The flocks lift or settle, the fans gear down, and the roar swells until you can't hear someone shouting beside you.

- **Know the times.** Ask your hosts on arrival, and make a habit of checking the sky and the light. The Turning comes every day, twice.
- **Get off the stairs.** The flocks at the Turning are dense enough to knock a person off a stairway. Find a wall, stand against it, and wait.
- **Finish your sentences first.** Ossaran children are taught this. As you feel the roar building, wrap up what you're saying. Don't start a new thought. Don't try to talk through it. Everyone waits in silence, or in what passes for silence.
- **Don't photograph with flash**, don't point lights at the flocks, and don't wave your arms.
- **Don't stand at the mouths of Throats or chimneys at the Turning.** A million birds entering a chimney in twenty minutes is a force like a river.

Treat the Turning the way you'd treat a moment of prayer in a place you don't fully understand. Watching it from a safe alcove, especially from the western rim at sunset, with the black spirals of swifts funneling down into a field of glowing wet towers, will probably be the most extraordinary thing you ever see.

### Time Is Measured in Strata
Ossarans think slowly and in layers. They date their histories by guano beds (the Year of the Thin Bed, the Deep Century). A new building isn't truly part of the city until a generation has colored it with films, the snails have softened its corners, and the swifts have taken it. Don't be impatient. Don't call something "old" if it's merely a century. Ask people about their district's strata. It may be the Ossaran equivalent of asking about someone's family history.

### Other Etiquette, by Intuition
- **Don't touch the walls casually**, especially old ones with deep bruise-purple films or grooves worn by eight hundred years of drips. These are probably loved things. Touch railings, not faces of buildings, and use gloves.
- **Don't peel the films**, even where they come away like wet paper. It's like peeling bark from a sacred tree.
- **Don't scrape or clean anything.** The snails are the city's cleaners and its sculptors.
- **Don't damage spider sheets** spanning courtyards if you can avoid them. They're likely valued for what they catch.
- **Be wary of gulls in the harbor wards.** They're huge, loud, vicious, and prey on young swifts. Don't feed them. Whether Ossarans tolerate them or hate them, feeding a swift predator is not a neutral act.
- **Rats are unafraid.** Don't leave food open. Don't sleep on the floor, and on a slope you wouldn't want to anyway. Keep bags closed.

## Districts and Dangers

**The eastern rim and the Throats.** This is where the Column pours over the lip. It has the thickest midges, strong updrafts, enormous fans, and ducts wide enough for carts. Never enter a duct or approach a fan intake without a guide. The suction of house-sized industrial fans is not something you'll survive falling into. At midnight, the Column becomes a vertical river of bats. Wear goggles and a hat, keep your mouth closed, and wear your mask.

**The lower Guttering.** Rain all year. Wear oilcloth. The stairs run with water and are the most treacherous in the city. Walk slowly and use handrails.

**The Kiln Heights.** No rain in a generation, only constant fine condensation. Expect heat, kiln smoke, and lime dust. The respirator matters here.

**The industrial wards and Guano Works.** This is the heaviest guano exposure and the highest fungal risk. Mask up, and don't go into the mining beds without explicit permission and proper protection.

**The Chitin Mills.** There will be fine insect-protein dust. Serious allergy risk. Wear a mask.

**The Fanworks.** Coal-fired, belt-driven, and ancient. It's loud enough to damage hearing, so wear full ear protection. Moving belts and gears mean loose clothing and hair should be secured.

**The undercity:** drainage tunnels, old quarries, and cisterns. This is bat territory. It is dark, wet, and full of rats. Go only with someone who knows it. Use a red light. Rabies vaccination matters here.

**The Cup.** The oldest quarter sits in a basin it has sunk into over time. It is always wet, always dim, older than any record. The oldest flues hold swifts nearly twice normal size, with nests like washbasins, and their scream is a half-tone deeper. My intuition says: visit the Cup by day, with a local, masked and quiet. Don't be there at dusk on your first visit. It's not because anyone has said it's dangerous. It's because it's the place where the city is most itself, and you should earn that.

## The Thing They Don't Like to Discuss

Ossarans don't like talking to visitors about the swifts getting larger, or about the patterns in the flocks, or about the notion of a second city in the air made of birds with its own fixed roads.

**Don't press.** If you've read about it, keep that to yourself unless someone raises it. Asking a nest-gatherer about the "roads in the air" on your second day is like asking a stranger about the family member no one mentions. If you spend enough time, show enough respect, stand quietly through enough Turnings, and maybe help carry something, someone may tell you on their own. That will mean something.

**Watch for it yourself.** On still evenings when the Column is calm, pick a spot and watch the same patch of sky for several nights running. See whether the same spirals form over the same districts. You don't need anyone's permission to look.

**Keep an honest mind about it.** The birds are growing. The city is sinking into its own stone while building upward. Something in this place moves on a timescale longer than any human life. You don't need to decide what it means. But prepare yourself emotionally for the feeling that you are a guest not only of the Ossarans, but of something else, something the Ossarans are guests of too. People who visit places like this sometimes come home changed, or unsettled, or with a dream that recurs. That's worth knowing before you go.

## Leaving

**Decontaminate before you go down.** Wash your clothes thoroughly or leave the worst of them behind. Clean your boots; guano and film residue carry fungal spores and bacteria. Check your bags for beetles, snail eggs, and spiders. You don't want to carry Ossary's ecology into another world, and a closed system like this shouldn't have its organisms scattered anyway.

**Watch your health for several weeks after.** Fungal lung infections can show up days to weeks after exposure. If you develop fever, a persistent cough, chest pain, or fatigue, tell a doctor exactly where you were: "a city built on bird and bat guano." Also watch for unexplained fevers (leptospirosis), and tell a doctor about any bat contact, however minor.

**Expect the reverse shock.** When you get home, the world will seem oddly flat. Tables will feel dangerous without cup-rings. Silence will ring in your ears. Dry air will feel thin and hostile against your skin. You may wake at dawn and dusk expecting the roar and find yourself holding your breath, waiting for the Turning. Some visitors say that for weeks afterward, empty skies look wrong, as if a city had been taken away from above them.

---

## In Short

Train your legs for wet stairs. Protect your lungs, eyes, ears, skin, and feet. Seal everything electronic. Never drink the walls. Learn not to swat. Stop and stand against a wall when the Turning comes, and finish your sentence first. Don't touch the living films or ask the nest-gatherers about the roads in the air. Watch the sky anyway.

Go to the western rim at sunset at least once. Stand there with the whole wet city glowing below you, pale towers sweating light, while a hundred million birds come down out of the sky into it like smoke running backward. That is what you came for.
