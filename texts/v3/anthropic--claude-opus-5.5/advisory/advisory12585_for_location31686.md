# Preparing for the Delta That Builds Itself

*A traveler's guide for an ordinary human body*

## The Short Version

Most visitors worry first about the slack. They imagine a floor dissolving under them. That can happen, and you will learn to avoid it. But what wears ordinary visitors down, and what sends them home early or badly hurt, is usually quieter: **the heat that won't let you cool, the noise that won't let you rest, and the wet that won't let your skin dry.** The slack is dramatic. Those three are relentless.

Prepare for them in that order. Then prepare for the rest.

---

## Part One: Before You Go (Weeks to Months Ahead)

### 1. Heat acclimatization is not optional

The description says the air sits "around body temperature" and heavy with moisture. That should alarm you. Your body cools itself by evaporating sweat, and in air that is both hot and near-saturated, sweat barely evaporates. It just runs off you. If the air is at or above your skin temperature and fully humid, you cannot shed heat at all, and your core temperature slowly climbs whether you exert yourself or not.

The delta is survivable, since people live there in huge numbers. The locals are adapted, though, and you aren't. So:

- **Train in heat for 10–14 days before departure.** Use a sauna, a hot room, or exercise in heavy clothing. Heat acclimatization is real and measurable. Your sweat gets more dilute, your plasma volume expands, and your heart rate under heat stress drops. It fades within a few weeks, so time it close to departure.
- **Get a medical checkup.** Heart conditions, kidney issues, diabetes, and medications like diuretics, beta-blockers, antihistamines, some antidepressants, and stimulants all affect how you handle heat. Discuss them with a doctor who understands you're going somewhere that will stress your thermoregulation for days on end.
- **Learn the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke** in yourself and in a companion: confusion, stopping sweating, headache, nausea, clumsiness, irritability. In a place where you can't hear each other, agree beforehand on a hand signal that means *"I am overheating, get me somewhere cool now."*

### 2. Your hearing will be under siege

Assume the ambient sound is permanently above safe exposure levels. The glaze-cracks, described as "a constant rain of distant gunshots," are impulse noise, which is especially damaging. The pump-beat is infrasound, which you feel rather than hear, and which is known to cause unease, nausea, pressure in the chest, and a vague sense of dread.

- **Get a baseline hearing test** before you leave, so you can tell afterward whether you came home damaged.
- **Get custom-molded earplugs** from an audiologist, the high-fidelity kind that attenuate evenly. Also bring a set of over-ear protectors to double up with in the loudest districts. Doubling protection helps, but not additively.
- **Don't plan to block everything.** The locals wear soft clay seals, not total isolation, because the ground's vibrations and certain pitches carry vital warnings. Your feet and skin will do most of the listening, so protecting your ears doesn't cost you safety. But practice moving through a loud environment with protection in, so the muffled world doesn't disorient you.
- **Expect tinnitus.** Bring whatever helps you tolerate it.

### 3. Learn to talk with your hands

Nobody here speaks aloud. You will be a mute stranger in a nation of fluent signers. Some preparation makes a huge difference:

- **If you know any sign language, refresh it.** It won't be the delta's language, but it trains the mental habits: watching faces and hands, using space, using your body for grammar.
- **If you don't, learn to mime well.** Practice clear, slow, exaggerated gestures for the essentials: water, food, sleep, pain, help, lost, thank you, *which way is the make?*, *is this safe?*, *I don't understand, please slow down.*
- **Prepare a small set of printed pictograms** on a waterproof card: water drop, cup, bed, a red cross, a question mark, a building falling. Don't assume the locals read, or read your script, but pictures often work.
- **Understand touch-speech before you use it.** Up close, people "press palms and read each other's vibrations." This is intimate communication, and there will be customs around who touches whom and how. When someone offers you their palm, watch what they do. Don't initiate it until you understand it.

### 4. Check your body for anything the fields might touch

The fields that lift the silt produce a skin-prickle and a metallic taste. Nobody knows what they are, and you should treat that as a warning.

- **Pacemakers, insulin pumps, cochlear implants, neurostimulators, and any electronic medical device: reconsider this trip seriously.** You cannot know how they'll behave.
- **Metal implants, piercings, dental work:** probably fine, but note any warmth, tingling, or pain and move away from strong-field zones if it happens.
- **Electronics:** assume they may fail, glitch, or drift. Bring them if you like, but depend on nothing electronic. Compasses may be unreliable.

### 5. Vaccinations and gut preparation

You'll be eating food cooked in dense markets, in hot, wet conditions, surrounded by eels, midges, roaches, and bacterial mats. Get your routine immunizations updated. Consider typhoid, hepatitis A, and hepatitis B, and ask a travel-medicine specialist what a hot, wet, dense, insect-heavy environment calls for. Bring a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics prescribed for travelers' diarrhea, plus oral rehydration salts, which you'll need anyway.

## Part Two: What to Bring

The first rule of packing: **everything you bring, you carry, always.** Nothing left in place survives. There is no hotel room to return to. Your room might be mud by tomorrow.

Pack light enough to run. That's not a figure of speech. When the dogs run, you run with them.

### Clothing

- **Loose, light, fast-drying synthetic or merino clothing.** Cotton will never dry here. Two or three sets at most. You'll be damp in all of them.
- **Light colors,** to reflect the strong, clear sun.
- **A wide-brimmed hat** that sheds rain and shade.
- **No rain jacket in the usual sense.** In body-temperature air, a waterproof shell is a sauna. Get soaked in the afternoon rain instead. It's warm, and it's actually one of the few times you'll cool down, as the wet surfaces steam afterward. Just protect your *gear* from it.

### Feet: the most important item you'll pack

You'll walk on warm new glaze that is **slippery when wet, and it is always wet**, on softening mud, and through brown gutter runoff full of eels.

- **Boots or sturdy sandals with aggressive, soft-rubber soles** for grip on glazed surfaces. Test them on wet tile before you commit.
- **They must drain.** Waterproof boots will fill with sweat and runoff and never empty. Choose footwear designed to get wet and shed water.
- **Many pairs of quick-dry socks** and a way to dry them, even partially, which usually means on your pack in the post-rain sun.
- **Foot care is survival care.** Trench foot and fungal infection are real risks when feet stay wet for days. Take your shoes off whenever you safely can, especially in the keels. Air your feet. Inspect them daily.

### Skin

- **Antifungal cream and powder.** Powder will cake in the humidity, but use it anyway at night and in the drier hours after rain.
- **An anti-chafing balm** for thighs, armpits, and under pack straps.
- **Zinc oxide or barrier cream** for maceration, the white, wrinkled, splitting skin you get from constant wetness.
- **Sunscreen** that is sweat-resistant, though you'll reapply it constantly.
- **Insect repellent** for the midges and mud-wasps.
- **A serious wound kit.** In wet heat, small cuts get infected fast. Include antiseptic, waterproof dressings, and antibiotic ointment, and clean every scratch immediately. The glazed edges of freshly set structures and the patchwork shells of the glass crabs may both cut.

### Water

The groundwater is "faintly metallic." The rain is "mineral-heavy, slightly grey with suspended silt, tasting of iron and chalk." Neither is something to drink raw for long.

- **A good filter** handles the silt and microbes.
- **Heavy metals and dissolved minerals are the harder problem.** Ordinary filters don't remove them. If you can, bring a reverse-osmosis unit or a supply of trusted water, and ask locals what they drink. They've solved this problem, so learn their solution.
- **Oral rehydration salts, lots of them.** You'll sweat continuously. Drinking plain water without replacing salts can make you sick in a different way.
- **Drink on a schedule, not by thirst.** In this heat, thirst lags behind need.

### Hearing and sleep

- Custom earplugs, plus a backup pair, plus over-ear protection.
- An eye mask, because you may be sleeping in crowded, lit markets.
- Possibly a **sleep aid** discussed with your doctor. You won't sleep well at first, and in the heat, sleep deprivation compounds everything else.

### Navigation

- **Don't bring a map. There isn't one.** Streets shift daily.
- **Bring a notebook and a waterproof pencil.** Your map will be a list of keels, the only fixed landmarks, with rough bearings between them taken from the sun. The light, as the description puts it, "behaves as expected." It's the one thing that does, so use it.
- **A simple analog watch.** Rhythms matter here: the afternoon storms, the dusk bat-flights, the pace of fronts.

### Other gear

- **A small, very tough waterproof dry bag** for anything that must stay dry.
- **A light cloth partition or sarong.** Locals hang cloth to make private spaces in new towers. You'll want one too, for sleeping, changing, and privacy. It also serves as shade, towel, and sling.
- **Something to trade or give.** Markets fill every terrace. Find out beforehand what's valued. Portable, durable goods might mean a lot in a place where nothing that stays put survives.
- **A small cooking pot and cup** only if you plan to prepare your own food. Otherwise eat at the stalls, which is part of the point.

## Part Three: Arrival

### The first hour

The noise will hit you before anything else. Put your ear protection in *before* you arrive if you can. Even so, the pump-beat will come up through your feet and into your ribs, and it may bring a wave of anxiety or nausea. That is normal. It's the infrasound, not danger. Breathe slowly. Find something to lean on. Let your body register that the pressure is constant and therefore, strangely, background.

Then the heat will land. Move slowly from the first minute. Don't hurry, don't carry heavy loads, and find shade.

### Find a guide

This is the most valuable thing you can do. Find a local, or a long-time outsider resident, and hire, trade with, or befriend them. They'll know how to read the swifts, the crabs, and the pitch of the fields. They'll teach you signs. They'll know which towers are fresh, which districts the slack is approaching, and where the nearest keel is.

Without a guide, you are a stranger on ground that will liquefy under you on a schedule you can't read.

### Learn the warning signs immediately

Before anything else, learn these:

1. **When the mudswifts leave a district all at once, leave.** They sense the slack about a day ahead. This is your long-range warning.
2. **Dull glaze and softening edges mean the slack has arrived.** Get off the structure and onto something freshly made.
3. **Glass crabs swarming mean you're in or near a slack zone.** Keep moving.
4. **When the dogs run, run with them.** This warns of an out-of-rhythm local collapse, the most dangerous kind, because it doesn't follow the pattern. Don't stop to understand it. Follow the dogs.
5. **The prickle and metallic taste mean a making is underway nearby.** Probably not dangerous in itself, but don't stand in the middle of it. Rising silt columns and curing glaze are not a place to linger.

## Part Four: Living There Day to Day

### The daily rhythm, as you'll probably experience it

**Morning** comes up pink over the rooftops, and it will be the most comfortable part of your day. Do your walking, climbing, and moving in the morning.

**Midday** is heavy and brutal. Rest in shade, under dripping arches, or in the interior of a new tower. Drink water. Don't exert yourself.

**Afternoon** brings the thunderheads, the second city of cloud. Watch them build. When the rain comes, warm, hard, and mineral-heavy, the whole delta becomes a drum, and for once you'll *hear* something distinct above the roar. It may move you more than you expect. Many visitors report crying during their first storm and not knowing why. Let it soak you. Protect your gear.

**After the rain,** the sun breaks through, surfaces blaze, and steam rises off the terraces. This is your chance to dry socks and skin, and the evaporation may give you some real relief from the heat.

**Dusk** brings the bat-rivers boiling out from under the arches, and the faint bluish glow of biofilms along the wet seams. It's beautiful. It's also when the midges are worst, so apply repellent.

**Night** is hot, crowded, and loud. You'll sleep in fragments at first.

### Where to sleep

Freshly set structures are the safest ground: they've got the longest before the slack. Climb in with everyone else while the glaze is still warm. Hang your cloth, sit near people, and watch how they settle. Sleep near a family or group if you're welcome. They'll wake before you if something changes.

**If you can reach a keel, sleep there sometimes.** The roar drops to "something like heavy surf." The water is cool. You'll rest better than anywhere else in the delta, and your body will get a real chance to recover from heat and noise. Treat it as your recovery base.

### Eating

- The **eels** are abundant and a staple. Eat them well cooked. They feed on bacterial mats, so undercooked eel is a gamble.
- Market food cooked hot in front of you is generally safer than anything that has been sitting.
- Nothing grows here. There are no vegetables and no fruit unless they're traded in from outside. Consider bringing **vitamin supplements**, especially vitamin C, if you're staying more than a couple of weeks.
- **Salt your food.** You need it.

### Moving around

- **Assume yesterday's route no longer exists.** Streets shift a few meters, plazas become stairs, domes become spires. The district will be "recognizably of the same family, but never identical." This is disorienting in a deep, animal way, like returning home to find your furniture subtly rearranged. Navigate by keels, sun, and the general direction of the sea, not by street memory.
- **Move with the crowd.** The human flow follows the make. If everyone around you is packing up, pack up.
- **Watch every step on glaze.** Falls on wet glazed ramps are probably the most common injury.
- **Avoid the gutters.** The mud runs fast and thick and writhes with eels. It's shallow, but the footing is terrible and the water isn't clean. Keep cuts well away from it.

## Part Five: Dangers You Should Take Seriously

### The heat, again

It's worth repeating because it's the danger most likely to kill a visitor. Heat builds slowly and clouds your judgment as it rises. You'll feel sluggish, then irritable, then confused, and a confused person makes bad decisions on dissolving ground.

**Watch your companion, and have them watch you.** If either of you stops sweating, becomes confused, or collapses, get to a keel or the coolest shade you can find, wet the skin, fan it, and seek local help urgently.

### Out-of-rhythm slack

The fronts are predictable. The sudden local collapses aren't. This is why you follow the dogs, stay near locals, and never linger alone in the older, softening parts of a district.

### Digging

**Don't dig.** Not for curiosity, samples, or any other reason. The description is clear: expeditions that dig deep find the mud "getting warmer and warmer and more intricately ordered, until it sings in the hand. Then they come back up, or they don't." Whatever runs beneath the delta is not something an ordinary human should go looking for.

### The biofilms and bacterial mats

Don't touch them with bare skin, especially broken skin. Don't taste them. Don't scrape them from the keels. The glowing ones in particular respond to the fields in some unknown way, and that's reason enough to leave them alone.

### The glass crabs

They probably aren't aggressive, but their shells are fused shards of glaze, sharp and hard. Don't pick them up or kick them. If the locals are right that they're "part of the city's machinery," interfering with them may carry consequences you can't foresee.

### Sensory exhaustion

This is real and cumulative. By day three or four you may feel frayed, jumpy, tearful, and unable to concentrate. That comes from noise, heat, fragmented sleep, and constant change. It isn't weakness. Plan keel visits specifically to recover. Stay long enough to hear your own voice. It will help more than you'd expect.

## Part Six: Customs and Respect

- **Don't speak loudly out of frustration.** It's pointless anyway, but raising your voice or shouting in a keel, where speech is reserved for weddings, grief, and hearing oneself, would likely be deeply rude.
- **Speak in the keels only as the locals do.** Watch first. These are the places of spoken ceremony. You may be invited to say something. If you are, treat it as the honor it is.
- **Don't take pieces of the city.** It may seem harmless to pocket a shard of glaze, but a crab might have worn it, a building was made of it, and you don't know what it means.
- **Don't take anything from a keel.** Not biofilm, not a mudswift's cup, not a chip of worn stone. These are the city's memory.
- **Respect the claim that they didn't build this.** The people are "adamant" that they inherited the city and did not make it. Don't praise them for building it or ask them how it works as though they're engineers. Ask instead how they *live* with it. That's what they know, and they know it with enormous depth.
- **Share when you're sharing space.** New towers fill within hours, people pile in together, and cooking, trading, and sleeping happen shoulder to shoulder. Be a good neighbor. Offer food. Hold a child's hand crossing a ramp if asked.

## Part Seven: Leaving

This part catches people unprepared.

### The silence will hit you

When you step out of the delta, the silence will be enormous and strange. The description notes that children raised there are frightened by silence and sleep poorly outside. You'll feel an echo of that. Your ears may ring loudly in the quiet. You may feel the pump-beat for days after it's gone, a phantom pressure in your ribs. You may feel oddly unmoored in a world where buildings stand still.

**Plan a decompression period.** Give yourself a few quiet, cool, restful days before resuming normal life. Keep low background sound on for sleep if you need it. Let your body relearn stillness.

### Get checked

- **A follow-up hearing test.** Compare it with your baseline.
- **A skin and foot check.** Fungal infections and slow-healing cuts can linger.
- **Watch your gut** for a few weeks.
- **Mention the fields to your doctor,** even though there's nothing specific to test for. Note anything unusual: persistent metallic taste, tingling, strange dreams.

### You may miss it

This is the part nobody warns you about. People who spend real time in the delta often come back aching for it: the drumming rain, the blaze of wet terraces in the low sun, the bat-rivers at dusk, the swifts pouring across the sky like smoke, the crowded warmth of a new tower filling with families, and the cool quiet of a keel where, for once, you could hear yourself speak.
