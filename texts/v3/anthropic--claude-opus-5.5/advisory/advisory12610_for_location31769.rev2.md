# Preparing for the Ringing Heights

The Heights will not kill you in any dramatic way. They will wear you down. You'll be cold and damp all the time, short of breath, half-deaf, sore-throated, cut in a dozen small places, and short on sleep, all under a sky that never gets properly bright. People who prepare well come back with stories. People who don't come back hoarse and shaking, with tinnitus and a cough that lasts a season.

The deeper preparation is psychological. This is a place where you cannot be neutral. Every sound you make adds something to the world, and every silence takes something away. Go ready to participate.

---

## 1. Your body: altitude, cold, and wet

### The altitude is the first real danger

The description is quiet about this, but the clues are all there. The sky is indigo at noon, you can see the brighter stars in daylight, and the sun gives no warmth. That is extreme elevation. The locals have lived here for nine centuries and are presumably adapted. You are not.

- **Don't ride the cable straight to a summit spire on day one.** The cable car comes up out of the cloud fast, and that is how people get altitude sickness. If at all possible, stay a few nights at a lower spire, or at the cable station itself, before going higher. Treat the cloud-top as your base camp.
- **Talk to a doctor about acetazolamide** and about carrying dexamethasone for emergencies. Learn the signs of HAPE and HACE (pulmonary and cerebral edema): a wet cough, breathlessness at rest, confusion, loss of coordination. The only reliable cure is to go down, and going down here means a cable ride. Know where the nearest downward line is at all times.
- **Pack a pulse oximeter.** It's small, cheap, and tells you honestly how you're doing when your own judgment has gone foggy.
- **Get fit before you go, especially for stairs and ladders.** The spires are vertical tangles of galleries. The old buildings are entered through their upper windows because stone has buried the ground floors. You'll be climbing all day in thin air. Train your legs, your grip, and your upper body, and do it with a weighted pack.

### The cold is wet cold, which is worse

It almost never rains, but the fog tide rolls up and down the mountains every day and leaves water on everything. Cables drip and iron is always slick. Wet cold steals heat far faster than dry cold, and this is the hypothermia that sneaks up on people.

- **No cotton. Anywhere.** Wool or synthetic base layers, a fleece or wool mid-layer, and a truly waterproof, breathable shell. Bring a spare dry base layer in a sealed bag, and keep one set for sleeping only.
- **Carry at least two pairs of gloves.** One pair should be thin, grippy, and cut-resistant. The other should be warm, insulated, and waterproof. You'll swap between them constantly.
- **Wear a warm hat that fits under ear protection.** More on ear protection below.
- **Bring wool socks, many of them, and foot powder.** Your feet will be damp for days. Trench-foot-style skin breakdown is a real risk on a long stay. Change socks every evening, dry your feet completely, and let them breathe.

### The light will starve you

The stone here drinks light, the smoke dims the sun, and the locals live by furnace-glow. On a long visit this affects mood and sleep.

- **Consider vitamin D supplements** if you're staying more than a couple of weeks.
- **A small light-therapy lamp** is worth packing if you're prone to seasonal low mood.
- **Glacier glasses anyway.** High altitude means intense UV whenever the sun breaks through the smoke. Snow-blindness can happen even when it doesn't feel bright.

## 2. Your ears and your voice

This is the part that makes the Heights unlike any other mountain.

### Protect your hearing, but don't seal yourself off

A steam hammer the size of a chapel lands every eleven seconds. Around it are trip-hammers, stamping mills, bells, deliberately unoiled cable wheels that shriek, and bellowers that moan across the valleys in shifts. Near a Strike, the noise is almost certainly past the level that causes permanent hearing damage within minutes to hours.

- **Get a baseline hearing test (audiogram) before you go,** and another when you come back.
- **Bring musician's earplugs,** the flat-attenuation kind that lower volume evenly without muffling speech. These will be your everyday wear. You can still talk, still hear warnings, and still take part in the life of the place.
- **Bring over-ear defenders** for close work near a Strike, stamping mills, or bell-towers, or for cable rides in the loud iron gondolas. Wear them over the earplugs when you're right next to the big machinery.
- **Bring foam earplugs for sleeping,** at least at first. Locals sleep through the noise the way sailors sleep through the sea. You won't, not for a while. After a week or so, try sleeping without them. Many visitors find the rhythm starts to feel like weather.
- **Expect to feel the Strike in your chest.** Low-frequency thumps travel through the body as well as the ears. Earplugs don't stop that. It's normal, and it's nothing to worry about.

**A social warning about ear protection:** locals may read heavy ear protection as a slight, a sign that you're shutting out the work that holds their world up. Wear what keeps you safe, but consider low-profile filters for social settings, and be ready to explain, cheerfully and loudly, that you're from somewhere quiet.

### Your voice is a tool, a courtesy, and a construction material

In the Heights, your voice literally becomes stone. A shout leaves a grain. The human voice makes the pale, fine-grained stone the most expensive houses are carved from. Whole choirs sing in rotation to thicken the ground under cathedrals. So your voice has economic and moral weight here.

You'll also be shouting all day just to be heard. Visitors lose their voices within days.

- **Learn to project from your diaphragm rather than your throat** before you go. A few voice lessons, or even a video course for teachers or actors, will pay off.
- **Pack throat lozenges, honey, and a thermos** for warm drinks. The fog air is damp, which helps, but the coal smoke doesn't.
- **Learn to whistle loudly.** Children here learn to whistle before they learn to read. A good two-finger whistle is practical: it cuts through noise and calls attention without straining your voice. It's also a small courtesy, because you're contributing.
- **Learn a few songs you can sing well,** or at least hold one steady note. If you're invited to join a choir-shift, even briefly, say yes. You'll leave a little of yourself in the foundations of someone's home, which is a rare thing to be offered.

### Communicate without sound too

When you can't be heard, you need other ways to talk.

- **Waterproof notebook and pencil.** Ink runs in the fog.
- **Learn local hand signs** as early as you can. Every loud industrial culture develops them. Ask a foundry hand to teach you the basics: stop, danger, come here, all clear, which way, how long.

## 3. The terrain: everything is sharp and everything is slick

### The rock cuts

These mountains have never weathered. Their edges are keen enough to cut rope, and the summits have the conchoidal ripple of volcanic glass. Treat bare rock like broken obsidian, because functionally it is.

- **Stiff-soled, heavy leather mountaineering boots,** well broken in before you go. Soft trail shoes will be sliced apart. Consider boots with a protective rand around the toe and sides.
- **Gaiters,** for the sharp scree and for the wet ferns.
- **Cut-resistant gloves** (the kind rated for handling glass or sheet metal) whenever you touch rock. Never put your bare hand down on a black, fresh-looking surface to steady yourself.
- **Knee and shin protection** if you plan to scramble.
- **Don't trust ordinary climbing rope.** Where rope must run over rock, use edge protectors, or use chain and steel cable the way the locals do. If you hire a guide for scrambling, ask what they use and why.
- **Bring a serious first-aid kit for lacerations:** wound-closure strips, sterile dressings, hemostatic gauze, tweezers, antiseptic, and a suture kit if you know how to use one. Glass-like cuts are deep, clean, and bleed well.
- **Update your tetanus shot.** Rusted iron is everywhere, and so is dirt from goat herds.

### The iron is wet

The galleries and walkways are old iron, dripping with fog-water and probably slick with soot and moss.

- **Microspikes or strap-on cleats** may help on iron walkways. Test them on your first day, because on some surfaces they make things worse.
- **Always keep one hand on a rail.** Galleries hang over drops of hundreds or thousands of feet into cloud. There's no ground visible below, only the grey plain.
- **Watch for stone creeping over thresholds.** Doorways in old buildings have new rock growing up over them. That makes a trip hazard, and on fresh rock, a cutting hazard.

### Near the machinery

These are working foundries, with sparks, steam, moving belts, trip-hammers, and coal dust.

- **Nothing loose.** No scarves, dangling straps, or long unbound hair around machinery.
- **Wear an outer layer of wool or leather,** not synthetic, near furnaces. Sparks melt synthetics onto your skin.
- **Bring a hard hat or climbing helmet.** Things fall off galleries, including swift-nest stone.

## 4. The air: smoke, coal, and dust

Every chimney trails a brown banner of coal smoke, and in thin, cold air it hangs. You're breathing hard because of the altitude, so you're breathing more of it.

- **Bring P100 or N95 respirator masks,** plenty of them. They get damp and clog quickly in fog.
- **If you have asthma or any lung condition,** think carefully about whether to go. If you do, carry more rescue inhaler than you think you need.
- **Stay away from the dust of dying mountains.** Dead spires shed their rock into the cloud as grey dust. Nobody knows what breathing stone that is becoming sound again does to a human lung, so don't find out.
- **Bring eye drops.** Between smoke, grit, and dry cold at the summits, your eyes will suffer.

---

## 5. Light, and the moths

You'll need your own light. Everyone lives by lamps and furnace-glow.

- **Carry a good headlamp and plenty of spare batteries,** kept warm inside your jacket. Cold kills batteries fast. Consider a hand-crank or dynamo backup. (Cranking makes a noise, which the locals may appreciate.)
- **Expect moths.** Any light draws them: dusty, slow, soot-and-bronze moths a foot across, in the thousands. They're harmless, but a foot-wide moth landing on your face in the dark takes some getting used to.
- **Moth-wing dust is an allergy risk.** If you're sensitive, bring antihistamines, and consider keeping your headlamp on its red setting when you don't need full brightness.
- **Close your windows at night,** or accept the soft drumming against the glass as part of the soundscape.

## 6. The wildlife: mostly friendly, one kind very much not

### Anvil-swifts

These are small black-and-rust birds that nest under foundry galleries in their hundreds of thousands. Their calls build their nests, and the foundries love them.

- **Don't damage or disturb swift colonies.** They're protected by custom if not by law, and they help hold the mountains up. An old colony is several tons of stone hanging overhead, so don't linger directly under a big one.
- **Expect droppings.** A brimmed hat helps.

### Sheergoats

Long-legged, silver-coated, with split hooves hard enough to stand on razor-edges.

- **Give them space.** Anything with hooves that can stand on glass-sharp rock can hurt you if it kicks.
- **Watch what the herds do.** The herders say a herd leaving a ridge is the first sign the rock under it is going quiet. If the goats won't graze somewhere, don't camp there.

### Clicklings

Small, pale, translucent crustacean-things that swarm in the cracks of young stone, rapping their claws and mending the fissures.

- **Don't put your hands in cracks.** They may not be harmful, but you don't know, and a swarm in a crevice is a surprise you don't want at the edge of a drop.
- **Check your boots and pack** before leaving a crag. They live in the stone, and you don't want to carry them home.

### The pale things in the fog

Long, blind, pale creatures live near the cloud-top, where the stone is oldest, and feed on the murmur of dying rock. Nobody likes to talk about them.

**Do not go looking for them.** This is the clearest piece of advice in this guide.

- **Avoid the fog margin below dead or dying spires.** That's their feeding ground.
- **If you hear a slow, wet, contented humming from inside the fog,** move uphill, toward noise and toward people.
- **When your cable dips into the cloud at the midpoint of a span, stay inside the gondola.** Don't open windows. Keep talking.
- **Think about what they eat.** They feed on sound leaking from stone, on the process of things falling quiet. You don't know whether a quiet, frightened human counts as that. Better not to test it. If you're ever near them, make noise.

## 7. The strange places: where the rule frays

These are the reasons many travelers come. They're also where your preparation should be humblest, because nobody fully understands them.

### Echoes that come first

Now and then, you'll shout across a valley and hear the answer half a second before you open your mouth. The old hands say it's stone made from your voice at a later time, releasing its sound early.

- **Prepare yourself mentally.** The first time is deeply unsettling. It's a small, clear break in cause and effect, and some people react with real panic.
- **If an early echo says words you weren't going to say, consider not saying them.** Nobody knows what happens if you refuse, but it's worth thinking about beforehand what you'd do.
- **Don't make a game of it.** The locals don't like it and say so plainly. Take your lead from them.

### Distances that drift

Spires move slowly relative to one another. A mile-long cable can become a mile and forty yards within a decade.

- **Don't trust old maps.** Get the most recent cable survey you can.
- **A compass is nearly useless here** among all this iron, and probably unreliable for stranger reasons too. Navigate by cable routes and by sight of the spires' glow, and hire local guides for anything off the main lines.
- **Build slack into every schedule.** Cables don't run straight, crossings dip into cloud, and routes shift.

### The Listeners

Listeners press their ears to ancient bedrock and sell what they hear: old hammer-strikes, and sometimes voices saying things that haven't been said yet. The city half-believes them.

- **Decide in advance how you'll treat prophecy.** It's easy to shrug at fortune-telling at home. It's harder in a place where you've already heard your own echo arrive early.
- **Be careful with what you learn about yourself.** A Listener might hear your voice in the stone, saying something you haven't said yet. Some people are better off not knowing.
- **If you try listening yourself, pressed to the pale old rock at night,** do it with a companion, and set a time limit.

### The silent summit

At the very top of the tallest peak, above the last gallery, on stone only months old, is a room-sized place with no sound at all. Not your breath, not your heartbeat, not the wind. Some believe the stone comes from the silence, and that the hammers are not building the mountains but holding them back.

**Strong advice: don't go.** Your guide will probably refuse to take you, and you should respect that.

If you insist:

- **Understand what you're walking into.** On Earth, people in anechoic chambers become disoriented within minutes, because they can still hear their own bodies and it unnerves them. This place is worse: you can't hear even your own heartbeat. No one can say whether the silence only takes sound or takes more.
- **Go roped, with a partner outside the silence** who can pull you back. Agree on hand signals, since voices won't carry.
- **Set a very short limit,** a minute or less, and have your partner time it, since your own sense of time may fail.
- **Make sure you're fully acclimatized.** Carry oxygen, and bring the oximeter. This is the highest, coldest point in the Heights.
- **Don't take anything from the rock there,** and don't leave anything either.
- **Be prepared to carry what you feel there for a long time.** People describe it the way they describe a near-drowning.

## 8. Culture and manners: how to be a good guest in a loud world

The central moral fact of the Heights: **silence erodes.** A quiet person is seen the way a wasteful person is seen elsewhere, and a dying district is one that went quiet. Noise here is civic duty, labor, prayer, and survival.

**Be loud, and be generous with it.**
- Greet people loudly. Laugh loudly. Whistle as you walk.
- Don't shush anyone, and especially not children. That would be deeply rude, and perhaps unsettling in a way you won't understand.
- If you need quiet, find a way to explain it politely rather than simply asking for it.

**Never "fix" the noise.**
- Don't oil a shrieking cable wheel. Don't hush a squeaky door. Don't muffle a clattering pipe. These are deliberate.

**Contribute.**
- Offer to take a short turn at a noise-works: ringing a bell, cranking a siren, pulling a bellower rope, joining a choir-shift. Some districts may see this as the best payment a visitor can offer.
- Consider bringing gifts that make noise: a fine brass bell, a good whistle, a hand-cranked siren, a drum. These are likely to be treasured.

**Be careful with machines that make sound.** A battery-powered speaker or recording would presumably make stone too. It might be seen as a miracle, or as something unnatural and offensive. Ask before using any sound-making electronics, and don't assume they'll be welcome.

**Respect the dead spires.** They're the most haunting places in the Heights: iron frames on peaks melting out from under them, girders tilting, the mountain murmuring as it unmakes itself.
- **Physically, don't go.** The ground is literally dissolving.
- **Socially, talk about them gently.** Each was someone's district and someone's family. They died loudly at first, then with increasing quiet.

**Don't ask where the coal comes from** unless you're ready for no answer. Nobody has been below the cloud in living memory. The coal simply arrives. It may be a sore spot, or a sacred mystery, or both.

**Don't damage the belljack, the moss, or the swift colonies.** They're signs of a living mountain. A hillside of shut belljack means the mountain below is going quiet.

## 9. Practical logistics

**Water:** The fog leaves water everywhere, but it's full of soot and whatever else is in the air. Bring a filter and purification tablets, or boil everything. Drink more than you think you need, because altitude and constant shouting dehydrate you.

**Food:** You'll likely eat goat, goat milk and cheese, and whatever is hauled up with the coal. Bring enough calorie-dense food of your own for the first few days while your stomach adjusts, and for emergencies if a cable line stops.

**Money:** Find out what the locals value before you go. Useful things like good lamps, tools, and warm clothing may trade better than coins, and noise-making gifts better still.

**Cable travel:**
- If you're afraid of heights, prepare seriously. Spans are a mile across, and every journey dips into blind grey cloud before rising again.
- Bring motion-sickness remedies for the sway.
- Wear ear protection. The gondolas are iron and loud on purpose.
- Keep a small emergency kit on you for every crossing: water, food, a warm layer, a light, and a whistle.

**Electronics:** Most of your gear will struggle with wet, cold, and constant vibration. Waterproof everything, and bring analog backups: a paper notebook, a mechanical watch, a hand-crank light.

**Timekeeping:** Your nearest Strike falls every eleven seconds. Locals probably tell time by counting strikes or shifts. Learn their system.

## 10. Your mind

The hardest part of the Heights may be emotional.

- **The first days are overwhelming.** Noise, altitude, cold, darkness, and exhaustion all hit at once, and you may feel anxious or irritable. It passes. The locals compare the noise to the sea, and for many visitors it becomes like weather after a week.
- **You'll have no refuge in silence.** The only quiet places are dangerous ones: the fog margin, where the pale things feed, and the summit, where even your heartbeat disappears. Find other ways to rest: warmth, lamplight, company, rhythm.
- **Watch for the uncanny eroding your certainty.** Echoes before words, drifting mountains, voices from stone saying things not yet said. They pile up slowly. Keep a journal so you can keep track of what actually happened.
- **Don't travel alone.** Bring a companion or hire a local guide, and check in with someone every day.

---

## 11. Leaving

**Expect withdrawal from the noise.** Children taken down to the quiet cloud-top come back frightened and sleepless, unnerved by the silence. You may feel the same at home. Your ears may ring, and silence may feel wrong for weeks. Give yourself time. A fan or some low background sound can help you sleep.

**Get your hearing checked again** against your baseline.

**Think very carefully before bringing stone home.** In the Heights, silence erodes sound-stone. At home, where no hammers fall, a pebble of it might slowly murmur itself away. It might release everything it holds at once. It might do nothing. You can't know until it's too late. If you do take a piece, don't keep it where you sleep.

**Decontaminate your gear.** Check for moss spores, clickling hitchhikers, moth eggs, and seeds of belljack, which might open its grey cups to your own voice in a quiet house far away.

## Packing list summary

**Health:** acetazolamide and dexamethasone (with medical advice), pulse oximeter, lozenges and honey, eye drops, antihistamines, inhaler if needed, vitamin D, laceration-focused first-aid kit, updated tetanus shot, P100/N95 masks.

**Ears and voice:** musician's earplugs, foam sleep earplugs, over-ear defenders, a loud whistle, and a few songs you know well.

**Clothing:** wool or synthetic base layers (no cotton), insulating mid-layer, waterproof shell, leather or wool outer layer for foundries, warm hat, two or more pairs of gloves (cut-resistant and insulated), lots of wool socks, foot powder.

**Footwear and terrain:** stiff-soled leather mountaineering boots, gaiters, microspikes, helmet, knee protection, edge protectors or chain instead of rope.

**Light:** headlamp with a red mode, spare batteries kept warm, hand-crank backup, glacier glasses, optional light-therapy lamp.

**Practical:** water filter and tablets, calorie-dense food, waterproof notebook and pencils, mechanical watch, dry bags, thermos, noise-making gifts for hosts.

**Mental:** a companion, a journal, a firm decision made in advance about the silent summit, and the willingness to be loud.

---

Go knowing this: every sound you make here becomes part of the mountains. If you shout greetings across the valleys, hum while you climb, and sing a note with a choir, a thin layer of stone made of you will remain in the Heights long after you leave. Somewhere in the pale bedrock, someday, a Listener may hear your voice. Make it a voice worth hearing.
