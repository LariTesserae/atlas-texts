# Preparing for the Level

## A Note Before the Lists

Most dangerous places are dangerous the way a cliff is: there is an edge, and you stay back from it. The Level has almost none of those. What it has is a set of slow pressures: heat that won't let you cool, water that won't let you dry, light that won't let you sleep, and a steadiness that gradually stops you from wanting to leave. Nothing there will attack you. The place simply makes no allowance for a body that needs contrast: cool after warm, dry after wet, dark after light, ending after beginning.

So prepare less like a mountaineer and more like someone going to live for a while inside a greenhouse that is also a factory that is also a clock. Most of what follows is about carrying your own contrasts in with you, because the Level won't provide any.

---

## Part One: Before You Go

### Get acclimatized to humid heat. This is not optional.

The most serious physical danger is the air. It is saturated and warm, near body temperature. Your body cools itself mainly by evaporating sweat, and in saturated air sweat does not evaporate. It just runs off you, and your core temperature climbs. The description says the heat "isn't enough to hurt," and at rest that's probably true. Carrying a pack through a furnace district for two hours is another matter.

- Spend at least 10 to 14 days beforehand training in humid heat: a steam room, a hot bath, or exercise in a heated, humidified space. Your body will learn to sweat earlier, hold more plasma volume, and lose less salt. This adaptation is real and substantial.
- Build general aerobic fitness. A fitter heart handles heat load better.
- Learn the early signs of heat illness in yourself and your companion: headache, irritability, nausea, clumsiness, confusion, and above all *stopping sweating*. Confusion is the dangerous one, because in the Level's dreamy atmosphere it will be easy to mistake for simple calm.

### Get a medical screening, and take some things seriously.

- **Pacemakers, implanted defibrillators, insulin pumps, cochlear implants: do not go.** The main power lines produce fields strong enough that visitors feel them in their dental fillings. Any implanted electronics are at real risk.
- Cardiac conditions, blood pressure medications, diuretics, and some psychiatric medications impair heat tolerance. Talk to someone who knows.
- Metal fillings will buzz near the big conduits. That's unpleasant but not dangerous. If you're prone to anxiety, know in advance that it's coming.
- Skin conditions that worsen with moisture, such as eczema or fungal infections, will worsen dramatically.

### Settle your departure before you arrive.

This sounds like logistics. It is really the most important psychological preparation, and there's more about it below. Before you step through, fix a return date and set up an external anchor: someone outside the Level who expects you back and will act if you're late. Write the date somewhere you can't lose it, ideally on your skin as well as on paper.

## Part Two: The Body in the Level

### Heat management

You cannot out-sweat this place, so you have to manage your heat budget deliberately.

- **Move slowly.** Watch the herds. They doze, ride, and soak. They have the right idea. Plan to cover perhaps half the distance you'd manage in a temperate climate.
- **Look for cold surfaces.** This is one of the few gifts the Level gives. Water condenses only on things cooler than the air, and the structures visibly *weep*, so many pipes and girders are cooler than you are. Some of the pipes may be carrying coolant or return water. A heavily weeping pipe is a place to lay your forearms, the back of your neck, your wrists. Test first with the back of your hand. Some pipes will be boiler lines and very hot, and they won't necessarily look different.
- **Rain is relief.** The pillars are described as warm, but rain falling from a mile up has lost some heat on the way, and being drenched does briefly carry heat off you. Walking through a pillar edge is a legitimate way to reset.
- **Avoid the furnace districts during exertion.** The pillars stand over the hottest ground. Pillars mean furnaces beneath them, so the rain is refreshing, but the area around it is the warmest part of the map.
- **Bring cooling gear that works in humidity.** Evaporative cooling towels are useless here. Phase-change cooling vests (packs that freeze solid at around 15–18°C) help, but you'll have no way to recharge them unless you find a very cold pipe or bring a refrigerating unit. For a longer stay, a small powered cooling device is worth the weight if your electronics survive (see below).

### Salt and fluids

You will sweat constantly and lose huge amounts of salt, and because sweat never dries, you won't *feel* how much you're losing.

- Bring electrolyte tablets or powder, far more than you think you'll need. Plan for several litres of fluid a day with salt added.
- Watch your urine. Dark means drink. No urine for many hours is an emergency.
- Muscle cramps are a sign you're behind on salt.

### Water

Water is everywhere, and the cleanest you'll find is **pillar rain**, caught before it touches anything. It is condensed from vapour, effectively distilled. Bring a wide collecting funnel or a tarp and a bottle, and you can fill up in minutes under any pillar.

- **Do not drink the film.** It carries swift droppings, whatever the tinkers leave, and dissolved minerals heavy enough to grow the white ferns. The scale shows how hard that water is.
- The gutters are fed by pillar rain but lined with lampreys, and whatever the lampreys filter is in the water. Treat gutter water as suspect.
- Purify everything anyway. Bring a filter plus chemical or UV treatment. You don't know the Level's microbiology, and warm water is ideal for things that like warm water.
- Distilled rainwater contains no minerals, which is another reason to salt it.

### Feet: the thing that will actually stop you

The film is fingernail-deep and permanent. The floor never dries, and neither do your feet. Over days, this causes immersion foot: skin softens, wrinkles, cracks, blisters, and then breaks down and gets infected. Soldiers in wet trenches were crippled by it, and the Level is a warm trench with no end.

Prepare as though this were the main risk of the trip, because on any stay longer than a couple of days it probably is.

- **Footwear:** Choose shoes that drain rather than shoes that try to stay dry. Waterproof boots trap sweat and fill through the collar anyway, and the result is worse. Use mesh-upper shoes with drainage ports, grippy rubber soles (the plates are wet iron, possibly slick with scale), and a stiff sole, because the rivets and plate edges are described as crisp and sharp. Rubber soles also give you a little insulation from the electrical hazard, though not much, since you'll be standing in water.
- **Dry socks in sealed bags.** Bring many pairs. Change them whenever you rest.
- **Foot care routine:** At every long stop, take off your shoes and socks, get your feet *out of the water* and elevated, and let them air, even in humid air. Apply a barrier balm (zinc oxide, or a lanolin- or silicone-based product) before putting them back into the wet. Carry antifungal powder or cream.
- **Inspect daily.** Check for white, pruned skin that stays white, cracks, cuts from sharp edges, and numbness or tingling. A cut foot in warm, crab-filled water is an infection waiting to happen.
- **Get off the floor whenever you can.** Stairs, gantries, raised belt-runs, and pipe bundles are all above the film. Use them.

### Skin generally

Everything you wear will cling and stay wet. Chafing will be relentless: inner thighs, armpits, under straps, under breasts, around the waist.

- Bring anti-chafe balm in quantity and apply it preventively.
- Bring antifungal cream. Your own skin fungi will thrive in this climate.
- Clean and cover any cut, however small. The edges here are machined sharp, and there is no worn corner anywhere. Expect scrapes, and carry plenty of waterproof dressings.

## Part Three: What Can Actually Hurt You

### Electricity

Look hard at the setup: live cables sagging overhead on porcelain insulators, current strong enough to lift the hair on your forearms, an iron floor that is certainly grounded, and a continuous film of mineral-rich water connecting you to all of it. You are standing in a conductor.

- **Never touch a cable, insulator, or anything electrical.** Treat every metal fixture near a main line as potentially live.
- **Don't climb near the main lines.** A hand on a live element and a foot on a grounded girder is the classic fatal circuit.
- **Watch for fallen or sagging cables** near the film. The Level repairs itself, but you don't know how quickly.
- **Pay attention to the tooth-pressure and forearm hair.** They are your built-in field detector. If they spike, back away.
- **Don't carry long metal objects** such as trekking poles or rods when moving through tight spaces near conduits.

### Machinery

The machines are quiet, which is exactly what makes them dangerous. Nothing clatters to warn you. Gears mesh silently, belts run on rollers as wide as a street, and pistons sigh.

- Keep loose clothing, straps, long hair, and cords tied down or stowed. Belts and rollers pull.
- Stay out of pinch points: where belts meet rollers, where gears mesh, and at belt junctions.
- **Riding the belts is tempting.** The herds do it, and the belts are elevated, dry-ish, and moving. Know that the loops may be continental in circumference and pass through sheds you can't see into. If you ride, be sure you can see where you're going and that you can get off safely. Never ride while tired, and never sleep on one.

### Sharp edges and falls

Everything is new, crisp, and knurled. That's good for grip and bad for skin. Wear light gloves with cut resistance for any climbing or for handling structures. Stair treads will be sharp-edged and wet. Iron plates with a mineral film may be slippery in places where the scale is thick.

### The fauna

Nothing here is described as hostile, but some points are worth holding onto:

- **Tinkers** take a dead swift to bone within an hour. Nobody says they eat living things, and they part politely around feet. Still, do not sleep, sit, or lie in the film. Don't leave an open wound in the water. Don't find out.
- **Midges** don't bite, but you'll inhale them, get them in your eyes, and find them on your lips. Glasses or light goggles help. A light mesh face veil helps in the thicker clouds, though it adds heat. A buff you can pull over your nose and mouth is a reasonable compromise.
- **The herds** seem placid. They're dog-sized and heavy. Don't get between one and a ramp, and don't startle one on a belt.
- **Lampreys** fix to the gutter walls. Don't swim in the gutters. You don't know what those mouths do to skin, and the walls are covered.
- **Beneath the plates:** something large, or many somethings, moves in the dark, and the rivets over those places are warm. Listen if you want to. Do not pry at seams, do not try to lift plates, and do not linger on warm rivets.

## Part Four: Gear That Survives

### Everything corrodes, except the Level

The Level repairs itself. Your equipment won't.

- Choose titanium, stainless steel, and plastics. Carbon steel will rust quickly. Oil any metal tools.
- **Electronics are in double jeopardy:** saturating humidity and strong electromagnetic fields. Seal everything in waterproof cases with desiccant packs, and bring spare desiccant. Expect some devices to fail anyway. Don't make any single electronic device essential.
- **Condensation:** anything you bring in cooler than the air will instantly bead with water, including lenses, screens, and the inside of cases. Let gear equalize to ambient temperature inside a sealed bag before you open it.
- Magnetic compasses won't work. You're surrounded by iron and current. Mechanical watches can be magnetized and run fast or slow, so choose an antimagnetic model if you can.

### Clothing

Nothing will dry, so choose clothing for protection, not comfort.

- Light, loose, long-sleeved shirts and trousers in thin synthetic fabric protect against sharp edges, midges, and chafing, and don't hold much water.
- A hat with a brim to keep weep-drips and rain off your face.
- Several changes of underclothes in sealed bags.
- Nothing cotton. It stays soaked and chafes.

### Sleep kit

- **A hammock**, with straps long enough to span between pipes or girders. This is the single best sleeping solution. It keeps you off the floor, out of the film, and away from the crabs. Choose anchor points far from any electrical line, away from moving machinery, and not under a row of swift nests unless you want droppings on you.
- **An eye mask.** The light never dims.
- Earplugs, probably unnecessary given how quiet it is, but useful if the silence is what keeps you awake.

## Part Five: Finding Your Way

This is a real problem. The Level is flat to the horizon and built densely enough that you walk through corridors and courts. The light has no direction, there is no sun, and compasses don't work. Every district looks like every other district, because every rivet is new and every surface is renewed. Nothing ages, so nothing is distinctive in the way a crack or stain makes something distinctive.

- **The pillars are your only natural landmarks.** They're stationary and permanent. Learn to triangulate between them. Their spacing and relative widths let you distinguish one from another.
- **The paint colour changes by district,** green or oxblood. That's coarse information, but it's information.
- **Bring your own markers**, and assume the Level may remove them. Anything that counts as wear might get corrected. Tie ribbon, chalk a mark, and check whether it's still there an hour later before you rely on marking as a strategy.
- **Keep a written route log** with pace counts, turns, and which pillar is on which side.
- **Consider a tether or rope system** if travelling as a pair through dense districts. Sound doesn't carry past the next shed, and you could lose each other within a minute.
- **Use radios with caution.** The electrical noise may swamp them. Agree on a whistle code with your companion, since a sharp high whistle may carry farther than a shout, and a rendezvous rule: "if separated, go to the nearest pillar edge and wait on its left side."

## Part Six: Food

Bring your own. Pack sealed, shelf-stable food in cans or retort pouches. Anything open will spoil fast in the heat, and everything will get damp.

Should you eat the fauna? The crabs are small and edible-looking, and the herds are large. I'd say no, for three reasons:

1. You know nothing about their biology. Animals that eat unknown things from rivet seams and filter unknown things from gutters may not be safe.
2. The ecology is described as *exactly* balanced. The Level almost certainly replaces anything you take; one swift dies, another fledges. But you don't know that it does, and you don't know what, if anything, notices the imbalance before it's corrected.
3. Instinct. It feels wrong to eat from a place like this, the way it feels wrong to take food from a stranger's table in a dream.

Pack out, or at least bury in a gutter's flow, anything non-organic. Organic waste, including your own, the tinkers will almost certainly handle.

## Part Seven: Time, Sleep, and the Mind

This is the section that matters most, and the one people skip.

### The problem

The Level has no night, no weather change, no seasons, and no aging. Your body keeps time by light, temperature, and social cues, and the Level offers none of them. Within days your sleep will drift. Within a week you won't know how long you've been there. Then something subtler happens. Visitors begin hearing their pulse, then the Level's pulse, the once-a-minute swell of the pillars and the lamprey-ripple answering it, and then they keep time with it without meaning to.

"Most who come here stop noticing the difference between a minute and a year."

That is not a figure of speech. It's the actual hazard. Nothing in the Level will harm you, but you may forget to leave. The place feels warm, soft, abundant, safe, and utterly without demands, and it does not care whether you go.

### The defences

- **Keep time mechanically and write it down.** An antimagnetic watch, a paper log, a mark for every sleep. Record the date and time *every time you look at the watch*. The act of writing is the anchor.
- **Impose a day.** Eat at fixed times. Sleep at fixed times with the eye mask on. Do something distinctive at the start of each "day": a routine, a sentence spoken aloud, a stretch.
- **Bring something that changes.** This matters more than it sounds. In a place where nothing ages, you need evidence that time is passing. Your own body helps: nails grow, hair grows, a beard comes in. Notice these things deliberately. Beyond that, bring an object that visibly progresses: a notebook you fill a page of each day, a candle you burn a notch of, a knotted cord you tie once a day, a book you read in order. One visitor idea worth borrowing: bring a small potted plant and watch it grow. It will be the only thing in a thousand miles that is going somewhere.
- **Don't consciously synchronize with the pillar rhythm.** It will be tempting, because it's soothing. Keep a counter-rhythm of your own: hum, count, tap a different beat. If you catch yourself breathing with the pillars, get up and walk.
- **Travel with a companion, and give each other the job of noticing.** Agree in advance: if either of us stops logging, stops asking when we're leaving, or says "there's no hurry," the other one takes us out. Arrange this before you go, while both of you still care.
- **Keep your departure date visible.** Written on your forearm, on the first page of your log, on your pack.
- **Watch for the specific drift:** loss of interest in the log, sleeping more, sitting in the film watching the tinkers for hours, feeling that leaving is somehow beside the point. That's the Level working on you. It is gentle, but it is not benign.

### Emotional preparation

Some people will find the Level horrifying: a closed system, perfectly efficient, with no purpose, no story, no exit, and no death that means anything. Others will find it the most peaceful place they've ever been. Many will feel both in alternating waves.

Both reactions are reasonable. It may help to decide beforehand what you want from the visit, so you have something to measure the experience against. A place that isn't going anywhere is hard to be in unless *you* are.

## Part Eight: Conduct

There are no inhabitants to offend, as far as anyone knows. But:

- **Take nothing.** Not a bolt, not a shell, not a flake of scale. The swifts need the scale for their nests. Mostly, though, no one knows what the Level does about removal. It replaces wear, and removal might count as wear. Leave it alone.
- **Don't try to break the balance** as an experiment: blocking a belt, damming a gutter, killing things to see if they're replaced. Whatever maintains this place has maintained it forever. Stay out of its way.
- **Don't pry at the plates.** Whatever moves underneath is best left unexamined.
- **Leave as lightly as you came.** You'll find it's surprisingly easy. The film closes behind your feet, and the tinkers close behind that.

## Condensed Packing List

**Body**
- Electrolyte tablets or powder (a lot)
- Anti-chafe balm, zinc barrier cream, antifungal cream and powder
- Waterproof dressings, antiseptic, a basic wound kit
- Heat illness knowledge (in your head, not your bag)

**Feet**
- Draining, stiff-soled, grippy rubber-soled shoes, plus a spare pair
- Many pairs of synthetic socks in sealed bags

**Water**
- Wide funnel or tarp for catching pillar rain
- Bottles, filter, and chemical or UV purifier

**Clothing**
- Light loose long sleeves and trousers (synthetic)
- Brimmed hat, light cut-resistant gloves
- Glasses or light goggles, buff or mesh veil for midge clouds

**Shelter**
- Hammock with long straps, eye mask

**Navigation and communication**
- Paper log, pencils (not pens, which smear when wet)
- Ribbon or chalk markers
- Whistle, rope or tether, radios (backup only)

**Time**
- Antimagnetic mechanical watch
- Something that changes: notebook, knot cord, potted plant
- Written departure date, in several places including your skin

**Gear protection**
- Waterproof cases, lots of desiccant
- Oil for tools; titanium and stainless where possible

**People**
- A companion who has agreed to drag you out
- Someone outside who is expecting you back

## Last Thought

The Level is not trying to keep you. That's what makes it dangerous. It isn't trying anything. It's warm and bright and full, and it asks nothing, and it will go on exactly as it is whether you stay or go. The machinery doesn't need you, and neither do the animals. There's no mystery at its center that you're owed an answer to.

Go and see it. Stand in a gantry-roofed court and watch the swifts thread the lattice. Kneel and listen to the plates. Walk into a pillar and out again. Feel the pulse arrive. Then look at your watch, write down the time, and remember that you are the only thing there that is on its way somewhere.
