# Preparing for Weir-in-the-Cloud

## The Short Version

You will be wet for your entire visit. You will be cold most of that time. You will never hear silence, never see far, and never find a surface that isn't growing something. The people are loud, busy, and practical, and they live by a few rules that matter enormously to them. If you pack for wetness rather than rain, protect your sleep, learn the water and net customs before you arrive, and make yourself useful, you will likely have one of the most vivid experiences of your travels.

The rest of this guide covers the details.

---

## 1. Understand What Kind of Wet This Is

Most travelers prepare for rain. Rain is not the main problem here. **Fog-drip** is. The air deposits water directly onto every surface, including you, continuously. A rain shell keeps falling water off, but in saturated air your own sweat can't escape either. After a day you'll be damp from inside and out regardless of what you wear.

So the goal is not staying dry. The goal is **staying warm while wet** and **having one dry thing to retreat into**.

**What this means for clothing:**

- **Wool, wool, wool.** Merino or heavier wool for base layers, socks, and a mid-layer. Wool stays warm when soaked and resists the mildew smell that will otherwise develop within days. Bring no cotton at all. Cotton wet at cloud-forest temperatures is how people get hypothermic while believing they're only uncomfortable.
- **A synthetic insulating layer** (fleece or synthetic-fill jacket), not down. Down will clump into useless wet lumps by day three.
- **A hooded rain shell** that is loose, well ventilated, and tough enough to be snagged on bamboo, thatch, and moss beards all day. Pit-zips help. A **wide-brimmed waterproof hat** worn under the hood is better still. The eaves drip in "beaded curtains" and you'll walk under hundreds of them.
- **A poncho or cape** long enough to cover a pack. Locals probably wear something like woven sedge or fiber capes. Consider buying one when you arrive; it will be suited to the place in ways your gear isn't.
- **One sealed set of sleep clothes** in a dry bag that you *never* wear outside. Dry wool socks and a dry base layer at night are what will keep your morale intact. Guard them.

**Everything else goes in dry bags.** Documents, electronics, medicine, spare clothes, fire-starting materials. Assume that anything not sealed will be damp within hours and moldy within a week. Pack small silica gel packets, and don't expect them to keep up.

## 2. Your Feet

Your feet face the hardest conditions of your trip. Plan for them first.

**The surfaces you'll walk on:**
- **Polished, dished granite steps**, clean in the center, mossy at the edges, each with a puddle in its worn bowl. Wet polished stone is extremely slick.
- **Lashed bamboo walkways** at head height, round, wet, and flexing.
- **Ladders with concave, glass-smooth rungs.**
- **The root-mat**, if you step off the path, which swallows you to the shin.
- **The lower switchback trail**, which is leech country.

**What to wear:**
- The best choice is **soft, grippy, flexible footwear** with a sticky rubber sole and shallow lugs. Canyoneering shoes or wet-terrain trail shoes that drain water are good. Stiff heavy boots are poor on bamboo and ladders, will never dry, and will rot your feet.
- Bring a **second pair** so one can (theoretically) dry by a fire while you wear the other.
- Watch what the locals do. They may go barefoot on the stones and bridges, or wear woven sandals. After a week you might find they're right.
- **Gaiters** or **leech socks** (tightly woven socks worn over your regular socks and tied above the calf) for the trail and lower paths.

**Foot care is not optional.** Constant wetness causes trench foot, maceration, and fungal infection. Every night:
- Dry your feet completely by the fire.
- Inspect them for blisters, cracks, and leeches.
- Apply a barrier balm (lanolin or a zinc-based cream) and antifungal powder.
- Sleep in dry socks.

Bring more foot-care supplies than you think you need.

## 3. The Approach and Your Arrival

You'll come up switchbacks through the lower forest, hearing the town long before you see it. This stretch has specific hazards.

**Leeches.** They "quest" from every leaf on the lower paths. They aren't dangerous, but they are demoralizing and they bleed a lot, because leech bites contain an anticoagulant. Tuck your trousers into leech socks. Check yourself at every rest, including your waistband, the back of your neck, and your boots. **Don't pull an attached leech off.** Salt, ash, or a fingernail slid under the sucker releases it cleanly. The locals carry a pouch of ash and salt, and you should get one as soon as you can. Bring your own salt for the climb up.

**The fog walls.** Fog pours over the col "in about twenty breaths," turning half-mile visibility into arm's length. On a switchback trail near a drop, **stop walking when the fog hits.** Stand still, get your bearings, and move only where you can see the path under your feet. Carry a **whistle**. In fog, voices behave strangely: close sounds seem closer and far ones disappear. Three blasts is a signal most people will understand.

**Timing.** Arrive in daylight. Aim to reach the saddle by midday, with time to find lodging before dusk. The trail after dark in fog is a genuinely bad idea.

**Travel with traders if you can.** Traders come up from both valleys to the midmorning market, often with ponies. Walking with them gives you guides, protection, and an introduction. Arriving as "the stranger who came up with the salt merchant" is far better than arriving alone out of the fog.

## 4. Choosing When to Go

The town has two distinct modes, and they are different trips.

**Migration season** (about six weeks in each of the two cool shoulder seasons):
- The sky is never empty: swifts in screaming sheets, pigeons with wings like surf, hawks sliding through the notch at rooftop height, a river of bats at dusk that lasts an hour.
- Exhausted birds are everywhere, on every branch, on the paths, and in the rafters above your supper.
- The town is at full intensity. Nets are up, catches are counted aloud, and smoking and trade are at their peak.
- It is also at its **most crowded, most noisy, most rule-bound, and busiest.** People have less time for you, and more ways for an outsider to accidentally offend.
- If you want to see what makes this place what it is, go now. It is one of the great spectacles anywhere.

**Between migrations:**
- It is still loud and crowded (frogs, monkeys, pigs, water-clappers, resident birds) but calmer.
- People have more time to talk, host, and teach.
- It's better for a longer stay, for learning the place, or for a traveler who is sensitive to sensory overload.

**A special date to know about:** the **night of the first fog of the season**, when no one nets. If you can be present for this, or for the **release of the first bird** from the highest roof by the youngest child who can climb there, you'll witness something the town clearly holds sacred. Ask permission to watch, and stay at the edges.

## 5. The Noise, and Your Sleep

The description says it plainly: *"It is never quiet and never still."* Take that literally.

Nighttime sounds include:
- frog choruses "until the air vibrates," including the big green one that raps like a knuckle on a hollow log, over and over;
- hundreds of water-clappers, each with its own pitch and rhythm, knocking all night;
- thrushes calling overhead through the dark during migration;
- the continuous hiss of the forest dripping;
- water running in a thousand channels;
- pigs under the floor, chickens in the eaves, and birds in the rafters.

Before dawn it gets louder: swifts, dogs, monkeys, cookfires, children.

**Bring:**
- **High-quality earplugs, several pairs.** Foam for sleeping. Consider silicone moldable ones for comfort over many nights.
- **An eye mask.** Houses are smoky and dim, but cookfires start before dawn.
- A mindset. Many people find that after two or three nights the sound becomes a kind of enveloping blanket, and the first silent night after leaving feels eerie. Others never adjust. Know which kind of person you are. If you struggle with constant sensory input, plan shorter visits with recovery time down in the valleys.

Also expect that **people will talk loudly to you**, because everyone has to here. This is not rudeness or anger. Match it. Speaking softly will make you seem unfriendly, and you won't be heard.

## 6. Health Preparation

This is a remote, cold, wet, crowded place with lots of animals and wood smoke. Take it seriously.

### Before you go
- **Tetanus** booster (bamboo cuts, stone scrapes, animal bites).
- **Rabies pre-exposure vaccination.** There are dogs, macaques, and bats pouring from caves. Pre-exposure shots make treatment after a bite much simpler, which matters when the nearest help is days away.
- **Hepatitis A and typhoid**, standard for places with shared water and livestock.
- A **flu vaccination.** People live closely and smoke irritates airways.
- **General fitness**, especially legs and balance. You'll climb ladders, cross flexing bridges, and walk wet steps all day. Practice ladders if you can.
- **Altitude.** Cloud forest saddles like this typically sit somewhere in the middle altitudes, high enough to feel breathless on the climb but usually not high enough for serious altitude sickness. Ascend at a steady pace and rest a day on arrival.

### Specific risks and what to do

**Macaques.** They are heavy-furred, bold, and at constant war with the town. **Do not feed them, carry food openly, make eye contact with challenging ones, or get between them and a drying rack.** Macaque bites and scratches can carry dangerous infections, including herpes B virus, which is rare in people but serious. Any bite or scratch: scrub it immediately with soap and flowing water for fifteen minutes, then seek medical care as soon as you can. Don't wait for signs of infection.

**Birds.** You'll be around thousands of wild birds, including in houses and during plucking and smoking. Bird droppings and handling can transmit respiratory and gut infections. Wash your hands before eating, avoid handling sick or dead birds without reason, and keep droppings off your bedding and food. If you develop fever and a cough after your visit, tell a doctor you were around large numbers of wild birds.

**Pigs and water.** Water flows downhill in a fixed sequence: drinking, then washing, then pigs, then taro beds. Pigs root under every house, and they very likely consume the town's waste. That means:
- **Drink only from the top of a channel**, meaning near the collecting roofs, or from what a host gives you from a drinking source.
- **Treat your drinking water anyway.** Bring a filter plus purification tablets, or boil water on a hearth. The fog-water itself is probably clean. What happens to it on roofs and in shared channels is less certain.
- Never drink from step-bowls, basins, or troughs. Frogs live in them.
- Wash your hands often, especially before eating.

**Smoke.** Houses have chimney holes, not chimneys, and indoor smoke will be heavy. If you have asthma, bring ample medication. Everyone else: expect a cough, sore eyes, and smoky-smelling everything. Sit low near the floor, where smoke is thinnest, and take breaks outside.

**Hypothermia.** This is the biggest quiet danger. Wet and cool around the clock means your body loses heat all day. Watch for shivering you can't stop, clumsiness, confusion, or irritability, in yourself and your companions. Eat well and often, especially fats. Change into dry clothes by the fire. Don't push through a squall to "just finish" something.

**Skin.** Constant moisture causes rashes, fungal infection, and slow-healing cuts. Clean and cover any wound, even tiny bamboo splinters. Bring antiseptic, antibiotic ointment, waterproof bandages, and antifungal cream.

**Falls.** Wet stones, ladders, bridges, and mud-slippage. A sprained ankle is a real crisis when the exit is a steep muddy trail. Move deliberately, keep three points of contact on ladders, and be alert on bridges at night.

### Medical kit (in a dry bag)
- Water filter and purification tablets
- Antiseptic, antibiotic ointment, waterproof bandages, blister care
- Antifungal cream and powder; foot barrier balm
- Oral rehydration salts; anti-diarrheal medication
- A broad-spectrum antibiotic for traveler's diarrhea (ask a doctor)
- Pain relievers and anti-inflammatories
- Antihistamines (pollen, mold, insect bites)
- Eye drops (smoke)
- Elastic bandage for sprains
- Tweezers, small scissors
- Salt pouch for leeches
- Any personal prescriptions, doubled

## 7. The Rules That Matter

The townspeople have customs that are "old and strictly kept." Breaking them could turn a welcome into hostility faster than anything else you could do.

### The nets
- **No net may be strung across the central gap.** It must stay open. Even if you're an avid naturalist, don't obstruct it with anything: no ropes, no hanging gear, no drying lines. It may be sacred, practical, or both; either way it's not your business to test.
- **Nobody takes the hawks.** Don't harm, catch, or bother raptors in any way. When they slide through the notch at rooftop height and people duck, duck with them. Don't swat, throw, or shout at them.
- **No netting on the night of the first fog.** Be quiet and respectful that night, and follow your hosts' lead about what's appropriate.
- **The first bird released** is a ceremony for a child. Observe; don't participate unless invited.
- **Don't touch the nets.** They are made of fine nettle-fiber cord, handmade and valuable. Don't walk into them, lean on their poles, or free a trapped bird out of sympathy. Freeing a bird is taking food and income from a family.

If you find bird netting upsetting, make peace with it before you arrive, or don't come in migration season. This has been the town's living "for as long as the stones have stood." Treat it as their work, not a spectacle for your judgment.

### The water
- The right to take water from a channel is **inherited**. You have no right to any channel. Always ask your host where you may drink and wash.
- **Never wash, rinse, or put anything dirty into a channel above where you're told to.** Doing this contaminates the drinking water of every household downstream of you. It's the worst thing you could do here besides breaking a net rule.
- Don't move, block, redirect, or lean on channels, trestles, or water-clappers. They are the town's infrastructure, carefully balanced.

### The paths
- **Stay on the stones and bridges.** Stepping off damages the sponge-mat and gardens, and you may sink to the shin. People have been keeping the centers of these stones clean with their feet for centuries. Walk where they walk.
- **Don't repair or "fix" anything on your own.** The walls lean on purpose, held up by roots and a settled logic. Offer to help, and let people tell you how.
- **Pigs on the stone paths, people on the bridges.** Children and dogs use the upper level freely. Give way, especially to anyone carrying a load.

### Houses
- Houses are small and crowded. When invited in, watch what your hosts do with shoes, where they sit, and where guests go.
- Birds may be roosting in the rafters. Don't disturb them. They're part of the household during migration.
- Walls are living moss and fern. Don't pick at them or pull plants out, however loose they look. They are insulation and often deliberately pressed into place.
- **Fire caution.** Hearths burn in houses made of bamboo and sedge. Thatch may be damp but dry sedge can catch. Never bring candles, stoves, or open flame without asking. Never leave a flame unattended.
- Be careful of your head. Walkways run at head height, eaves nearly touch, and ladders and rafters are low.

### Animals
- Don't feed, provoke, or pet the dogs unless your host introduces you. They are in a constant screaming war with the monkeys and will be keyed up.
- Don't interfere with the children guarding drying racks with slings. And don't stand downrange of them.
- Watch the goats on the terrace walls. If one gets loose on the roofs, let the owner handle it.

## 8. Money, Trade, and Useful Gifts

This is a trade town that has lived off an exchange economy for centuries. It probably values goods over any unfamiliar currency.

**What's valued here** (based on what comes up the mountain):
- **Salt.** Both a necessity and part of the leech pouch. Good for gifts and trade.
- **Iron knives** and small metal tools, including fishhooks, needles, and awls.
- **Cotton cloth.** (Curious, given the climate, but it clearly sells. Probably for use indoors or for trade onward.)
- **Dried fish.**

**Good gifts for hosts:** salt, good steel needles, a small sturdy knife, strong thread or cord, a sharpening stone. Practical things that are hard to make on a ridge in the clouds.

**Avoid** bringing things that could disrupt customs, like fine synthetic netting (which could undercut local nettle-cord work and tempt rule-breaking) or anything that encourages feeding the monkeys.

**What to buy:** smoked birds in leaf bundles, moss-bundled honey, nettle cord, medicinal orchids (ask what they're for and how they're used), and a local rain cape and ash-salt pouch. Buying locally made gear is a good way to build relationships and is often better suited to the place than what you brought.

**Languages:** market haggling happens in three languages. Before you go, learn greetings, thanks, numbers, and a few trade phrases in at least one valley language. Numbers matter here. Men come back from the nets "yelling the count."

## 9. Food

Expect smoke, pork, taro, and birds.

- **Smoked birds** are the staple and main trade good. They're likely rich, salty, and smoky. Accept them graciously.
- **Taro** from the lower terraces, probably boiled, roasted, or pounded.
- **Pork** and **eggs** from the pigs and chickens under and around the houses.
- **Honey**, possibly foraged from the forest.
- Probably goat milk or cheese, greens, and forest foods you won't recognize.

**Practical notes:**
- Eat everything well cooked. Be cautious with any raw greens washed in channel water.
- Bring a supply of **high-calorie, compact food** (nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, chocolate, fatty jerky) in sealed packaging, both as a buffer and for staying warm. Keep it sealed and hidden. Macaques and a certain big crested jay "steal from everyone and shout about it." Assume anything left out will be taken.
- If you have dietary restrictions (vegetarian, allergies), be prepared for difficulty. Options will be limited and hospitality usually means offering what's valued: meat.

**Clothing**
- Wool base layers (2 sets) + 1 sealed sleep set
- Wool socks (5+ pairs) + 2 pairs leech socks
- Synthetic insulating jacket
- Loose, tough, hooded rain shell; waterproof overtrousers
- Wide-brimmed waterproof hat
- Poncho/pack cover (or buy a local cape)
- Wool or synthetic gloves (fingerless may be best for ladders)
- Gaiters
- 2 pairs soft, sticky-soled, drainable shoes

**Shelter and sleep**
- Earplugs (many), eye mask
- Compact synthetic sleeping bag, liner in a dry bag
- Small ground pad (floors are bamboo and may be drafty)
- A tarp and cord (for emergencies on the trail only; don't hang anything near nets or the central gap)

- Water filter + purification tablets + a metal cup or small pot
- Sealed high-calorie snacks
- Small sealable food containers (monkey- and jay-proof)

- Headlamp with red mode (fog scatters bright white light badly; you'll mostly use it indoors and on ladders)
