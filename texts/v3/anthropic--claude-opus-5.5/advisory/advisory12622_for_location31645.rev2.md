# Preparing to Visit the Throat

## What This Place Actually Is

The Throat looks serene from the far ridge. Up close it is a hostile environment with a sanctuary inside it. The room itself is fairly benign: cold, wet, loud, bright, and still. Nearly all the danger sits in three places:

- **The approach:** altitude, weather, and razor-edged new rock.
- **The two passages through the wall:** the way in and, more dangerously, the way out.
- **Time:** cold, noise, and disorientation slowly take away your ability to leave.

So prepare less like a pilgrim and more like a cave diver. The room is the easy part. The door is what kills people.

A rough sense of the forces helps. For water to rise about three times the column's width, perhaps twenty metres, it has to leave the fissure at something like 70 kilometres an hour. The "thin" moment in the stutter is thin only in comparison. The description says it hits like a falling tree, and you should believe that literally.

---

## Months Before: Your Body

**Altitude.** The fissure sits just below the snowline. Depending on the mountain, that could be very high. Spend at least a week acclimatizing at intermediate elevation. Arriving with a headache and poor judgment is dangerous anywhere, and here it will cost you the count.

**Cold-water tolerance.** Practice deliberate cold immersion for weeks beforehand, and learn to control the gasp reflex. The first strike of glacial water through the wall will make your body try to inhale. If that happens while your face is in the sheet, you aspirate water. Train until you can go into cold water exhaling, not gasping.

**Neck, shoulders, and core.** You will be hurling yourself shoulder-first into a moving wall that wants to lift you and twist your head. Strengthen your neck, your shoulders, and the explosive drive of your legs. Practice under real waterfalls if you can, starting small, so you learn how moving water grabs a body.

**Hearing baseline.** Get a hearing test before you go, so you can tell afterward what the Throat took.

## Gear

Bring only what you need. Everything you set down begins turning to stone, and anything synthetic you leave behind will be sealed into the spire more or less forever.

### Body protection
- **Drysuit with thick insulating layers underneath,** or at minimum a heavy wetsuit. You will be soaked for the entire visit in near-freezing water and spray. Hypothermia is the most likely thing to kill you here.
- **Neoprene hood, gloves, and boots.** Gloves also protect your hands from the fresh rock outside, which cuts rope and will cut you.
- **Boots with soft, grippy soles.** The floor is sometimes soft like wet sugar and always slick. Hard cleats would scar the handprints and frills.
- **Kayaking or canyoning helmet,** ideally with a face guard.
- **Knee pads.** You land on your knees on stone, every time.
- **Sealed goggles or a dive mask,** strapped under the helmet so the jet can't rip it away.

### Breathing
- **A breathing cloth,** as the visitors use, or a snorkel-style arrangement that lets you breathe while spray streams over your face. The air is "barely air." Practice breathing through wet fabric for an hour at a time before you go. It is tiring, and panic makes it worse.

### Hearing
- **Deep foam plugs under sealed ear defenders** or a neoprene hood that covers the ears. In an enclosed cylinder of falling water, the noise may reach levels that damage hearing within minutes to hours.
- Understand the trade-off. Protection will not stop you *feeling* the sound. The low floor and the stutter come through your bones and breastbone regardless. What it protects is the delicate part of your hearing. You will still get the full experience. You simply won't go home with permanent ringing.

### Timing and orientation
- **A waterproof dive watch with a stopwatch and ideally a vibrating alarm.** Sound is useless for timekeeping in there, and your sense of time will dissolve. Set hard turnaround alarms before you go through.
- **Don't trust a magnetic compass.** Young dark volcanic stone is often strongly magnetic and can pull the needle off true. Orient by the room itself. The ladder leans on the *north* wall, and the exit gap is on the *downhill* side, where the fissure is narrowest. Fix that relationship in your mind before you enter.
- **A waterproof headlamp, plus a backup.** It is for emergencies only. See the section on night below.

### Other
- **A small waterproof gas monitor for carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulphide,** clipped to your chest. Water rising from deep hot rock often carries dissolved gases. Most will be scrubbed away by the open top and the spray, but low hollows, especially down by the fissure, could hold bad air.
- **Chemical heat packs** tucked against your core under the suit.
- **High-calorie food and water** in sealed containers. Shivering burns energy fast.
- **Spare goggle lenses.** Mineral film will cloud them within hours.
- **An offering, if you want to leave one.** Bring natural materials only: a stone from the slope outside for a cairn, or a braided cord of plant fibre or your own hair. No plastic, no metal trinkets, no names scratched onto manufactured things.

### What not to bring
- **Cameras and electronics you care about.** They will fog, scale, short out, or be ripped away in the passage. Even if one survives, it cannot capture the sound, and the sound is the place.
- **Anything you want back.** Remember the first rule: nothing you bring stays yours.

## On the Mountainside: Learning the Count

Don't go in on your first day. Maybe not your second.

**Watch the wall from outside for hours.** Stand where the visitors stand, back to the mountain, on the downhill side. Time the stutters with your watch and write the intervals down on waterproof paper. You want to know the range as well as the average: the shortest gap, the longest, and whether the rhythm changes with time of day. It may. Deep pressure systems breathe.

**Learn the warning signs.** A stutter probably has a precursor: a change in the low tone through your feet, a flicker in the wall's texture, a thinning of the curtain above. The wall "goes glassy" when it thins, and you should learn what the moment *just before* glassy looks and feels like. Committing on the precursor instead of the glassiness gains you a fraction of a second, and that fraction matters.

**Understand the layers you'll pass through.** From outside, going in:
1. **The falling outer curtain.** Heavy, broken, pounding, but survivable.
2. **The seam of shuddering air between the sheets.** This is narrow and genuinely dangerous. If you hesitate here, you can be trapped between two walls of moving water.
3. **The rising inner sheet.** This is the one the stutter thins, and the one that hits like a tree.

You have to go through all three in one committed motion. Hesitation in the seam is how people disappear.

**Choose your season and hour.** Late spring to late summer gives the most daylight and the least snow on the approach. Arrive in the morning, so you are inside at noon for the disc of light and out with hours of daylight to spare.

**Arrange a watcher.** Visitors come alone or in pairs. Two is better. Have your partner wait outside, or go in with them, but either way someone should know your planned exit time and be outside when it comes. Agree on simple hand signals beforehand: *okay, cold, leaving now, help.* You will not be able to hear each other at all.

**About ropes.** It is tempting to tie a safety line to a partner outside. Think hard before you do. The rock edges cut rope, and a line caught in a vertical jet could hold you *in* the water rather than pull you out. If you use one, use a quick-release harness you can operate with numb gloved fingers, and practice releasing it.

## The Passage In

- Stand ready, low, weight forward, arms up to protect your head.
- Watch for the precursor. Breathe out slowly as you commit. You want empty lungs meeting the water, not a gasp.
- Drive forward and low. The inner wall will try to lift you. Staying low means it lifts you *into* the room instead of up and over.
- Go shoulder-first, chin tucked, and don't stop.
- You'll land on your knees on wet stone in a sudden bright hush. Stay down for a moment. Check your mask, your gear, and your body. Then breathe through the cloth.

Expect to be shaken. Most people's hands tremble for several minutes afterward from adrenaline and cold.

## Inside

### The first minutes
The light will overwhelm you before the sound does. Everything seems to be rising: the bright bands crawl up the walls, the rainbows slide upward, and your inner ear may insist the floor is sinking. Some people feel faint. Sit. Let your eyes adjust. Look at the still water in the bowl. It is the only thing in the room that isn't moving, and it steadies you.

### The sound
Within a minute you will stop hearing your own voice, and within an hour you will stop *wanting* one. That is normal. The feeling of being "held" by the noise is real and not unpleasant for most people. Some find it unbearable, though. The low floor of infrasound can cause nausea, dread, and a sense of presence. If you feel a rising panic you can't explain, that may be part of the cause. It does not mean you are in danger, but it *can* make you do something dangerous. Don't bolt for the wall without the count.

On the words: the human brain is built to find speech in noise, and a roaring rhythmic place like this will give you voices whether or not anything is speaking. That doesn't settle the question. People who stay a full day and night report something more structured than that. Just know what your own mind will do, so you can listen honestly.

### Footing and fragility
- The floor is new stone, sometimes still soft. Walk slowly and softly.
- Don't step on the frilled shelves at the base of the walls, and don't lean on them. They snap.
- **Do not climb the ladder.** It is stone now, and too fragile. It holds the only resting hollow in the room, and that hollow is closed to you.
- The old handprints lie in layers under the surface. Walk where the floor is plain if you can.

### Air
Check your gas monitor now and then, especially if you go near the fissure. **If you want to see the pale thing, look without lying flat with your face in the opening for long.** Stay ready to pull back. Don't reach in. Don't drop anything in. Whatever it is, it has lived undisturbed since the rock split. Treat it the way you'd treat something sacred, or something with teeth. Possibly both.

### The birds
Fall-swifts come through the curtain like thrown darts, with no warning. Your helmet will protect you from a strike. Stay away from the nesting towers under the shelves, and don't touch the stone-cup chimneys. There are only a handful of birds, and fewer each spring.

### Cold
It creeps up on you. The warning signs:
- Clumsy fingers that can't work a buckle.
- Shivering you can't stop.
- **Shivering that stops on its own.** This is the dangerous one.
- Apathy, or confusion about the count.

Check yourself every half hour: *Can I still touch each fingertip to my thumb? Do I still know which way the downhill wall is? Do I remember the stutter interval?* If the answer to any of these is no, leave on the next good stutter.

## Etiquette

The rules of the place are not laws, but they are what has settled here over generations, and you are a guest.

- **Don't speak.** You couldn't be heard anyway, but the gesture matters. Use your hands.
- **Drink from the bowl.** Everyone does. A caution, though: water that tastes of cold metal and comes from deep hot rock may carry dissolved metals. One or two ritual mouthfuls are almost certainly fine. Don't use it as your drinking supply. Drink your own water.
- **Press your hand into the floor if you wish.** Choose a fresh soft patch. You'll be adding yourself to the frozen lake of hands, which is the most human thing in the room.
- **If you build a cairn, carry the stone in from outside.** Don't pry anything off the floor or walls.
- **If you tie a cord, tie it gently and don't expect it to be read.** Tie it to your own cairn or loosely to a cord that is already stone. Don't tie new cords onto the ladder, which is failing, or over someone else's soft recent message.
- **Take nothing.** The shrouded cairns, the stone sticks, the old cords, the sealed moss: nothing here is loose, and nothing is a souvenir.

## Night

The description is blunt: at night there is nothing. The water swallows the stars, and the roar goes on in total black.

This matters practically as much as spiritually. **The count depends on seeing the wall go glassy.** In the dark you can't see it. A headlamp will light the spray in front of your face and nothing else. You'd be trying to feel the stutter through your body alone, in the cold, exhausted, with no reference points. That is almost certainly how many of the trapped visitors became trapped.

**Unless you are deliberately staying the full day and night, be out with at least two hours of daylight left.**

---

## If You Mean to Stay a Day and a Night

The fifth rule says that is how long it takes for the rhythm to line up and something to be said. Some travellers will want that. Understand what you are signing up for:

- Twenty-four or more hours continuously soaked in near-freezing spray, without shelter or a dry spot, and without the ladder hollow to curl in. That is survivable only with a proper drysuit, heavy insulation, plenty of heat packs, calories, and a body that has been trained for it.
- A long night of black and roar, in which you can't see the exit and can't safely leave. You are committing to stay until daylight no matter what you feel.
- Profound sensory overload. Hallucination is likely, whatever its source.
- A good chance you'll come out changed. People who've heard it "come out quieter than they went in, and they tend to come back."

Do this only on a later visit, after at least one short one. Have a watcher outside who knows exactly when you are due out. Sit somewhere you know the orientation of, perhaps your back to the north wall under the ladder, facing the exit side. Then you'll know where the downhill wall is without having to see it.

## The Passage Out

This is the most dangerous moment of the visit, because you will be cold, tired, deafened, and a little enchanted.

- Before you leave, sit near the downhill side and **recount the stutters for several minutes.** Don't trust the rhythm you learned outside. It may have drifted.
- Check your gear. Tighten your helmet. Seal your mask.
- Go on the stutter with the same low, committed, exhaling drive. From inside, the thinning may look *brighter*, with daylight showing through the glassy wall.
- **Don't stop in the seam.** Go through both sheets in one motion.
- Be ready for the landing. You'll come out onto sharp, wet, downhill-sloping rock, possibly tumbling. Get your hands and knees down and move away from the curtain before you stand.
- **Never try the wall without the gap.** Whatever happens to those who do, whether that is being lifted and dropped twenty metres, pinned in the seam, or something the multiverse knows about and the mountain people don't, it isn't worth it. If you've lost the count, wait. Warm yourself as best you can. Watch the wall until it teaches you again. Someone may come.

---

## Afterward

- **Rewarm gradually.** Get out of wet layers into dry insulation, with warm sweet drinks and shelter from wind. Don't plunge frozen hands into hot water.
- **Expect ringing ears and muffled hearing** for hours, perhaps days. Get your hearing retested when you can.
- **Mineral crust** will stiffen your hair and gear. Rinse it off soon, before it sets.
- **The silence outside will feel wrong.** Many people find the ordinary world thin and unsettling for a while. The wind on the slope may sound like nothing at all.
- If you came out quieter than you went in, let yourself be quiet. Write down what you can, knowing it won't really capture it.

---

## What It Will Feel Like

My intuition is that the Throat is less a sight than a state you enter. The first minutes are violent: the strike of the wall, the gasp you don't allow yourself, the crawling light. Then, surprisingly fast, everything goes still inside you while nothing around you is still at all. The roar becomes a solid thing you sit inside. You stop trying to think in words, because words need a voice and yours is gone.

What stays with you is probably small. The weight of your hand pressing into the soft stone floor, knowing that in a season it will be sealed under a fingernail's depth of glass, one more hand among hundreds going down into the green. The one flat still surface in a room made entirely of motion. A sooty little bird going through the curtain like it's nothing. The awareness, underneath all of it, that the room is closing, and that you are one of a dwindling number of people who will ever stand in it.

Prepare your body for the cold and the door. Prepare your mind for the size of the silence inside the noise. Learn the count, and keep it.
