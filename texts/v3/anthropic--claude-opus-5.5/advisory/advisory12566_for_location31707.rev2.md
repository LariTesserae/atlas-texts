# Preparing for the Kiln Garden

## A Note Before Everything Else

Most dangerous places are dangerous in one way. The Kiln Garden is dangerous in several small, ordinary ways, and strange in one large way that is not really dangerous at all. You will get cut, burned, soaked, and chilled if you are careless. You will probably not be harmed by the thing that makes the place remarkable. But it may change how you think about your own footsteps for a long time afterward.

So prepare your body for a mountain, a glassworks, and a greenhouse, all within three hundred paces. Prepare your mind for a place that already knows roughly what you are going to do.

---

## Part One: Getting There

### Altitude comes first

The garden sits in a saddle between peaks that hold snow all year. That means real altitude, and the thin, scentless wind described on the approach confirms it. Treat the approach like any serious high-mountain trek:

- **Acclimatize before the final climb.** Spend at least two or three nights at intermediate elevation. Headache, nausea, and breathlessness on the scree are warnings, not inconveniences.
- **Expect the bowl to trick you.** When you step over the lip into warm, wet, heavy air, your body will feel relief. The humidity makes each breath feel richer, but you are still at altitude. People overexert in places that feel gentle, so move slowly for the first day inside.
- **Carry altitude medication** if your home world has it, and know the descent route in case of serious symptoms. Going down the mountain is the cure. Going down into the bowl is not.

### The approach itself

The final stretch is scree and snow. You will need your full cold-weather kit here: insulated layers, a windproof shell, gloves, sunglasses or glacier goggles against snow glare, and probably microspikes or crampons, depending on the season.

**Take the traction devices off before you step onto the glass.** Metal points on obsidian will skate unpredictably. They will also scratch and chip the risers, sending off flakes sharp enough to cut through fabric. You will be damaging a surface that is still being made. Pack them away at the rim.

Navigation is easy in clear weather: follow the plume. It stands over the notch all day. In a whiteout, though, the steam blends into cloud, so carry a real way of finding the saddle and don't rely on seeing the vapor.

## Part Two: Crossing the Threshold

The transition at the lip is described as stepping through a door, and you should prepare for it as if it were one. In a single stride you go from freezing, dry, thin air to hot, saturated, dripping air.

**What happens to you:**
- Your glasses, goggles, and any camera lens will fog instantly and completely. Bring anti-fog treatment and a soft cloth kept in a sealed pocket. Better still, let optics warm up slowly inside a closed bag before exposing them.
- Your cold-weather layers will become far too warm within minutes. You will start sweating heavily inside insulation designed to trap heat.
- Condensation will form on every cold surface you carry, including metal buckles, water bottles, and the outside of your pack. Anything cold becomes wet.

**What to do:** Stop just below the lip, on the first terrace, and change. Strip down to light, quick-drying clothing. Pack your insulation into a **fully waterproof dry bag** and keep it sealed for the whole visit. This is the single most important piece of preparation, for reasons that become clear when you leave (see Part Nine).

## Part Three: Clothing and Gear for the Bowl

### Hands: the glass will cut you

The stone is volcanic glass, new and unweathered, and it breaks in shell-shaped curves whose edges are finer than a surgical blade. The terraces are several feet high, so you will be climbing them by hand. Every handhold is a potential laceration.

- **Cut-resistant gloves** (aramid, steel-mesh-lined, or your world's equivalent) are essential. Ordinary leather will be sliced.
- **Heat resistance matters too.** The risers carry heat in stripes along their internal filaments, and the lower terraces are hot enough that the moss growing there smells scorched. A pair of gloves that handles both cutting and heat is ideal. If you can't find one, bring two pairs and know which is which.
- Cut-resistant gloves are still not cut-proof. **Never slide your hand along an edge.** Place, grip, lift.

### Feet: thick soles, soft soles

You need boots that will not be opened by sharp stone, that will not transmit heat, and that will not slip on wet glass. That is a hard combination.

- **Thick, dense rubber soles** of a slightly soft compound grip wet glass better than hard lug soles. Hard soles skid.
- **Insulated midsoles** matter, because the lower terraces are sun-soaked black stone that "gives heat back doubled" at midday.
- **High ankles and gaiters** help. A slip on a terrace edge is also a slide past a sharp riser.
- Bring **camp shoes** with thick soles as well. Never walk barefoot here, not even for a moment, not even on the moss.

### Eyes

Wear **clear, sealed eye protection** at all times inside the bowl. Glass flakes break away when stone is stressed, when you catch an edge with a boot, or when a cooling sheet on the lowest terrace cracks as it contracts. That ticking sound is the stone moving. A sliver of obsidian in the eye is a genuine emergency far from help.

### Everything else gets wet

It rains in the garden every afternoon from its own steam. Every overhang drips. Beads of water run down every surface. Plan as if you were camping inside a cloud:

- Keep **all electronics, paper, and medicine in waterproof containers**, and assume the air inside any container you open is saturated.
- A **waterproof notebook** and pencil will outlast any device.
- Bring **food sealed in individual packets**. Opened food will soften and may mold within a day or two in this warmth and moisture.
- **Clothing will not dry.** Bring enough changes to rotate, and accept that "damp" is the best you will achieve. Synthetic or wool fabrics stay tolerable when wet. Cotton becomes miserable.
- **Foot care:** wet feet in warm boots for days leads to maceration and blisters. Bring foot powder or balm and spare socks, and air your feet at night.

### Poles

Trekking poles help on the terraces, but fit them with **rubber tips**. Metal tips slip on glass and chip it.

### Rope

The carved stair on the northern rim has a groove along one side that once held a rope. The treads are shaped for feet slightly smaller than yours, so every step will be slightly wrong: too shallow and too short, the rhythm off. A light static line rigged along that groove, anchored at the top, makes the descent much safer. Remove it when you leave.

## Part Four: Heat, Water, and Air

### The water gradient

Meltwater enters at the rim ice-cold. By the second terrace it steams. By the fourth it will burn you. The lowest basins simmer without bubbles, and that is exactly what makes them treacherous. Water that does not visibly boil looks harmless. It is not.

- **Treat any water below the third terrace as scalding** until you have tested it with a thermometer, not your hand. Bring a probe thermometer and use it often.
- **Never step into a basin**, even one that looks shallow. The floors are smooth glass, and the edges are either sharp new lips or soft, half-formed skin.
- **Burn treatment** should be the largest part of your first-aid kit: cooling gel, sterile non-stick dressings, and pain relief. Cool any burn in the colder upper runoff.
- **Steam scalds** too. Don't lean over the basins, especially in the still early hours when vapor is thickest.

### Drinking

Collect drinking water **at the rim, where the falls first come over**, before the water has touched much of the garden. Filter and treat it anyway.

Be very cautious about drinking water from the lower basins, even after cooling it. That water becomes stone overnight. Whatever lets it do that is either dissolved in it or acting on it, and neither possibility is something you want inside your body. The safest assumption is that basin water is not water in the sense your kidneys understand.

### Heat exhaustion

Midday in the bowl is still and very hot, and the black stone radiates heat back at you. Combined with the humidity, sweat will not evaporate and cool you. You can overheat quickly while feeling merely "warm and sticky."

- **Rest through midday** in shade: under the tree ferns, beneath an overhang, or on the upper terraces nearer the cold rim.
- **Drink steadily and replace salts.** Bring electrolyte tablets. You will sweat far more than you notice.
- Watch for headache, confusion, and a stop in sweating. At altitude these overlap with altitude sickness. Either way, the answer is cooling, rest, and water.

### The air in the bottom of the bowl

Nothing in the description mentions a sulfur smell or foul air. The garden smells green, of cloves, of growth. But you are in an enclosed hollow over a heat source, and gases heavier than air collect in hollows. Bring a small **gas monitor** for carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide, and keep it clipped to you at waist height or lower when you descend to the basins. It is especially important if you lie down with your ear to the glass to feel the hum. If the monitor alarms, climb up at once.

## Part Five: Moving Through the Garden

The bowl is small. You could walk its circumference in the time it takes water to boil. You will be tempted to rush it. Don't.

- **Move deliberately, terrace by terrace.** Each riser is a few feet of sharp stone. Test every foothold. Wet glass is slick, and dry glass is sharp.
- **Avoid the lowest terrace near the pools**, where the glass is still soft enough to dent with a fingernail. It may be hot. It may crack. It may not hold your weight at the edges. And you will mark it (more on that later).
- **Don't walk the green seams.** The vegetation follows the water, so the green strips are where the plants live and where the trickles run. Walk on bare glass wherever you can do so safely, stepping over the seams rather than along them.
- **Be especially careful around the dark-red moss.** It grows only on the hottest stones. The clove smell is a warning that you have stepped on hot rock and crushed something living. Treat that color as a hazard marker.
- **Dawn fog makes the bowl a "white room."** Visibility drops almost to nothing. Don't move around on the terraces until the sun burns the steam thin. Sit, listen, and wait for the gold light.

## Part Six: The Living Things

Everything alive here is sparse, slow, and irreplaceable. The ecosystem is a handful of species on bare new rock. Hurting even one creature is a meaningful loss.

- **The glass snails** are hard to see and easy to crush. They shelter under moss at midday and travel across bare stone at other times. Watch where you put your hands and feet. Their shine-trails harden into raised lines by morning, and on the older terraces those lines are everywhere. You are walking on their writing.
- **The heron-bird** is silent and seems unafraid of heat. Don't approach it, don't follow it, and don't feed it. It keeps a schedule, returning to the same basin at the same hour. If you learn that schedule, stay away from that basin at that time.
- **The translucent shrimp** are visible only in exactly the right light. Don't try to catch them. Your hand would be scalded, and there may be very few of them.
- **The pale lizard** appears perhaps once a day. Its skin is so thin you can see its heart beat. Be still. Let it bask. That is all.
- **Plants:** don't pick, taste, or break anything. The figs are unknown and their fruit may be poisonous. The trumpet flowers open and close with the steam. They are among the most beautiful things here, and they are fragile.
- **There are no insects.** You won't need bug spray, and you shouldn't bring it. Don't bring any in by accident, either. Before entering, inspect your gear, pack seams, and food for hitchhikers. An insect let loose in a world with no insects, no predators for them, and endless warmth and plant life could reshape everything.
- **Seeds:** clean your boots, gaiters, and pack thoroughly before the final climb. The garden's plants came on the wind, on birds, or by someone's planting. Don't add to that list.

## Part Seven: The Things Someone Left

### The stair
Use it with rope, respect, and care. It is the oldest human-shaped thing here, or near-human-shaped. The worn treads are evidence of many passages long ago. Don't add wear you don't need to.

### The glass hut
It is tempting to shelter here. It is the only roofed space in the bowl. I would advise against sleeping in it. Not because it is dangerous, but because it is clearly *someone's*: a shelf, three stones, a wire, a lens, arranged. It has a doorway and no door, which reads less as abandonment than as an invitation to look, not to stay. Look.

**The lens:** You may look through it. Be aware that it shows the garden slightly more grown: ferns taller, moss thicker, walls farther away. The most reasonable reading, given what the stone does, is that the lens shows what the garden is becoming. Some people will find this lovely. Some will find it deeply unsettling, as though the room were quietly preferring a version of itself without them in it. Don't look for long, and don't take it. Put it back exactly where it was.

**The warm wire:** It is warm while touching nothing, which means it is drawing heat or energy from somewhere unseen. Don't pocket it. Don't straighten it. Don't touch it to anything else, especially not the posts.

### The posts
Seven slender pillars of iridescent metal, just above head height, humming below hearing. Resting a hand on one for a moment seems to be harmless. Visitors report only vibration. But don't hang from them, strike them, scratch them, or wrap anything around them. Remove any metal jewelry from that hand first, as a sensible caution around unknown energized objects.

### The machine
Half-submerged in the largest basin, slumped as though partly melted, surrounded by the clearest water and the fastest-forming glass. Consider this the heart of the garden and treat it the way you would treat a live electrical transformer standing in boiling water. **Do not touch it.** Do not wade toward it. Do not throw anything into its water. Watch it from the terrace above.

## Part Eight: Where and How to Camp

- **Camp high, on the old northern terraces** near the rim, where the stone is oldest and has begun to dull. Those surfaces are more stable and less sharp. They are also cooler, closer to your drinking water, and closest to the exit.
- **Never camp on the lowest terrace or near the basins.** Glass skin creeps outward from the basin edges each night, visibly, advancing as you watch at dusk. Anything left within reach of that skin may be fused into the new stone by morning. That includes your gear, your trash, and in principle you.
- **Use a thick, puncture-resistant ground pad**, and put a heavy groundsheet under your tent. Glass edges will cut thin tent floors.
- **Your tent must breathe.** Condensation inside a sealed tent here will be relentless. Ventilate it, and accept that the inner walls will drip.
- **Sleep:** the garden never goes silent. The glass ticks all night as it cools. The drip falls in a rhythm that almost becomes a beat and never quite does. Some people find this soothing. Others find it maddening, precisely because it never resolves. Bring earplugs if you are the second kind.
- **Leave nothing behind, and leave nothing unattended low in the bowl overnight.** Ordinary leave-no-trace principles become literal here. Anything you drop may become a permanent fossil in the stone. A candy wrapper could end up preserved in obsidian for as long as the mountain stands.

## Part Nine: Leaving

This is where visitors come to real harm, and it is the reason you kept your insulation sealed.

When you climb back out over the lip, you go through the door the other way. From saturated heat into thin, freezing, blue wind. If you are wearing clothes soaked from days of drip and sweat, they will begin to freeze against your body within minutes, at altitude, while you are tired. This is how people die of hypothermia on the way home from a hothouse.

**Before you crest the rim:**
1. Stop on the uppermost terrace, where it is still warm.
2. Dry yourself as well as you can with a small towel kept in your dry bag.
3. Strip off **everything** damp, including base layers and socks.
4. Put on the **dry layers** from the sealed bag: base layer, insulation, dry socks, shell, dry gloves, hat.
5. Wipe or bag any wet gear on the outside of your pack. Water bottles, straps, and anything else will freeze stiff.
6. Replace eye protection with snow goggles, and put your traction back on only once you are off the glass.
7. Then step through.

Plan to leave in the **morning**, after the dawn fog lifts and with the whole day ahead of you. Don't leave in the evening. Descending a mountain in the dark in freezing wind while your body is still adjusting from the heat is how a lovely week becomes a rescue.

## Part Ten: The Rule, and How to Hold It in Your Mind

Now the part that no gear can handle.

### What you will see

If you stay more than a couple of days, and you should stay at least four or five to see it clearly, you will notice that the new stone forming at the basin edges carries impressions. A fern frond. A leaf. A snail track. A bird's three-toed print. Then you will realize they are impressions of things that *haven't happened yet*. A frond that won't unroll for three days. Bird prints the bird will later walk into, placing its feet precisely.

At some point you may find **your own footprints**: your boot tread, the particular drag of your heel, leading toward a basin you haven't approached.

### How to prepare for that moment

**Decide in advance what you will do, because you will not want to decide it on the spot.** I suggest: nothing. Look at them. Photograph or sketch them if you like. Then go about your day, as most visitors apparently have.

Several reasons support this:

- **The prints are not commands.** Visitors who never followed them report that the prints were simply still there in the morning, unfilled, and the garden did not seem troubled. It proposes. It does not insist.
- **Following them may be physically dangerous.** Your prints lead toward a basin, and the basins are scalding. If you find yourself drawn to walk your own path as though it were an instruction, remember that the heron-bird's legs apparently feel nothing. Yours will.
- **Deliberately defying them is its own kind of interference.** You may feel a mischievous or defiant urge to test the forecast, to do the opposite on purpose. Consider that the place "becomes more itself the less you interfere." You are a guest in a hearth someone left burning. Kicking the logs to prove you can is poor manners, even if nothing bad happens.

### The quieter effect

The people who stayed longest describe something more subtle: the whole garden leaning, gently, toward a finished form it already knows. Plants grow back into their predicted shapes. Water finds its intended channels. The bird keeps its hours.

Long enough inside that pattern, you may start to feel the lean yourself. You may start keeping hours. You may find yourself sitting at the same place at the same time each evening, walking the same route to the rim for water, as though being written toward a shape. This is not necessarily sinister. It may simply be what it feels like to live somewhere peaceful and ordered. But it is worth noticing.

**Practical safeguards:**
- **Set your departure date before you arrive, and keep it.** Write it down and tell someone outside. If you find yourself wanting to extend it, and especially if you can't quite say why, treat that as a reason to leave on schedule.
- **Travel with a companion if you can.** Two people can check each other's sense of what is normal. One of you should keep a plain written log of what you actually did each day, to compare against what the stone showed.
- **Notice if the stone stops showing you.** If your prints stop appearing, it may mean the garden has stopped expecting you. Or it may mean nothing. Either way, it is a reasonable moment to go.
- **Afterward,** some visitors find that ordinary life feels slightly unscripted and loud. That is normal. Give yourself time.

### A way of seeing it that may help

You can think of the forecast stone as something like a tree's rings, laid down in the wrong direction. The garden is remembering what it will be. Seen that way, your footprints aren't a trap or a prophecy. They are the garden's polite guess about its guest: you will probably walk down there, to look at the water, because everyone does. Being guessed about accurately is a little humbling. It is not a cage.

## Part Eleven: Timing Your Days

Because the garden has strong daily rhythms, plan around them:

| Time | Conditions | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Dawn** | Thick steam, white-out, cold, dripping, ticking | Stay put. Listen. Wait for the light to turn the steam gold, one of the best moments here. |
| **Morning** | Clearing, trumpet flowers opening, bird appears | Best time for careful movement and observation. Fetch water at the rim. |
| **Midday** | Still, very hot, sharp shadows, heat shimmer | Rest in shade on upper terraces. Hydrate. Snails hide. Don't descend to the basins. |
| **Afternoon** | Condensation rain, falls at their loudest | Shelter under ferns or overhangs. Write notes. Protect gear. |
| **Evening** | Basins cool, glass skin forms visibly, posts warm, lizard emerges | The richest hour. Watch the basin edges from a safe height for the first shapes surfacing. |
| **Night** | Dark, starlit, ticking | Stay high. Headlamp for camp chores only. Don't move among the terraces in the dark. |

## Part Twelve: The Short Checklist

**For the approach and exit:** full cold-weather layers (kept in a sealed dry bag inside the garden), snow goggles, microspikes or crampons, navigation tools, altitude medication.

**For the bowl:** cut- and heat-resistant gloves, thick soft-soled insulated boots, gaiters, camp shoes, sealed eye protection, quick-drying synthetic or wool clothing in several changes, towel, rubber-tipped poles, light static rope.

**Safety:** probe thermometer, gas monitor, extensive burn and laceration first aid, electrolytes, foot care supplies.

**Camp:** puncture-resistant pad and groundsheet, ventilated tent, earplugs, sealed food, water filter and treatment, bags for carrying out *everything*.

**Records:** waterproof notebook, pencil, camera with anti-fog protection, a written departure date.

**Before entry:** gear cleaned of seeds and insects.

**Leave behind:** nothing. Take with you: nothing that was here.

---

## Last Thought

You are going to a place that is still being born. Most wild places you'll ever visit are old: worn down, settled, indifferent. This one is new, sharp-edged, attentive to its own becoming. It will ask very little of you except care. Walk lightly on the stone, drink from the top, sleep high, leave on time, and when you find your own footsteps waiting for you in the soft glass by the water, you can simply look at them for a while, and let the garden keep its guess.
