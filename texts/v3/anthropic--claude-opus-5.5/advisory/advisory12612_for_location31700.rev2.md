# Preparing to Visit the Crown of the Stack

## First, the Honest Summary

The Crown is not dangerous the way a jungle or a battlefield is dangerous. Nothing here wants to hurt you. The place is simply indifferent to the body you're bringing. It is built for rising air, the swifts that live in it, and a note that never stops. You arrive as the only thing in the room that needs a floor, needs to hear, needs to talk, and falls down instead of up.

Almost every preparation below answers one of five facts:

1. **There is no floor.** There is only a narrow ring of old grating over a drop that does not end.
2. **The sound is not really a sound.** It is a physical condition, and you will be inside it the whole time.
3. **The air is saturated and skin-warm inside, and freezing and thin outside,** with a boundary as sharp as a knife between them.
4. **Everything light rises.** That includes your belongings.
5. **You will not be able to communicate by voice** with anyone, including your companions and yourself.

My intuition is that the visit feels less like exploring and more like being admitted somewhere. It feels like entering the engine room of a ship that has been at sea for ten thousand years. Prepare accordingly: quietly, thoroughly, and with the expectation that you will do very little except stand, look, and hold on.

## Choosing When to Go

### Know the phase of the breath

The furnace slackens roughly every seven years, for about a season. Find out where in the cycle the Crown is before you commit. The two phases are almost two different places.

**The full breath (most of the time).** This is the Crown as described: roaring, rising, strung with silver threads, swifts sleeping in the column. It is the harder visit and the truer one.

**The lull (a few weeks every seven years).** It is quieter. You might hear rain on the iron and frogs calling aloud. It is tempting, and in many ways gentler on your body. But it has its own hazards:

- Everything that normally floats comes down, including spider threads hundreds of meters long, seed fluff, rust flakes, and whatever the threads caught. Expect to be draped and dusted. Wear eye protection.
- The swifts are grounded, bewildered, and clinging to vines. Having never landed, they are extremely vulnerable. Your presence during the lull is a much bigger imposition on the life here than during the full breath.
- The heat will come back. Try to learn how suddenly the roar returns. If it swells back over hours, fine. If it returns in a surge, you do not want to be halfway up the ladder when your inner ears get hit with it.

**My recommendation:** a first visit should be during the full breath. That is what the place *is*. Visiting only during the lull is like visiting the sea only at dead calm and saying you've seen it. If you have the luxury, come back during a lull later, keep it brief, and stay off the vines.

### Time of day

The circle of sky is your only real light source. The garden is brightest around midday, when the sun is highest over the opening. The frog-light shows only in the long evenings. A good first visit arrives late morning, lets you settle in daylight, and stays into evening for the lanterns. It should not include an overnight stay unless you've prepared for one (see below).

## Your Body: Getting Ready Before You Leave

### Fitness

You need to be able to:

- Climb roughly 80 meters of vertical iron ladder, some of it slick with moss and condensation, while clipped in and wearing a pack. Train for this specifically, with ladder climbing, via ferrata routes, or tower climbing, not just general cardio.
- Stand for hours on grating in a vibrating environment. Your calves and feet will take a beating. Muscles stay slightly tensed against a constant tremor.
- Work in a harness comfortably. If you have never hung in one, practice. Learn to clip and unclip your lanyards without looking, by feel, with wet hands.

### Medical preparation

- **Tetanus booster.** You will be handling rusted iron constantly, and small cuts from flaking plate edges are likely.
- **Allergy assessment.** The entire garden is seeded from below by airborne spores and fluff. The air is a thick soup of biological particulates. If you have spore or mold allergies or asthma, take this seriously. Bring antihistamines, and a rescue inhaler if relevant.
- **Anti-nausea medication.** Low-frequency sound at this intensity commonly causes nausea, disorientation, and a vague bodily dread. Meclizine or something similar, taken before you enter, is wise. It will not stop the feeling of the note, only blunt the seasickness.
- **Altitude.** This is easy to overlook. The Crown sits above the clouds. The gallery is inside a warm, humid, roaring column, and it will not *feel* high. But the air pressure inside is the same as the air pressure outside, and outside is thin enough to freeze. Your body will be working at altitude the whole time, while the noise and heat mask the usual signals. Acclimatize beforehand if you can. Bring a pulse oximeter and check it every hour or so. Headache, breathlessness on the ladder, and poor judgment may be altitude rather than the note, and you won't easily tell the difference.
- **Hearing baseline.** Get your hearing tested before and after. Even with protection, you want to know what this place did to you.

### Who should reconsider

People with inner-ear disorders, severe acrophobia, panic disorder, pacemakers or other implanted devices (the vibration is pervasive and unknown), or significant heart or lung conditions should think carefully. This isn't gatekeeping. The Crown gives you no quiet corner to recover in, and no way to call for help by voice.

## The Sound: How to Survive It

This is the heart of the preparation. Everything else is mountaineering and wet-weather practice. The note is unique.

### Understand what protection can and can't do

The roar has audible components and components below hearing. **Hearing protection helps with the first and does almost nothing for the second.** The infrasonic part goes through your skull, chest, and belly. You will feel it in your ribs, teeth, and eyeballs whatever you put in your ears.

Still, wear protection. The audible grind alone will damage hearing over hours.

- **Double up:** foam earplugs, properly inserted, plus over-ear muffs rated for industrial noise.
- **Bring spares.** Earplugs will get damp and lose their seal. Change them every few hours.
- Choose muffs with **sealed, moisture-resistant cushions.** Foam cushions will turn to wet sponges within an hour.

### What it will feel like

- **The first minutes:** stunning. You may feel physically pushed. Some people report that their vision seems to shimmer, which is likely the eye itself resonating slightly. Your heart may seem to beat oddly against the rhythm. Sit down on the grating if you need to, clipped in.
- **The first hour:** your teeth hum. You'll feel a low-grade dread or awe with no clear object. That is the body's reaction to infrasound, not a sign that something is wrong. Name it to yourself, silently, so it doesn't run you.
- **After an hour or so:** the note stops being a sound and becomes a condition, like altitude or temperature. This is when you're adapted. It is also when your judgment slips, because you've stopped noticing that you're impaired. Keep checking your lanyards deliberately.

### Practice beforehand

If possible, spend time near large low-frequency sources before your trip: big industrial fans, ship engine rooms, very large pipe organs played at their lowest stops. None will match the Crown, but they teach your body that a pressure in the chest from sound is survivable.

### Limit your exposure

Plan for **no more than four to six hours** inside on a first visit, with breaks at the rim if you're able to climb. The rim is not silent, since the stack still hums beneath you, but it is a different and gentler condition.

## Communicating Without Sound

You cannot speak. You cannot shout. A whistle is useless. You will not hear your companion fall, your gear drop, or your own voice cry out. Prepare to be, functionally, deaf, and to rely on sight and touch.

### Before you enter, agree on:

- **A hand-signal vocabulary.** Keep it small and unmistakable. At minimum: *stop*, *look*, *I'm okay*, *I'm not okay*, *I need to leave*, *come here*, *check your clip*, *going up*, *going down*. Practice until it's reflexive.
- **Tug codes on a shared line.** If you're tethered to a partner along the gallery, a code of tugs works well: one for attention, two for "come to me," three repeated for emergency. Touch travels where sound doesn't.
- **Light signals.** A small lamp with a momentary switch lets you signal across the room. Use a red or amber lamp (see the frogs, below), and keep it dim.

### Bring:

- **A waterproof slate or waterproof notebook** with grease pencils or pencils. Regular paper will be pulp within an hour. Ink pens may run.
- **Pre-written cards** for common messages. Laminated, on a lanyard.
- **Vibration is not a usable alert.** Your phone buzzing, a vibrating pager, or a haptic alarm will be lost in the constant tremor. Any alarms you rely on, such as a gas detector, an altitude warning, or a turnaround timer, need to be **visual**: bright flashing lights. Set them to trigger well before you actually need to act.

### A note on being alone with your thoughts

With speech removed and every other sound swallowed, you'll be unusually alone even with companions a meter away. For some visitors this is profound. For others it is frightening. It helps to decide beforehand what you'll do with the silence inside the roar. Some people count rivets. Some watch the swifts. Some simply let it happen.

## Heights, Grating, and Tethering

### The gallery

The gallery is a ring of old iron grating a few paces wide, bolted to the wall over an infinite drop. It has been there a very long time in saturated air, and rust is the soil of this place. **Do not assume the grating is sound.**

- **Test each section** before you commit weight. Tap it with a trekking pole or a staff. You won't hear the answer, so press, watch, and feel for flex.
- **Walk along the wall side**, over the bolts and brackets, rather than the railing side. The grating is best supported where it's bolted.
- **Don't trust the railing** as a load-bearing anchor until you've inspected it. Look at the brackets. Look at whether the uprights are rusted through at their bases.
- **Watch for grating-snails.** They are landmarks, and they move slowly enough that one might be on the same patch of grating for decades. Do not step on them, move them, or pry them off. Their polished trails are also slicker than surrounding rust, so watch your footing on them.

### Tethering yourself

Wear a **full-body climbing harness** with **two lanyards** (a Y-lanyard or twin slings with locking carabiners). Keep at least one clipped to something solid at all times.

- **What to clip to:** structural brackets, heavy brass pipe mounts that are actually bolted to the plate (inspect them), ladder rungs near the base, and bolt heads if they're sound. Consider bringing slings long enough to wrap around a thick pipe.
- **What not to clip to:** fern trunks, fig roots, vines, the railing (until verified), gauges, and **absolutely not the valve wheel** (see below).
- Consider running a **fixed line** along the gallery for your party, anchored at intervals, so you can slide along it rather than leapfrogging clips on every step.

### What falling means here

Don't let yourself think about the updraft as a cushion. It is warm air at skin temperature. It lifts seeds, fluff, and threads, not people. A human who goes over the railing falls, and there is no bottom anyone has found. This is the one truly unrecoverable mistake the Crown offers. Let it make you methodical, not paranoid.

### Vertigo and the pull of the void

The void is described as the most important thing in the room, and it will feel that way. Expect **the call of the void**: the involuntary thought of stepping off, which heights provoke in many people. It is normal and harmless if you know it's coming. The warm breath rising out of that dimness, carrying the smell of bread and hot metal, will make it feel strangely inviting. Keep your back to the wall when you're tired. Look *up* at the circle of sky when you need to steady yourself.

## Everything Rises: Managing Your Gear

On the gallery, light things go up and heavy things go down. Neither comes back.

### Tether everything

- **Hats:** Don't wear anything that isn't strapped on. Better, wear a close-fitting cap under a hood, or a climbing helmet. A loose hat will rise into the column and join the fluff.
- **Hair:** Tie it back and tuck it in. Long loose hair will stream upward and tangle in anything nearby, including spider threads.
- **Loose clothing:** Avoid flapping jackets, scarves, and drawstrings. The draft will catch them constantly.
- **Tools, cameras, notebooks, water bottles:** each on its own **tether** clipped to your harness or pack. Tool lanyards of the kind used by tower workers are ideal.
- **Paper:** Any loose leaf released near the railing will rise. Keep notebooks bound and closed when not in use.

### Things walk

Loose objects crawl across the grating on their own, walked along by the vibration. **Anything you set down will not stay where you put it.** It may walk to the edge, through the gaps in the grating, or under the railing. Never set anything down unclipped. Not for a moment, not "just while I adjust this."

### Opening bags

Open packs **with your body between the bag and the void**, facing the wall. Wrappers, tissues, and receipts will leap upward the moment they're free.

### Waste

Pack out everything, including human waste. Do not relieve yourself into the void. Whatever goes into that column either rises back onto the walls or joins the breath. It is a closed ecology fed from below, and you'd be seeding it with yourself. Bring a waste kit designed for climbers or big-wall ascents.

### Don't throw things to test the depth

You'll want to. Almost everyone would. Drop a coin and count, toss a stone and listen. But you will not hear it land. A light object may simply rise back past your face. And the depth is, by all accounts, not a number you can have. Resist it.

## The Wet

The air is saturated and skin-warm, and the walls are cold and running with condensation. You'll pass through both constantly.

### Clothing

- **Base layer:** synthetic, fast-drying. Merino wool is a fine alternative: it stays warm when wet and resists odor, both of which matter here. Avoid cotton entirely. It will be sodden within minutes and stay that way.
- **Mid layer:** thin and breathable. You don't need warmth inside the column. The air is at body temperature, and exertion on the ladder will overheat you.
- **Outer layer:** a lightweight, waterproof shell. Its job inside is to keep condensation off you when you brush the walls, which you will, constantly.
- **Gloves:** grippy, waterproof or quick-drying, with good dexterity for handling carabiners. Bring several pairs. They'll soak through. Iron in saturated air stains everything rust-orange, so don't bring anything you want to keep pristine.
- **Footwear:** stiff-soled boots with good grip on wet metal. Grating can catch soft soles and twist ankles. Bring multiple pairs of spare socks in sealed bags, and change them at every break. Hours in saturated air invites trench foot and fungal infections.

### Your gear will rust

The Crown turns iron into soil. Your carabiners, knives, and zippers are also metal.

- Prefer **aluminum, titanium, or stainless steel.**
- Wipe down and oil anything steel after the visit.
- Inspect climbing hardware afterward for corrosion.

### Electronics and optics

- Condensation will fog lenses, screens, and anything that enters from colder air. Let cameras acclimatize inside a sealed bag before exposing them.
- Waterproof cases for anything electronic. Assume everything will be damp.
- Batteries may drain faster in the cold at the rim. Keep spares close to your body.

### Water

You'll sweat heavily and may not notice, because you're already wet. **Drink on a schedule.** Bring your own water. The running condensation on the walls is drinkable in an emergency, but it's full of dissolved iron and minerals and has run over centuries of rust, moss, and droppings. Filter and treat it. **Do not drink from the pitcher plants.** That water is the frogs' home and the plants' digestive fluid.

## The Air

- The rising column smells of hot metal, wet ash, and something like bread, and it comes from an unknown furnace. **Bring a gas detector** for carbon monoxide at minimum, with a **visual alarm.** The air is probably fine, because life thrives here. But you don't know what's burning, and you won't hear an alarm.
- **Particulates:** the air carries rust dust, spores, insect fragments, and seed fluff. A light respirator or a good-quality mask will help, especially for allergy sufferers. It will get damp and uncomfortable, so bring several.
- **Eye protection:** clear, anti-fog glasses. Things rise past your face constantly. During the lull, things fall on you.

## The Living Things: How to Behave Around Them

The Crown's life is spread thin, delicate, and unused to visitors. You are huge and clumsy, and you make light.

### The swifts

They have never landed, sleep in the updraft, and mate on the wing. You'll see them as ash on the breath. Do nothing to them. Don't reach toward them from the railing. Don't disturb their nests: felted cups of silk and fluff glued to the rising vines. During the lull, if they're clinging to vines near you, move away and give them room.

### The moths

Blind and palm-sized, they navigate by feeling the note, and they cluster around the gauges. Your body subtly changes how vibration travels near the walls. You may find moths bumping into you, landing on you, or clustering around you as if you were a gauge. Let them. Move slowly. Don't brush them off roughly; their wings are delicate. Their wings smell of hot iron, which is a lovely, strange detail to notice.

### The frogs

Translucent, living in the pitcher plants and moss, they speak in orange light from their throat sacs. **This is the most important behavioral rule in the Crown: be careful with your lights.**

- Use **red or dim amber light** only, especially in the evening.
- Never sweep a bright white beam across the walls.
- Don't flash signal lamps toward the frog-covered walls. Signal toward the wall your companion is standing against, or keep signals shielded and low.
- Consider not using lights at all in the evening, and simply sitting in the dark watching the wall flicker with thousands of silent lanterns. That is one of the two or three most worthwhile things you can do here.

### The long spiders

Their threads rise hundreds of meters into the draft and once caught a whole live bird. **They are strong.**

- Don't grab them, pull them, or walk through them.
- Watch for threads drifting across the gallery. They are nearly invisible except where they catch the light.
- Keep hair, straps, and loose ends controlled. A thread that tangles in your gear could be very difficult to remove without destroying it.
- During the lull, threads that normally point skyward will sag and drape. Expect to walk through fallen silk, and be gentle about it.

### The plants

- **Don't cut, pull, or climb the vines.** Some are forty meters long and have never hung down. They also hold swift nests.
- **Don't use tree ferns or fig roots as handholds** if you can avoid it. They are rooted in rust and wall-seams. Their grip on the plate is only as good as the iron behind it.
- **Fig fruit:** when it ripens and drops, it falls up for a while before falling down. Watching this is a small joy. Eating it is a gamble on an unknown plant in an unknown mineral soil. I'd leave it.
- **Don't peel the rust shale.** It *is* the soil. A plate the size of your hand may be decades of accumulation.

## The Machinery: Leave It Alone

The Crown's apparatus is still running, with no one watching it.

- **The valve wheel.** It turns on its own, a fraction of a degree a year, and the ferns around it have grown bent to make room for its spokes. **Do not touch it, do not clip to it, do not lean on it, and do not stand in its path.** Its motion is slow enough to be invisible, but it has a purpose it has kept for a very long time, and you should not be part of that purpose. My instinct says the wheel matters more than it looks.
- **The hatch** no longer opens. Don't try.
- **The gauges.** Look at them. Their needles drift, pointing at numbers no one reads. Photograph them, note them in your waterproof book if you like. Don't wipe the fog off their faces, don't tap them, and don't disturb the moths clustered there.
- **The brass pipes.** Some are as thick as a person. Some may be hot, cold, or pressurized. Check before you touch or clip to them. Look for leaks and corrosion before you trust a mount.
- **The maker's mark.** A circle with a line through it: a stack, or an eye. Look at it. Don't scrape the moss away to see it better.

You may find yourself wanting to understand the machinery, to figure out what the furnace is, what the gauges measure, and whether the other stacks are part of one system. That's natural. But the Crown's rules include *nothing is made anew*. Your visit shouldn't change anything the place has kept running.

## The Ladder and the Rim

### The ladder

About eighty meters of iron rungs from gallery to rim, furred with moss, wet with falling damp.

- **Climb with a proper fall-arrest system.** Ideally a rope-grab or cable system. If there is none, clip your two lanyards alternately to rungs (and check each rung before you trust it) as you ascend. This is slow and exhausting. Plan for it.
- **Moss is slick.** Place hands and feet deliberately. Test each rung.
- **Pace yourself.** You're at altitude, in saturated heat, in a vibrating environment, carrying a pack. Rest often, clipped in. Check your oximeter partway up.
- **The top rungs are polished bright,** as though someone still climbs. You'll feel this when you reach them: suddenly clean, dry-worn metal under your hands after eighty meters of moss. It's unsettling. My advice is not to linger on the question, not to wait to see who comes, and not to leave anything on the ladder as a sign or a test. Climb, use the rungs courteously, and move on. It isn't your business.

### The transition

This is where many visitors could get hurt. Below the rim you're in skin-warm, saturated air. You're drenched with sweat and condensation. Above the rim you step into thin, freezing, sideways wind, above the clouds. **Your wet clothing will begin to freeze almost immediately.**

- **Before you reach the frost line**, which is sharp and visible, a few meters below the edge, stop, clip in, and put on your cold-weather layer.
- Carry a **dry insulated jacket, hat, and gloves in a sealed waterproof bag** specifically for the rim. Do not open that bag inside the column until you need it, or it will be damp too.
- Consider swapping your wet gloves for dry, insulated ones. Wet gloves on freezing iron will leach heat from your hands very quickly. Bare skin on frozen metal can stick.
- The orchid-to-frost boundary is as clean as a painted stripe. It's beautiful. Pause and look at it. Then keep moving.

### At the rim

- **Wind.** It's lateral and strong. Stay clipped in the entire time. Don't stand fully upright on the edge unless you're secured and confident.
- **Altitude.** You'll feel it much more sharply here. Breathe deliberately.
- **What you'll see:** a white floor of cloud to every horizon, and perhaps a dozen other stacks rising through it, each with its green smudge of garden, each breathing a faint plume. Like the chimneys of a drowned city.
- **The chord.** If you stand very still and let your body stop trembling, you can feel the other stacks' notes, faint and far, at slightly different pitches, together forming a slow chord that never resolves. You can't do this in the first minute. You have to let the Crown's own roar settle out of you. Budget time, maybe fifteen or twenty minutes, just standing, clipped in, warm enough, and waiting. My intuition is that this is the reason to make the climb at all.
- **Don't stay too long.** Cold, wind, altitude, and exhaustion stack up quickly. Set a visual timer before you arrive.

### Descending

Going down is harder than going up in almost every respect: fatigue, slick moss, and the long drop visible beneath you the whole time. Before you re-enter the column, swap your dry layers back into their sealed bag. They'll be damp by the time you're back at the gallery otherwise, and you'll want them dry if you need to climb again.

## Staying Overnight

I'd advise against it on a first visit. If you do:

- **Sleep clipped in**, in a harness, with your back to the wall. A portaledge or hammock rigged to secure anchors is ideal. Don't sleep loose on the grating. You and your gear will be walked slowly by the vibration.
- **Expect strange sleep.** The note never pauses. Some people may find it oddly lulling, like sleeping in an engine room. Others won't sleep at all and will feel the hum in their teeth all night.
- **The evening frog-light** is the main reason to stay. Keep your lights off.
- **Watch the swifts.** At dusk and dawn, they rise sleeping through the column and wake near the rim, then glide down in long spirals.
- **Dampness is worse overnight.** Put on dry socks before sleeping. Keep your sleeping bag in a waterproof bivy.
- **Keep your gas detector** where you can see its alarm light.

## Leaving, and Afterward

- **Re-adjusting to silence.** After hours in the Crown, the ordinary world will feel eerily, even alarmingly, quiet. You may feel a phantom note in your ribs for hours or days afterward. Some visitors may feel slightly bereft, as though a floor has been taken away. That is the note's absence, not your ears failing.
- **Get your hearing retested.**
- **Watch for delayed effects:** persistent nausea, lingering vertigo, headaches, or respiratory symptoms from spores. Treat them the way you'd treat after-effects of altitude or a dusty environment, and see a doctor if they don't fade.
- **Inspect and clean your gear.** Wipe and oil anything steel. Check your harness and lanyards for rust damage. Wash all clothing promptly. Everything will smell of wet iron and bread.
- **Dry out your skin,** especially feet. Watch for fungal infections.

## The Short Packing List

**Safety:** full-body harness; two lanyards with locking carabiners; spare slings; fall-arrest device for the ladder; climbing helmet; fixed line for the gallery; staff or trekking pole for testing grating.

**Sound:** foam earplugs (many spares); sealed industrial earmuffs; anti-nausea medication.

**Communication:** waterproof slate or notebook; grease pencils; laminated signal cards; red/amber signal lamp with momentary switch; agreed hand-signal and tug-code vocabulary.

**Clothing:** synthetic or merino base layers; lightweight waterproof shell; multiple pairs of grippy gloves; stiff-soled boots; many spare socks in sealed bags; **sealed bag with dry insulated jacket, hat, and gloves for the rim.**

**Health:** first aid kit; antihistamines; inhaler if needed; pulse oximeter; tetanus booster before departure; CO detector with visual alarm; respirator masks; anti-fog glasses.

**Gear management:** tool tethers for everything; waterproof cases for electronics; waste kit; water with filter or treatment backup.

**Light:** red or dim amber headlamp. No bright white lights toward the walls.

## Closing Thoughts

The Crown asks almost nothing of you except that you not add to it and not subtract from it. It has been running without anyone for a very long time. Its seeds come up from below, its water rises as warmth and falls back as dew, and something far down in the dark keeps burning without ever asking for anything.

You're a visitor in a place built for rising, and your whole preparation is to make yourself, briefly, something that doesn't fall. Be heavy where you have to be: clipped in, boots on sound grating. Be light everywhere else: no lights on the frogs, no hands on the wheel, nothing let go into the column.

If you do it right, the reward is a few hours standing inside a sound too large to hear, with swifts sleeping above you. Then a cold climb to the rim, where for a few minutes, if you are very still, you can feel a dozen distant chimneys holding one long chord that has never resolved and probably never will.
