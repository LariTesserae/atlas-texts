# Preparing for the Tableland of Unfallen Walls

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Most dangerous places are dangerous in obvious ways. They have predators, cliffs, storms, and hostile people. The Tableland has almost none of these. There is nothing here that wants to hurt you, and there is nothing sharp enough to fall from.

It will still try to kill you, slowly. It will do it with cold and wet, with altitude and distance, and with a gradual erosion of your sense of time and direction. On top of that, it is a place where time runs strangely around the one human thing in it. Your preparation has two halves: surviving a high, cold, foggy wilderness for weeks, and conducting yourself sensibly around something you do not understand.

Hold both in mind. People will be tempted to over-prepare for the uncanny and under-prepare for the wet socks. The wet socks are more likely to end your trip.

## Part One: The Body

### Altitude

Four thousand meters is serious. Air pressure is roughly 60% of sea level. You can expect breathlessness, headaches, poor sleep, and loss of appetite. At worst you risk acute mountain sickness, high-altitude pulmonary edema, or cerebral edema, and the last two can kill.

- **Acclimatize before you go.** Spend a week or more at 2,500–3,500 meters somewhere ordinary. Arriving straight from sea level onto a plateau with no quick way down is the single most dangerous thing you could do. On a mountain you descend when you get sick. Here there is no "down" within reach: the table runs past sight in every direction.
- **Know the warning signs** in yourself and your companions:
  - a headache that doesn't respond to painkillers;
  - confusion or loss of coordination (test by walking heel-to-toe);
  - breathlessness at rest;
  - a wet, crackling cough.
- **Carry medication** if your world has it: acetazolamide for prevention, dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies. Learn the doses from a doctor before you leave.
- **Take the confusion symptom seriously.** It will be very hard to tell altitude-induced confusion from the Tableland's own disorientation. If a companion says their shadow is lagging, that may be true here. If they can't walk a straight line, treat it as medical first and metaphysical second.

### Cold and Wet Together

The description says the chill is "not bitter, just unrelenting." That is the most dangerous kind of cold. Near-freezing temperatures combined with total saturation cause more hypothermia deaths than deep cold does. Fog will bead on everything, your clothes will never truly dry, and the noon sun gives light but no heat.

- **Avoid cotton entirely.** Wear wool or synthetic next to skin, a fleece or synthetic-fill midlayer, and a genuinely waterproof, breathable shell with hood.
- **Choose synthetic insulation over down.** Down fails when it gets damp, and everything here gets damp. If you must bring down, keep it in a waterproof bag and use it only inside your shelter.
- **Consider vapor-barrier liners** for sleeping, and possibly for socks, so your own sweat doesn't soak your insulation from inside.
- **Dress for nights colder than days.** Nights are clear and radiate heat away to the stars, which is where the frost comes from. Bring a sleeping bag rated well below freezing.
- **Keep a sacred dry set** of base layers and socks, sealed in a waterproof bag. Wear them only to sleep, and never walk in them.

### Feet

You will walk through knee-deep sphagnum that "presses out a small cold spring" with every step. Your feet will be wet for weeks. Trench foot, or non-freezing cold injury, is a real risk and can cause lasting nerve damage.

- **Boots:** waterproof boots will eventually flood, so some travelers prefer quick-draining boots with neoprene or waterproof socks. Either way, plan for wet feet.
- **Daily routine:** dry your feet every night, check them for white, wrinkled, numb, or painful patches, and rotate socks.
- **Protection:** bring a foot balm or barrier cream.
- **Gaiters** help in the moss.

### Sun

At noon the sky goes "nearly violet at the zenith," and the light is a "clean white glare." At this altitude, thin air means intense UV even when you feel cold.

- **Eyes:** wear glacier glasses or high-grade wraparound sunglasses. Snow blindness from the glare off wet stone and frost is possible.
- **Skin:** wear high-SPF sunscreen and lip balm on exposed skin during the clear window. The two hours of sun will burn you while you shiver.

### Terrain

The ground is gentle but not safe.

- **Grus** (rotted granite gravel) is loose and crunchy, and can roll underfoot. The tors crumble under a thumb, so don't climb them expecting solid holds.
- **Bog and peat** are the real hazard. Peat here may be many meters deep, because nothing decomposes and it just accumulates. Bring trekking poles and probe ahead in doubtful ground. Travel with others, and carry a length of rope for pulling someone out.
- **Cushion plants** are firm enough to stand on. **Don't.** A cushion "grows a finger's width in a lifetime," and the largest are older than anything else living. Your boot print on one may outlast your grandchildren. Walk around them.

## Part Two: Supplies

### Food

There are no animals and nothing you should assume is edible. The plants are unknown to any botany you have, and "never rots" suggests a biochemistry you shouldn't test on yourself.

- **Bring every calorie you'll need.** Cold and altitude together can push requirements to 4,000–5,000 calories a day.
- **Pack dense, simple food:** nut butters, fats, dried meats, hard cheese, chocolate, oats, and dehydrated meals.
- **Plan for weeks.** "Travelers speak of weeks, then stop speaking of time." Figure out the longest plausible trip, then add a margin, because distances stretch and "a tor that seems an hour away at noon is still an hour away at dusk."
- **Eat even when you don't want to.** Altitude kills appetite, so bring food you genuinely enjoy.

### Water

Water is everywhere but not simple.

- **Tarns and streams** are slow, peaty, probably acidic, and stained with tannins. Filter them and treat them. If there are truly no microbes here the water may be safe, but you can't confirm that, and you are about to bring microbes in yourself.
- **Moss** can be wrung out in an emergency.
- **The rosette crown pools** should not be your water supply. See Part Four.

### Fuel and Fire

- **Bring a stove and your own fuel.** Canister stoves lose performance in the cold, so keep canisters warm in your sleeping bag overnight, or use a liquid-fuel stove. Water boils at about 86°C at this altitude, so cooking takes longer and uses more fuel.
- **Don't burn the landscape.** Fallen bark lies in drifts "dry beneath and damp on top." In a world without rot, fuel accumulates for millennia. A careless fire in a peat bank or bark drift could smolder underground for years. Peat fires are notoriously hard to put out, and you would be burning ten thousand years of record.
- **Never light the hearths.** They are laid with rosette-skirt fuel and have never been lit. This is less a safety rule than a rule of decency, and possibly more than that. More on this below.

### Shelter

- **Bring a sturdy tent** that handles condensation well. A double-wall design with good ventilation will cope best with the fog furring every surface. Carry a small cloth to wipe down the inner walls.
- **Pitch on grus or firm ground**, not in hollows. The fog gathers there first, and so does the frost.
- **Plan not to sleep in the finished houses**, however tempting their corbelled roofs and sleeping platforms look after a week of wet nylon. See Part Four.

## Part Three: Navigation, Time, and the Mind

### Finding Your Way

This is where ordinary expeditions go wrong here. For most of each day you will see ten paces. The land is nearly flat with no distinctive landmarks, and it is so large the eye cannot judge distance. The strangeness makes all of this worse.

- **Use the roads.** The roads are "dead straight, running from district to district." In a land with no other reliable lines, they are your handrails. Learn where they run during noon clarity, and follow them in fog. They are the most permanent human feature here, and they are what the city travels along.
- **Compass:** bring one, but don't trust it blindly. Old gneiss and granite can carry magnetic anomalies, and this place may have others. Cross-check against the sun during the noon window.
- **GPS** will almost certainly not work. Don't count on it.
- **Use the noon window.** You get one or two hours of clear sky each day. Before the trip, practice the routine you'll use then: climb a swell, take bearings on distant tors and wall lines, sketch the layout, and plan the next day's route.
- **Rope up in fog.** Tie companions together with a short cord, or at least stay within arm's reach. A person twenty paces away is a person you have lost.
- **Carry whistles**, and agree on signals in advance. Stone-on-stone tapping carries here, and so will a whistle. You want your companion to know your signal from anything else.

### Marking Your Route

Normally you would build cairns. Here, think carefully.

- **Moss grows fast.** It can "visibly advance across a bare stone over the course of a week," so any marker you leave may be swallowed.
- **Stones may not stay where you put them.** A cairn built from local stone is built from the city's material, and it may be incorporated or unbuilt. Don't use the city's stones for your own purposes.
- **Use your own markers**, such as brightly colored reusable stakes, and collect every one on the way back. Anything you leave here does not rot. A strip of plastic flagging tape dropped today will still be here in ten thousand years.

### Keeping Time

"Travelers who cross it speak of weeks, then stop speaking of time at all." Treat that as a warning.

- **Wear a mechanical, self-winding watch** that shows the date, and bring a backup.
- **Keep a written log** every night, however brief: the date, what you ate, your food remaining, where you camped, and whether the tapping was heard. Number the entries.
- **Carry a knotted cord** and tie a knot every night. It's old-fashioned, but it works when your mind doesn't.
- **Set a turn-around rule before you arrive.** Decide in advance that when half your food is gone, you turn back, regardless of how you feel or what you think you are about to find. Write it on the inside cover of your log.

### Silence and Scale

Expect the psychological weight to be heavy.

- **The silence is total.** There is no birdsong, no insect hum, and no rustle of anything alive. "On still evenings in the fog, a person can hear their own blood." For people raised among animal sounds this can be profoundly unsettling. Some find it peaceful for a day, then oppressive.
- **Go with at least one companion**, ideally someone you trust completely and can talk to. Talk every day. Sing if you want. You will not disturb anything.
- **You will be the only animal for hundreds of kilometers.** That loneliness is a kind of sensory deprivation, and it can produce hallucinations. That is an awkward fact in a place with real anomalies. Agree with your companions that you will each describe strange things aloud, as they happen, and log them. Two witnesses are worth far more than one.
- **The scale can overwhelm you.** Seeing "swell after swell, tor after tor, the far horizon slightly curved" can trigger something like agoraphobic vertigo. Focus on the walls, which are "the only human scale."

## Part Four: Conduct Among the Walls

Nothing here has been observed to harm anyone. But very little here is understood, and you are an ordinary human walking into the middle of a process that runs over centuries. The best posture is that of a guest in a working house whose occupants you cannot see. Your intuition is your best guide. What follows is mine.

### The Tapping

You will hear stone on stone in the fog, rhythmic and unhurried, sometimes from several directions.

- **Don't chase it.** Walking toward it only takes you "a little farther on," always, until you are off the road in a bog in fog with no bearings. People who try to catch the builders get lost. If you want to see where the work happened, wait for the noon clearing and walk to the wall then.
- **Stop and listen instead.** Sit down. Note the direction and the time in your log. Many travelers say this is the most beautiful thing on the Tableland: the sound of patient, competent people working just out of reach. Let it be that.
- **Don't try to trick it** with hidden cameras, sudden movements, or thrown lights. Photographing the walls at dawn and dusk to record the change is harmless and genuinely valuable. Stalking the builders is a different thing. Whatever keeps them "elsewhere" is not something you will defeat with a flashlight, and I don't think you should want to.

### The Tools and the Stones

- **Don't touch the hammerstones**, especially the warm ones. They are the only warm thing in the land, and you will want to. They are in use.
- **Leave everything where it lies:** stones, rope, copper wedges, and dust. The stone lying in the moss at dawn is scheduled for the wall by dusk. Moving it means interfering with work in progress, done by people you cannot negotiate with.
- **Take no souvenirs**, not even from the ruins. The ruins are not abandoned. They are the same city at another moment, and the fallen stones are "the material for the next rising." A stone in your pocket is a stone missing from a wall that has not been built yet.

### The Finished Houses

The finished houses will be the most tempting places on the Tableland. They are roofed, dry-looking, and furnished, with hearths laid, sleeping platforms ready, and small objects waiting in niches.

**Don't move in.** Look through the doorway. Step inside briefly if you must, with respect. But:

- **Don't sleep on the platforms.**
- **Don't light the hearth.**
- **Don't take the copper pin, the wool, or the carved pebble.**

My intuition, which you are welcome to disagree with: these houses are waiting for someone specific. The inhabitants "arrive only in the moment between completion and collapse, a moment nobody has been able to find." It is possible that nobody has found it because nobody has tried to be it. I would not want to be the one who lights the hearth and sits down, and finds out that the moment of habitation is something you can step into from the wrong direction.

There are practical reasons too:

- **Fog pools in the rooms** "filling them to the lintel," sometimes when the air outside is clear.
- **It pours out slowly, upward.** You don't want to be asleep inside that. Even if nothing strange happened, you would wake soaked and chilled.

### The Doorways

You may find the same doorway, with the same crack and the same lichen, both in a finished house and standing alone in the moss days away.

- **Decide before you arrive** whether you will walk through a freestanding doorway. Don't decide on the spot, in fog, tired and awed.
- **The known consequence is small but real.** People who have walked through say "the tapping in the fog comes more clearly and seems nearer." Nobody has reported harm. But "nearer" is a direction, and you don't know where it ends. If you do step through, do it once and deliberately, log it, and watch for changes in yourself afterward.
- **If you won't go through, don't go halfway.** Walk around it.

### The Rosette Pools

The pools in the rosette crowns are perfectly still, even in wind, and they sometimes show other skies: blue beneath fog, night at noon, and once, a hand setting a stone.

- **Don't drink from them.** They are the plants' water. Also, a pool that shows a different sky may not contain your sky's water. That's not a risk to take for convenience when tarns are everywhere.
- **Don't put your hand in.**
- **Looking is fine**, and may be one of the few ways to glimpse the builders. Look briefly, then look away. Log what you saw, and note whether your companion saw the same thing.

### The Stairs and the Shadows

- **Watch for stairs that grow.** Some stairways have steps that "grow imperceptibly taller as they climb until the last is a wall of its own." Treat them as you would ice on a trail. Notice them early, and don't climb them. Several are said to exist. A climb that gets harder with each step and leads to nothing is a good metaphor, and a bad place to twist an ankle four thousand meters up.
- **Don't let a lagging shadow panic you.** Your shadow may arrive "a half-step after" you. Note it, and have a companion confirm it. It appears to be harmless. It is also one of the best indicators of where the strangeness is strongest, so log where it happens.

## Part Five: Leave No Trace, Taken Literally

This part matters morally as much as practically.

**Nothing here decomposes.** There are no worms, insects, or fungi breaking things down, and possibly no bacteria. That means:

- **Everything you leave stays**, essentially forever: food crumbs, a dropped wrapper, a broken tent peg, a bootprint in a cushion plant. Worst of all is your bodily waste.
- **You are a biological contaminant.** You carry billions of microbes, including decomposers. This whole ecosystem, with its peat many meters deep and its bark drifts and its leaves ten thousand years old, depends on nothing rotting. You could introduce rot to a world that has never known it. I cannot overstate how much damage that could do.

So:

- **Pack out all solid human waste.** Use waste bags with a gelling agent, and double-bag them. Ideally pack out urine too, or at least urinate onto bare grus or rock far from moss, tarns, and plants. Never into water.
- **Clean your gear before you arrive:** boots, poles, tent, packs. Remove all soil and seeds. Wash and dry everything.
- **Bring no fresh food that might shed spores or seeds.** Dry and sealed is best.
- **Handle food carefully in camp**, over a groundsheet, and gather every crumb.
- **Do not bury anything, ever.** Burial here is preservation, not disposal.

Be honest with yourself. Even the most careful visit leaves something. That is a reason to keep your group small and your visit purposeful. It is also a reason to be tender with the place.
