# Preparing to Visit the Floor of the Unbuilt Works

## The Short Version

If you remember nothing else:

1. **It is a wet cold that kills slowly.** Dress for a flooded cellar in winter that you cannot leave for many hours.
2. **You will be standing in water that is being electrified.** Insulate your feet, and learn to move between discharges.
3. **Keep time.** The things that fall out of rhythm here are removed.
4. **When the whistle blows, stop. When everything turns, turn.**
5. **Take nothing out, especially the bolts.**
6. **Decide before you arrive exactly when and how you will leave.** Nothing here will tell you it is time to go.

The rest of this guide explains these rules and adds detail.

---

## I. Understanding What You're Walking Into

Most dangerous places are dangerous because something wants to hurt you. This one is dangerous because nothing here cares about you at all, and because the whole place works like a system that sorts things into two kinds: things that keep time and things that don't.

There is no malice. The bellows-backs will not trample you on purpose. The arc-eels are short-tempered, but they are not hunting. The tallymen are not predators in any ordinary sense. The basin simply has a function, and it handles everything in it according to that function. You are neither a worker nor an owner. You are more like a visitor wandering onto a factory floor without a badge, and the floor is very old, very large, and not entirely there.

Your preparation has three parts:
- **Physical survival:** cold, wet, electricity, soot, crowds, predators.
- **Behavioral camouflage:** fitting into the rhythm well enough to be ignored.
- **Psychological integrity:** fitting in *without* being absorbed.

The third is the hardest. The account ends with a traveler turning along with everything else "because by now it would feel strange not to." Read that line as a warning.

## II. Before You Go

### Physical Conditioning

You will be wading, often shin-deep, through cold slurry that fills every footprint from below. You will be pushing through herds of animals the size of overturned boats. You may walk for many hours, because the basin repeats and you cannot know how many bays you must cross before the rim appears.

Train for:
- **Long-duration wading.** Walk in mud or shallow water in heavy boots for hours. The suction of slurry is exhausting in a way dry hiking is not.
- **Cold tolerance.** Gradual cold exposure helps, but this is not about toughness. Hypothermia from damp cold creeps up on strong people who think they are fine.
- **Load carrying.** You must bring all your own water and food.
- **Breath control.** During the whistle, the bellows-backs hold their breath. You may want to be very quiet too. Practice slow, silent breathing and a comfortable breath-hold of 30 to 60 seconds.

### Medical Preparation

- **Tetanus booster, without fail.** You will walk on crunching snail shells and rusting iron, and you will be cut.
- **Cardiac screening.** You will almost certainly receive some electrical shock. If you have a pacemaker, an arrhythmia, or any heart condition, do not go. Be careful with the word "arrhythmia" in this place in general.
- **Photosensitivity screening.** The arc-eels produce constant stuttering, flickering violet-white light. If you have any history of photosensitive seizures or migraines, reconsider.
- **Respiratory baseline.** The air carries iron vapor, soot, and probably ozone and nitrogen oxides from the arcs. People with asthma or COPD should not attempt this.
- **Hearing baseline test,** so you can check for damage afterward.

### Mental Training

This is unusual, but I'd argue it is the most important part.

- **Rhythm training.** Practice walking to a metronome at different tempos, including irregular ones. Practice falling into step with a beat you hear, then holding it after it stops. You need to be able to match a local striker tempo within a few strokes of entering a new district.
- **Freeze drills.** Have someone sound a long, falling tone at random intervals while you walk, work, eat, or talk. On the tone, stop completely, mid-step if needed, and hold until a second signal. Then make a quarter-turn and resume. Do this until it is a reflex. You do not want to be the one thing still moving when the basin stops.
- **Counter-rhythm training.** This is the protection against absorption. Learn to keep a *private* internal rhythm that differs from your outward one. Examples: a song in a different meter from your walking pace, or a slow count of your own breaths that you never let lock to the bellows-backs. Outwardly you keep time with the works. Inwardly you keep your own. Practice until you can hold both at once.
- **Disorientation tolerance.** You will hear things before they happen, including your own footsteps. You will cross the same stretch of water repeatedly. If loops and precognition upset you, rehearse the experience mentally beforehand. Know that it is coming, and that the correct response is to not fight it.

## III. Gear

### Feet and Legs: The Most Important Section

Your feet will be wet and cold for the entire visit. They will also stand in water carrying electrical discharge.

- **Dielectric-rated, insulated rubber boots,** knee-high at minimum. Chest waders are better if you can manage the bulk, since the slurry deepens without warning. Choose a stiff, puncture-resistant sole for snail-shell carpets and hidden iron. Check the boots for pinholes before every trip. One pinhole in electrified water is a very bad day.
- **Liner socks plus thick wool socks,** with at least three spare sets in waterproof bags. Change them at every chance, ideally standing on a dry schist tread. Trench foot is a real risk on longer visits.
- **Gaiters** sealed over the boot tops to keep soot and slurry out.
- **Foot powder or anti-maceration balm.**

### Body

- **Wool or synthetic base layers.** No cotton anywhere. Cotton in this humidity turns into a cold compress.
- **An insulating mid-layer** that keeps working when damp, such as synthetic fill or fleece. Down will soak and fail in the vapor.
- **A waterproof, soot-shedding outer shell** with a hood, in dark gray. It will turn dark gray anyway.
- **Non-conductive clothing only.** Avoid metallic threads, metal zippers where possible, and heating elements with exposed wiring. Carry no chains, jewelry, or loose metal against the skin.

### Head, Face, Lungs, Eyes, Ears

- **Full-face or half-face respirator** with combination cartridges: P100 particulate for the soot plus acid-gas/organic vapor for whatever the arcs produce. Carry spare cartridges. The soot falls constantly and will load filters faster than you expect.
- **Sealed goggles with light shade tinting,** around shade 3. Arc-eel discharges are, in effect, welding arcs, and long exposure without protection can cause arc-eye, a painful UV burn to the cornea. The tint also softens the flicker. Bring anti-fog treatment, because the cold vapor will fog everything.
- **Hearing protection that attenuates evenly** across frequencies, such as high-fidelity "musician's" earplugs. Do **not** use heavy foam plugs that block everything. You need to hear the whistle, and the early arrival of sounds is your best warning system (see Section V). You want to lower the volume without losing information.
- **A warm hat and a neck gaiter** under the hood.

### Hands

- **Insulated, waterproof gloves** with thin liners you can work in.
- Consider **rubber electrical gloves** as an outer layer when crossing flats or bracing against wet stone.

### Tools

- **A long non-conductive staff:** fiberglass or treated wood, never metal. Use it to probe slurry depth, test footing, steady yourself on shell carpets, and gently fend off a bellows-back that is leaning your way. If you stumble near an arc, keep the staff from bridging you to the water.
- **A mechanical hand tally counter,** the thumb-click kind. Use it to count bays: each time you recognize a repeated water sheet, click. The count is your only real measure of progress, and it seems fitting to carry a counter here.
- **A small notebook and grease pencil** to note each recognized landmark (skeleton shape, drip pattern) and the striker tempo in each district.
- **Chemical light sticks** as backup light. Electronics are unreliable here (see below).

### Navigation

Assume that nothing you normally use will work:
- **GPS:** meaningless.
- **Compass:** hopeless. There is iron in every layer of sediment and constant electrical discharge.
- **Sun:** absent. The light has no direction.
- **Distance:** in the account's words, "counted, not measured."

Your navigation is the tally counter, the notebook, and patience. The rim ridges, the knee-high concentric remnants of ancient mountain roots, are your only fixed feature. When they appear, you have reached the edge.

### Electronics

Assume every electronic device will fail, glitch, or be damaged by the constant electrical activity. Bring a mechanical watch with antimagnetic protection, though timekeeping here may be unreliable in other ways. Do not stake your life on anything with a battery.

### Food and Water

- **Carry all your drinking water.** Do not drink basin water under any circumstances. It is iron-saturated, often rust-red, skinned with mineral film, and carries soot and whatever the eels fix into it.
- **Carry dense, easy food** you can eat quickly without taking off much equipment. Eat while moving, in rhythm. Never eat during a whistle.
- **Hot drinks in insulated flasks** are enormously valuable for morale and core temperature.

### Medical Kit

- Hypothermia supplies: emergency blanket, chemical heat packs (non-electric), high-calorie food.
- Burn dressings, for electrical burns especially.
- Eye wash and eye patches for arc-eye.
- Wound cleaning supplies. Clean every cut immediately, since everything here is rusty and filmy.
- Blister and foot care.

### What NOT to Bring

- Anything you would mind losing.
- Bright lights or strobes. It is not clear how the eels or the tallymen would react, and nothing about the place suggests you should find out.
- Your own rhythmic noisemakers, such as music players or tapping habits. You do not want to introduce a competing beat.
- Iron or steel objects you are attached to. Go light on metal generally.

## IV. The First Hour

When you arrive, do not move for a while if you can avoid it. Find a schist tread if possible, but not one a striker is working, and stand still. The mineral film grows on things that stand still too long, so "a while" means minutes, not hours. Use the time to:

1. **Let your senses adjust.** The dim, directionless pewter light, the violet stutter of the eels, and the soft shadows will make depth hard to judge.
2. **Feel the thrum.** Let the bellows-back breathing settle into your chest. Notice how the vapor layers rise and fall with it.
3. **Find the local striker tempo.** Listen to the nearest district. Is it locked into a single beat or broken into clatter? Start tapping it with a finger. You will be walking to it soon.
4. **Notice the sound lag.** Watch a nearby striker. You will hear the ring before you see the hammer fall. Get used to it now, while nothing depends on it.
5. **Look for chimney-shadows.** Note where the long straight shadows lie, and plan to stay out of them.
6. **Look for tallymen.** Small, dark, quick, mostly eye. Note where they are and let them see you behaving.
7. **Start your private counter-rhythm.** Begin it now and keep it going the whole visit.

Then begin to move, in time.

## V. Moving Through the Basin

### Pushing Through the Crowds

The basin is packed, and you cannot go around the animals. You have to go through them. Think of a crowded station at shift change: everyone moving with purpose, no one hostile, but no one making room.

- **Bellows-backs** are huge, low, and slow, and they breathe in sync. Move through gaps as they exhale and contract, not as they inhale and swell. Don't get pinned between two of them when the herd breathes in together, because the squeeze could crush a rib. Their warm exhalations are the only warmth in the basin, and it is tempting to linger in them. Warm your hands briefly and move on. The midge swarms are worst there, and you do not want to be where a bellows-back is feeding.
- **Strikers** are knee-high and armored, with a heavy forelimb swinging in a regular stroke. They are not aggressive, but their strike could break your foot. Give them room, and never put a hand or foot on the schist they are working. You are standing on generations of their labor.
- **Carrion flocks** work in shifts over the dead. Avoid them. Do not stumble, fall, or lie down near them.

### Crossing Snail Carpets

The smelt-snails carpet the wettest ground so densely that you walk on their shells. They crunch and hold your weight. Walk steadily without stamping. The runoff is rust-red and the ground is slick. Your staff helps. Accept that you will crush some, since the basin has plenty of snails. Stamping or stopping to pick shells off your boots will make you a noticeable irregularity.

### Crossing the Flats: The Arc-Eels

This is where people get hurt.

The flats are broad, finger-deep water sheets. Arc-eels move through them in schools, throwing a web of blue-white lightning a hand's breadth high across the surface. You are standing in the conducting medium.
