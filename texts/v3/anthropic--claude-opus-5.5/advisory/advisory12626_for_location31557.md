# Preparing to Visit the Undercroft of the Fallen Bank

## First, Understand What You're Agreeing To

This is not a dive site. It is a sealed, room-sized pocket of near-freezing water, completely dark and packed wall to wall with living soft material. It holds broken stone sharp enough to open a drysuit, and its time is stacked in layers. There is no entrance. There is no open water to swim through. The place keeps anything that enters it, and it has already kept an eel and possibly a crayfish in ways those animals did not choose.

You will be the largest object that has entered it since the bank fell. Everything you do will be remembered exactly, possibly for centuries. Moss will grow around your mistakes the way it grows around the cairn.

If that sounds like a reason not to go, that's a reasonable conclusion. If you still want to go, go as a guest in someone's held breath. Go small, slow, cold, and brief.

## Who Should Go

Not most people. The ideal visitor is:

- **An experienced overhead-environment diver.** Cave or wreck penetration experience is close to essential, especially in zero visibility and entanglement conditions. Open-water experience, however extensive, is not relevant preparation.
- **Deeply comfortable with confinement and sensory deprivation.** You will spend your entire visit with soft things touching your face, no space around you, no light, and a river pressing overhead like a hand on a door. If you have ever felt panic in a tight space, even once, do not go. Panic here means thrashing, and thrashing means entanglement, torn equipment, and silt clouds that will hang for days.
- **Patient in a way that feels unnatural.** The correct speed of movement is roughly the speed of the eel.
- **Willing to come back with nothing.** No samples, no souvenirs, and possibly no photographs.

Go alone, with a surface or other-side team monitoring you. A second diver doubles the displacement, the silt, the risk of snagging each other, and the amount of *you* the water must absorb. The pocket is too small for a buddy team. Your redundancy has to be in your equipment and your extraction plan instead.

## Getting In and Getting Out

This is the most important section, and it is the one people get wrong.

### Do not dig.

There is no way in from the river without breaking the seal, and breaking the seal ends the place. River water floods in and the layers mix. The kept dusk escapes into the ordinary current and is gone in seconds. The braid browns. Whatever you came to see, you will have destroyed it by arriving. Any approach through the bank, even a small borehole, is out of the question.

### Your transit must exchange volume, both ways.

The pocket is sealed and water doesn't compress. When you arrive, your body and gear, perhaps 100 to 150 liters, have to go somewhere. If your transit simply inserts you, it pressurizes the pocket. It may crack the fold of the bank from inside, or force water out through the soil in ways that tear the root ceiling. When you leave, the space you occupied becomes a void and the pocket implodes around it.

Use a transit method that **swaps an equal volume**. Your arrival should take an equivalent body of water with it, held somewhere safe on the other side. Then:

- **Return that exact water when you leave.** Not fresh water, not "equivalent" water. The water here *is* the place; it contains specific nights. The water you displace is a portion of the undercroft's memory, and you are borrowing it. Store it undisturbed, at temperature, in the dark, in a container that preserves stratification if you can manage it. Put it back where you took it from.
- **Choose where you displace from.** Ideally, arrive in the upper-middle water, just below the dusk layer. Do not arrive in the dusk layer itself, because it's the most fragile and the most precious. Never arrive near the floor, where the black band is.
- **Arrive in a compact posture.** Knees up, arms in, gear stowed. You will be materializing inside a dense tangle of grass sheaves, lace-weed, and sponges. Whatever occupies your arrival volume is displaced with the water, which means you may be taking some of the meadow with you. Accept this as a cost, keep your arrival volume as small as possible, and return that material with the water.

### Your extraction must not depend on you.

Assume you may become disoriented, cold-impaired, or subtly kept. Your team on the other side should pull you out on a fixed schedule whether or not you signal. Set the hard limit before you go, and do not give yourself the ability to extend it from inside.

## Equipment

### Breathing: closed-circuit rebreather, absolutely.

**No bubbles. Not one.** Open-circuit exhaust would rise and gather against the root ceiling, trapping itself in the fleece of the drowned meadow. It would form a pocket of air in the dusk layer, the first air this place has held since the meadow went under. The air would press on the roots, dry the braid, and put a surface where there has only ever been depth. It would not leave when you did.

A closed-circuit rebreather produces no exhaust in normal operation. Configure it carefully:

- **Vent nothing.** Plan your depth so you never need to dump loop gas. The pocket is shallow, perhaps a couple of meters of total vertical range, and its whole depth is only a few arms' lengths beneath the riverbed. You shouldn't need to adjust much, but rehearse holding the loop steady.
- **Bring bailout.** If the rebreather fails, bubbles are unavoidable, and your life matters more than the ceiling. Carry an open-circuit bailout and accept that using it will scar the place. Then leave immediately.
- **Don't trust the water to be ordinary.** Sealed, dark, plant-packed water ought to be low in oxygen and possibly high in dissolved gases from the lower layers. Nothing about it may behave as chemistry predicts. Don't let it into your mouth, and don't let a leak go unaddressed.

### Thermal protection: plan for four degrees and total stillness.

The water sits just above the temperature at which roots stop growing, a few degrees above freezing. That is cold enough on its own. Worse, you'll be moving almost not at all, so you'll generate very little heat.

- Drysuit, heavy undergarments, and an actively heated vest or undersuit with a reliable battery.
- Dry gloves with a thin heated liner if possible. Your hands are your eyes here, and cold hands stop seeing.
- A thick hood and full-face mask. Cold water on the face triggers a gasp reflex and a heart-rate response. You'll be breathing from a loop, so a gasp won't drown you, but it will jolt you, and a jolt in this place is a disaster in slow motion.
- Expect your dive to be limited by cold more than anything else. Plan for well under an hour of actual presence unless your heating is excellent.

### Abrasion and cut protection: the floor has teeth.

The slate edges are freshly broken and unrounded, like the spines of books. A drysuit puncture in four-degree water is an emergency.

- Wear a cut-resistant outer shell (aramid or heavy cordura) over the drysuit, especially on knees, shins, forearms, and seat.
- Protect your rebreather counterlungs and hoses from contact below you.
- Plan to stay off the floor almost entirely.

### Entanglement: you are swimming through fabric.

Grass sheaves, root curtains, lace-weed in sheets, watercress towers. Every gesture parts one curtain and meets another. Treat the whole volume as a single entanglement hazard.

- Streamline ruthlessly. Nothing dangles. Clip and tuck every hose. No loose straps, no gauges on long hoses, no reels hanging from your harness.
- Carry two line cutters in easily reached, separate positions. You will want to never use them, since each cut is a wound in something that will remember it. But if you're caught, cut. You're not allowed to die here. A body is the worst thing you could leave behind.
- Use short, finless propulsion, or small flat fins and minimal kicks. Pulling yourself hand over hand along stable surfaces is better. Stable surfaces are rare; the moss domes are soft and won't bear weight, and the root mat will tear. Your best handholds are the fitted threshold stones, but those are one of the precious things. The honest answer is that you'll barely move.

### Light: bring it, and probably don't use it.

It is completely dark, and it is meant to be. The meadow is growing downward, blindly, and it has organized itself around the absence of light. A beam would be the first light in years. It might turn every pale grass blade toward it, and nothing would ever turn them back.

- Carry one small, dim, red light for emergencies only, shielded and aimed at your own instruments.
- Carry a backup, stowed, not clipped where it could switch on by accident.
- Don't bring a video light. Don't bring a strobe. If you want to *see* this place, the only way is to be there when it lights itself (see below). Otherwise, you'll know it by touch.

Consider bringing a thin tactile marker: a small, smooth, body-temperature object you hold in one hand throughout as a reference. In total darkness, with soft things pressing from every side, it helps to know where *you* end.

### Instruments

- A timer on your wrist, plus a mechanical watch. Keep the mechanical watch because it is honest about what time does to it. Glance at it with your dim light when you change layers. If it slows, stops, or runs backward, you've learned something, and you should move up.
- Depth gauge and temperature readout. The temperature steps between layers will tell you which night you're in better than anything else.
- **No sonar, no acoustic positioning.** Sound doesn't behave causally here. An acoustic ping might return before you send it. Nobody knows what a ping sent into a time that hasn't happened yet would do.

### Biosecurity

Bring nothing alive. River systems are devastated by hitchhikers. Crayfish plague spreads on wet gear, and there is a crayfish here, possibly in seven places at once. Clean, disinfect, and fully dry every item of equipment. Put on fresh suit layers that have never been in another river. Consider carefully whether even your skin microbiome is appropriate here. Minimize exposed skin.

## Finding Your Way

### Distance lies.

The pocket is slightly larger inside than the bank that holds it, and it keeps getting larger. The far wall is always a little farther than it should be. If you estimate distance by arm-reach and body-length, you will be wrong, consistently, in the same direction.

- Run a single fine line from your arrival point. Tie it off to *yourself*, not to anything in the pocket, and to a small anchor weight you set gently at your arrival point and retrieve when you leave. Reel it in as you return.
- Don't be alarmed if the line seems to pay out more than the distance you've covered. That's the debt accruing. Trust the line, not your sense of how far you've gone.
- Don't try to cross the whole pocket. The far wall doesn't want to be reached. The root-strands can't reach the floor in a whole season. You don't need to succeed where they fail.

### Navigate by temperature and texture.

Moving vertically moves you between nights.

- **The dusk layer** is at the top, against the root ceiling. It will feel faintly warmer, a difference you may notice on the exposed skin around your mask before your gauge shows it. The grass here is broad and soft. You may smell cut grass inside your mask. This is impossible, and you will smell it anyway. Don't let it surprise you into a sharp breath.
- **The winter-night layers** lie beneath, colder and heavier. The weed there is narrow, stiff, nearly transparent to the touch, almost like cellophane. These layers are pressed thin like pages, and there are a handful of winters' worth. Passing through them may feel like leafing through a book too fast. You may experience small, unaccountable shifts: a sudden sense that it's very late, that you've missed something, that it's snowing somewhere.
- **The black band** is near the floor, thin and very dark, darker than the darkness around it. Weed grows backward there. **Do not enter it.** No one knows what it does to something as large and complicated as a person. Perhaps it would heal a scratch. Perhaps it would unmake a decision, or un-happen your arrival while leaving you in place. You'll recognize it by the weed: strands that feel like they're retreating from your fingers, shortening under your touch. If your mechanical watch starts running backward, you're too close. Rise slowly.
