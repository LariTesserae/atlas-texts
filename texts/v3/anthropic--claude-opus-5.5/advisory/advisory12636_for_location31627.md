# Preparing for the Casting Grounds of the Upper Reach

## A Traveler's Briefing

The first thing to understand is that the Upper Reach will not try to kill you. It has no weather to speak of, no predators worth the name, no cold that will freeze you in the night. People who prepare for it as though it were a hostile mountain tend to bring the wrong things. They pack ice axes and heavy down, and they forget eye protection good enough, gloves tough enough, and some reliable way of knowing who they are.

The Reach is dangerous in quieter ways. Your eyes burn slowly. The edges are sharp. The same peak turns up three times. The place is silent, you are small, and it has a steady, patient habit of repairing anything that stays still too long. Prepare for those things.

---

## Part One: Before You Leave

### Physical conditioning

This is high country, above the cloud deck, and the air is thin. The Reach itself is gentle on the lungs in most other respects, but you will be climbing constantly. The density means you are rarely walking flat. You go up gantry stairs, over pipe-bundles, along catwalks, and down again. A "day's walk across the valley" is really a day of scrambling through a three-dimensional lattice.

- **Train for climbing and scrambling, not distance.** You need grip strength, shoulder endurance, and comfort with exposure. Ladder work, bouldering, and stair intervals with a loaded pack are all useful.
- **Train your balance.** Catwalks and flashing ridges are narrow, and the flat, shadowless light makes depth hard to judge.
- **Acclimatize to altitude first**, somewhere ordinary. Spend at least a week at high elevation before you go, and ideally two. Altitude sickness is miserable anywhere. Here you would suffer it in a place where your sense of reality is already under strain, and headache and confusion will blend into the Reach's own disorientations until you can't tell which is which.
- Talk to a physician about **acetazolamide** or a similar altitude medication, and carry it.

### Mental preparation

This matters more than the physical side. Read the descriptions of the Reach several times, especially the parts about where it breaks. You need to know in advance that:

- Peaks will repeat, or move.
- Shadows will lag, including your own.
- Towns on the horizon cannot be reached.
- Water will occasionally run uphill.
- The sky may contain an inverted copy of the ground.

None of these things will hurt you directly. What hurts people is being surprised by them, because a surprised mind makes bad decisions: it chases the town, walks toward the peak it recognizes, or panics and moves fast across sharp ground. If you have already accepted these events as the normal weather of the Reach, you can note them calmly and carry on.

**Decide your rules before you arrive**, while your judgment is still entirely your own. Write them down. You will not make as good decisions once you are there.

## Part Two: The Light

The light is the hazard you will feel first and underestimate most.

It is white, even, and total. It comes from everywhere, and it reflects off fresh, bright, pale surfaces in every direction. You are effectively standing inside a snowfield that extends upward into scaffolding. Photokeratitis, or snow blindness, is a real and serious risk. It is painful and temporarily disabling, and in a place where you already cannot trust your eyes for navigation, losing them entirely would be very bad.

### Eye protection

- **Glacier glasses with full side shields**, category 4 lenses, with a wraparound fit so no light gets in from below or the sides. The light comes up from the ground as much as down from the sky.
- **Bring a second pair.** The edges here are sharp and you will fall at some point. If your only pair breaks, you are in trouble.
- **Goggles** as a third option. They also keep out the fine white dust.
- **Lubricating eye drops** and **sterile saline** for rinsing out dust.
- **Anesthetic eye drops are for emergencies only**, if you develop snow blindness and need to move to shelter. Don't use them routinely. They mask damage.

Note the line in the description that "after an hour your eyes forget how to stop." Don't let squinting become your normal state. If you are squinting through your glasses, the glasses are not dark enough.

### Skin

- High-SPF sunscreen, reapplied often, including under the chin, inside the nostrils, and behind the ears, where reflected light reaches.
- SPF lip balm.
- A wide-brimmed hat will help less than you expect, since the light comes from all sides. A **neck gaiter or buff** that can be pulled up over the lower face is more useful.
- Lightweight long sleeves and long trousers. You should not have bare skin here, for reasons that go beyond the light.

### Sleep and time

The description says "if it is ever dusk." Assume it may never really get dark. The light may be continuous, or dusk may be rare and unpredictable.

- Bring a **proper blackout sleep mask**, and a spare.
- Bring a **mechanical watch** with a date function, plus a battery watch as backup. Don't rely on a phone.
- **Keep a written log of time**: when you woke, when you slept, when you ate. Your body will lose its rhythm, and the Reach's slow cycles offer nothing to set it by. You need an external record.

## Part Three: Air and Dust

Fresh castings shed a fine white powder, and the broad dry faces are coated in it. You will breathe it, walk through it, and get it in your food.

Nobody knows what it is made of. On Earth, fine mineral dust from fresh fractured stone, such as silica, is genuinely dangerous to the lungs over long exposure. Assume the worst.

- Bring **N95 or P100 respirator masks**, several of them. Wear one whenever you are crossing dusty faces or anywhere near a new casting. They are uncomfortable at altitude, so practice wearing one while climbing before you go.
- Goggles, as above.
- Rinse your face and hands before eating.

### The mists

The description mentions thin mists in the shaded clefts behind the mold-halves that "hang there" and that "the air never quite moves." My instinct is to treat these as places where heavier-than-air gases might pool, such as outgassing from curing castings or something venting from the works. Still air in a low, enclosed, industrial space is a classic hazard on Earth.

**Do not linger in the mists.** Do not camp in them, and do not rest in the clefts behind mold-halves. If you have to pass through, do it quickly. If you feel lightheaded, get to higher, open ground. A small **personal gas monitor** (O₂, CO, CO₂) is worth its weight in your pack.

## Part Four: Clothing and Protection

The Reach is "cool, never cold," so you don't need heavy insulation. What you need is **armor against edges**.

The arêtes are knife-sharp. The flashing, the fins of material squeezed out along mold seams, stands proud of the rock and has never been trimmed. It is translucent, catches the light like frost, and runs along whole ridgelines. It is almost certainly as sharp as broken glass, and since it is pale against pale, it will be hard to see.

### Hands

- **Cut-resistant gloves** (ANSI A4 or higher), with a grippy palm. This is the single most important piece of clothing you bring. You will be putting your hands on cast surfaces, iron gantries, and ladder rungs thousands of times.
- Several spare pairs.
- Thin liner gloves for fine work.
- **Never grab flashing to steady yourself.** Train yourself to recognize the seam-lines and keep your hands off them.

### Body

- Durable, abrasion-resistant trousers. Canvas or reinforced synthetic work trousers are better than lightweight hiking trousers.
- **Knee pads** and possibly elbow pads. You will kneel and crawl through the dense lattice between castings.
- Long-sleeved durable shirts in layers, with a light fleece for shade and a windproof shell.
- **A climbing helmet.** The density means there is always something above your head: gantries, pipe, scaffold edges, the underside of a catwalk. You will hit your head, repeatedly, especially with dark glasses on and flat light that hides depth.

### Color

**Wear bright, saturated, unnatural colors**: orange, red, electric blue. There are three reasons.

1. Your companions will be able to see you against the white-and-iron landscape.
2. You will be able to see yourself. That sounds strange, but in a place of endless repetition and pale sameness, looking down at your own vivid sleeve helps you stay oriented.
3. You should not blend in here. The things that blend in, the ibex that lie still on warm flashing, become part of the rock. Being conspicuously *not cast* seems wise.

### Feet

- Stiff-soled mountaineering or approach boots with **protective, cut-resistant uppers** and good grip on smooth hard surfaces. The cast rock is fine-grained and pebbled, and iron catwalks are slick when damp with morning condensation.
- **Gaiters**, to keep out dust and to guard your ankles against flashing.
- Several pairs of socks. Your feet will get wet from the condensation, which runs everywhere by mid-morning.

## Part Five: The Arrival — Coming Up Through the Cloud

"The cloud is warm. Everything above it is not."

When you climb up out of the warm cloud, you will be warm and damp, and you will step into cool air among cool surfaces. The Reach sweats every morning because warm, moist air meets cool cast rock. You are about to do the same.

- **Your gear will fog and condense.** Glasses, goggles, camera lenses, electronics. Carry electronics in sealed dry bags and let them come to temperature before opening them.
- **Your clothing will be damp** from the cloud. Change into dry base layers once you are above it. Pack those layers in a dry bag at the top of your pack.
- Expect a brief shock of chill, then the glare. Have your glacier glasses on *before* you break through the top of the cloud. The transition from grey to total white will be abrupt, and your pupils will be wide open from the dim cloud.

**Mark your arrival point.** This is critical and is covered below under navigation. The place where you came up through the cloud is the one thing you know connects to the world beneath.

## Part Six: Navigation

This is where most people will get into serious trouble.

The Reach defeats every normal method of finding your way:

- **Landmarks repeat.** Peaks recur with the same sprue and the same notch in their flashing. The one you think you are walking away from may appear ahead of you.
- **Texture repeats** every forty paces.
- **Lichen repeats**, stamped in identical sigils.
- **Even the crows repeat.**
- **The towns are false destinations.**
- **The sky may contain a copy of the ground**, which will scramble any instinct about up and down in the peripheral vision.
- **Things move**, slowly. Presses shift over a week. New mountains are released. Damage is repaired. Your markers may be cast over.
- **Magnetic compasses are probably unreliable.** There is iron everywhere, in bolted mold-halves, gantries, pylons, and pressure vessels, and there are current-carrying cables. Bring one anyway, but trust it only in open areas away from large metal structures, and cross-check it.
- **GPS and electronic navigation** depend on whether the multiverse traveler's tools work here at all. Assume they don't.

### What to do instead

**1. Anchor to your arrival point.** The edge of the plateau where the cloud meets it is your one true reference. Stay in contact with that edge, at least in sight of it, for your first days, until you understand how the Reach behaves.

**2. Bring your own non-repeating markers.** Nothing in the Reach is unique, so bring things that are. Good options:
- Brightly colored flagging tape, each strip with a **handwritten number and date** on it, so that no two are alike.
- Hand-painted or engraved tokens, each with a distinctive design you made yourself.

When you see a marker, check its number. If you find marker #14 ahead of you when you left it behind, you have learned something about the Reach. If you find a marker you don't remember writing, **stop and think very carefully.**

**3. Consider a physical line.** For short excursions from camp, run out a long, thin, brightly colored cord behind you, like a cave diver's guideline. The density of the terrain will actually help here, since there are endless things to tie off to. A line that physically connects you to your camp cannot be fooled by repeating peaks.

**4. Count paces, and use the repetition.** The texture repeats every forty paces, so the Reach hands you a built-in odometer. Count the cycles of the pebbling pattern and log them. It gives you a rough, honest measure of distance traveled that doesn't depend on recognizing anything.

**5. Keep a detailed route log.** Write down directions, pace-counts, marker numbers, and what you saw. Draw sketch maps. Record the lichen patterns you pass and whether they had three branches or four. The act of writing forces you to notice, and the log lets you check later whether the landscape agrees with your memory.

**6. Do not navigate toward the towns.** Ever. Treat them as part of the sky.

**7. Do not navigate toward the inverted range.** Don't try to reach high saddles for a better view of it. Notice it, note it in your log, and look away.

**8. Set a hard turnaround rule.** Before arrival, decide the maximum number of days you'll walk away from the edge, and turn back when you reach it no matter what. Distance here may not work the way you expect. Walking back along your own line and markers is the only reliable way out.

## Part Seven: Water

Water is available but not in the usual form. There are no streams or lakes. Instead, the whole Reach sweats each morning, the beads gather into gutters, and the gutters feed pipes that descend into the works.

### Collecting

- Mornings are your water-gathering time. Condensation stands on iron and in the pebbled pits of the slopes before it runs into the gutters by mid-morning.
- Bring **absorbent cloths or sponges** to wipe dew from surfaces and wring into a container.
- Bring **a lightweight tarp or plastic sheet**. Spread it at night over a cool surface, or angled in a hollow, to collect condensation of your own.
- The **gutters** hold standing water all day in the seams and hollows, "dark and very still." A collapsible bottle, or a length of flexible tubing to siphon from narrow channels, will help.

### Treating

The water has run over fresh cast surfaces coated in white dust of unknown composition. It may be heavy with minerals or worse.

- Use a **good mechanical filter** (0.1 micron or finer) for biological contaminants.
- Also consider an **activated carbon stage** or a filter rated for chemical and heavy-metal reduction.
- Let dusty water **settle** before filtering, since the fine powder will clog your filter fast. Decant the clear water off the top.
- **Purification tablets** as backup.
- Carry **enough water to get back to the edge** at all times, in case you find the local water undrinkable or it makes you ill.

### What to avoid

- **The deep collecting-tanks where the ferns grow.** They are green and alive and attractive, but I would not drink from them. Still, deep, enclosed tanks in an industrial space may hold whatever runs down from the whole system. They are also possibly an entry into the works themselves.
- **Water that is running uphill.** It will "think better of it" soon enough. Wait until it is running the ordinary way again. It is probably harmless, but there is no reason to test that.
- **Main collecting pipes and pipe-bundles.** Don't tap into them. Water in the pipes belongs to the works.

## Part Eight: Food

The description says the ibex "can feed you." I would treat this as a last resort, not a plan.

The ibex are cast. So are the lizards. They breathe, graze, and drink, but they have mold-seams down their spines, and some shed flashing. Nobody knows what they are made of or whether they were meant to be here. Eating a cast animal means taking the substance of the Reach into your body, and one of the things the Reach does is absorb still, pale, seamed creatures into its surfaces. I would not want my body to be any more like the ibex than it has to be.

The hares in the pipe-bundles and the swifts may or may not be cast. The crows are clearly something odd. Leave them alone.

The lichen and moss are unknown and probably inedible. The ferns may be real, but you don't know which ferns they are, and many ferns are toxic.

**Bring all the food you will need, plus a generous reserve.** You won't have to haul water, so food can take a larger share of your pack weight. Good choices:

- Calorie-dense, lightweight food: nuts, nut butters, hard cheese, cured meats, dried fruit, chocolate, freeze-dried meals, energy bars.
- Food with strong flavors and varied textures. A monotonous, repeating environment will make monotonous food intolerable, and you'll want distinct tastes as small reminders of where you come from.
- A small stove and fuel for hot meals. Hot food matters for morale. Cook in open, moving air, never in the still-air clefts.

If you are ever truly forced to hunt an ibex, then as a precaution eat only a small amount first, wait a day, and watch yourself carefully, including your skin, particularly along your spine.

**Pack out everything**, including food waste. See the section on the repair cycle for why leaving things behind is unwise.
