# Preparing for the Delta That Builds Itself

## A Traveler's Briefing

The delta is not hostile. Nothing there wants to hurt you. It is simply indifferent on a scale that is hard to grasp until you're standing in it: a continent-sized machine that makes and unmakes its own ground, running at full volume in heat close to your own body's. Millions of people live full lives there, and you can visit safely. But nearly every instinct you've built in a world of fixed floors, quiet nights, green things, and evaporating sweat will be slightly wrong.

The three things most likely to hurt you are **the heat, the noise, and the ground**, roughly in that order. The heat is the one travelers underestimate. The noise is the one they fixate on. The ground is the one that kills people who ignore the locals.

---

## Part One: The Heat Is the Real Danger

### Understand the problem

The description says the air sits "around body temperature" and is heavy with humidity. Take that seriously. Your body cools itself almost entirely by sweating, and sweat only cools you when it evaporates. In saturated air at 35–37°C, it barely evaporates. It just runs off you. Your body cannot shed heat into air as warm as itself, so the heat you generate by walking, climbing, and carrying accumulates.

In physiological terms, the delta sits near the edge of what an unacclimatized human can tolerate for long periods. The locals have presumably adapted over generations. You haven't.

The failure modes are heat exhaustion (headache, nausea, weakness, cold clammy skin, fainting) and then heat stroke (confusion, stumbling, hot skin, sometimes no sweating, seizures), which is a medical emergency that can kill within hours. In the delta, early heat illness will be harder to notice because the noise already makes you disoriented, nauseous, and headachy. You may not be able to tell the two apart. Plan as if you won't.

### Before you go

- **Acclimatize for 10–14 days** in the hottest, most humid environment you can reach: a tropical coastal city in the wet season, or a sauna-and-exercise regimen if that's all you have. Acclimatization raises your sweat rate, lowers the salt content of your sweat, and expands your blood volume. It won't make the delta comfortable, but it may make it survivable at a walking pace.
- **Get fit, but for endurance, not strength.** You'll walk and climb constantly while carrying everything you own.
- If you have heart disease, are on medications that affect heat tolerance (many blood pressure drugs, diuretics, some psychiatric medications, antihistamines), are pregnant, or are elderly, get serious medical advice. Some people should not go.

### Cooling gear

- **Phase-change cooling vests** with packs that solidify around 15–28°C. Ice melts quickly and can't be refrozen here, but some phase-change materials re-solidify in water cooler than their melting point. **The keels have the only cool water in the delta.** Find out whether you can recharge packs there. If you can, the keels become your cooling stations, and you should plan your routes around them.
- **Loose, pale, lightweight clothing.** Some travelers prefer as little as is culturally acceptable. Watch what the locals wear; they've solved this problem.
- A **wide-brimmed hat**. The sun is strong between storms.
- A **small handheld fan**. Moving air helps a little even when evaporation is poor. It may fail near strong fields (see below), so don't depend on it.

### Behavior

- **Move slowly.** Every bit of exertion is heat you can't get rid of. Walk at the pace of the oldest local you see.
- **Rest in the shade of arches**, and ideally near the dripping undersides where condensation gathers. The water is warm, but wetting your skin still helps a little.
- **The afternoon rain is your friend.** It's warm, but it's movement and water. Stand in it.
- **Drink constantly and replace salt.** You may need six to ten liters a day. Bring electrolyte tablets in quantity. Weigh yourself if you can; losing more than 2% of body weight in a day means you're falling behind. Watch your urine: dark or scant is a warning.
- **Know the cool places.** If you feel confused, clumsy, or strangely calm and detached, get to a keel or into shade and water immediately, and signal your companion.
- **Never travel alone.** Agree on a gesture that means "I'm overheating" and one that means "you look wrong, stop."

## Part Two: The Noise

### What you're actually facing

Several distinct sounds, layered:

1. **The grinding hiss of rising silt.** Broadband and constant, like standing near a waterfall that never ends.
2. **The field-thrum.** Frequencies that "rattle teeth," probably mid-to-low range and strong enough to feel in your skull.
3. **The glaze-crack.** Sharp, percussive, gunshot-like reports by the thousand, every second. Impulse noise like this damages hearing more than steady noise at the same average level.
4. **The wet roar of the slack.** Collapsing walls, sheeting mud, groaning struts.
5. **The pump-heartbeat.** Infrasound, felt rather than heard.
6. **The afternoon rain** on glazed rooftops, loud enough to be heard over all of the above.

It's reasonable to assume sustained levels well into the range that causes permanent hearing damage within hours, not days.

### Protection

- **Get an audiogram before you leave** so you have a baseline.
- **Double protection:** high-attenuation foam or custom-molded plugs, plus over-ear muffs. Bring many spare plugs; they'll get fouled with mud and sweat. Bring a way to clean them.
- **Consider local clay ear seals.** The people of the delta mold soft glazed clay into their ears. These are probably tuned to their environment, damping the damaging frequencies while letting through the sounds that matter: the change in field-pitch before a slack, the wet roar of a nearby collapse. Complete silence could be dangerous here. Ask about them, pay for a proper fitting, and ideally learn from a local what they are *supposed* to let you hear.
- **Earmuffs are hot.** That is a real cost given Part One. You may end up alternating: plugs plus muffs in the loudest districts, plugs alone elsewhere, and bare ears only inside keels.

### What you can't block

The pump-heartbeat arrives through your ribs and feet, and no ear protection touches it. Low-frequency infrasound like this is associated in some people with unease, nausea, a sense of dread or of being watched, disturbed sleep, and pressure in the chest. Knowing where the feeling comes from helps. When you feel inexplicably anxious in the delta, it may just be the pumps. Breathe in time with them if you can; some travelers find that the beat becomes almost soothing after a few days.

Expect **tinnitus**. Expect **fatigue** from noise alone, which is exhausting in a way that's hard to explain until you've lived in it. Expect your first night of sleep to be poor.

### Communication

No one speaks aloud, and you won't be able to either. Prepare for that seriously.

- **You can't learn the delta's gestural language in advance.** You can make yourself a better gestural communicator. Practice charades. Learn basic signs from any signed language you can access, for vocabulary habits rather than direct use. Learn to use your face, posture, and space expressively. Travelers who arrive stiff and self-conscious struggle.
- **Travel with a companion and develop a private gesture set before you go**: stop, go, danger, water, hot, sick, follow, wait, lost, run, yes, no, which way, sleep, eat, and "I need a keel."
- **Palm-pressing** is how locals speak up close, reading each other's vibrations. It will feel intimate to many outsiders. Don't flinch from it or treat it as a romantic gesture; it's just talking. Let people take your hands.
- **Bring a waterproof slate or synthetic-paper notebook and a grease pencil.** The delta has "older texts," so some form of writing exists, but paper is a plant product and there are no plants. Don't assume people read your script, but drawings work almost universally. A quick sketch of a keel, a swift, or a cup of water will get you far.
- **Hire a guide.** This is probably the most important logistical choice you'll make. A local who reads the fronts, knows the markets, and will patiently teach you signs is worth almost any price.

## Part Three: The Ground

### The core rule

**Solid ground here is a temporary agreement.** Everything you stand on, sleep in, or walk under will dissolve, usually within days or weeks. Your safety depends on knowing where you are in that cycle.

### Reading the fronts

Locals read these signals, and you should learn them from your first hour:

- **The mudswifts.** When the swifts abandon a district all at once, the slack is roughly a day away. **When the swifts leave, you leave.** No exceptions, no "just one more hour."
- **The glaze.** Fresh-made structures are crisp, glossy, often still warm. A structure approaching the slack goes dull, and its edges soften. **Dull glaze means get out from under it.**
- **The glass crabs.** They swarm in slack zones. Arriving crabs mean dissolution is starting or imminent.
- **The field-pitch.** Locals listen to the hum. You'll struggle at first, especially behind ear protection. Watch the locals' reactions instead.
- **The dogs.** They are the early warning for *unscheduled* slacks, local collapses out of rhythm with the main fronts. **When the dogs run, you run with them.** Don't stop to grab anything. Follow them; they know which way is solid.
- **The people.** When a crowd starts packing up, pack up. When a market empties, don't stay to haggle.

### Where to be

- **Just behind the make is safest.** Newly set structures have the longest life ahead of them. The locals live "a few days or weeks ahead of the slack," following the make. Do what they do.
- **Wait for the crack.** Don't enter a structure while it is still forming. The fields are strongest there, and silt is still climbing. Wait for the glaze to cure with its ringing crack, and ideally wait for locals to climb it first. They'll be up within hours.
- **Never sleep in a building the swifts have left.** Never sleep in a building with dull glaze.
- **The keels are the only fixed points.** Orient yourself by them.

### If you get caught in the slack

The mud of the slack is warm, liquefied sediment that behaves like quicksand.

- **Carry a light, sturdy walking staff.** Use it to probe suspicious surfaces, to spread your weight, and as a lever or lifeline for yourself or someone else.
- If you start to sink, **don't thrash**. Drop any heavy load, lean back, spread your arms, and try to float. Liquefied sediment is denser than water, and you're more buoyant in it than you fear. Move slowly toward firmer ground. Work your legs free gently, one at a time, to let the mud flow back around them.
- **Stay out of the gutters when they're running fast** after rain or during a slack. Brown, heavy, fast-moving mud can knock you down and carry you, and it's full of eels.
- **Be aware of what's above you.** Walls "sag like wet paper," and struts groan before they fail. A long, deep groan overhead means move out from under it now.

### Footwear and slipping

Wet glaze is slick. Wet glaze coated in condensation, silt, and biofilm is treacherous. Bring **grippy, quick-draining shoes**: soft soles with aggressive tread, like good river sandals or water shoes that protect your toes. Boots will fill with mud and never dry. Ramps and stairs are everywhere and constantly wet, so descend with care and use your staff.

### Navigation

- **Maps are useless.** Streets shift by meters, plazas become stairs, domes become spires. Anything you draw today is wrong within the week.
- **Compasses may be unreliable** because of the fields.
- **The sun is reliable.** It rises pink in the morning and casts shadows where it should. Learn to navigate by sun and time. It's the one thing that behaves as expected.
- **The keels are your landmarks.** Everything spirals around them. Learn the direction to the nearest one at all times.
- **The sea is downhill.** The gutters all run toward it. If you're lost and need an edge, follow the water.

## Part Four: The Fields

You'll feel them as a prickle on the skin and a metallic taste on the tongue. Nobody fully understands them, so treat them with caution.

- **Assume your electronics will be unreliable.** They may glitch, drain, fail, or be damaged. Don't depend on a phone, GPS, or any electronic device for anything critical. Bring cheap duplicates and analog backups: a mechanical watch you're willing to ruin, a paper notebook, sun navigation.
- **Implanted medical devices** such as pacemakers, insulin pumps, cochlear implants, and neurostimulators are a serious concern. Get expert advice; you may need to reconsider the trip.
- **Metal fillings and piercings** may be why you taste metal. That's probably harmless, but if piercings get uncomfortably warm or tingly, take them out.
- **Avoid lingering in forming structures** where the fields are strongest.
- **Lightning.** The afternoon thunderheads are real storms. Don't be on the tallest fresh spire when they build, and get under cover when the rain begins. Charged fields and charged clouds are not a combination to test.
- **Nobody knows the long-term effects.** Keep your first visit short.

## Part Five: Health

### Water

The seep is "faintly metallic," the rain "mineral-heavy" with silt, and the gutters full of eels, bacterial mats, and whatever the slack releases.

- **Don't drink untreated water.** Boiling kills pathogens but won't remove dissolved metals. Bring a purifier that combines fine filtration with activated carbon, or better, a portable reverse-osmosis unit if you can carry one. Pre-filter the silt through cloth first or it will clog everything.
- **Rainwater collected from clean, freshly glazed surfaces** is probably the best local source. Ask your guide what locals drink.
- **Keel water** is cool and precious. Ask before taking it, and never waste it.

### Skin and feet

Constant wetness breaks down skin. Expect **heat rash, fungal infections, chafing, and maceration** of the feet that can progress to something like trench foot.

- Antifungal powder and cream, zinc oxide or barrier cream for chafe points.
- Several changes of socks and underwear in sealed dry bags. Change at least once a day.
- Air your feet whenever you rest.
- Treat any cut or scrape immediately. Warm mud, mineral grit, and biofilms make wound infection very likely.

### Food and diet

Nothing grows. The local diet is presumably built on eels, sea fish from the edges, and whatever the markets trade. That has consequences:

- **Cook everything thoroughly.** Eels and fish from warm gutters are prime carriers of parasites.
- **Be cautious about glass crabs.** They eat residue of the binding and may not be entirely animals. Wait until locals clearly eat them, and even then, think twice.
- **Bring vitamin C and a multivitamin.** A diet with no plants can cause scurvy within a couple of months. That won't matter on a short trip, but it matters for longer stays.
- **Bring some fiber and familiar foods.** A sudden switch to an all-animal diet in extreme heat can wreck your digestion.

### Vaccinations and prophylaxis

Consult a travel medicine specialist and describe the environment honestly: a warm, dense, wet urban setting, packed with people and animals, with contaminated water. Real-world analogs suggest:

- Tetanus booster (you *will* get cut)
- Hepatitis A and typhoid
- **Pre-exposure rabies vaccination**, because of bats in the thousands and roaming dog packs
- Consider **leptospirosis prophylaxis**, which is common wherever people wade through warm water shared with animals
- A thorough kit for gastrointestinal illness: rehydration salts, antibiotics as prescribed, antidiarrheals

### Bugs and animals

- Midges, roaches, and mud-wasps fill "every warm crevice." Bring strong repellent and permethrin-treated clothing, plus antihistamines and an epinephrine auto-injector if you have any history of sting allergy.
- **Bats:** don't touch them, and don't sleep directly under their roosts.
- **Dogs:** they're allies, not pets. Don't approach or feed them. Follow their lead when they run, but don't get between packs.
- **Eels:** keep your hands out of the gutters.

### Air

The ozone tang comes from the fields. Most people will be fine, but if you have asthma, bring your inhalers and expect some irritation.

## Part Six: Living Among the People

### Respect the rhythm

Everything that can be carried is carried, because nothing that stays put survives. Adopt this completely.

- **Pack as if you'll move every day, because you might.** Keep your load light, around 8–10 kg including water if you can manage it. Put everything in dry bags inside one pack. Never unpack more than you can grab in thirty seconds.
- **Never set something down and walk away.** It will be mud by the time you come back.
- **Don't try to claim a space.** Space here is borrowed, shared, and temporary. People hang cloth partitions within hours of a building setting. Pay for your corner, and leave it gracefully when everyone else leaves.

### Trade and gifts

It's hard to predict what's valued, but some reasonable guesses:

- **Anything from plants may be astonishing.** Cloth, wood, paper, dried fruit, cotton, spices. They have no everyday word for leaf. A pressed leaf might be a marvel, a curiosity, or something unsettling. Offer carefully, watch reactions, and don't make it a spectacle.
- **Metal goods** might be valued in a world made entirely of clay.
- **Durable, carryable, useful objects** are probably appreciated everywhere.
- **Don't introduce living seeds or organisms.** They won't survive anyway, but it isn't your place to test that.

### Manners

- **Don't speak aloud in the streets.** There's no point, and it marks you as someone who doesn't understand.
- **Accept palm-talk and gesture fully.** Being clumsy and willing earns more goodwill than being fluent and stiff.
- **Don't take glaze fragments as souvenirs**, and especially don't take them from crabs. You don't know what they mean or what they do.
- **Don't dig.** "Expeditions that dig too deep find the mud getting warmer and warmer and more intricately ordered, until it sings in the hand. Then they come back up, or they don't." If anyone invites you on such an expedition, decline. If you're tempted to scratch at the ground to see what happens, don't.
- **Don't treat the people as custodians or builders.** They're adamant that they inherited the city and didn't make it. Don't press them for its secrets as though they're hiding something. It's a real mystery to them too.

## Part Seven: The Keels

These are your sanctuaries, and they are sacred to the people who live around them.

- **They are quieter.** The roar drops to heavy surf. Here you can finally hear your own voice.
- **They are cooler**, and their water is the only cool water in the delta.
- **They are stable.** The fronts pass around them.

Plan your trip around them. Arrive near one if you can. Spend your first night close to one. Return whenever you're overwhelmed, overheated, or lost.

But **behave as you would in a temple, a hospital chapel, and a family home at once.** People go there for weddings and grief, to say things that matter aloud. Keep your voice low. Don't treat the space as a rest stop. Don't scrape the biofilms, which are layered like canyon walls and thousands of years old. Don't disturb the swifts that return to the same nests year after year. Take cool water sparingly and only with permission.

The first time you step inside a keel and take out your earplugs and say something, even just your own name, it will probably hit you harder than you expect. Many travelers cry. That's normal.

## Part Eight: The Mind

### The sensory load

You'll be flooded: noise, vibration, heat, a crowd beyond counting, a landscape rearranging itself at walking pace, and nothing green anywhere. The first day can feel like standing inside an engine that never stops. Some people panic. Some go numb. Some feel an unexpected exhilaration.

Most people who stay a few days report a shift. The chaos becomes a pattern. You begin to read the swifts. You notice when the glaze dulls. Your hands start to speak. The heartbeat of the pumps becomes a clock you breathe to. You stop seeing collapse and start seeing weather.

### What helps

- **A daily keel visit** as decompression, if one is within reach.
- **Short stints.** Keep your first visit to three to seven days.
- **A companion** you trust, with whom you have a private gesture language.
- **Something green**, a photograph or a small token from home. Many travelers find they miss plants with a physical ache they didn't anticipate.
- **Routine.** Wake, drink, check the swifts, move, eat, rest through the worst heat, stand in the rain, watch the city blaze in the low sun, sleep. The locals live by the fronts the way farmers live by seasons. Borrow their rhythm.

### The impermanence

This is the part that stays with people. You'll see a tower you slept in turn to warm mud and run down a gutter while crabs click over it. You'll walk a street that no longer exists the next morning. Some find this terrifying. Some find it freeing. Almost everyone finds that it changes how they think about their own homes, which are also temporary agreements, only slower.

## Part Nine: Leaving

- **Plan your exit near a keel or the coast**, where you have fixed reference points.
- **Expect the silence to feel wrong.** Children born in the delta sleep poorly when they leave because silence frightens them. You won't be that extreme, but your first quiet night afterward may feel eerie, even threatening. Some returning travelers sleep with a fan or white noise for a week or two.
- **Get another audiogram** to check for hearing damage.
- **Watch for delayed illness** such as fever, gut trouble, or infected wounds over the following weeks, and tell your doctor exactly where you've been, in whatever terms make sense.
- **Your balance and your sense of solid ground may take a few days to recalibrate.** Some travelers keep glancing at walls to check their glaze, or listen for birds leaving.

## Packing Summary

**Heat:** phase-change cooling vest and spare packs, wide-brimmed hat, loose pale clothing, handheld fan, electrolyte tablets in quantity, large water capacity (bladder plus bottles)

**Noise:** high-attenuation earplugs in bulk, over-ear muffs, a plug-cleaning kit, a budget for local clay ear seals

**Communication:** waterproof slate or synthetic notebook, grease pencils, a pre-agreed gesture set, a budget for a local guide

**Ground:** walking staff, grippy quick-draining shoes, dry bags, a light pack you can grab in seconds

**Water:** purifier with carbon and fine filtration (or reverse osmosis), cloth pre-filters

**Health:** antifungal powder and cream, barrier cream, many sock changes, a wound-care kit, insect repellent, permethrin-treated clothing, antihistamines, epinephrine if needed, rehydration salts, prescribed antibiotics, vitamin C and a multivitamin, inhalers if asthmatic

**Navigation:** a cheap mechanical watch, sun-navigation knowledge, a habit of always knowing the direction of the nearest keel and of the sea

**Trade and gifts:** small, durable, useful items, possibly metal, and cloth. Anything plant-based, offered with care.

**Comfort:** something green from home

---

## A Final Word

Go slowly. Watch the swifts. Follow the dogs. Drink more than you think you need. Let people take your hands. When you find a keel, go inside, take out your earplugs, and listen to your own voice for a while.

Then go back out into the roar. The rain will come in the afternoon, and for twenty minutes the whole delta will be a drum. When the clouds tear open and the low sun hits the wet glaze and the steam rises off a million terraces, you'll understand why so many people live here and don't want to leave.
