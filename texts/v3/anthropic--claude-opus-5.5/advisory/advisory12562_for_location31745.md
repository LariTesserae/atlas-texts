# Preparing to Visit the Foundry Blister

## The Short Version

The Blister is not hostile. It has no interest in you at all, and that is the whole difficulty. It is a machine that runs without an operator, and you will be the first moving part in it that nobody designed. Most of the danger comes from ordinary physics: altitude, heat, sharp volcanic glass, an active mountain. The rest comes from the room's rules, which are few, consistent, and not built with you in mind.

Go in light and dry. Touch as little as possible. Leave nothing, take nothing, and do not lean on the cool wall no matter how much you want to.

---

## 1. The Mountain Itself

Before any of the strangeness, there is a young volcano near its summit, and the approach is hazardous in its own right.

**Altitude.** The air is thin, dry, and sharp. Acclimatize properly beforehand, with several days at progressively higher camps. Hypoxia and heat stress compound each other badly, and you will face both at once inside. A mild altitude headache will feel much worse in an oven. If you arrive at the seam already breathless and foggy, you are not ready to go in.

**The slope.** A "raw slurry of black glass and rust-colored scoria, still settling" means:
- **Obsidian and glassy scoria cut like broken bottles.** Wear cut-resistant gloves (Kevlar or Dyneema, with leather palms), gaiters, and durable long sleeves and trousers. A fall here causes lacerations as well as bruises.
- **Scoria rolls underfoot like ball bearings.** Bring stiff-soled mountaineering boots and trekking poles with carbide tips. Expect to slide back half a step with each step up.
- **"Still settling" means unstable.** Keep off fresh slump scars. Don't climb directly below anyone else.
- **The ground is warm even at altitude.** This isn't dangerous on the slope, but it warns you about what's coming.

**Volcanic gases.** A mountain this young is degassing. Carry a multi-gas monitor covering at least CO₂, SO₂, and H₂S. CO₂ is heavier than air and pools in hollows and cracks, so the entrance seam is exactly the kind of place it collects. The bellows plants seem to ventilate the chamber, but don't trust vegetation with your life. Trust the meter. If it alarms, back out.

**Eruption risk.** Find out whatever can be known about this mountain's activity. The deep knocks described below may be something other than ordinary volcanism, but they come from inside a mountain that is still forming. Tell someone where you are and when to expect you back.

## 2. The Crack

The entrance is "a crack no wider than a shoulder," angled so that no sunlight enters.

- **Practice squeezing beforehand.** Wear your full kit and work through a similar gap at home. People jam in tight passages because they panic, not because they don't fit.
- **Keep your pack small and bring a haul cord.** Push or drag it ahead of you instead of wearing it. Nothing should stick out and snag.
- **The crack is lined with glass.** Protect your face and neck. Go slowly. A slice here is miles from help.
- **Minimize trips in and out.** The plants near the entrance draft are already stunted and paper-pale, so the crack is where the room's heat leaks away. Every passage shifts the draft a little. Go in once and come out once.
- **Pay attention to the temperature change as you pass through.** You're moving from thin, cold mountain air into an oven, and the transition is abrupt. Pause just inside, let your eyes and body adjust, and take your first readings.

---

## 3. Heat and Dryness: The Real Constraint

"An oven set low and left on for a century." It is constant, even, and rises from the whole floor. Measure it on arrival, but plan for something in dry-sauna territory, possibly well above body temperature.

In air hotter than your skin, the air heats you. Your only defense is sweat evaporation, and in air "so dry it seems to have been ironed" sweat evaporates instantly. That cools you well but drains your water fast. You won't feel wet. You will just become dehydrated without noticing.

**Clothing (desert logic, taken further):**
- Cover everything. Bare skin absorbs heat, and loose light layers give some buffer. A reflective outer layer is worth considering.
- **Thick, insulated boot soles are essential.** The heat comes up from the floor, and thin soles will burn you within minutes of standing still.
- **Never sit, kneel, or lie down** unless you have an insulated pad, and preferably not even then. Keep a folded closed-cell pad for emergencies.
- A wide-brimmed hat does little here, since the heat comes from below and all around. A loose hood and face covering do more.
- **Cooling:** phase-change cooling vests work well. Air this dry has almost no dew point, so they shouldn't condense on the outside. Ice vests would create meltwater, which leads to the next section.

**Time limits.** Set a hard turnaround time before you enter and don't renegotiate it inside. Heat exhaustion impairs judgment early, and a room this beautiful and this slow will make you want to stay "just a bit longer." Know the signs: headache, nausea, confusion, stopping sweating, racing pulse. A partner should watch you for them, because you won't notice them in yourself. If you plan a long observation (see Section 8), do it in short stints, cooling down between them outside the crack.

**Your body is a cold spot.** At 37°C in a room this hot, you are one of the coolest objects present. The plants grow toward heat and yellow at cool margins. Standing near a fern or bellows plant for a long time may stress it slightly, the same way the entrance draft stresses the plants near the crack. Keep moving. Don't loom over anything for long.

## 4. Water: Handle It Like a Hazardous Material

There has never been water in this room. "There is barely even the idea of it." Yet you will carry liters of it, sweat it, and breathe it out.

Look at that pale mineral ring on the wall, "left where something might once have evaporated but probably never did... a stain from the future, it belongs to a flood that has not happened." In a room where consequences are sometimes set in place before their causes, it is at least possible that the stain belongs to a visitor, maybe one who spilled a bottle, maybe you.

I can't tell you whether the ring can be avoided. But be careful regardless:

- **Carry water only in sealed bladders with bite valves.** No open bottles and no screw caps you have to fumble in the heat.
- **Drink deliberately, over the finished floor, well away from walls and plants.** Better still, drink heavily just outside the crack before you go in and after you come out, and sip only as needed inside.
- **No liquid food, no wet wipes, no sprays.**
- **Don't spit.** Deal with a runny nose away from walls and plants.
- **If you spill, don't panic and don't scrub.** In this air it will be gone in seconds. It may leave a ring. Perhaps it already has.

Your breath and sweat can't be helped, and they vanish into the dryness immediately. The bellows plants keep "the room ventilated for occupants who are not there." You are, uneasily, the nearest thing to such an occupant the room has had. Be a quiet one.

## 5. Air and Dust

- **Bring a well-fitted respirator (P100 or equivalent) and sealed goggles.** Volcanic glass dust is made of microscopic shards, harmful to lungs and eyes. It sits on the flanges and rains down whenever the mountain knocks. Keep them on your person, ready to pull on in seconds, even if you don't wear them the whole time.
- The bellows plants exhale once a day for nearly a minute, pushing up "a thread of hot dry air" that smells of cut metal and crushed stems. Don't stand over one when it's inflated and nearly spherical. The exhale is probably harmless, but it will be hotter than the surrounding air, and you don't need extra heat on your face.
- Keep the gas monitor running inside the whole time.

## 6. What to Bring, and What Not to Bring

The room follows rules about identity, spending, and conduction. These decide your packing list.

### Nothing identical

"What is duplicated stays in step. What is reused cannot be separated." The room pairs identical objects, such as two fist-sized stones with the same comma of crystal, and moves them together.

**Don't give it anything to pair.** Two matching water bladders, two identical carabiners, a pair of fresh batteries from the same packet, two new copies of a mass-produced item: these are exactly the kind of "same part" the room might economize on. And if you link something inside the room to something in your pocket, you could walk out carrying half a pair still bound to the other half.

- Scratch, tape, or mark every item distinctly.
- Where you can, choose mismatched gear: different brands, different colors.
- Don't bring an item and a spare of the same model. Bring a different spare.
- Left and right gloves and boots are mirror images rather than identical, so I doubt they're at risk. Still, a quick nick of the knife on one boot heel costs nothing.

### Minimal metal, nothing that bridges

The wire vines carry a live current in a closed loop, "powering nothing except its own continuing." A palm across two vines gives a numb buzz.

- **People with pacemakers, implanted defibrillators, insulin pumps, cochlear implants, or neurostimulators should not visit.**
- Leave metal jewelry behind. Take off rings and watches, or at least keep them covered.
- Don't carry long metal items such as poles, rods, or tripods where they could touch two vines at once. Leave the trekking poles at the entrance.
- Expect electronics to misbehave near the vines. Bring a backup: a mechanical compass (expect it to wander), a paper notebook, and a pencil. Pencil works in heat and dryness where ink may fail.

### Cameras: optional, unreliable

The unfinished floor looks like gravel when viewed directly and like a drafting grid in peripheral vision. There's no telling which a camera will record, or whether it will record something else. The light has no source and the shadows converge. Take photos if you like, but don't count on them showing what you saw. Your notebook and memory will serve you better.

### No crumbs, no organics, no seeds

The chamber is sealed, sterile, and has never held an animal. You are a walking reservoir of microbes, skin flakes, hair, and whatever seeds are caught in the tread of your boots.

- Clean boots and outer gear thoroughly before the approach, and again at the seam.
- Eat outside only. Bring nothing edible inside.
- Wear a close cap or hood to reduce shed hair.
- The heat will probably sterilize most of what you bring. Still, "probably" is a weak word to lean on in a place where the floor becomes whatever is spent into it.

## 7. Conduct Inside: A Map of What Not to Touch

Picture the room as zones.

### The finished floor: stand here

The third of the floor that is polished stone is where you belong. It's solid, it doesn't change, and it is the part of the room that has already decided what it is. Keep to it wherever you can.

### The unfinished floor: cross only if you must

"Step on it and it holds." It will. But this floor "fills in only when something is spent into it: a fruit, a fallen frond, a flake of shed bark."

- **Drop nothing on the lattice.** A dropped glove, pencil, or lens cap may not be yours afterward. It may become floor.
- **Don't bleed on it.** Be especially careful with the glass cuts from the approach. Bandage any wound fully before entering.
- **Don't linger on it.** You're shedding constantly in ways you can't feel. Cross quickly, never stand still, and never sit.
- **Don't test it by staring.** Looking directly turns it to gravel, and your sense of footing will jump between two versions of the same ground. That's disorienting enough to cause a stumble. Look at your destination, not your feet.

I don't believe the lattice would take a whole living person. It seems to want things that are *spent*, meaning shed, finished, released. But I wouldn't want to be unconscious on it.

### The tree: don't take the fruit

The fruit are units, ejected and allowed to lie, and over years they sink into the undecided floor and make it solid. They are the room's building material.

- **Don't eat one.** Obviously.
- **Don't take one home.** Each fruit is ground the room hasn't laid yet. Removing one leaves a patch of floor that won't be finished. Given how this room treats cause and effect, that gap might already exist somewhere in its structure, waiting for you to explain it. Don't be the explanation.
- **Don't kick or scatter them.** Walk around the pile.
- If you want to feel one, and you will, since they're heavy and warm and uniform "to within a hair," pick one up with a gloved hand, feel the weight, and put it back exactly where it was.
- **Watch for the fruit with unsettled dust on them.** That dust hasn't fallen yet. Leave it undisturbed.
- Keep clear of the branches' ejection points. A ripe fruit is pushed out, not dropped. It's probably slow, but you don't want one landing on your head.

### The flywheel ferns: stay out of the sweep

A rotation takes about eleven days, too slow to hurt you, but the arcs worn into solid basalt show what persistence does.

- **Don't set anything down in a fern's sweep.** Your pack left in the path of a rim is an obstruction to a process that runs at exactly the pace its tolerances require.
- **Never try to stop, push, or "help" a fern turn.** You'd be interfering with a mechanism you can't see, the ring of fleshy valves at the hub.
- Don't brush the discs. They're living fronds under slow pressure.

### The bellows plants: give them room

They're squat, pleated, and dark jade, in the corners. Admire them from a step away. Don't press on the pleats to see what happens, and don't stand over one mid-exhale.

### The wire vines: don't complete the circuit

- **Never lay a palm across two vines.** The description says you'd feel only a mild numb buzz, but you're dehydrated, overheated, and at altitude, and your heart is already working hard. Don't add current.
- Don't grip a junction. The spark-flowers there give off cold blue points of light, and you don't want to be part of whatever closes the loop.
- **Use the flowers as a clock.** They blink at intervals of about an hour, and the hum at the edge of hearing rises and falls with them. In a room where time is assembled out of order, a local pulse is more meaningful than your watch.
- Note that the newest coils lean toward the dark patch. Watch that direction, and see below.

### The paired stones: don't carry one out

There are at least four known pairs, and likely others you won't spot. "Moving one moves the other." Turn away and both have shifted identically.

- **Don't pocket any stone in this room.** Any one of them could be half of a pair. You don't know what happens when one half crosses the threshold and the other can't follow. It might drag its twin across the floor. It might refuse to leave, and you'd feel your pocket grow heavy on the way out. Either way it would be bad for the room, and possibly for you.
- If you want to test the pairing (it's tempting and nearly irresistible), nudge the comma-stone near the entrance a finger's width, turn your back, count to thirty, and look at its twin under the bellows plant. Then put the first stone back and check again. Do this once and only once. It's a demonstration, not a toy.

### The dark patch: do not touch it

This is the most important rule in the room, and the one you'll most want to break.

Everything in the Blister is hot except one door-sized patch of wall that is slightly darker and **cool**. You'll be deep into heat stress by then. Your whole body will pull you toward the one cool surface in the room, wanting to press your palms and cheek against it.

**Don't.**
