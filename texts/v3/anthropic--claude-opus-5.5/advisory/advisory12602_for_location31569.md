# Preparing to Visit the Tabletop Sea

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Most dangerous places are dangerous because something is trying to hurt you: currents, predators, storms, heat. The Tabletop Sea mostly isn't. Nothing here hunts you, the water doesn't move, and there's no weather below the surface. The sea is dangerous because it is extreme along several axes at once: altitude, cold, noise, emptiness, sharpness, and isolation. And it is so perfectly stable that it lulls you into forgetting any of them.

Your preparation should reflect that. You are not armoring yourself against an enemy. You are building a life-support bubble that has to work flawlessly for hours in a place with no margin, no help, and no soft surfaces.

You are also a guest in a system balanced on a knife-edge. Part of preparing is making sure you don't tip it.

## Part One: Before You Leave Home

### Training you genuinely need

Do not treat this as a recreational dive destination. At minimum, get competent in:

- **Drysuit diving in near-freezing water.** Train in real ice-diving conditions, not a cool quarry. You need to handle a regulator free-flow at 1°C calmly, because in water this cold it will happen.
- **Altitude diving.** The plateau sits in thin air. Surface pressure is lower than at sea level, so every meter of depth is a proportionally larger pressure change. Standard dive tables and default computer settings will under-predict your decompression obligation. You need altitude-corrected procedures and practice using them.
- **Blue-water (open-ocean) diving with no visual reference.** This is the closest Earth analogue to hanging in the Tabletop's void. Blue-water divers work tethered to a boat or a downline for good reason. Learn their protocols.
- **Line and tether signaling.** Ice divers communicate through rope pulls, and you will need to as well, for reasons covered below.
- **Technical or deep diving**, if you intend to reach the floor in most places. Much of the floor lies hundreds of meters down, far beyond recreational limits. Some ridges and plains sit shallower, and the light description suggests usable depths around 40 meters. **Plan your first visit around the shallow features.** The deep plains will still be there in a thousand years. Almost literally.

### Medical preparation

- **Get a baseline audiogram.** Your hearing will be tested by this place, and you want to know afterward exactly what it cost you. Get another hearing test when you return.
- **Get an altitude assessment.** If you've never been above 4,000 meters, find out how your body responds before you arrive somewhere you also plan to dive.
- **Screen for conditions the environment will aggravate:** cardiac issues (cold shock and exertion at altitude are a bad pairing), photosensitive epilepsy (see the pump hall), vestibular disorders, migraines, and anxiety disorders, particularly any that are sensitive to open space or sensory overload.
- **Acclimatize before diving.** Don't step through a portal from sea level and get straight in the water. Spend days at a comparable altitude first, ideally somewhere with a hyperbaric facility, then arrive already adapted.

### Think about your mind as equipment

People who spend time here report two strange psychological effects:

1. The roar becomes "a very thick silence."
2. Real quiet, on return, "feels like falling."

Take both seriously. If you have a history of panic, tinnitus distress, or difficulty with sensory deprivation or overload, this place will find it. Practice sustained exposure to loud broadband noise, carefully and with hearing protection, before you go. Then practice the opposite: sitting in true silence afterward.

## Part Two: The Surface World

You won't spend most of your time underwater. You'll spend it on the surface, prepping, recovering, and waiting out fog. The surface will try to kill you in quieter ways.

### Altitude and air

The air is thin and cold. Expect headaches, breathlessness, poor sleep, and impaired judgment. Impaired judgment matters most, because you'll be doing complex gas and decompression planning. Bring:

- **Supplemental oxygen** for the surface, and **pulse oximeters**.
- **Altitude sickness medication**, used on the advice of someone who knows your medical history.
- A rule that **every dive plan gets checked by two people**, because at altitude you are not as sharp as you feel.

### Cold, and the freezing fog

Slow fronts of freezing fog drift over the plateau for *weeks*. When you surface wet, even in a drysuit, into sub-zero, low-oxygen, windless fog, the danger isn't dramatic. It's creeping: fogged and iced masks, frozen valves, frozen zippers, numb fingers that can't work a clip.

- Bring a **heated, enclosed shelter on your surface platform** that you can reach directly from the water, ideally with a wet entry well so you never stand exposed in the air while wet.
- Pack **spare regulators** and keep them warm and dry until use. A regulator left out in freezing fog can ice up the moment you breathe through it.
- Carry **multiple layers of insulated clothing** for between dives. Assume the fog will last your whole trip.

### Your platform

There is no shore in any useful sense. The plateau is flooded to the brim. You'll need a boat, raft, or floating platform. Conveniently, the surface barely moves, so a small, stable craft will sit like a table on a table.

But:

- **Assume a slow drift toward the lip.** The sea pours over its edge in a sheet eleven hundred kilometers long, so somewhere there is net movement toward that edge. Near most of the sea it may be imperceptible. It is never zero. The water is hundreds of meters deep, so you can't anchor conventionally. Use a **sea anchor**, keep a reliable position fix, and have a motor that can outrun any drift.
- **You can't navigate in the fog by sight.** You need a positioning method that works without landmarks or stars. See the navigation section for why the obvious ones may fail.
- Keep the platform **far from the lip**. More on that below.

### Water and food

The sea is fresh and remarkably pure, so pure that it tastes of stone. It is tempting as a water supply, but:

- It contains almost nothing dissolved. Drinking a lot of it without supplements may slowly deplete your electrolytes. **Bring electrolyte salts.**
- It carries a haze of "mineral-feeding particles, not quite alive by any familiar standard." Nobody knows what those do inside a human gut. **Filter it finely and boil it.** Remember that water boils at a lower temperature at altitude, so boil longer, or rely on filtration you trust.
- Bring all your own food. Nothing here is edible, and you shouldn't try.

## Part Three: Dive Equipment

### Thermal protection

The water sits at one degree above freezing, sometimes half a degree, everywhere, at every depth, forever. There is no warmer layer to retreat to.

- **Drysuit** with heavy undergarments, plus **active heating** (a heated vest or undersuit) if you can manage the power safely.
- **Dry gloves**, not wet gloves.
- **A full-face mask or a helmet**, not a half-mask. This matters for several reasons: it keeps near-freezing water off your face (which can trigger gasping and disorientation), it reduces some of the sound conducted into your skull, and it lets you breathe normally if your jaw goes numb.
- **Cold-water-rated regulators** built for ice diving, in redundant pairs on independent first stages.

### Cut protection: the most underrated requirement

This floor is **new**. Nothing has been worn smooth. Basalt columns have hexagonal edges "that could cut a hand." Obsidian stands in fractured plates, and obsidian is sharper than surgical steel. There is no sand or mud to cushion anything, and the cables are crusted with shed clickwright shells.

A drysuit cut in 1°C water is an emergency. Your suit floods, you lose insulation and buoyancy at the same time, and you get very cold very fast.

- Wear **cut-resistant outer layers**: Kevlar or Dyneema overgloves, knee and shin guards, and ideally an armored outer shell over the drysuit.
- Bring **redundant buoyancy**, such as a backup wing or a lift bag, so a flooded suit doesn't drag you down.
- Practice **excellent buoyancy control**. The rule is "never touch the floor," not "try not to touch the floor." Sweep this floor mentally the way the description does: as something that was just swept, and that you should not step on barefoot.
- Keep a **patch kit** on the surface and assume you'll use it.

### Breathing gas

For shallow dives, open circuit with redundant supply is fine. For longer or deeper dives, a **rebreather** makes sense: it's gas-efficient and warms your breathing gas slightly, which matters here. However:

- Rebreather scrubbers work less well in extreme cold. Insulate the canister and plan conservatively.
- Rebreathers don't release a stream of bubbles, and bubbles may be one of your few reliable up-indicators. More below.

### Alarms and instruments

**Any audible alarm is useless.** Your computer's beep, a rebreather's warning tone, anything that relies on your hearing. You "cannot hear [your] own breath."

- Every critical alarm should **vibrate** or show a **bright visual signal** you can't miss, ideally in a head-up display in your mask.
- Carry **backup depth gauges and timers** you check by habit, not only when prompted.

## Part Four: The Noise

This is the defining feature of the place, and it will shape everything you do.

### What it actually is

Trillions of transparent crustaceans are each snapping a claw fast enough to tear a cavitation bubble in the water. When each bubble collapses it gives off a crack "as loud as a gunshot at close range" and a pinprick of heat. Earth's snapping shrimp do something similar, and their colonies are among the loudest natural sounds in the ocean. Here that sound fills a sea the size of a continent, evenly and without pause.

### Protecting your hearing

Underwater, sound doesn't reach you mainly through your ear canal. It travels through your skull, jaw, and teeth. That's why the description says it makes "the skull hum and the teeth ache." Ordinary earplugs won't do much and can be dangerous, since they can trap air and interfere with equalizing your ears.

What helps:

- **A helmet or full-face mask with a thick, well-fitting insulated hood**, which dampens some bone conduction. A commercial-style hard helmet with acoustic padding is best.
- **Strict limits on exposure time.** Treat this like working next to industrial machinery. Keep dives short, take long breaks topside, and track your total hours.
- **Watching for warning signs:** ringing that persists topside, muffled hearing, ear fullness, or dizziness. If they appear, stop diving for the day, or for the trip.
- **Keeping away from anything that concentrates the noise.** Above all, that means the pump hall.

### Communicating without sound

You can't talk, shout, or use voice communication gear. Underwater acoustic modems will likely drown in the static. Options:

- **Hand signals.** Agree on an extended vocabulary before you go and practice it until it's automatic.
- **Slates and wet notes.**
- **Light signals.** The water is extraordinarily clear, so a strobe or signal torch will be visible over great distances. Agree on a light code: *come to me, emergency, ascending, I'm fine*.
- **Tether line signals.** A physical line between divers, or between a diver and the surface, can carry pull signals. Ice divers rely on this, and it's one of the few channels here that doesn't depend on hearing or line of sight.
- Stay within **arm's reach** of your buddy whenever you're doing anything that matters. You can't call for help.

### The psychological side

Within an hour or two, the roar stops feeling like sound and becomes "the medium itself." That's adaptation, and it's partly a gift: it lets you function. It also means you've stopped noticing something that's still affecting you, including your hearing, fatigue, and concentration. Plan your breaks by the clock, not by how you feel.

Then there's the return. Coming back into true quiet "feels like falling." Expect:

- A sense of vertigo or bottomlessness.
- Heightened awareness of your own heartbeat and breath, which you haven't heard in hours and which may suddenly seem enormous.
- Anxiety or disorientation.

This is a good place to be deliberate. Bring a **small white-noise or brown-noise device** for the surface shelter so you can ease down from the roar instead of dropping straight into silence. Build in quiet recovery time after each dive, and after the whole trip. Don't schedule anything important for the day you come home.
