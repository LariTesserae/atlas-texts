# Preparing for The Stair

## The Short Version

You are going to a place that is very high, very bright, very wet, very cold at night, very quiet, and never still. Everything you bring and everything you do must account for all six at once. The Stair is not hostile. The people seem patient and the animals indifferent. But the place asks something of everyone in it. The ground goes down. If you stay, you will be expected to help carry. Come fit, come humble, come with your eyes protected, and don't set anything down that you want to find again.

---

## 1. Before You Go: Your Body

### Altitude

The Stair is above the treeline, in a cirque ringed by permanent snow, with air thin enough to deaden sound and turn the sky violet. That suggests something like 4,500–5,500 meters, possibly higher. This is the single greatest physical risk of the trip, and multiverse travel makes it worse. Walking up a mountain acclimatizes you gradually. Stepping through a threshold does not.

- **Acclimatize first, somewhere safe.** Spend one to two weeks at progressively higher elevations in your home world before you go: a few days around 2,500 m, then 3,500 m, then as high as you can reasonably reach. If you can arrange a staged crossing, arriving first at a lower valley in the Stair's world and walking up, do that. It is by far the better option.
- **Consider acetazolamide** after talking to a physician who knows altitude medicine. Start it before crossing. It tingles your fingers and makes carbonated drinks taste flat. Neither will matter to you there.
- **Learn the warning signs cold.** Headache, nausea, and poor sleep are ordinary altitude sickness. Stumbling, confusion, and an inability to walk heel-to-toe in a straight line mean HACE (cerebral edema). Breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, and pink froth mean HAPE (pulmonary edema). Both can kill within hours. The only reliable cure is descent. Before you go, know exactly how you would descend. Is there a route out of the cirque, or is your return threshold your only exit? If it's the threshold, make sure you can reach it quickly, ill, at night, on ice.
- **Carry supplemental oxygen or a portable hyperbaric bag** if at all possible. Nobody there will have either.

### Conditioning

You will almost certainly carry stone. It is the central act of the town, a third of the population is doing it at any hour, and a visitor who only watches will be conspicuous in a way that matters here. So train for it:

- Weighted pack carries uphill on uneven ground, 20–30 kg, for an hour at a time.
- Grip and forearm strength. The stones are wet, often glazed, and you will be lifting them with numb fingers.
- Neck strength, if you intend to try the forehead sling (tumpline). The locals carry with a braided goat-hair strap across the forehead. It is efficient, but to an untrained neck it is agony. Practice with a real tumpline for weeks beforehand, or accept that you'll carry smaller loads in your arms.
- Balance and ankle stability. Practice on scree, boulder fields, and talus. Every surface in the town is loose rock that shifts underfoot, and some of it is moving on its own.

### Eyes

Check with an ophthalmologist before going if you have any history of light sensitivity, migraine, or **photosensitive epilepsy**. The midday Stair is a field of flickering signals: thousands of mirror flashes, plus mica faces tilting in and out of the sun across the entire tongue. For most people this will be beautiful. For some it may be a trigger. If you're at risk, plan to spend the brightest hours indoors.

## 2. What to Pack

### Eye Protection: the Most Important Thing You Bring

The old people there have milky eyes. That is not age alone. It is a lifetime of reflected ultraviolet at altitude, and the damage accumulates. Snow blindness (photokeratitis) can set in within hours. It feels like sand ground into your eyes, you may be effectively blind for a day or two, and in a place where you navigate by sight and communicate by light, that is disastrous.

- **Glacier glasses, category 4**, with full side shields. Light is coming from everywhere: above, below, and all around the snow rim. Ordinary sunglasses leave your eyes exposed from the sides and beneath.
- **A second pair.** Things fall through gaps in the rubble and don't come back.
- **A dim-light pair or clear goggles** for fog, squalls, and dusk.
- Consider having a bone or bark slit-visor made for you locally, the kind the residents wear, as a courtesy and a backup. Slit goggles work surprisingly well.
- Lubricating eye drops. The air is thin and dry above the damp ground.

### Skin

- SPF 50+ sunscreen, reapplied constantly, including **under your chin, inside your nostrils, and behind your ears**. The light comes up off the snow and the wet stone.
- SPF lip balm. Your lips will burn and crack within a day otherwise.
- A brimmed hat plus a buff or neck gaiter that can cover your lower face.

### Clothing: Layers for Two Climates a Day

The Stair runs through freeze-thaw several times a day. Noon is blazing sun, and wet from sweat and meltwater. Night is hard frost that glazes everything. Afternoons bring fog that soaks you. Squalls arrive without warning.

- **No cotton anywhere.** Wet cotton at freezing temperatures is how people die.
- Merino or synthetic base layers, several sets. Something will always be drying.
- A fleece or light synthetic insulating mid-layer.
- A **fully waterproof, breathable shell**, jacket and trousers. Fog condenses on everything, and you'll kneel on weeping stone.
- A warm puffy jacket for dusk and night. Synthetic is better than down, because down fails when damp and everything here is damp.
- Insulated trousers or heavy long underwear for sleeping.
- Wool socks, many pairs, plus a vapor-barrier liner if you know how to use one.

### Hands

- **Heavy-duty, waterproof work gloves** for handling stone. The rock is sharp, wet, cold, and full of mica flakes. You'll wear through ordinary gloves in days, so bring spares.
- Thin liner gloves for fine tasks, like handling a mirror.
- Warm mittens for night.
- A small first-aid kit specifically for hands: tape, blister care, antiseptic. Stone carrying destroys hands. The grinding of stones against one another is also a crushing hazard (see Hazards).

### Feet

- **Stiff, waterproof mountaineering boots with high ankle support.** The ground shifts and heaves, and holes open up. Trail runners will get your ankle broken.
- Gaiters, to keep grit and meltwater out.
- **Microspikes or light traction devices** for mornings, when every lane is clear ice. Full crampons are probably overkill and awkward on rubble. They would also scrape the lichen off stones that a family has kept for twenty generations. Wear spikes in the lanes, and take them off indoors and on anyone's wall.
- Soft indoor footwear, such as felt booties. Houses are small, close, and lived-in.

### Sleep

- A sleeping bag rated to about −15 °C or lower, with a waterproof bivy cover.
- A closed-cell foam pad under an inflatable one. The floor is cold stone over ice, and it is moving.
- Earplugs are optional. You won't need them against noise. Some people may find the silence harder than any sound.

### Light

- A headlamp **with a red-light mode and a way to fully cover it.** See Communication: casual light at night may be meaningful or rude here. Use it only indoors or in an emergency.
- Spare batteries, kept warm inside your clothing. Cold kills batteries.

### Communication Gear

- **A signal mirror**, the kind with a sighting hole in the center that lets you aim a flash. This is how the town talks. Without one you are mute beyond arm's length.
- A spare. Mirrors break.
- A small notebook and pencil (pens freeze). Use it to record flash patterns as you learn them, and for any written communication that might work.
- A **whistle** may carry better than your voice, though probably not far. It is useful mainly as a last resort.

### Water and Food

- A water filter or purification tablets. The drip-trough water that sweats out of the house walls is clear and probably clean, being glacial melt filtered through stone. Still, you don't know what the goats and pikas have been doing on those walls.
- **Never drink the tarn water.** It is milk-green with rock flour, finely ground stone suspended in the water, and it will wreck your gut.
- An insulated bottle, kept inside your jacket at night. Otherwise it will be a block of ice by dawn.
- **High-calorie, low-bulk food** for your whole stay, plus a margin. Carrying stone at altitude in the cold burns astonishing amounts of energy, possibly 5,000+ calories a day. The town has goats and pikas, lichen, moss, and sedge, and it cannot spare much. Do not count on being fed.
- Your own stove and fuel if you want hot food. Their fuel is goat dung, precious in a place with no wood, and you shouldn't burn theirs.
- Electrolyte packets. You'll lose fluid fast to the thin, dry air and to labor, and you won't feel thirsty.

### Medical Kit

Beyond standard supplies:
- Altitude medications (acetazolamide; dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies, with a prescription and instructions).
- Splinting materials for ankles and fingers.
- Eye-injury supplies: sterile saline, eye patches, and anesthetic drops if you can get them prescribed.
- Treatment for hypothermia and frostbite, including chemical heat packs.
- Treatment for GI upset.
- Any antibiotics a travel doctor recommends. Rodents can carry disease: in our world, pikas' relatives are associated with plague and tularemia. Don't handle the pikas. Avoid eating them unless the meat has been thoroughly cooked.

### Gifts

Think carefully here. The obvious gift, a good glass mirror or a polished steel one, would be extraordinarily valuable. It would be far better than wet slate or mica. It might also shift status, change who can signal whom, or unsettle something you don't understand. Small, practical gifts are safer: good wool socks, a sturdy strap, needles, a sharp knife. Ask someone you trust before giving anything that touches on light.

## 3. Arrival

### Mark Your Way Home

If your return point is anywhere on the rock glacier, **it is moving.** A body length a day, downhill toward the lip. After a week it could be several meters from where you left it, perhaps under someone's rebuilt wall. After long enough, it goes into the tarn.

- If you can, arrive and depart from the **headwall**, the solid living rock at the top of the tongue, or the cirque rim. These don't move.
- If you must use a point on the tongue, triangulate it against fixed features (distinctive headwall rock, peaks on the rim) and recalculate daily.
- Do not rely on "the third house from the corner." The houses hold position through constant labor, but not perfectly, and a household that falls ill will drift.

### Don't Put Anything Down

This is the rule most visitors break. Set your pack on a stone and wander off for an hour, and it has moved. Leave it overnight, and it's glazed in ice, a body length downhill, and possibly lodged in a gap that has since closed. Small objects fall into the voids and are simply gone, down in the wet dark where the dippers swim. Carry everything, or hang it from something attached to a maintained house wall, with permission.

### The First Hours

Expect a headache and breathlessness. Walk slowly and sit often. You will want to look at everything, and you should. But look *through your glasses*, never directly at the snowfields, and never unprotected at noon.

## 4. Hazards

**The ground.**
- Stones heave upward without warning when water freezes beneath them.
- Holes collapse open when ice melts out. A lane can change shape in an afternoon.
- Walls split "between one breath and the next." Don't lean on walls, and don't sit against them.
- **Stones grind past one another.** Never put fingers, hands, or feet into the gaps between large blocks, and never sleep with a limb wedged between stones. The movement is slow, but it is the movement of a glacier, and it does not stop.
- Watch where the locals step and step there. They have been reading this ground since childhood.

**The lip.** The downhill front is a steep face of loose rubble twice a person's height, dropping into a near-freezing tarn. Stones tip over it constantly. Stay well back. Falling into water at that temperature means cold shock within seconds and incapacitation within minutes. Even if you avoid the lip itself, the edge is unstable.

**Ice.** Every morning the entire town is sheathed in clear glaze. Wear your spikes. Move like the elderly do: slowly, counting, feeling each step.

**Silent avalanches.** You will see them and not hear them. Look up often, especially in warm afternoons and after squalls. Know which parts of the rim have loaded snowfields above them.

**Squalls.** A violet sky can turn to snow and back within the hour. Always have your shell and warm layer on your body or in your hands, never in a pack you've left somewhere.

**Silence as a hazard.** A shout dies within ten paces, so **you cannot call for help.** If you fall into a hole, twist an ankle in a back lane, or get trapped, no one will hear you. This means:
- Never go anywhere alone until you can flash for help.
- Tell someone where you're going, face to face.
- At night, when there is no sun to flash with, stay indoors.

**Light as a hazard.** Don't flash your mirror into anyone's eyes, especially not an elder's already-damaged ones. Be aware that a careless reflection off your gear, glasses, or phone screen might be read as a signal, or taken as rude.

**Cold.** The daily cycle means you'll be sweating at noon and freezing at dusk. Change out of wet base layers before the sun drops. Don't wait until you're cold.

**Fumes.** Houses are small, sealed against cold, and lit by fat lamps. Watch for headache or drowsiness beyond what the altitude explains, and crack a door if you can.

## 5. Communication

### Speech

Talking happens face to face, faces nearly touching, in a murmur, often with hands laid on each other's forearms to feel the voice through the skin. For many visitors this will feel startlingly intimate. It is not intimacy. It is simply how speech works here, the only way it *can* work. Prepare yourself for it.

- Keep your breath fresh. You'll be inches from people.
- Learn to let someone take your forearm without flinching, and learn to offer yours.
- Speak quietly and slowly. Raising your voice gains you nothing and may look aggressive.
- Gesture more than you're used to. Gestures carry farther than words.
- If you have hearing loss, the Stair will be unusually hard for you, but touch-speech may help. Tell people early.

### Light

Everything beyond arm's length is said in mirror flashes: short and long, doubled and tripled, bounced off snow or sky. Children learn it before they learn to talk. Your options:

- **Find a child to teach you.** Children will be the most fluent, the most patient with an incompetent adult, and possibly the most delighted.
- Learn the essentials first: *help*, *danger*, *stop*, *yes*, *no*, *come here*, *I'm lost*, and whatever sign means *hole* or *moving stone*.
- Practice aiming. A mirror's flash is very hard to direct at a specific person without training. Your sighting-hole mirror will help.
- Remember that **flashing only works while the sun is up.** Dusk is when the town falls mute. If you need to say something important, say it before the sun drops behind the western rim.
- Watch for the flashes that concern you. Warnings go out constantly: a wall splitting, a hole opening, a slide coming. Early in your stay, someone may be flashing a warning straight at you. Look up often, and learn which flash means *look at me*.

### Night

At night the town goes dark and silent. Families sit close indoors. Don't wander. Don't shine lights around outside. Whether a light at night would read as a signal, an alarm, or merely a strange intrusion, it's a disturbance. If you must go out, use red light, cupped low.

## 6. Etiquette and Ethics

### Height Is Status, and Stones Are Houses

Every stone in every wall belongs to a household and is part of that household's continual climb. **Never take a stone from any wall**, even a fallen-looking one, and even to sit on or to use as a step. You may be dismantling someone's standing. Never move a stone unless someone has asked you to and shown you where it goes.

Some stones carry lichen that a family has kept in its walls for twenty generations. They know those faces the way they know their grandmothers'. Don't scrape, lean on, or step on lichened stones. Think of them as family portraits. If someone shows you theirs, take it as an honor.

### Carrying

If you stay more than a day, you'll likely be hosted by a household, and you'll be taking up room on their floor. That room drifts downhill unless someone carries. Offer to help. Let them show you which stones come from where and go where. There is a right way, and you won't know it. Start small. Nobody expects a lowlander to carry like a native, but people will notice whether you try.

Be honest about your limits. An altitude-sick guest collapsing under a load is a burden, not a help.

### Drifting

"They're drifting" is how people speak of decline. Be sensitive to it. If your hosts are an old or ailing household whose house is sliding past its neighbors, your labor may matter more than you realize. It may also be a matter of pride. Ask gently, face to face.

### The Lower Quarter

The unmaintained houses near the lip hold the dead, the abandoned, and the departed. People speak of this plainly, without sorrow, but don't assume that means it's open to tourists. Treat it as a graveyard. Don't pick through it, don't take souvenirs, and don't hang around to watch stones go over unless someone invites you. Watching family stones fall is something some people do and most don't. It's theirs.

### Plants

On the headwall grow cushion plants, tight domes that may be centuries old and flower only briefly. The people climb up to look at them and do not pick them. Neither should you. Don't step on them either. Walk only on bare rock up there.

### Snow-Fleas

The people read the drifting black patterns of springtails on the snow for weather, luck, and marriage. If you're shown this, don't offer your own interpretations, and don't walk through the patches.

### Animals

- **Pikas** go wherever they like, including through the house. Let them. Don't handle them.
- **Goats** will ride the walls and possibly stand on your gear. They are part of the household economy (milk, hair, fuel). Don't feed them anything you brought.
- **Dippers** may surface through the floor of the house you're sleeping in, dripping and calm. This is normal. Don't scream, not that anyone would hear. Let it be.
- **Ravens** watch from the headwall. The old people say they flash too. Be courteous. Assume nothing.

### Water

Each house fills its own soapstone drip-trough from its own north wall. Ask before drinking. Water is plentiful here, but it still belongs to the house.

## 7. Daily Rhythm: What to Expect

**Dawn.** Everything is glazed in clear ice. The only sounds are the tinkle of icicles as the first carriers knock them from doorways, then quiet. Pale ice-crawlers walk slowly across the frozen stones. It is intensely cold. Put on your spikes before you step outside.

**Morning.** The sun hits the snowfields and the light builds fast. Glasses on. The ice begins to weep. Carrying begins. The first flashes cross the town.

**Noon.** Maximum brilliance. The sky is near-violet, and the sun is a small white hole in it. Shadows turn pale blue. The whole bowl glitters in slow motion, and thousands of mirror signals flicker between lanes. Meltwater runs under everything, and you'll hear it close at hand if you put your ear near a gap. It's hot in the sun and freezing in the shade. This is the most dangerous time for your eyes and skin, and the most beautiful. If the glare and flicker overwhelm you, go indoors and rest. There's no shame in it.

**Afternoon.** Fog climbs up from the tarn and wanders through the lower lanes, beading on your eyelashes. It burns off, then returns. Squalls may come over the rim. Things refreeze and remelt. Holes open, and stones heave.

**Dusk.** The sun drops. The snowfields go pink, then gray. The flashing stops. The carriers set down their stones, and everything goes still except the ground.

**Night.** Indoors with the fat lamps and felts, close to your hosts. This is when you'll feel it most: a faint, steady slide of the floor beneath you toward the lip. Some people find it lulling, like a ship. Some find it deeply disturbing, and may feel something like seasickness or a quiet dread. You might hear a pika's thin *eenk* from nowhere, then nothing. Then, from very far down, a single soft *plock*.

## 8. The Psychological Side

Prepare for these as seriously as you prepare for the altitude.

**The silence.** Coming from a noisy world, many people find extreme quiet uncomfortable. They notice tinnitus they never knew they had, their own heartbeat, a sense of pressure. Worse, you'll be surrounded by enormous activity that makes almost no sound: hundreds of silent birds, silent avalanches, crowds of people working without a word. It can feel dreamlike or unreal. Know that this is coming. Some visitors report it becomes restful after a few days.

**Isolation.** Until you learn to flash, you can talk only to whoever is within touching distance. That is a very small world. It may make you lonely, or it may make every conversation feel unusually important.

**Impermanence.** Everything here is passing through: the stone, the houses, the lichen, the dead. The town stays the same shape only because people never stop working. Some visitors find this bleak. Many find it clarifying. You may catch yourself thinking about your own life in its terms: what you're carrying up, what you're letting drift. Let the thought come. Don't impose it on your hosts. For them this isn't a metaphor. It's the ground.

**The motion.** The sense that nothing underfoot is fixed can quietly unsettle you. Some people develop a kind of land-sickness, where they keep checking whether objects have moved. They usually have. Accept it early.

## 9. Leaving

- Leave well before your food runs short, and before any altitude symptoms worsen.
- Leave nothing behind. Anything you drop will ride the tongue down and eventually go over the lip into the tarn. You'd be adding something foreign to the stones of other people's grandmothers.
- Recalculate the position of your return point if it's on the tongue.
- Say goodbye face to face, forearm to forearm, while there's still enough light that people can flash the news of your leaving to those you can't reach.
- If you helped carry, the house you stayed in is a little higher than it would have been. That's the only thing you should leave.

---

## One Last Thing

If someone murmurs it to you, face nearly touching yours, hands on your forearms, *the ground goes down; we go up; that is where we live*, understand that you've been told something true, and probably told it kindly. You've also been told what's expected of anyone who wants to stay.
