# Preparing for the Blanched Steps

## A Traveler's Briefing

The Blanched Steps are not dangerous the way a war zone or a predator-rich jungle is. Nothing here wants to hurt you. The dangers are quieter: the light, the air, the edges, the cold that arrives every night, and the risk that you will damage something that took centuries to grow. The place is patient and absorbent, and it rewards travelers who are the same. Most of what follows is about how to be a careful, quiet guest in a place that is slowly and deliberately growing.

---

## Part One: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before packing, it helps to absorb a few facts about the Steps.

**It is very high.** The sky is violet at the zenith, shadows are pitch black, and you can hear your breath crackle. All of this points to extreme altitude, likely comparable to Earth's higher Andean or Himalayan plateaus, or beyond. That single fact drives much of your preparation: breathing, sun, cold, hydration, and how fast you can safely move.

**It is a desert that happens to be wet.** It almost never rains, and there is no running water. Everything is soaked every morning anyway, because the cloud rises and drenches it each night. You will be both dehydrated and damp, often at the same time.

**It is a built place.** The lushness is partly engineered by machines that are still running and still maintained by something you will not see. You are not exploring ruins. You are walking through someone's garden while they are out of the room, or possibly while they are in it and simply not showing themselves.

**It is alive and moving.** The rock is rising, and the plants, the machines, and the valley are all growing together. Nothing you do here should interrupt that.

## Part Two: The Light (Your Most Serious Hazard)

Most visitors underestimate this.

### Why it's worse than it sounds

At high altitude under a thin atmosphere, ultraviolet radiation is dramatically stronger than at sea level. The Steps compound this in a way few places do. **The plants are mirrors.** They are furred white, waxed to a gloss, or filmed in metallic sheen, and they are wet for half the day. Light comes at you from below and from the sides as much as from above. The description compares a thicket at noon to "a heap of polished cutlery." Take that literally.

A sunhat protects you from a sun that is overhead. It does nothing against a sun that is reflecting up off every leaf around your ankles.

### Eye protection

This matters more than any other item on your list. Snow blindness (photokeratitis), a sunburn of the cornea, is a real and likely risk here. It is intensely painful and can leave you temporarily blind in a place full of head-high drops.

- **Glacier glasses, category 4 lenses**, with wraparound frames and **side and bottom shields**. Ordinary sunglasses let reflected light in underneath and around the edges.
- **A spare pair.** Losing or breaking your only glasses here is an emergency.
- **Goggles as a backup**, ideally with a tinted lens.
- **Lubricating eye drops.** The dry, thin air and constant glare will leave your eyes gritty and sore.
- **An eye mask for sleeping and resting.** The dawn white-out can be bright even through closed lids.

Know the symptoms of photokeratitis: a gritty, sandy feeling, tearing, and light sensitivity that often appear **hours after exposure**, typically that evening. If you notice them, rest your eyes completely, cover them with cool damp cloths, and do not travel until they recover.

### Skin protection

- **High-SPF broad-spectrum sunscreen**, reapplied often. Apply it to places you would never think of: **under your chin, under your nose and inside the rims of your nostrils, under your earlobes, the underside of your jaw, the backs of your hands, and your wrists.** Reflected light burns these areas first.
- **SPF lip balm.** Lips burn and crack very quickly at altitude.
- **A neck gaiter or buff** that can be pulled up over your lower face. Choose a light, breathable, UV-rated fabric.
- **Long sleeves, long trousers, and thin gloves**, all UV-rated. This also protects you from plant hairs and sharp rock (see below).
- **A wide-brimmed hat with a chin strap.** Wind gusts can take it over a ledge.

### Managing the day around light

- **At arrival, stand still.** Close your eyes, let them adjust, and let the place "develop" before you move. Walking through the initial white-out is how people step off ledges.
- **Rest through the harshest noon hours.** Find shadow and stay there. This is also a good time to eat, rest your eyes, and watch the valley.
- **Be wary of shadows.** They are black and sharp-edged, and your eyes won't adjust quickly between bright rock and shadow. A shadowed step can hide a drop, a crevice, or a shard. Slow down every time you cross from light into shadow.
- **Consider matte clothing.** You don't want your own gear adding glare. Keep one brightly colored, non-reflective item, such as a hat or bandana, so companions can pick you out against the silver-green.

### For photographers

The dynamic range will defeat most cameras. The highlights blow out and the shadows go completely black. Bring neutral density and polarizing filters, bracket your exposures, and accept that the place may not photograph the way it feels.

## Part Three: Altitude and Breathing

### Acclimatization

You arrive by climbing up out of the cloud bank on the lower slopes, which gives you a natural chance to go slowly. Take it.

- **Spend at least a day or two at lower elevation**, near or just above the cloud line, before moving up into the basin.
- **Ascend gradually.** Sleep lower than the highest point you reach during the day when you can.
- **If you have access to altitude medication** such as acetazolamide, talk to a physician beforehand about whether it suits you.
- **Know the warning signs.** Headache, nausea, loss of appetite, and poor sleep indicate mild altitude sickness. **Confusion, loss of coordination, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, or extreme fatigue indicate something serious** (HACE or HAPE). The treatment is to descend. Conveniently, down here always means **east**, toward the cloud sea. Know your descent route before you need it.

### Exertion

The terrain is a staircase, which is about the most exhausting shape a landscape can take at altitude. You will be stepping up and down knee-high and head-high ledges constantly. Expect to cover a fraction of the distance you would on flat ground. **A mile here might take hours.** Plan accordingly, and don't set ambitious daily goals.

### Physical preparation

Before you go, train for **step-ups, lunges, and scrambling**. Build strength in your quads, glutes, and core, and do cardiovascular work. Practice lowering yourself down ledges using your hands. Your knees will take a beating, so strengthen what supports them.

## Part Four: The Terrain

### What makes it hazardous

- **Sheer little drops everywhere.** They are knee-high to head-high, with crisp edges and flat faces. None of them is individually a cliff, but falling off a head-high ledge onto sharp shards can break bones.
- **Moss that hides edges.** The moss is a hand's width thick and cushions like a mattress. It also softens the visual line of a ledge, so you may not see exactly where the step ends.
- **Slick rock under the vegetation.** Beneath the plants, the rock is "always cool and slick." Moss can slide off the rock under your weight.
- **Fault lines and crevices.** Water disappears downward into them. Some may be concealed under growth. Probe before trusting ground that looks unusually lush, soft, or hollow.
- **Sharp scree.** The shards are forearm-length, fresh-fractured, and rigid. They are not gentle gravel. They cut.
- **Fresh fracture faces.** The rock is still rising and splitting. New scree may be unstable.
- **Column tops.** The snapped-off organ pipe columns form "staircases leading nowhere." They are tempting to climb for the view, but they may be unstable and are difficult to descend.

### Footwear and gear

- **Sturdy boots with grippy, fairly soft rubber soles** for friction on smooth, wet stone. Waterproof, because the moss seeps.
- **Gaiters**, to keep seep water and sharp fragments out of your boots.
- **Durable gloves** with good grip and cut resistance. You will be using your hands constantly on sharp edges.
- **Knee protection** such as light pads or supportive sleeves, if your knees are vulnerable.
- **Trekking poles**, used thoughtfully. They help enormously on the descents but can puncture moss beds and cushion plants. Place tips on bare rock wherever you can. Rubber tip covers help on stone.
- **A short length of rope or webbing** for lowering packs down ledges or offering a handline to a companion.
- **A small first aid kit weighted toward lacerations, sprains, and fractures**: wound closure strips, irrigation syringe, splint, compression wrap, and plenty of blister care.

### Moving well

- **Face into the rock when descending taller steps**, like going down a ladder.
- **Test every foothold** on moss before committing weight.
- **Move slowly in the early morning**, when everything is wettest.
- **Never move in the cloud.** More on this below.

## Part Five: Water

There are no streams, rivers, or springs. Plan your water carefully.

### Sources, in rough order of trust

1. **What you carry in.** Bring enough to cover a margin of safety, especially for the first day and the exit.
2. **What you harvest yourself.** This is the most elegant option, and it follows the valley's own logic. **Bring a small, lightweight fog net**, such as a square of fine mesh on a collapsible frame, and set it up at your camp before the cloud rises. By morning you'll have water to funnel into a bottle. You can also collect condensation by leaving a tarp or groundsheet angled to drain into a container.
3. **Column-top pools.** Each holds a cupful of clear water, and there are thousands. It is likely rainwater and condensed fog in stone, which is probably quite clean. **Treat it anyway.** Take from many pools lightly rather than draining a few, since these may matter to the birds, the insects, or the grazer.
4. **The pipe spouts.** This water has passed through the fog nets, which host mysterious green threads that appear and vanish daily, and through miles of iron pipe running near high-voltage lines. It feeds the plants, and the valley's whole design seems to depend on it. **Avoid it unless you have no other choice.** It may be perfectly fine. It is also the valley's lifeblood and part of a system you don't understand.
5. **Bromeliad cups. Do not drink from these.** They are tiny ecosystems. The bird feeds on something living in them. Leave them alone.

### Treatment

Bring a filter **and** a chemical or UV purifier. Boiling works but costs fuel, which is precious at altitude, where water boils at a lower temperature and cooking takes longer.

### Hydration

Dry, thin air at altitude pulls moisture out of you with every breath. You will lose more water than you realize, even though everything around you is damp. Drink steadily, and watch your urine color.

## Part Six: The Cloud and the Night

This is the second most important thing to plan for.

### What happens

Every evening the cloud sea rises from below and fills the basin to a depth of a few hundred feet. It is **cold, silent, and total**. It covers everything, including you, unless you are camped high. By midnight only the top of the tower and the highest pylons stand above it. It withdraws at dawn, leaving everything beaded and dripping.

### The core rule

**Once the cloud begins to rise, stop moving.** In zero visibility, on terrain made of sheer knee- and head-high drops and concealed crevices, walking is how people get badly hurt. Choose your camp in the late afternoon, set it up in the golden-to-lilac evening light, and be settled by the time the cloud reaches you.

### Choosing where to sleep

You have two options:

**Camp above the cloud line.** Some of the higher steps toward the western edge or on the tallest outcrops may stay above the cloud. You'd have a dry night under an astonishing sky: stars "very many and very sharp," the white sea below, and the tower's top and the pylons rising out of it with their faint blue corona. It will be very cold, exposed, and possibly windy. It may also put you closer to the smoking western peaks (see below). It is the more beautiful and probably the drier option.

**Camp in the basin and accept the cloud.** You will be inside a cold, saturated fog all night. Everything not sealed will be soaked by morning. This is manageable with the right gear, and it may be the more intimate experience: you are inside the valley's drinking.

Either way:

- **Don't camp directly on the moss.** It is a sponge, and your weight will squeeze water up into your bedding and crush centuries of growth. Find bare rock, a flat column top, or a clean ledge.
- **Don't camp under the pylon cables** (see Part Eight).
- **Don't camp in fault lines or depressions**, where cold air, water, and possibly volcanic gases collect.
- **Mark your camp clearly before dark** so you can find it if you step a few yards away.

### Sleeping gear

- **Synthetic insulation, not down.** Down collapses when wet, and it will get wet. If you must bring down, use a waterproof or water-resistant shell and keep it inside a bivy.
- **A waterproof bivy sack or a well-sealed tent**, with some attention to condensation.
- **A closed-cell foam pad**, possibly paired with an inflatable. Insulation from cold, wet rock is essential.
- **Dry sleep clothes in a waterproof bag, used only for sleeping.** Wake up, change back into your damp day clothes, and keep the sleep set dry. This is a mountaineering habit, and it will matter here.
- **A warm hat and gloves** for the night.
- **Dry bags for everything**, especially electronics, food, and your spare eyewear.

### Cold

Expect nights near or below freezing, with wet fog wicking heat from you. Hypothermia is a real risk if you get soaked. Layer well:

- A moisture-wicking base layer
- A synthetic or wool insulating mid-layer
- A breathable waterproof shell
- A warm puffy jacket (synthetic) for camp

## Part Seven: The Silence

This will affect you more than you expect.

### Practical implications

**Your voice carries about twenty yards.** In a thicket, or around a column cluster, you can lose a companion within seconds and be unable to call them back.

- **Travel in pairs or small groups, and stay within sight of one another.** Agree on this before you set out.
- **Carry a whistle.** A high, sharp tone may carry farther than a voice, though the moss absorbs it too. Test it on arrival to learn its range.
- **Bring handheld radios** if they work in this world. Test them early. The electrical infrastructure may cause interference.
- **Use visual signals.** A bright, non-reflective flag or bandana is easier to spot than a signaling mirror, whose flash will be lost among thousands of glittering leaves.
- **Agree on rally points** such as the tower, a specific pylon, or the railway trestle, in case you separate.
- **You might carry a small bell.** The shards ring clearly for a moment. A bell might carry a little farther, and its sound seems to cut through the padded quiet. Use it only for signaling and let the valley stay quiet otherwise.

### Psychological implications

After a few days, you will begin to hear your own body: your blood, your jaw, your breathing. Some people find this meditative. Others find it deeply unsettling, especially combined with the thin air (which can cause anxiety and poor sleep) and the sense of being in a place that is maintained by something unseen.

- **If you have had anxiety, panic attacks, or trouble with sensory deprivation**, think about this seriously before you go.
- **Bring earplugs anyway**, not for noise but for comfort, since some people sleep better with a sensation to focus on.
- **Consider bringing something to listen to** with headphones for moments when the silence becomes too much. Keep it inside your own ears. Don't broadcast sound into the valley.
- **Bring a journal.** Writing helps many people process strangeness and quiet.
- **Pay attention to your companions.** Check in with each other daily.

## Part Eight: The Machines

### Electrical hazards

The pylon cables carry live current, strong enough that you can feel it: hair lifting, a metallic taste, a blue corona at night. That is **high voltage**, and it is especially dangerous in wet conditions.

- **Do not climb the pylons.**
- **Do not linger under low spans.** Pass under quickly if you must, and take a detour where possible.
- **Do not touch the warm, iridescent leaves directly beneath the cables.** They are warm for a reason you don't understand, whether induced current, heat, or something else.
- **Be careful with metal objects** like trekking poles, pack frames, and tent poles near the lines.
- **Anyone with a pacemaker, implanted defibrillator, insulin pump, cochlear implant, or other electronic medical device should not visit**, or should seek serious medical advice first. The fields may be strong.
- **Expect electronics to misbehave near the lines.** Keep critical devices shielded and at a distance.

### The pipes, sheds, and railway

- **Don't disturb the pipes.** Don't open joints, block spouts, or lean on trestles. The sliding joints are carefully tended, and the system is visibly alive.
- **Don't enter the generator shed.** Its roof is carpeted in cushion plants that you would crush climbing on it. The machines inside may be dangerous in ways that aren't obvious, since slow does not mean weak.
- **Don't ride, push, or touch the ore car.** It has moved half a mile in a lifetime. Whatever it is doing, it is doing deliberately. The scratched dates on the trestle posts are someone's record-keeping.
- **Don't climb the tower.** It is iron, eight stories high, in the geographic center of the basin. It is the heart of the system. It is also an obvious lightning and electrical hazard.

### The caretaker

Someone or something maintains the machines. The iron doesn't rust. The fog-net threads vanish by evening. The joints get extended. You will likely not meet it.

- **Don't try to.** Don't set camera traps at the tower, don't stay up waiting beside the fog nets, and don't follow anything. You are a guest.
- **Don't take anything.** No rivets, no mesh, no shards of iron, no samples of the green threads.
- **Leave everything exactly as you found it.** A place that tends itself this carefully may notice when something is out of place.
- **If you do see something**, keep still and quiet. Let it continue its work. Observe from a distance, and let it decide whether the encounter continues.

## Part Nine: The Plants and Animals

### Protecting the ecosystem

This is an extraordinarily dense, slow-growing, fragile community. Cushion plants the size of sleeping cattle may be **centuries old**. A single footprint in the moss fills with seep and may take a long time to recover.

- **Walk on rock wherever possible**: bare fracture faces, clean ledges, scree. Choose your route to minimize contact with living growth.
- **Never step on cushion plants.** They look hard as stone, and they are both fragile and ancient.
- **Spread out rather than walking single-file** across unavoidable moss, so you don't wear a trail. On rock, single-file is fine.
- **Don't pick, collect, or press specimens.** Photograph and sketch instead.
- **Don't build fires.** There is no dead wood to spare, and every plant matters. Use a stove, and use it carefully on bare rock.
- **Pack out everything**, including human waste if you can manage it. The fractured rock channels water downward, and there is nowhere to dig. Bring waste bags.

### Biosecurity (for a multiverse traveler, this is essential)

You are carrying seeds, spores, bacteria, fungi, and possibly insects from other worlds. The Steps have no bare ground: every niche is filled, and it is hard to imagine a more vulnerable place for an invasive species to take hold.

**Before arrival:**
- **Clean all gear thoroughly**: boots (every lug), gaiters, pack straps, velcro, tent, poles, clothing seams and pockets.
- **Inspect for seeds and burrs.**
- **Bring no fresh food** with seeds or live organisms: no fruit, no fresh vegetables, no soil-contaminated anything.

**Before leaving:**
- **Clean everything again.** Don't carry the Steps' spores, the green threads, or silver plant hairs into another world.

### Personal safety with plants

- **Assume every plant could be toxic or irritating.** Don't eat anything, and wash your hands before eating.
- **The fine white hairs** on furred leaves may irritate skin, eyes, or lungs, much as some Earth plants' hairs do. Long sleeves and gloves help.
- **The metallic-sheened leaves** may be concentrating minerals or metals. Don't handle them bare-handed, and don't put them near your mouth.

### Animals

There is very little that moves, and nothing that bites. **You won't need insect repellent.**

- **The dun bird**: small and silent, hopping between branches near the pipe ends. Don't disturb its feeding in the bromeliads.
- **The pale moth** rising at dusk: lovely, and harmless. Avoid attracting it with lights. It rises just before the cloud, and bright lanterns may disrupt whatever it is doing.
- **The polished beetle**: look, don't collect.
- **The grazer**: a slender, pale, goat-sized, unafraid animal with large dark eyes. It will come within a few yards and look at you. **Let it look.** Don't approach, touch, or feed it. Its trust is a gift you don't want to spoil for the next visitor. Some visitors never see it at all, so if you do, consider yourself lucky. Don't try to follow it when the cloud rises. No one knows where it goes, and the cloud is exactly where you mustn't walk.

## Part Ten: Navigation

### What won't work

**GPS almost certainly won't function.** It depends on satellite networks that don't exist in this world. **Compasses may be unreliable** near iron pipes, the tower, the generators, and the high-voltage lines, and possibly near magnetic minerals in the fresh volcanic rock.

### What will work

The Steps have an unusually legible structure:

- **East is down**, toward the cloud sea. This is your exit and your emergency descent.
- **West is up**, toward the smoking peaks.
- **The tower is at the center.** At eight stories on the tallest column, it is likely visible from most of the basin.
- **The pylon line runs straight** across the valley, a perfect bearing line if you note its direction.
- **The railway trestle marks the western edge.**
- **The pipes radiate from the tower**, so following one back toward thicker iron leads to the center.
- **An altimeter** will help you track your position along the east-west slope.

### Good habits

- **Note landmarks at every stop.** From a high step, sketch or photograph the view in each direction.
- **Keep a written log** of bearings, times, and distinctive features.
- **Use temporary, removable markers** (a strip of bright tape tied to a rock, not a plant) if you need to mark a route. Remove them on your way back.
- **Don't build cairns or scratch marks on rock.** The only marks on the trestle posts should be the ones already there.

## Part Eleven: Other Hazards

### The western peaks

The raw peaks to the west "still smoke a little in their upper cracks." This is active volcanism.

- **Don't approach the smoking cracks.** Volcanic gases, including CO₂, sulfur dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide, can be deadly.
- **Be cautious in low depressions and deep fault lines**, especially on still nights. Heavy gases can settle and pool.
- **If you smell rotten eggs or sharp sulfur, or feel sudden headache, dizziness, or breathlessness beyond ordinary altitude effects, move up and away immediately.**
- **A portable gas detector** (CO₂ and H₂S) would be a sensible, small investment.

### Rising rock

The shelf is still lifting and splitting. New fracture faces appear yearly. You are unlikely to witness a significant split during a short visit, but:

- **Avoid camping directly at the foot of fresh, clean-white fracture faces** or on loose new scree.
- **If you feel a tremor, stop, crouch, and hold on** until it passes.

### Afternoon rain

It arrives as a brief hiss and lasts minutes. Keep your shell accessible. The rock is slickest immediately afterward.

## Part Twelve: Food and Fuel

There is nothing here you can or should eat. Bring everything.

- **Calorie-dense, lightweight food.** Altitude often suppresses appetite, so bring things you actually enjoy eating.
- **Salty snacks** to help with fluid balance.
- **Warm drinks** for the cold evenings. They help with morale.
- **A reliable stove that works at altitude**, with more fuel than you think you need. Liquid fuel stoves generally handle cold and altitude better than canisters.
- **A windscreen**, used on bare rock, far from vegetation.
- **Pack out all packaging and food scraps.**

## Part Thirteen: A Suggested Rhythm

The Steps have their own daily shape. Work with it rather than against it.

**Dawn: the white-out.** Stay put. Wear your glacier glasses before you open the tent. Let the cloud retreat and the valley ignite. Watch the dripping, then pack a dry-bag-organized camp while you wait for visibility.

**Mid-morning: travel.** This is the best window. The light is bright but not yet brutal, colors emerge, and the rock begins to dry on its upper surfaces. Watch your footing on the still-wet undersides.

**Noon: rest.** Find deep shadow. Eat, drink, rest your eyes, and watch the valley. Try watching a generator's rivet for a full minute until you're sure it has moved.

**Afternoon: travel again, lightly.** Keep your shell accessible for the brief rain. After it, pause and watch the plants "lift and gleam."

**Late afternoon: choose and set up camp.** Do this well before the gold light begins. Set your fog net.

**Evening: stillness.** Watch the gold, rose, and lilac. Watch the moths rise. Watch the blue corona begin along the cables. Eat dinner. Change into dry sleep clothes.

**Night: the cloud.** Don't move. Lie still. Listen to your heart and breath. Feel the valley drink.

### How long to stay

The Steps reward time. The strangeness only reveals itself if you stay: plants leaning toward cables, threads on the fog nets, the generators responding to the season. A visit of **several days to a week**, after acclimatization, lets you see those patterns without wearing out your supplies or your welcome.

Stay long enough to feel the patience, but not so long that you become part of what the caretaker has to tidy.

---

## Part Fourteen: Leaving

- **Start early in the day**, once the cloud has fully withdrawn below you, giving yourself maximum daylight for the descent.
- **Descend east, toward the cloud bank.** You will pass back through it. Expect grey, wet, low visibility, the opposite of your arrival. Move slowly, stay close to companions, and use your recorded bearings.
- **Clean your gear thoroughly** before stepping into another world.
- **Check your eyes and skin** for delayed sunburn or irritation.
- **Remove any temporary route markers** on the way out.

## A Final Word

The Blanched Steps are one of those rare places that ask something of you beyond physical readiness. The rock, the plants, and the machines are all rising together "patiently and without hurry, into the light." The best way to prepare is to become a little like them: slow, careful, and quiet.

Bring good glasses and warm, dry clothes. Keep your feet off the cushions and your hands off the machines. Don't walk in the cloud. And when you lie awake in that absorbent silence, listening to your own blood while the valley drinks around you, understand that the place will keep growing long after you leave. Make sure you leave nothing behind to interfere with that growth.
