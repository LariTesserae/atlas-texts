# Preparing to Visit the Floor of the Unbuilt Works

## First, the Intuition

Before the gear lists, understand what kind of place this is, because it changes how you prepare.

Most dangerous places are dangerous because they are hostile, chaotic, or empty. The Floor is none of these. It is **orderly**. It is a functioning system with a logic, and that logic is the logic of a workplace. Everything there has a job. Everything is counted. Things that fall out of rhythm are removed.

So the question isn't really "how do I survive the environment?" It's "how do I pass through a factory floor during working hours without being mistaken for a defect?"

The cold, the slurry, the arc-eels, and the crushing crowds will hurt you. But the thing most likely to end your visit permanently is standing out in the wrong way.

Consider what the text quietly implies. Strikers that drop out of the beat are visited by a tallyman and never strike again. New bolts turn up at the surface near where tallymen have recently been. No one has seen one appear. I would not assume those two facts are unrelated. **Plan as if things removed from the line become product.**

With that in mind, everything else follows.

## Part One: The Body

### Cold and Wet: The Cellar Problem

This is not arctic cold. It is worse in some ways, because it is *damp* cold that never lifts, with water rising into every footprint. Damp cold kills slowly. You won't notice it happening. You'll just get stupid, then clumsy, and on the Floor, clumsy means out of rhythm.

Prepare as you would for multi-day cold-water fieldwork or caving:

- **Insulated rubber chest waders or dry-suit bottoms.** They must be fully rubber or neoprene, with no metal buckles, clips, or shanks. This matters for electrical reasons (see below). Get a pair with a thick, rigid, *non-conductive* sole.
- **A proper layering system under the waders:** synthetic or wool base layer, a fleece mid layer, and nothing cotton anywhere. Cotton is how cellars kill people.
- **A waterproof, soot-resistant outer shell with a hood.** Choose a smooth fabric that sheds soot rather than a textured one that traps it. Black or dark gray, for reasons of blending in, not fashion.
- **Neoprene gloves under rubber gauntlets.** Your hands will be touching wet stone, wet shells, and occasionally the hides of animals you're pushing past.
- **Spare everything dry, in sealed bags.** Budget for a full change of base layers every "day," whatever a day turns out to be.
- **Chemical heat packs.** Many of them. Tuck them where your blood runs close to the surface: the armpits, the groin, the back of the neck.

The bellows-backs are warm on the exhale. Standing in the lee of a herd will feel wonderful. Don't linger. The midges swarm that warmth, and the bellows-backs eat the midges in the same breath. You do not want to be anywhere near the intake of a breath like that.

### Breathing: Soot, Vapor, and Iron

The air is thick with cooled steam that smells of iron and scorched hair, and black soot falls constantly from nowhere. Assume it's fine particulate and treat it like foundry air.

- **A half- or full-face respirator with P100 filters, plus spares.** Filters will clog faster than their rating suggests. Change them on a schedule, not when breathing gets hard, because by then you've been breathing around the seal.
- **A full-face mask or sealed anti-fog goggles.** Your breath condenses, and so does everyone else's. Without anti-fog treatment you'll be blind within minutes. Bring anti-fog wipes and a soft cloth kept in a sealed pocket.
- **Mind the vapor layers.** The brown-gray layers hang at head height and rise and fall with the synchronized breathing of the herds. You'll notice the air getting thicker on the plain's great exhale. It may help to breathe *with* the bellows-backs, inhaling when they inhale. This is your first lesson in the principle that governs the whole visit: moving with the rhythm is safer than moving against it.

### Hearing: The Thrum and the Warnings

The noise is continuous, layered, and felt in the sternum. Days of exposure will cause fatigue, headaches, and possibly hearing damage. But you *cannot* simply block it. Sound on the Floor arrives **before** its cause. That lag is the single most valuable warning system you have.

- **Musician's earplugs**, the flat-attenuation kind that lower volume without muffling detail. You need to hear the crack of an arc-eel discharge clearly, a fraction of a second before it happens.
- **Train your ears before you go.** Spend time in genuine industrial environments: a working foundry, a stamping plant, a steel mill. Learn to pick individual sounds out of a wall of noise. Learn what a rhythm sounds like when it's about to break.
- **Expect the thrum to get into your chest and stay.** Some visitors find their heartbeat drifting toward the herds' rhythm. This is probably harmless. It's also probably the beginning of something, so notice it.

### Fitness

You'll be pushing through crowds of large animals, walking on crunching snail shells and slick polished stone, and wading through slurry that grips your boots. Train for:

- **Endurance walking in mud** while carrying load.
- **Balance.** The treads of schist have "the dull sheen of a stair tread in an old public building," and they are always wet. Practice on wet, polished stone.
- **Keeping a steady, metronomic pace for hours.** This is the most important one, and it's covered in Part Three.

## Part Two: The Workforce (Who Will Hurt You, and How)

### Bellows-Backs: Crush Risk

They are the size of overturned boats, they stand in herds of thousands, and they are indifferent to you "the way crowds at a shift change are indifferent."

- **Never get between two of them.** When a herd shifts, the gaps close.
- **Move with the flow of the crowd, never against it.** If you have to cross a herd, go diagonally, drifting in their direction of travel.
- **Don't stand still near a stationary one.** The mineral film grows on the backs of animals that stand still too long, and your back counts.
- **They avoid the chimney-shadows.** That can make a shadow's edge a useful gap in a crowd, but read the section on shadows before you use this.

### Strikers: Keep Your Feet Out of Their Work

Knee-height, crab-heron things with a single massive mineralized hammer, striking exposed schist at a patient, absolutely regular stroke. They've flattened every outcrop in the basin over generations.

- **The striking surfaces are the treads, and the treads are where you'll want to stand**, because they're the only solid ground. Share them carefully. Stand clear of the arc of the hammer. It won't aim at you, but your foot is not its business and it won't adjust.
- **They're predictable.** Their tempo is regular, and you'll *hear* each stroke before it lands. This makes them one of the safest things on the Floor, as long as you respect the beat.
- **Never interfere with a striker's rhythm.** Don't bump one, block one, or startle one out of time. You don't want to be the reason a tallyman comes over.

### Smelt-Snails: The Floor Beneath the Floor

They carpet the wettest ground so densely that you walk on their shells.

- **The crunching is fine.** They "bear your weight without complaint."
- **The runoff is rust-red**, with nearly pure iron bloom coming out of them. It will stain everything. Plan for your gear to come back orange.
- **Don't drink or wash in the water.** It is saturated with iron and who-knows-what reduced at "no temperature anyone could measure." Bring every drop you'll drink.

### Arc-Eels: The Real Danger Underfoot

These are the hazard most likely to injure you physically. Long, flat, nearly transparent, living in the water sheets and the slurry. They discharge blue-white arcs between individuals across the surface, a hand's breadth tall. They're short-tempered and hunted by nothing.

**Insulation is everything:**

- **Rubber-soled, rubber-bodied footwear rated for electrical work**, like lineworker's boots or insulated overshoes worn over your waders. Get the highest dielectric rating you can find. Arcs a hand's breadth tall means your ankles are in range, so choose boots with high uppers.
- **No metal below the waist.** No zippers, no rivets, no buckles, no metal-shanked boots, no hiking poles with metal tips. If you must use a walking staff (and on this footing you probably should), make it fiberglass or wood.
- **Minimize metal everywhere.** You're walking on hundreds of feet of iron strata, beneath soot, among iron-bloom runoff. Everything conductive wants to be part of the circuit.

**Crossing a flat:**

This is where the sound-before-cause rule saves your life. You will *hear* the crack of a discharge before you see the flash. Learn the pattern:

1. Stand at the edge of the water sheet on solid tread.
2. Listen. The discharges come in travelling webs as schools move. Let one wave pass.
3. The lag gives you a brief window of knowledge. When you hear the crack, that discharge is *already committed*. What you need is the gap *after* the flash and before the next sound.
4. Step, step, step through the gap, in rhythm.

Practice this timing at home with a partner: they clap, and you step on the beat after, over and over, until it's reflexive.

Don't carry anything that looks like, smells like, or *is* a fishing implement. Don't touch the eels. Don't step on them deliberately. They're short-tempered.

### The Stalkers: Edge of Visibility

Long-legged, heavy-jawed, wading the flats at the edge of visibility. They take snails, strikers, and "occasionally the old and slow among the bellows-backs."

- **Don't be old and slow.** Don't limp, don't stagger, don't hurry and then stop.
- **Travel in the flow of a herd** when possible. The crowd is protection.
- **Keep your pace steady.** Predators everywhere key on irregular movement. Here, where everything moves in rhythm, irregularity will be especially visible.

### Carrion Flocks, Slurry-Worms, Midges

Mostly nuisances. The carrion flocks pick over the dead in *shifts*. If you see them gathering near you, take it very seriously: it means something near you is dying, and it might be you. The slurry-worms make the mud heave, so walk the treads wherever possible, or you may find the ground moving under your feet in ways that throw off your step.

### The Tallymen: Do Not Be Counted Out

This is the part to take most seriously.

They're small, dark, quick, with long fingers and heads that are mostly eye. They move in ones and twos, touching things. They seem to be *counting*. They remove whatever falls out of rhythm.

Your intuition should tell you several things:

1. **They will notice you.** You are a new thing on the floor. At some point, you will probably be touched. Don't flinch. Don't pull away. Don't break stride. Let the touch happen the way a bellows-back or a snail lets it happen. Think of it as an inspection, and pass it.

2. **Being counted is not the danger. Being counted *wrong* is.** Something in rhythm is being tallied as part of the work. Something out of rhythm is a defect. Your goal is to be tallied as present, working, and in time.

3. **Don't hide.** Hiding is a kind of stopping. Stopping is a kind of falling out of rhythm.

4. **Don't try to communicate.** Don't wave, don't speak to them, don't offer them anything. No tallyman has been observed eating. Offering food to an auditor is not a gesture that translates well.

5. **If you see a tallyman approach a striker that just fell out of beat, don't watch.** Look away, keep walking, and keep your rhythm. There's nothing you can do, and attention is a kind of stopping too.

6. **Never pick up the bolts.** Especially not the new ones rusting in the water sheets near where a tallymen has recently been. You don't know what they were. Taking product off the floor is theft in any factory. And if the bolts are what I think they might be, it's something worse than theft. Leave them.

## Part Three: The Rules, and How to Live Inside Them

### Rhythm Is Your Passport

This is the core preparation, and it's partly mental.

Before you go, **train yourself to walk in time**. Use a metronome. Start at a comfortable walking cadence and practice holding it for hours: over rough ground, while carrying weight, while tired, while distracted. Practice *shifting* tempo cleanly on a cue, without stumbling through a transition.

On the Floor:

- **Listen for the local beat of the strikers** and match your stride to it. Districts lock into a single beat for hours before falling apart. When a district falls into clatter, keep your own steady pace until a new beat emerges. Don't flail between rhythms.
- **Breathe with the bellows-backs.**
- **Step between eel discharges** in time.

You'll be surprised how quickly this becomes natural. That's part of the danger (see Aftereffects), but while you're there, it's your protection.

### The Whistle: Stop, Hold, Turn

This happens several times a "day." The long, falling note comes from no direction, and everything stops. Bellows-backs hold their breath. Strikers freeze mid-lift. Eels go dark. Snails pull in. For the length of a long exhale, there is only dripping and settling soot.

Then everything turns a quarter-rotation, the same direction, all at once, and resumes.

**Do exactly what they do.**

- **Stop instantly** when you hear the whistle. Mid-step if necessary, so practice freezing on a cue. Balance training helps here.
- **Hold your breath if you can,** or at least breathe as silently and shallowly as possible. Your respirator will make noise, so practice silent breathing through it.
- **Don't move during the stillness.** Not your head, not your hands. The eels are dark, and this is actually the safest the water will be all day, but don't use it as a chance to cross. Crossing during stillness is doing the one thing no one else is doing.
- **Watch which way the animals nearest you turn,** and turn with them. It's the same direction every time for everyone, so learn it on your first whistle and you'll know it after. If you're unsure, turn a beat late rather than wrong.
- **Resume at the new tempo.** The strikers come back at a new beat. Find it and fall in.

A shift has changed. Something has been handed over. You, by turning, have been handed over too. That seems to be fine. It seems to be what keeps you on the books.

### The Chimney-Shadows: Where the Factory Is Almost Real

Long, straight shadows of chimneys, gantries, and half-mile sheds, with nothing casting them. Animals route around them. Bellows-backs won't stand in them. Strikers inside them keep *perfect* time. The sound-lag grows long enough near them that you can hear the whole next minute of a district before it happens.

My intuition: **the shadows are where the unbuilt works is closest to being built.** They are the interior of the factory, and the rest of the Floor is the yard.

Recommendations:

- **Don't enter them.** The bellows-backs don't. Follow their judgment. They've been here longer.
- **Use them as landmarks.** They're one of the few straight lines in a place without direction. But remember they depend on the angle of a light that has no angle, so they may not be there next time you look.
- **Their edges can be useful,** because the extended sound-lag lets you hear far ahead. Standing near the edge of a shadow is like standing outside a building listening to the work inside. You might learn when an eel school is coming, or when a district's beat is about to break. Use this sparingly.
- **If you hear your own footsteps ahead of time,** that's normal near the shadows. Don't panic. Don't try to change the step you've already heard. Take it. Stepping differently from the sound you've already heard is the most out-of-rhythm thing a person could possibly do.

### Navigation: Distance Is Counted

You'll cross the same water sheet again and again: the same drip-dimples, the same half-sunk bellows-back skeleton, with the snails and eels moved. Travellers who walk in a straight line report crossing the same stretch dozens of times, then suddenly reaching the rim ridges, "as if a tally had been completed."

- **Leave your compass at home,** or treat it as a curiosity. You're walking on hundreds of feet of iron strata. It'll lie.
- **GPS won't help either.** The plain repeats, like an assembly line.
- **Bring a waterproof notebook and a grease pencil** (not a metal pen). **Keep your own tally.** Every time you recognize a repeated bay, mark it. Note the skeleton, the drip patterns, the shape of the nearest tread.
- **Don't fight the repetition.** Don't turn back, don't try to "break out" by changing direction. Walk steadily, in rhythm, in one direction, and let the count run. The exit comes when the tally is complete. You don't know what the number is. You don't need to. Just keep working your way out.
- **My intuition:** the count may depend on how well you've kept time. A traveller who walks in rhythm might be tallied out faster than one who stumbles. A shift ends for those who worked it. So walk well.
- **Enter at the rim ridges and leave by them.** The faint concentric growth-rings of the old mountains are the only real boundary. When you reach them, you're at the edge of the works. Step over one and keep going.

## Part Four: The Stillness Problem

There's a contradiction built into the Floor, and you need a plan for it.

**Things that stand still too long grow the mineral film.** It's gray-green, crusty, slow, and grows on stone, water, and the backs of animals. It is "the only thing here that is quiet."

But **you'll need to rest,** and the whistle demands stillness.

- **Rest in motion when possible.** Slow your pace, but don't stop.
- **When you must stop, stop in turns with a partner** (see below). One sits, one watches and brushes.
- **Brush yourself down constantly.** Carry a stiff nylon brush and go over your shoulders, back, hood, and the tops of your boots. The film is probably harmless in small amounts, but I'd guess it's the Floor's way of reclaiming things that have stopped working. I would not let it establish itself on me.
- **Sleep is the hardest problem.** If you're staying more than a day, consider a short-duration sleep schedule: brief naps, woken by a partner every twenty or thirty minutes to brush and shift. Or consider whether you really need to stay long enough to sleep. I'd recommend a visit short enough that you don't.

## Part Five: Go With a Partner

The tallymen work in ones and twos. So should you.

- **Watch each other's rhythm.** You'll start losing track of your own. A partner notices when your step goes ragged from cold or fatigue.
- **Brush each other.** You can't reach your own back.
- **Watch each other's temperature.** In damp cold, the person getting hypothermic is the last to know.
- **Watch each other's mind.** See Aftereffects. Agree beforehand on a word or signal that means "you're drifting," and agree that if one of you says it, the other listens.
- **Agree what happens if a tallyman touches one of you.** The answer is: nothing. Keep walking. Don't react. Don't pull your partner away.

## Part Six: Packing List

**Body:**
- Insulated rubber chest waders, no metal fittings
- Dielectric-rated rubber boots or overshoes, high uppers
- Wool or synthetic base layers, multiple sets, in sealed bags
- Fleece mid layers
- Smooth, dark, waterproof, hooded shell
- Neoprene gloves and rubber gauntlets
- Chemical heat packs, lots

**Head:**
- Full-face respirator with P100 filters (or half-face plus sealed goggles), with many spare filters
- Anti-fog wipes and cloth
- Musician's (flat-attenuation) earplugs
- A dim red headlamp with a plastic housing. The real navigable light is the eels. Your lamp is for your notebook, not for the plain. Keep it low, keep it pointed down, and don't sweep it across the animals. A foreign light source moving out of rhythm is exactly the kind of thing that gets noticed.

**Movement:**
- Fiberglass or wooden walking staff, no metal tip
- Stiff nylon brush for removing mineral film and soot

**Navigation:**
- Waterproof notebook and grease pencils
- Pocket metronome, for re-calibrating your pace if a district's beat collapses and you lose yours. Use it through an earpiece, silently, and not during a whistle.

**Supply:**
- All drinking water and food you'll need, plus margin
- A small insulated flask for something hot
- Sealed bags for everything, and a bag for your soot-covered outer layer when you leave

**Don't bring:**
- Anything metal you can avoid
- Anything that might be mistaken for a tool, trap, or fishing implement
- Any intention of collecting samples, especially bolts

## Part Seven: What It Will Be Like

You step off the rim ridge. The ground goes from hard to soft under your boots, and cold comes up through the rubber immediately, a cellar cold. The air is brown. The smell of iron and scorched hair gets through your respirator anyway, faintly.

And the noise. You felt it through the ridge before you crossed, but now it's inside you. A thrum under everything, and over it the hammering, thousands of strokes resolving at a distance into something like a beat. For the first hour you'll be overwhelmed. Then you'll start picking out layers. By the second hour you'll find yourself walking in time without meaning to.

You push into the first herd of bellows-backs. They're warm on the exhale, and the vapor around you rises and falls. The soot is settling on your sleeves in a fine black snow. Somewhere ahead you hear a crack, then see a violet flash light the vapor from below, and your stomach drops as you realize you *heard it first*.

A tallyman passes. It doesn't look at you. It doesn't need to; it's mostly eye. Its long fingers brush your arm, the same way they brushed the snail at your feet. You don't break stride.

You cross a water sheet with a half-sunk skeleton in it, and you mark it in your notebook. Later you cross it again. And again.

Then the whistle. Everything stops. You stop. The dripping is suddenly the loudest thing in the world. You hold your breath with the whole plain. And then, with everything else, you turn.

## Part Eight: Aftereffects

Plan for these, because they'll follow you home.

- **Rhythm entrainment.** You may find yourself walking in time for days afterward, matching your stride to clocks, washing machines, a dripping tap, your own heartbeat. Mostly harmless, but some visitors report irritation or anxiety when the rhythms around them are irregular. Spend time somewhere deliberately arrhythmic: a forest, a beach with irregular waves, a busy café. Let your body relearn the idea that not everything is counting.
- **Whistle reflex.** Any long falling note, like a train horn or a kettle, may make you freeze and hold your breath. You may find yourself turning a quarter-rotation. Warn the people you live with. It'll fade.
- **Anticipatory hearing.** Some visitors report, for a while, a vague sense of hearing things just before they happen. I don't know if this is real or a trick of attention. Either way, don't lean into it.
- **Respiratory irritation, iron taste, tinnitus.** See a doctor about the soot. Get a chest check if you were there more than a day.
- **Orange everything.** Your gear will be stained with rust. It may never come out. Don't mind.
- **A strange sense of having been employed.** This is the hardest one to describe. You were somewhere where everything had a job, including you, for a while. Some visitors feel oddly bereft afterward, as if they'd been laid off. Others feel like they left something on the floor. Let yourself feel it. Then go and do something that isn't work, that nobody is counting, just because you want to.

---

## Last Advice

The Floor doesn't want you, but it doesn't hate you either. It doesn't care about you at all, the way a factory doesn't care about a visitor walking through during a shift, until the visitor gets in the way of the line.

So don't get in the way. Keep time. Stop when it stops. Turn when it turns. Leave what it makes where it lies. Walk steadily toward the rim until the tally comes up complete.

And when you reach the rim ridge and step over it and the noise finally drops away behind you, and you find yourself standing on a cold ordinary hillside in ordinary silence, you will probably keep walking in time for a little while longer. That's all right. Just let the rhythm go when you're ready. You're off the clock now.
