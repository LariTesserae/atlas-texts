# Preparing for the Hollow of Unceasing Voices

## The Short Version

You are going to a place that is, in order of immediate danger:

1. **Toxic to breathe** (ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, fungal spores)
2. **Loud enough to deafen you permanently**
3. **Completely dark**
4. **Constantly wet and cold**, except where it is warm, wet, and poisonous
5. **Without landmarks, days, or walls**
6. **Inhabited by trillions of small animals** and at least one large one no one has ever sensed directly

None of these is exotic on its own. Cavers, miners, industrial workers, and bat researchers deal with every one of them. What makes the Hollow hard is that they all arrive at once, and they interfere with each other. The respirator fogs your goggles. Hearing protection muffles the silence you need to hear. The rain soaks the filters. The roar makes it impossible to talk to your partner about any of it.

So prepare as though for a hazardous-materials cave expedition with no rescue. That is exactly what it is.

Nobody has ever been here. No one will come for you if something goes wrong.

## Part One: Before You Leave Home

### Medical preparation

**Get a baseline audiogram.** You will want to know what you lost. You will probably lose something even with good protection. Plan to repeat the test afterward.

**Get pre-exposure rabies vaccination.** The flyers are not bats, and whatever they carry may not be rabies. But you will be struck by flying mammals, probably many times, during a Heaving. Rabies vaccination is the cheapest insurance against the most familiar version of that risk. Keep your tetanus current too.

**Understand histoplasmosis.** On Earth, deep bat guano grows *Histoplasma*, a fungus whose spores cause a serious lung infection. The Hollow has meters of guano, warm and moist, compacted over millennia. Assume it grows something similar or worse, and that every step raises a cloud of it. Talk to a doctor about the symptoms: fever, cough, and chest pain appearing one to three weeks after exposure. If they appear after you return, tell your physician exactly where you've been, or at least "a very large bat cave."

**Get fit in specific ways.** You need:
- Leg endurance for walking on soft, yielding ground, which is exhausting like sand or deep snow
- Grip and balance for slick flowstone
- The ability to work hard while breathing through a respirator

That last one is harder than it sounds. Practice hiking in a full-face respirator before you go. Many people panic the first time.

**Screen for claustrophobia's opposite.** The Hollow is not a tight space. It is an infinite dark with no walls. Some people find this far worse than a crawlway. Spend a night alone in a large, unlit space before committing. A big cave chamber is ideal, or a moonless desert.

### Training

- **Caving experience:** at minimum several trips with an experienced club, including wet caves. You need to know how to move on flowstone and how to manage lights. You also need to know what cold-and-wet exhaustion feels like before it becomes hypothermia.
- **Confined-space / hazardous-atmosphere training:** the kind given to sewer and tank workers. You must understand how heavy gases pool, how gas monitors work, and why you never go in after a collapsed partner without your own air.
- **Tactile and light-signal communication with your partner.** Drill this until it is automatic. Details below.

### Go with a partner. Ideally, go with three people.

Standard caving practice is a minimum of three: one hurt, one stays, one goes for help. Here there is no help to go for, but a party of three can still carry an injured person. Two can only watch each other struggle.

## Part Two: Breathing

This is the part most likely to kill you, and the part a romantic reading of the description skips.

### The hazards

**Ammonia.** The guano smell is described as "so thick it has texture." That is not a metaphor you can ignore. Ammonia at high concentrations burns eyes, throat, and lungs. In poorly ventilated bat caves on Earth, ammonia can reach levels that are dangerous to unprotected humans. Your eyes will stream within minutes without sealed protection.

**Hydrogen sulfide.** The springs "reek of sulfur." H₂S is the most dangerous gas you will meet here, for three reasons:
- **It kills your sense of smell** at moderately high concentrations. The rotten-egg smell vanishing is a warning, not relief.
- **It is heavier than air.** It collects in hollows, the same hollows where fog forms during the Heaving.
- **It knocks people unconscious fast** at high concentrations. Many H₂S deaths are would-be rescuers who ran in after a collapsed colleague.

**Carbon dioxide and low oxygen.** Deep, still caves with lots of decomposition (meters of warm guano) can accumulate CO₂ and lose oxygen, especially in low spots.

**Spores and particulates.** As above: assume fungal pathogens in the guano. Add fine calcite dust, and dried insect and flyer debris.

**Unknown compounds.** This is chemistry from the deep earth that feeds an entire ecosystem. There may be methane, or dissolved metals aerosolized in the spring mist. Assume the unknown is not friendly.

### The equipment

**A multi-gas monitor, worn at chest height, with a second one carried low or on a probe.** It should read O₂, H₂S, CO₂, NH₃, and combustible gas (LEL). Get one with a **vibration alarm and a very bright visual alarm.** You will not hear an audible alarm. Calibrate it before you go and bring calibration gas if the trip is long. Carry a spare monitor.

**A full-face respirator** with combination cartridges:
- P100 particulate filtration (spores)
- Ammonia/methylamine protection
- Acid gas protection (low-level H₂S and SO₂ exposure)

Full-face, not half-mask. It protects your eyes from ammonia, larvae, and flyers all at once.

Better still is a **PAPR (powered air-purifying respirator)** with a hood or full-face piece. The positive pressure keeps insects, spores, and gas out even if the seal shifts. It is also much easier to breathe through while hiking on soft ground. Battery life matters: bring spare batteries for your entire trip plus 50%.

**Cartridge supply:** cartridges saturate faster in high humidity, and the humidity here is effectively 100%. Change them on a schedule, not by smell, since ammonia and H₂S both fatigue or deaden smell. Bring far more than you think you need. Keep them sealed in waterproof bags until use. Wet cartridges may perform poorly.

**An escape breathing apparatus**, a small self-contained air supply (10–15 minutes), on your body at all times. Air-purifying cartridges **do not protect** against high H₂S concentrations or low oxygen. If your monitor screams during a Heaving, you put on the escape set and go uphill. You do not stand and troubleshoot.

**Anti-fog measures.** Your faceplate will fog constantly in this humidity. Use anti-fog coatings and a respirator design with nose-cup airflow. A PAPR's airflow helps enormously. Carry lens wipes in a sealed pouch.

### The rules

- **High ground during the Heaving.** The "uplands no taller than a house" are your refuges. Gas rolls outward from the springs and fog settles in the hollows. Get above it.
- **Never enter a fog-filled low spot.** Not for a better view of the embers. Not for anything.
- **If your partner collapses in a low area, do not go in on filtered air.** Escape set on first, or rope and haul from above. This is the classic way two people die instead of one.
- **Don't camp beside springs.** Don't camp in basins or rimstone-pool depressions.

## Part Three: Hearing, and the Problem of Silence

### How loud is it?

The description says literally deafening, like "standing inside a waterfall made of insects." A large cricket chorus, amplified by stone and multiplied by millions, plus a flyer swarm and resonant columns, could plausibly sit well above the level where hearing damage begins within minutes. It may approach levels that are painful. There are three distinct bands to worry about:

- **Mid-range** (crickets, croaks, trills): standard hearing protection handles this reasonably well.
- **Ultrasonic** (the flyers' calls, the "ache behind the teeth"): earplugs block high frequencies well. That ache may partly be sensation through your skull and jaw, which no earplug fully stops.
- **Low-frequency** (the stone hum, the "bubbling bass" of the springs): this is the hardest to block. Passive earmuffs are weakest here, and low frequencies travel through your body and bones.

### The equipment

**Double protection:** foam or custom-molded earplugs, plus over-ear earmuffs. Together these may give roughly 30–40 dB of real-world reduction. That may still not be enough for prolonged exposure. So you must also **limit exposure time**, which shapes the whole trip.

**The muff problem:** earmuffs must fit over or around respirator straps or a PAPR hood without breaking the seal of either. Test this combination at home. Some PAPR helmets integrate hearing protection. That is worth seeking out.

**Active noise-cancelling electronic earmuffs** help with the low-frequency hum. The kind used by shooters and industrial workers also let quieter sounds through while clamping down on loud ones. Choose ones rated for wet conditions or seal them.

**A sound level meter**, ideally a small wearable unit logging your total noise dose. Watch your cumulative exposure like a diver watches depth and time.

### Silence, the thing you must not miss

The Hollow has one warning system: **travelling rings of silence**, marking the passage of the silent hunter. Everything in the basin uses them. You should too.

Here is the problem. You will be wearing heavy hearing protection. You will be exhausted, disoriented, possibly asleep. The drop from "overwhelming roar" to "hum and rain" should be dramatic enough to notice even through plugs. But don't rely on that.

**Rig a silence alarm.** Program a sound level meter or a simple microphone-and-microcontroller device to **vibrate on your wrist when the ambient level suddenly drops** by a large amount. A 15–20 dB fall within a few seconds would do. This is simple electronics. Test it with recordings before you go. Keep one running while you sleep.

When the alarm goes off:
- **Stop moving.** Do what the flyers do: flatten, hold still, make no sound.
- **Turn off anything that emits sound.** That includes ultrasonic devices.
- **Do not shine your light upward hoping to see it.** It almost certainly has no eyes, so light won't attract it. But light won't help you, and you'll waste the moment. If you want to know what it is, a thermal camera pointed up is the only tool with a chance. Consider carefully whether you want to know.

**How dangerous is the hunter to you?** Nobody knows. It hunts small flyers. You are enormous, earthbound, and warm. It may regard you as a rock. But it navigates, perhaps, by listening to everyone else's echoes. When the flyers fall silent, you become nearly invisible to it, as they do. When the flyers are singing around you, their calls bounce off you and paint you on its map. The safest assumption is that it notices you, has no reason to care, and has no reason to learn otherwise.

### Communication

Speech is useless. Radio voice is nearly useless. Plan for:

- **A bone-conduction or throat-microphone intercom** with noise-cancelling. These are used in extreme-noise military and industrial settings. Worth bringing, but don't depend on it.
- **Light signals.** This is your gift: *nothing here has eyes.* You can flash lights freely without disturbing anything. Agree on a simple code. Circle = OK? / OK. Rapid flashing = come to me now. Side-to-side = stop. Steady beam on your partner's chest = look at me, I need to show you something.
- **Touch signals.** Hand squeezes, shoulder taps, and rope tugs for moving in low visibility or fog. Classic diving and caving rope signals work: one tug = OK, two = advance, three = retreat, continuous = emergency.
- **A waterproof writing slate** for anything complicated.

## Part Four: Light

There is no ambient light except faint bacterial glow and the brief rising embers of luminescent larvae after a Heaving. You are responsible for every photon you see.

- **Rule of three:** every person carries three independent light sources. Typically that's a primary helmet lamp, a backup lamp, and a small emergency light. Each has its own batteries. Standard caving practice, non-negotiable here.
- **Waterproof lamps**, rated for immersion. The rain never stops somewhere.
- **Battery budget** for the whole trip plus a large reserve. Keep spares sealed and warm; cold, wet batteries die fast.
- **A wide flood beam and a spot beam.** The flood lets you walk on treacherous ground. The spot reaches the columns. It will vanish into the dark overhead, which is its own kind of lesson.
- **Marker lights** (see Navigation).

### Light and your mind

With a helmet lamp in a boundless dark, your world will be a small moving circle. Everything outside it is noise. Some people find this intensely claustrophobic despite the openness; others find it cozy. Practice beforehand.

Consider also the wasted beauty. Several times during the trip, when you are on safe high ground after a Heaving, **turn every light off.** Wait for your eyes to adapt, twenty minutes or more. Watch the mats glow blue-green below you and the embers climb and go out. You may be the first eyes that have ever seen it. This is worth planning the whole trip around.

## Part Five: Clothing and Body Protection

You need to be protected from **water, cold, insects, venom, spores, caustic ammonia, and being struck by flying animals**, while hiking for hours. Think of it as a caving suit crossed with a beekeeper's suit.

### Layers

- **Synthetic or wool base layer.** Never cotton; it will be soaked and stay soaked.
- **Fleece or pile mid-layer** (a caving "undersuit"). Pile keeps some warmth even when wet.
- **Waterproof, abrasion-resistant outer suit**, in the style of a PVC caving oversuit or a heavy chemical-resistant coverall. It should be **smooth-surfaced** so crickets, spiders, and debris slide off rather than cling. It must be seamless at the joins to boots and gloves. Tape the cuffs with duct tape or use integrated boot socks.
- **Hood or PAPR hood** sealed to the suit collar.

### Temperature management

The description gives mixed signals, so prepare for both. The ceiling rain is cold. Large caves on Earth often sit at a steady cool temperature, and constant wet drives hypothermia even at mild temperatures. The springs and the Heaving are warm and humid, and the guano is warm from decomposition. You could overheat inside a sealed suit near the springs, then chill badly once you walk into a cold, wet district.

- Adjust layers aggressively. Don't sweat through everything.
- Watch your partner for hypothermia signs: shivering that stops, clumsiness, confusion, apathy.
- Carry an **emergency bivvy** and chemical heat packs.

### Hands and feet

- **Boots:** tall rubber boots or sealed, lugged caving wellies. The guano is soft, seethes with insects, and may be meters deep in places. You do not want it in your boots.
- **Gaiters** over the boot–suit junction.
- **Consider flotation on the deep guano:** wide overshoes or small snowshoe-like pads for the deepest, softest patches. At minimum, carry a **walking pole** to probe depth before each step in unknown ground.
- **Gloves:** a nitrile inner glove for chemical and pathogen protection, with a heavier abrasion- and bite-resistant outer glove. Many-legged cave predators on Earth (centipedes) bite and are venomous. The "long, flat, many-legged predators that flow over the rimstone like spilled oil" are probably not interested in you. They may be interested in the crickets on your sleeve.

### Face and head

- A full-face respirator or PAPR hood covers eyes and face. It protects against ammonia, larvae, and flyers bumping into you.
- A **helmet**, as in caving. The flyers fly within "finger-widths of one another, never colliding." They will avoid you most of the time. But during a Heaving there will be millions of them, and some will hit you. Getting struck by a small furred body at speed on an unprotected face is unpleasant and potentially dangerous to both parties.
- A **fine mesh net** over the whole head assembly during the Heaving stops the smoke-columns of weak, blind, newly winged larvae from clogging your filters and vents.

## Part Six: Water and Food

### Drinking water

**Do not drink the spring water.** It is sulfurous and chemically unknown, and it supports bacterial mats that feed on deep-earth chemistry, possibly including dissolved metals.

**Be cautious with rimstone-pool water.** It is extremely mineral-heavy ("slick between the fingers"). Heavily mineralized water can cause gastrointestinal upset in quantity. It may also carry pathogens washed from guano, which is everywhere.

**Ceiling rain** is condensation, so it may be the cleanest source. But it drips through the air over the guano and past the colonies.

Plan to:
- **Carry your primary water supply in** for short trips.
- For longer trips, **collect ceiling rain** in a clean funnel-and-bottle rig in a "wet district." Then **filter** it, removing particulates and bacteria, **and treat** it chemically or with UV for viruses. A **small reverse-osmosis or distillation unit** is ideal for removing minerals and unknown dissolved compounds, if you can carry one.
- Test a sample with water test strips before relying on any source.

### Food

Bring all of it. Nothing here is food for you. Grazing snails and blind shrimp are probably edible in some technical sense, and eating them is a terrible idea: unknown toxins, unknown parasites, and a delicate ecosystem.

Choose food you can eat fast. **Eating is dangerous here**, because it requires breaking your respiratory seal in ammonia- and spore-laden air. Options:

- **Liquid or gel food through a respirator drink port.** Some full-face respirators and PAPRs have drinking tubes. This is the safest method.
- **A clean-air eating shelter** (below).
- If neither is available, eat on high ground, far from springs and away from the deepest guano, during the slack hours between Heavings. Check your gas monitor first. Be quick. Wash hands and face with clean water first.

### The clean-air shelter

Consider bringing a **small sealed tent or bivvy with a filtered air supply**: a lightweight shelter you pitch, seal, and pressurize using a PAPR blower or dedicated fan through the same cartridges. Inside it you can:

- Remove your mask to eat, drink, and treat minor injuries
- Get some partial sound insulation
- Sleep with somewhat less risk
- Clean fogged equipment
- Recover mentally from the unbroken sensory assault

It is heavy, and it's worth it for any trip longer than a day. It needs a floor that insects can't get through, sealed zippers, and a protected site. Pitch it on bare flowstone or a column plinth, never on deep guano (warmth, gas, insects, sinking) and never in a low spot (gas).

### Waste

**Pack out everything**, including human waste, in sealed bags. Caving ethics are clear about this. The basin's vast chemistry won't notice a little more organic matter. But you are bringing surface bacteria, fungi, and viruses into a world that has never met them. Don't seed it.

## Part Seven: Moving Across the Ground

### Terrain types and how to cross them

**Flowstone** ("cream and ochre and rust, laid down in thin glossy sheets"): slick when wet, and it is always wet. Walk flat-footed, keep your weight over your feet, use a pole. Lugged rubber soles help somewhat.

**Rimstone dams** ("low scalloped walls of calcite a hand's width high"): **do not step on them.** They are fragile. You will crack walls that took thousands of years to grow, and the pools behind them will drain. Step in the pools or between the terraces, and choose routes that avoid rimstone slopes where possible. The mineral water is slick, and each pool has a crisp calcite skin you will break with every step. Accept that you will leave some marks. Minimize them.

**Guano fields:** soft, warm, alive, sometimes meters deep. Probe with your pole. The top layer is fresh and loose; the lower strata are compacted. You could sink to your knees or further in unexpected soft spots. Walk in single file on your partner's tested footprints. Moving through guano will stir up spores (respirator on) and thousands of crawling animals. Crickets as long as a hand will scatter around you, and some will cling. Expect to be crawled on constantly.

**Bacterial mat fields near springs:** "slick gelatinous sheets that ripple in the current like wet silk." These are the base of the entire food web. **Avoid walking on them** as much as possible. You'll damage them and they're as slippery as wet ice. They also sit at the lowest, warmest, most gas-prone spots in the basin. Go around.

**Column forests:** easier ground, because columns root on stone. But the columns are covered with roosting flyers ("a pelt of roosting bodies"), and the draperies hanging from them are thin, fragile, translucent calcite. **Never touch a drapery.** They are the voice of the basin; each rings at its own pitch. Snapping one would silence a note that has sounded for millennia, and could strip a colony of the resonant stone it has spent generations tuning. Walk through the column groves carefully, and keep your helmet, pack, and pole well clear.

### Pace

Expect to move slowly: perhaps a kilometer or two an hour on good ground, much less on soft guano or rimstone slopes. The respirator, the suit, the noise, and the unstable footing all add fatigue. Rest often.

## Part Eight: Navigation and Time

### Space

The basin has no walls anyone has touched, no sky, and no landmarks visible beyond your lamplight. You could walk for days and never see a boundary. You could also walk in a slow circle and never know.

**Your arrival point is your lifeline.** However you enter the Hollow, whether a portal, a gate, or a threshold, you must be able to find it again. Mark it with:

- **A long-life strobe beacon.** Nothing sees it, so it disturbs nothing. Visible for a surprising distance in total dark, if no columns block the line of sight.
- **A radio beacon** with a directional receiver. Radio penetrates the dark far better than light and doesn't care about columns.
- **A physical cairn or marker** you'd recognize by touch.

**Dead reckoning:** carry a compass, but verify that it works; local magnetic conditions may be strange. Carry a pedometer or pace-counting method, and a notebook. Record heading and paces for every leg. Consider an **inertial navigation device** (the kind used in underground mining and firefighting). These track your path without GPS, though they drift over time.

**Marker lights:** carry a supply of small, cheap, long-duration LED markers, and leave a trail of them on stone at intervals as you go. Collect them on the way back.

**A tether** is impractical for long distances, but a **reel of line** is invaluable for short exploratory forays from a fixed point. Examples: moving from camp to a spring and back, or into a fog.

### Navigating by ear

The description notes that you "could map the basin by ear," and nearly every creature here does. You can partially borrow this:

- A **spectrum analyzer app** (or a dedicated device) on a sealed phone or tablet will show the dominant frequencies of wherever you stand. The eastern flats have dry fast ticking at one band. The column galleries have a shimmering whistle-chorus higher up. The springs have bubbling bass.
- Record the spectrum at your arrival point. Record it at each stop. You'll build a crude acoustic map, and you'll be able to tell which region you're in even when everything looks the same.
- Each great column sings "with its own voice." A **bat detector** (see below) can identify individual pillars by their dominant colony frequency. In principle you could navigate the column forest pillar by pillar, like reading street signs.

### Time

There is no day, only the Heaving, which arrives "every few hours, on a rhythm that drifts and has never quite repeated." Your body will lose track of time within a day or two.

- **Wear a watch.** Better, two: one showing surface time, one logging elapsed time.
- **Keep a written log** of every Heaving: start time, duration, notes. You'll begin to anticipate them, though never perfectly.
- **Set a fixed schedule** for eating, sleeping, and cartridge changes. Base it on your watch, not how you feel.
- **Set a hard turnaround time** before you arrive, and honor it. Disorientation, fatigue, and wonder all push people to stay longer than they should.

## Part Nine: The Heaving Protocol

The Heaving is the most spectacular event in the Hollow and the most dangerous. It is when gas surges, water floods, noise doubles, and trillions of animals take to the air at once.

### Warning signs

- **The great chord slides upward**: a slow glissando in the low hum. You may feel it more than hear it through your protection. Your spectrum analyzer will show it plainly.
- **Warm, humid, sulfurous air** on your suit and a rising H₂S reading.
- **The rain thickens** into drizzle.
- **Fog forms in low places.**

### What to do

1. **Move to high ground immediately.** An upland, a column plinth, a rise. Know where the nearest refuge is at all times, especially near springs.
2. **Check your gas monitor.** Have your escape set ready.
3. **Secure your gear.** Close vents and lock down your head net.
4. **Check your hearing protection.** The noise will double. Consider an extra layer or a planned exposure break.
5. **Stay put.** Moving during the Heaving means walking through fog, gas, flooded mats, and a lattice of flying bodies. Don't.
6. **Let the flyers flow around you.** They will pour off the columns in waves and sweep through the air "in great looping sweeps." Stay still and they will mostly avoid you. Their echolocation will register you as a stationary obstacle. Don't wave your arms or swat.
7. **Watch the silences.** The busiest Heavings bring several drifting rings of silence at once. If your silence alarm triggers, go still.
8. **Wait for the chord to slide back down**, the rain to thin, and the fog to settle into the pools and vanish. Check gas levels before moving again, especially toward low ground.

### What to watch for

If you've prepared well and are safely on high ground near a spring, this is the moment. Watch the larvae rise "in columns that look like smoke." Watch the luminescent ones drift up as faint embers, "climbing into nothing and going out one by one as they are eaten." Turn your lights off.

## Part Ten: Sleeping, and Your Mind

### The sleep problem

There is no quiet time, only "overwhelming, not unbearable." You will be trying to sleep in noise louder than a construction site, in a sealed suit or tent. You'll be damp and cold, and gas and a silent hunter may be moving around you. Expect poor sleep.

- Sleep in your **clean-air shelter**, on high ground, away from springs and deep guano.
- **Sleep in shifts** with your partner. One stays awake, watching the gas monitor and silence alarm.
- **Consider a short-acting sleep aid**, discussed beforehand with a doctor. Not one that makes you hard to wake.
- **Keep hearing protection on while sleeping.** Your dose clock keeps running.
- **Keep trips short.** Several days is probably the maximum for most people. Cumulative noise exposure, sleep deprivation, and respiratory burden will wear you down faster than you expect.

### Sensory overload and deprivation at once

The Hollow is a strange double assault: almost total darkness for the eyes, total saturation for the ears and nose. Some expected effects:

- **Hallucinations** in the dark. Your visual cortex will invent shapes, lights, and movement, especially when you're tired. The description notes the mat glow is "barely distinguishable from the afterimages the eye invents in total dark." Know this is coming, and don't chase lights you're not sure are real.
- **Auditory hallucinations.** In continuous roaring noise, many people start "hearing" voices, music, or their name in the chaos. The place is literally called the Hollow of Unceasing Voices. Expect it.
- **Irritability, anxiety, and a feeling of being unable to think.** Constant loud noise is exhausting. Build in rest periods inside your shelter.
- **Distorted sense of time.** Already covered; rely on your watch.
- **Profound loneliness.** You are unable to talk freely to your partner, in an endless dark, in a world never touched by anything that makes things. Some people will find this transcendent. Some will find it unbearable. Most will feel both.

**The silences may be the most intense experience of all.** When a ring of stillness washes over you, the roar will suddenly drop. You'll hear the stone hum, the patter of rain, maybe your own heartbeat, and the absolute stillness of trillions of animals pressed flat and not breathing, all around you, waiting. Then the noise floods back in behind it. Prepare yourself emotionally for that moment. It may be the thing you remember most.

## Part Eleven: Conduct and Ethics

You are the first visitor to a place that has existed in "old, rounded balance" for longer than it would take to tell. Every shape here is the product of countless slow processes, and the life and the stone are folded into each other. Behave accordingly:

- **Touch nothing you don't have to.** Especially draperies, rimstone walls, and columns.
- **Do not harm the tuned pillars.** Some columns are "more guano than rock," shaped over generations by colonies to resonate at exactly their frequency. Chipping or breaking one could leave a colony effectively blind.
- **Avoid producing loud ultrasonic noise.** Don't use ultrasonic rangefinders, ultrasonic pest devices, or high-frequency speakers near colonies. The flyers "sing to see," and you could jam their vision or confuse the balance of silence and song that guards them. Passive listening is fine. Broadcasting is not.
- **Don't walk on the bacterial mats.** They are the foundation of all life here.
- **Take no specimens** unless you've thought hard about why, and never take anything large or rare. Absolutely do not disturb the "long pale swimmers" in the big springs. There are few of them.
- **Leave no trace** and pack everything out, including your waste.
- **Decontaminate before you arrive**, not just when you leave. Clean boots and gear to avoid bringing surface organisms in.

## Part Twelve: Things Worth Doing

Once you are safe and prepared, the Hollow offers experiences available nowhere else.

- **Bring a bat detector.** This is perhaps the single most rewarding piece of gear you can carry. It shifts ultrasound down into the range you can hear, either heterodyne or time-expansion. Wear it through your hearing protection via a sealed earpiece at safe volume. Tune it to different frequencies and you'll hear the flyers' actual songs, colony by colony. Walk through the column forest and tune to each pillar's band: you will hear what the flyers hear, each tower "a tower of a single note." A recorder with a high sample rate lets you bring it home.
- **Feel a column sing.** Press a gloved hand, or better a bare hand briefly in a clean spot, against a great column and feel its resonance through the stone. Do this away from the draperies, gently, without disturbing the roosting colony. Try it again during the Heaving, when the chord slides upward.
- **Stand in a silence**, on safe ground, with your silence alarm primed.
- **Watch the embers rise** with your lights off after a Heaving.
- **Map a region by ear** using your spectrum analyzer. You'll feel, for a moment, what it's like to live here.
- **Watch the rimstone pools.** Their calcite skins are "so fine it breaks like frost." Watch a single drop of ceiling rain fall into one.
- **Listen to the Hollow from a distance.** From a high rise, turn your bat detector and your own attention outward. You'll hear "the roar of the Hollow threaded with silences," pockets of hush wandering through the noise like shadows. In a place without light, they are the shadows.

## Part Thirteen: Leaving

- **Decontaminate thoroughly** at the threshold, before you return to your home world. Rinse your suit with clean water, then disinfect. Remove it inside out and bag it. Bag all used cartridges and filters. Wipe down all electronics. Assume everything is coated with fungal spores and unknown microbes.
- **Shower and wash your hair as soon as possible.** The ammonia smell will be in everything.
- **Quarantine your gear.** Isolate or incinerate anything you can't clean.
- **See your doctor.** Get a follow-up audiogram. Watch for respiratory symptoms for several weeks and tell your physician about the exposure. Report any bite or scratch, even a minor one, from anything that lives there.
- **Expect ringing ears for a while**, possibly permanently. Expect the ordinary silence of your home world to feel strange. After the Hollow, even a quiet room may seem eerily hushed, like a hunter is passing.

## Packing List Summary

**Breathing**
- Full-face respirator or PAPR (preferred), with P100 + ammonia + acid-gas cartridges; large supply of sealed spare cartridges
- Spare PAPR batteries (trip + 50%)
- Escape breathing apparatus (10–15 min), one per person
- Multi-gas monitor (O₂, H₂S, CO₂, NH₃, LEL) with vibration and visual alarm, plus a spare and calibration gas
- Anti-fog supplies

**Hearing**
- Earplugs (many pairs) and electronic/active noise-cancelling earmuffs compatible with the respirator
- Wearable sound-level dose meter
- Silence alarm (SPL-drop detector with wrist vibration)
- Bone-conduction or throat-mic intercom

**Light**
- Three independent waterproof light sources per person, plus batteries for the full trip and reserve
- Strobe beacon for arrival point
- Supply of small LED trail markers

**Clothing**
- Synthetic base layer, pile undersuit, waterproof smooth outer suit
- Tall rubber caving boots, gaiters, optional flotation overshoes
- Nitrile inner and heavy outer gloves
- Caving helmet
- Fine mesh head net
- Emergency bivvy, heat packs

**Shelter and life support**
- Sealed clean-air tent with filtered blower and insect-proof floor
- All food, preferably liquid or gel options usable through a respirator drink port
- Water supply; rain-collection funnel and bottles; filter plus UV or chemical treatment; RO or distillation unit if possible; water test strips
- Waste bags for everything

**Navigation and time**
- Compass (verify function), pedometer, inertial navigation device if available, notebook and waterproof slate
- Radio beacon and directional receiver
- Reel of line
- Two watches

**Science and wonder**
- Bat detector (heterodyne or time-expansion) and high-sample-rate recorder
- Spectrum analyzer on a sealed device
- Thermal camera (optional; for the brave)

**Medical**
- Full first-aid kit including eye wash, wound irrigation, antiseptic, and bite treatment
- Personal medications, sleep aid if prescribed
- Record of vaccinations (rabies pre-exposure, tetanus)

**Decontamination**
- Disinfectant, rinse water, bags for gear, spare clothes for the return

## A Last Word

The Hollow is not hostile. Nothing here wants to hurt you. It simply was not made with you in mind. It was not made at all. It is the breath of the deep earth, feeding a world that sings to see itself, balanced and old and entirely indifferent to eyes, lungs, and ears like yours.

Go carefully, go briefly, and go with people you trust. Touch as little as you can. When you stand on high ground after a Heaving, with your lights off and the embers climbing into the dark and the whole basin roaring around you, you will understand why you came.

Then go home, and leave it as you found it: singing.
