# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Terraces of the Upper Tarn

## The Short Version

This is a high-altitude alpine hike that ends at a very cold, very clear, very fragile underwater site. Most of the danger comes from the mountain and the cold, not from the town. Most of the damage you could do comes from your fins, your hands, and the microbes on your boots. Come slowly, move gently underwater, leave nothing, take nothing, and plan your exit before you enter the water. The way home goes *uphill*, over a pass higher than the lake, and that matters more than it sounds.

---

## 1. Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Before packing anything, adjust your expectations. This is not a dramatic dive site. It has no wreck to penetrate, no current to ride, and no big animals. It is small: forty or fifty roofless stone structures across three terraces, all visible in one long glance. Its value lies in stillness, clarity, slowness, and sound. The ideal visitor treats it like a quiet chapel, not a theme park.

That shapes everything below. You want gear and skills that let you hang motionless in the water for a long time without disturbing anything, and enough warmth and patience to watch a weed sway for a full minute.

---

## 2. Choosing Your Season

The tarn has four distinct faces. Pick deliberately, because each needs different preparation.

**Late summer (best for a first visit).**
- The approach has the least snow, though north gullies still hold it.
- The top few meters are warm enough for a brief unprotected swim.
- Visibility is at its peak, and at noon light reaches the deepest terrace.
- On bright afternoons the stonewort meadows are silvered with oxygen bubbles.
- The weather is most stable, but expect afternoon thunderstorms.
- The drawback is that the frog chorus is over. You'll hear water boatmen, beetles, and the crackle of bubbles, but not the stone organ.

**Spring (for the sound).**
- The frog chorus runs for a few weeks after ice-out, and it is the reason many people will want to come.
- The approach may be snow-covered, with avalanche and cornice risk on the cirque walls.
- The water is at its coldest, and the spring turnover may cloud visibility for a time.
- Hundreds of frogs are migrating down the scree, so you must watch every step on the final descent.
- This is the hardest, most technical trip. Come only if you are an experienced cold-water diver or content to listen with a hydrophone from shore.

**Autumn.**
- The char spawn on the gravel of the upper terraces, their bellies red.
- The ice-forming sounds (booms, groans, long descending whistles) begin late in the season.
- Weather turns fast. Early snow can close the pass.
- Char spawning gravel is off-limits. Do not fin over it or touch it.

**Early winter, the clear-ice window.**
- For a short time between freeze-up and the first real snow, you can lie on clear ice and look straight down into the town.
- This needs no diving, but it demands serious ice knowledge: an ice-thickness auger or spud bar, ice picks worn around the neck, a throw rope, and a partner.
- It is bitterly cold, windy, and brief. If snow comes the night before you arrive, you'll see nothing.
- It is arguably the most beautiful way to see the place, and the least intrusive.

If you can visit only once, choose late summer. If you can visit twice, add a return in spring with a hydrophone.

## 3. Your Body: Altitude and Cold

**Acclimatize.** The cirque sits above the treeline. Depending on the world's geography, that could be anywhere from about 1,800 m to over 3,500 m. Assume it is high enough to matter.
- Spend at least one or two nights at intermediate altitude before the final ascent.
- Know the symptoms of acute mountain sickness: headache, nausea, poor sleep, and unusual fatigue. Know the danger signs of HAPE and HACE: breathlessness at rest, confusion, and loss of coordination. The treatment is to descend.
- Your exertion capacity will be reduced. Hauling dive gear will feel much harder than it would at sea level.

**Train for cold.** If you plan to enter the water at all, practice cold immersion beforehand.
- The cold-shock gasp when your face hits 4°C water is involuntary and dangerous if you have a regulator out or your head is under.
- Controlled exposure (cold showers, then cold-water swims) teaches your body to override it.

**Be fit enough to self-rescue.** This place is remote, and help may not exist in this world at all. You must be able to carry an injured partner, or at least get yourself out.

## 4. The Approach

You'll likely come in by the old path over the pass, where the cairn stands.

- **Footwear:** stiff mountain boots that handle scree, plus microspikes or light crampons for the snow gullies, even in summer.
- **Scree:** the final descent to the waterline is loose. Use trekking poles, test every step, and keep well away from people below you. The cirque walls shed stones on their own, and you don't want to add to it.
- **Navigation:** carry a map if one exists, a compass, and your own route notes. Mark the pass visibly on the way in. In cloud, a cirque is a bowl with one correct exit and many wrong ones.
- **Weather:** afternoon lightning in a bowl of bare rock is serious. Be off exposed ridges by early afternoon. If you're on the water or in it when thunder starts, get out and away from the shoreline and high points.
- **Wind:** constant and thin. It will chill you badly after any immersion. Plan for that before you get wet.

## 5. Camping at the Tarn

- **Site selection:** camp well away from the base of the cirque walls, because of rockfall, and off the lakeshore itself. The shallows are frog habitat in spring, and the edge vegetation is fragile. A flat spot on the rockfall dam near the outflow, or back up toward the rim, is probably best. Check the ground above you for loose stones.
- **Shelter:** a four-season tent or a very sturdy three-season one, well guyed. Nights are cold even in late summer.
- **Changing shelter:** strongly recommended if you're diving. You need somewhere out of the wind to strip out of a drysuit and into warm clothes quickly. A small bothy bag or a second tent works.
- **Stove and fuel:** bring enough for hot drinks after every water session. Warm sweet drinks are part of your safety system, not a luxury.
- **Water:** the snowmelt inflow is probably very clean, but treat it anyway (filter, UV, or boil). Wildlife uses the lake, and you have no idea what this world's microbes do to you.
- **Leave no trace:** pack out everything, human waste included, using wag bags or a similar system. The system is nutrient-poor. That is *why* the water is so clear. A small influx of nutrients from careless camping could trigger algal growth that clouds the water. You could damage the site's defining quality just by peeing near the shore.

## 6. Ways to See the Town

Choose one or more, from least to most intrusive.

### From the rim
On a clear, calm day with the sun high, shapes resolve from above. Bring **polarized sunglasses**, which are essential because they cut surface glare and are the difference between seeing water and seeing streets. Binoculars help. Go at midday, when the light is highest and the wind is often lightest. Early morning calm can also work if the light is enough.

### From the ice (winter)
See Section 2. Lie flat, spread your weight, and shade your eyes with a dark cloth or jacket over your head. Bring an insulated pad to lie on.

### Surface snorkeling
- The upper terraces are in the brighter, shallower water, so you can see a good deal from the surface.
- The top few meters warm enough in summer for a brief swim, but "brief" is the key word. Wear at least a 7 mm wetsuit with hood, gloves, and boots, even in summer, and you'll still get cold within 20–40 minutes.
- Snorkeling lets you hear the water too. Put your head down and listen.
- Bring a mask, snorkel, and floating line or buoy so your shore partner can track you.

### Freediving
Freediving to the upper terraces is plausible for a trained freediver, but:
- The cold shortens breath-holds dramatically.
- Altitude affects your physiology and your blackout risk.
- Never freedive alone. Your buddy must be on the surface watching you on every dive.

### Scuba
This is the only way to spend real time among the lower terraces and the stonewort meadows. It's also the most demanding, so it gets its own section.

## 7. Diving the Tarn: Technical Preparation

### Qualifications you should have
- **Drysuit diving**, with real experience, not just a course.
- **Cold-water diving**: this means water near 4°C, the temperature of the depths year round.
- **Altitude diving**: this is non-negotiable (see below).
- **Excellent buoyancy and trim**, able to hover motionless and horizontal, and to move using frog kicks and back kicks without ever touching the bottom. If you can't do this, do not dive here. You will destroy things.

### Altitude diving
This is the part people get wrong. At altitude, surface pressure is lower, so:
- Standard sea-level dive tables **underestimate** your decompression stress. Use altitude-adjusted tables or a dive computer set correctly for the altitude.
- Your depth gauge may read incorrectly unless it's set for altitude and fresh water.
- You'll be arriving at the lake after a climb, which is itself a change in pressure. Some protocols suggest waiting after arriving at altitude before your first dive. Follow them.
- **The exit problem.** To leave, you must hike **up** over the pass, which is higher than the lake. Ascending after diving is like flying after diving and can bring on decompression sickness. **Plan a long surface interval before hiking out**. Twenty-four hours after your last dive is a sensible conservative rule. Spend your last day at the tarn on dry land: listening, watching, sketching.
- There is no chamber and no evacuation. Dive very conservatively. The town is shallow enough that you have no reason to push depth. Stay well within no-decompression limits, and add a long safety stop.

### Gear for 4°C water
- **Drysuit** with thick undergarments. The deeper water is cold enough to punish any shortcut.
- **Dry gloves** or very thick wet gloves, plus a thick hood. Your hands and face will suffer most.
- **Cold-water-rated regulators**, environmentally sealed first stages. Regulators can free-flow in cold water, especially if you breathe hard or purge them. Carry a **fully redundant second regulator on a separate first stage**. Practice shutting down a free-flowing regulator.
- **Freshwater weighting:** you'll need less weight than in the sea, but the drysuit and undergarments add buoyancy. Do a proper weight check in the shallows first.
- **Lights:** the deep terraces are green-gold and blue-edged. A good light brings back the true colors: the white of the marl, the red of a char's belly, the brown of caddis cases. Use it gently, and don't shine it directly into animals for long.
- **Surface marker buoy** and **reel**.
- **Knife or line cutter:** the milfoil and pondweed stand in tall columns and could snag gear. Cut only in a real emergency.
- **Hood and hearing:** a thick hood muffles sound, which matters here. Consider a hood with small ear holes or a thinner hood for short listening sessions in shallow water. Balance this against the cold.

### Getting the gear up there
Tanks, weights, and drysuits are heavy, and the approach is a mountain path. Options include:
- porters or pack animals, if this world has them;
- multiple carries over several days;
- a **rebreather**, which needs far less gas, though these carry their own complexity and risks;
- smaller cylinders, accepting shorter dives, which is fine here since the site is shallow and small.

Plan fewer, longer, better dives rather than many short ones. And you don't need to dive every day. You'll learn more lying still on the surface than rushing.

### The buddy system
Dive with a buddy, always. A third person on shore is even better. They can tend a line, keep watch, have warm drinks and dry clothes ready, and go for help if needed. Agree on hand signals for "cold," "listen," and "look but don't approach."

## 8. How to Move Through the Town

This is the heart of the visit.

**Never touch the bottom.** The marl is soft, precipitated limestone built by algae over centuries. One careless fin kick will:
- raise a white cloud that ruins visibility for you and hangs in the still water for hours;
- break stonewort stems that took years to grow;
- scar a meadow that will not heal within your lifetime.

The water barely moves. Currents here are measured in one particle drifting across a street over an afternoon. Anything you kick up **stays up**. Use frog kicks, keep your fins high, and stay well above the meadows.

**Do not hold onto the walls.** Every surface is alive: moss cushions, marl crust, stonewort bristle, and diatom film. Your glove would crush animals living in the moss and scrape off growth. The walls are also mortared only by algae, so pulling on stones could loosen them.

**Do not enter the houses.** The doorways are low, and in the lower terraces marl has filled them almost to the lintels. Entering means:
- overhead environments, since lintels are still in place;
- a near-certain silt-out in a confined space, the classic way cave divers die;
- damage to the resonating chambers that give the frog chorus its voices. Every centimeter of disturbed marl changes a house's pitch.

Look in through doorways from outside, with your light angled in. That's enough.

**Keep clear of the standing stone.** It rises out of the silt in the central square, a clear focal point, and it will tempt you to touch it or pose with it. Don't. Hover nearby and look.

**Respect the upper-terrace gravel.** In autumn, the char spawn on the clear paving of the upper terraces. Even outside spawning season, it's their nursery ground.

**Avoid the mussels.** Some are decades old, sitting half-buried in the marl of an old garden. Don't lift them, turn them, or kick silt onto them.

**Leave the caddis cases.** Those tiny drystone cases, made of schist flakes, marl, and old hearth charcoal, are miniature echoes of the town. It is extraordinarily tempting to pocket an empty one. Don't. They are part of the marl's future, and they may carry the only trace of the town's charcoal left anywhere.

**Take nothing at all.** Not a stone, not a charcoal flake, not a shell. The people who lived here swept their hearths and left in good order. Leave it the way they did.

## 9. Biosecurity: The Most Important Section

This lake is isolated, cold, nutrient-poor, and full of amphibians, which makes it about as vulnerable as an ecosystem gets. And you are a *multiverse* traveler, so you could be carrying organisms from other worlds.

**Before arrival:**
- **Clean, check, dry.** Thoroughly clean all gear that will touch water or wet ground: wetsuits, drysuits, fins, masks, BCDs, boots, trekking pole tips, and tent pegs.
- **Disinfect** with an appropriate agent, such as a Virkon-type disinfectant or dilute bleach followed by rinsing, and then **dry completely**, ideally for 48+ hours. Some pathogens survive only in moisture.
- Pay special attention to **amphibian pathogens**. Chytrid fungus and ranaviruses have devastated frog and newt populations on our world. The spring chorus exists only because the frogs are healthy. You could silence it by carrying a fungus in your boot treads.
- Watch for **invasive organisms**: tiny mussels, invasive algae such as didymo or "rock snot," invasive plant fragments, and fish eggs. A single fragment of the wrong weed caught in a velcro strap could take over the stonewort meadows.
- **Across worlds**, assume you carry things no one here has ever met. If you've recently been in any other freshwater environment, in any world, treat your gear as contaminated.

**At the site:**
- Don't release or move any animal or plant.
- Don't handle frogs or newts. If you absolutely must move one out of harm's way, for example off your path in spring, wet your hands first and do it gently and quickly.
- Don't feed the char.
- Don't wash dishes, soap, sunscreen, or insect repellent into the lake. Wash well away from the water and scatter the grey water on rock.

**Sunscreen:** you'll need a lot of it for altitude UV, but apply it well before entering the water and wipe excess off your face. Even small amounts matter in a lake this clear and this nutrient-poor.

## 10. Hazards Summary

| Hazard | Preparation |
|---|---|
| Altitude sickness | Acclimatize gradually, know the symptoms, descend if serious |
| UV exposure (high altitude, reflective water and snow) | Strong sunscreen, glacier or polarized glasses, lip balm, sun hat |
| Hypothermia (in water and after) | Drysuit, changing shelter, dry warm layers, hot drinks, limited dive times |
| Cold shock / gasp reflex | Cold acclimatization, enter slowly, face in first while standing in shallows |
| Regulator free-flow | Cold-rated sealed regs, full redundancy, practiced response |
| Decompression sickness | Altitude tables, conservative profiles, **long surface interval before crossing the pass** |
| Rockfall | Camp away from cirque walls, don't dive or swim directly under active slopes, listen for falling stones |
| Lightning | Off exposed ground and out of the water by early afternoon storms |
| Snow and ice on approach | Microspikes or crampons, ice axe early or late season, avalanche awareness in spring |
| Thin ice (winter) | Auger or spud bar, ice picks, throw rope, never alone, know ice thickness |
| Entanglement in weed | Streamlined gear, cutting tool, stay out of the milfoil columns |
| Silt-out | Perfect buoyancy, never enter houses, never touch bottom |
| Leeches | Harmless nuisance. A drysuit protects you. If one attaches, let it drop off or ease it off with a fingernail, don't yank |
| Remoteness | Buddy, shore support, thorough first-aid kit, emergency shelter, plan for self-rescue |
