# Preparing to Visit the Cut Cistern

## What You're Actually Signing Up For

This place looks like a quiet, glowing room, but physically it is something else. It is a **flooded confined space with an overhead ceiling, a narrow vertical entry, a gas-producing hot spring, and a drain.** Each of those is manageable. Together they need respect. Most of your preparation is about getting down, breathing safely while you're there, not overheating, and getting back up. The rest is about leaving the place as you found it, which matters more here than almost anywhere.

A visit is essentially a **warm-water snorkel and freedive in a tiny, extremely clear, extremely loud artificial cave**, reached by a rope descent of roughly seven meters through a shaft about as wide as your shoulders.

---

## 1. Finding the Entrance Without Falling Into It

The shaft opening is **flush with the ground and half hidden by a thornbush.** On a grassy hillside, that is a textbook way to break a leg or fall seven meters onto stone and water.

- Approach the hillside slowly, probing ahead with a pole if you aren't sure where the shaft is. Look for the fitted rim blocks and the thornbush.
- Arrive in daylight. Never approach at night, when mist curling out of the shaft is the only clue.
- Wear heavy gloves and long sleeves for working around the thornbush. **Do not cut it back more than you absolutely must.** Its root dangles down the shaft toward the water, and it is part of what the place is becoming. Tie branches aside rather than lopping them.

## 2. Anchoring and the Descent

The shaft is "a little over a body's width across" and about four body-lengths deep, roughly 60–80 cm wide and 6–8 m down to the water.

- **Anchor to something independent of the shaft:** a driven stake, a tree, a vehicle, or a buried deadman anchor. Do not trust the rim blocks, the thornbush, or the mortar. Pad the rope where it goes over the rim so it doesn't grind the blocks or fray.
- **Use a rope system you can climb back up while wet, tired, and overheated.** A rope ladder with rigid rungs is kindest to beginners. Otherwise use a proper rope with ascenders and a foot loop, and practice beforehand until it's boring. Chimneying up a smooth-walled wet shaft with nothing but your hands is not a realistic exit plan.
- **Have a partner on the surface** whenever someone is below. They manage the rope, watch the time, and can haul or call for help. The roar below means you cannot shout up to them, so agree on rope-tug signals: one tug okay, two tugs coming up, three or continuous tugs emergency or pull.
- **Descend feet first, slowly.** You'll pass the soil layer, where the fern grows from a mortar joint and moss clings. Keep your body and gear off them. Then comes the rock section. Then you drop into the air lens under the vault and into the water.
- **Don't grab the hanging root.** It won't hold you, and it is "reaching and not quite reaching." Let it keep reaching.

## 3. The Air: Test Before You Breathe It

This is the most important safety point and the least obvious one.

The spring is depositing **travertine**, and it releases **bubbles that collect under the vault.** Travertine forms when carbon-dioxide-rich water loses its CO₂, so this spring is almost certainly degassing. CO₂ is heavier than air, and the chamber's air is a thin pocket pressed between ceiling and water. That means **the layer of air right at the water's surface, exactly where your face will be when you come up to breathe, is the most likely place for CO₂ to pool.** The spring may also carry traces of hydrogen sulfide or radon, depending on the rock.

- **Bring a multi-gas monitor** that reads at least O₂, CO₂, and H₂S. Lower it on a line to just above the water surface and leave it for several minutes before anyone goes down. Check readings near the shaft and, if you can, at the edges of the air lens.
- **Plan around the day's ventilation.** Night convection is visible: mist rises up the shaft. Midday is warmer and may be more stagnant. Check the air at the time you'll actually be there.
- **Clip the monitor to yourself** while inside, at water level, with a vibrating or flashing alarm. You won't hear an audible one.
- **Never breathe from the gas skin.** The "silvery skin" of collected bubbles on the vault underside is spring gas, not air. Do not surface into it, and don't surface near the east end where the bubble threads rise.
- **Know the symptoms of CO₂ buildup:** headache, rapid breathing, a feeling of air hunger even while breathing, and confusion. If you feel any of them, leave immediately rather than pushing on.
- On days when the spring "pulses harder," the roar rises, the haze thickens, and more gas likely comes with it. **If you arrive and the roar is noticeably louder than your last visit, or the water is milky, retest the air and consider postponing.**

## 4. The Overhead: This Is a Cave-Diving Environment

The air lens is thin. The waterline sits only a hand's breadth below where the vault begins curving, so **most of the ceiling area has little or no breathable space under it.** Near the side walls you may have almost no room at all.

- **Your safe surfacing zone is the center of the vault, near the shaft.** Before you dive, float at the surface and look along the length of the chamber so you know where the air actually is.
- **The water is so clear it lies to you.** Surface ripples project moving nets of light onto the ceiling, and from below, the surface, the ceiling, and the reflections blur together. You can easily misjudge whether you're under air or under stone. Always ascend with one hand raised overhead, and always turn back toward the bright column under the shaft.
- **Freedive only. No hyperventilating. Never alone.** One person dives while the other watches from the surface. Shallow-water blackout gives no warning, and the warm water makes it worse because your body burns oxygen faster when it's hot.
- **Keep your dives short and shallow.** The room is small and you can see everything. There is nothing to go looking for in the corners.
- **Scuba is possible but hard to justify.** Getting tanks down a shoulder-width shaft is miserable, the overhead is total, and this is a very small room. If you do bring it, you need actual cave or overhead training and a guideline back to the air zone. For most visitors, mask, snorkel, and breath-holds are the right tools.

## 5. The Heat: This Is a Hot Bath You Can't Get Out Of

The water is "close to body temperature and a little above," probably 37–40°C. It sounds pleasant, and at first it will be. The danger is that **water at or above body temperature cannot cool you, and neither can the air lens,** which is warm and saturated with humidity. Every stroke you swim and every rope you climb adds heat that has nowhere to go.

- **Limit each session to about 15–20 minutes in the water,** the same guidance given for hot tubs, and less if you're exerting yourself. Then climb out to the surface to cool down.
- **Remember the climb is exertion too.** You'll be hauling yourself up seven meters after soaking in hot water, which is exactly when dizziness and fainting happen. Leave the water before you feel tired, not after.
- **Hydrate well before you go down, and drink again as soon as you come up.** You will sweat in the water without noticing.
- **Don't drink the spring water.** It's mineral-heavy and deep-sourced, and people downstream depend on it.
- **Stay out entirely** if you're pregnant, have heart or blood pressure issues, have been drinking, or are taking medications that affect heat tolerance.
- **Know the warning signs of overheating:** a pounding head, nausea, a racing heartbeat, lightheadedness, or feeling oddly lazy and content. Any of these means leave now.
- **No thick wetsuit.** You'll cook. Wear a thin rash guard and leggings or a very thin suit, purely for abrasion protection.

## 6. The Stone: Everything Is Sharp

The cutting is fresh. "The arrises of the vault ribs are still sharp enough to cut a palm," and the chisel grooves on every wall are unsoftened.

- **Wear thin neoprene or rubber-palmed gloves** and cover your knees and elbows. The rash guard and leggings do double duty here.
- **Keep off the walls wherever you can.** Float and hover. You'll want to steady yourself against the stone, especially while looking at the tool marks, but it will abrade skin and gear alike.
- **Protect the ropes.** Where the rope enters the vault from the shaft, the edge may be sharp new stone. Pad it.
- **Don't touch the lime patches.** The mortar around the two plugs in the dark band on the north wall is still curing and whiter than the rest. Don't press, pick at, or test it.
- **Carry a small wound kit topside.** Clean any cut properly once you're out.

## 7. The Water's Movement: The Drain and the Source

Two openings matter.

**The overflow channel (west wall, at the waterline):** a squared trough two hands wide, running full, dropping over a carved step into an unseen basin inside the wall.
- The pull may feel gentle in the chamber but concentrates at the mouth. **Keep your hands, feet, hair, and gear away from it.** Anything that drifts in is gone, and it could lodge in the channel.
- **Never block it, even briefly.** The whole chamber's water level is pinned by that channel. Obstruct it and the water rises, and the thin air lens is the first thing to disappear. It's also someone's water supply downslope.
- **Tether everything:** mask straps, camera, gas monitor, and snorkel keepers. Loose items will migrate west with the current. You can see that the current sweeps grit that way.

**The spring opening (east end, near the floor):** the one rough, undressed hole in the room.
- Water pushes *out* here, so there's no suction. But it's hotter than the rest of the water, it's where gas enters, and the "knocking" means pressure pulses move through the rock behind it. **Look, don't reach in.**
- **Don't stand on or scrape the travertine crust.** It's the beginning of a centuries-long process and only a fingernail thick. Your boot would erase years of it.

## 8. The Sound: Prepare to Be Deafened and Mute

The roar of the overflow step, magnified by the vault, is continuous and heavy, "like heavy rain on a roof heard from inside a drum." Underwater it becomes a throb in your chest and jaw.

- **Earplugs for the air portions** of the visit, including the time you spend floating in the air lens, which sits right under the amplifying vault. Vented or swimming earplugs work best, since you'll be going under repeatedly. Don't use sealed plugs while diving, because they cause pressure problems.
- **Agree on hand signals beforehand.** At minimum: okay, problem, going up, air feels bad, too hot, and look at this. You won't be able to talk.
- **Expect some disorientation.** Constant loud noise plus a shadowless, glowing room can make it hard to judge up, down, and distance. Take a minute at the surface to settle before your first dive.
- Under the roar you'll still hear the drips of condensation hitting the water, "like someone tapping a pot in a crowded room." It's a strange comfort. Listen for it.

## 9. Your Health, and the Water's

This water currently has almost nothing living in it: no fish, no snails, no insects, just a mat of algae under the light and a haze of green in the corners. Two consequences follow.

**For you:** Warm fresh water can harbor amoebae that are dangerous if forced up the nose. This water is likely too new and deep-sourced for that, but it costs nothing to be careful:
- **Wear a nose clip or a mask that covers your nose,** and don't let water go up your nose when you dive or surface.
- Don't go in with open wounds.

**For the place:** Look at it honestly. The text says "something will eventually come down the shaft or up the channel and live here." **You are something coming down the shaft.** You could be the one who introduces the first snail, the first seed, the first foreign microbe to a sterile new habitat.
- **Clean and fully dry all your gear** before the visit: rope, fins, mask, gloves, and suit. Check for seeds in rope fibers and pockets, mud in boot treads, and water from your last swim somewhere else.
- **No sunscreen, insect repellent, lotion, soap, or scented anything.** It will form a film on the surface, drift into the algae, and flow straight downstream to the village's water. Cover up with clothing instead.
- **Don't urinate in the water.** It's a closed, slow-exchange chamber feeding someone's supply. Go before you descend.
- **Never touch the algae patch.** It is a year-long record of where the sun falls, stretched east to west. Your hand would smear that record.
- **Don't collect anything.** No stone dust, travertine, fern fronds, or moss.

## 10. Timing Your Visit

The chamber is a sundial. When you go determines what you see.

- **Around noon** is why you come: the incandescent hour, when the light plunges through the water, the floor and walls blaze, the tool marks cast tiny shivering shadows, and the spring's heat haze becomes a visible trembling column. It's "almost too bright to look at." A mask with a lightly tinted lens helps. Above water, let your eyes adjust rather than staring straight up the shaft.
- **Plan a late-morning descent** so you're settled in the water and have checked the air before the sun reaches the floor. The ideal session is timed so the column of light is directly on the floor during one of your 15–20-minute soaks.
- **Be aware that you'll block the light while you're in the shaft.** The person descending eclipses the room. Coordinate so no one is in the shaft during the brightest window.
- **Dawn and dusk** are dim, gray-blue or warm-dark, and the roar feels louder. They're good for experiencing the place's mood but worse for safety, since the overhead, the surface, and your partner are all harder to see. If you want a dim visit, bring a waterproof dive light, used sparingly.
- **Get out before dusk.** You'll climb out into cooling evening air soaking wet, and steam will rise off your body the way it rises from the shaft. **Keep a towel and warm, dry layers at the top.** Going from 39°C water to a cool hillside causes a real chill.
- **Avoid the day after heavy rain.** The water turns milky, the tool marks vanish, and visibility drops. It is beautiful, "a room made of cloud," but that's also when you can't find the air lens by sight. If you want to see it that way, stay near the shaft and don't dive at all.
- **Check the spring's mood when you arrive.** A louder roar, thicker haze, and grit on the floor mean a surge day: more heat, more gas, more flow. Consider waiting.

## 11. The Human Question

Someone cut this room recently. The stone dust hasn't washed out of the grooves, the mortar hasn't aged, and the water leaves toward "some field or village downslope." The builders exist, they know about this place, and it probably matters to them. It may be a water supply, a sacred spring, or both.

- **Be discreet.** Don't leave rope rigged, anchors in the ground, trampled paths, or cut branches.
- **If locals find you,** you are a stranger climbing into their water source. Be humble, get out, and don't argue. Don't plan a visit that depends on nobody noticing.
- **Leave no trace at all,** including scuffs on the rim blocks.
