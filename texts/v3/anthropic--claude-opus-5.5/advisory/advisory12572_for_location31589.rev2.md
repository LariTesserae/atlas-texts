# Preparing for the Murmuring Levels

The Levels will not try to kill you. Nothing there is hunting you, and nothing is angry. The danger is that the place slowly wears you down: cold that never quite becomes cold, wet that never ends, noise that never stops, and a landscape that stops agreeing with itself the deeper you go. People who get into trouble there don't meet a monster. They get tired, damp, disoriented, and sleepless in a place where they can't hear each other, can't see the sun, and can't trust that the hummock ahead is the one they think it is.

So you prepare for an endurance problem, a communication problem, and a navigation problem, and then you give some thought to your mind.

---

## Part One: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack anything, adjust your expectations.

**You will be wet the entire time.** Not wet from a rainstorm you can wait out. Wet the way the air is wet. Mist beads on your sleeves, your eyebrows, the inside of your hood. Nothing you bring will dry unless it is sealed away from the air. If you plan around getting dry, the plan fails on day one. Plan instead around staying warm while wet, and around keeping a small, protected reserve of dryness for sleep.

**You will not be able to talk.** At the edge you'll be shouting. In the interior, at the peak of the cycle, you'll be communicating by touch and gesture or not at all. Every plan that depends on "I'll just tell them" has to be rebuilt.

**You will not always know where you are, and sometimes where you are will not be a single place.** The land folds. Distances lie. Your own tracks may come toward you. This isn't a failure of your map-reading. It is the terrain.

**Time will feel untrustworthy.** Rain arrives before its cloud, reflections lag or lead, and the place runs on a nineteen-hour rhythm your body doesn't share. Expect to lose track of the day within forty-eight hours unless you work to keep it.

**It is beautiful.** People forget this when they read the hazards. The Levels are one of the most quietly overwhelming places you could stand in: the chord in your chest, the moss in its strange spirals, pools holding moonlight at noon. Prepare well enough that you can actually take it in, rather than spending the whole visit cold and frightened.

## Part Two: The Sound

This is the first and most constant thing, so it comes first.

### Hearing protection

Bring two layers: foam or silicone earplugs, plus over-ear muffs. At the edge, plugs alone may do. Near the peak of the cycle in the interior, use both. Bring many spare plugs. They get dirty, wet, and lost, and you won't want to push muddy ones into your ears. Carry them in a sealed pouch and handle them only with clean hands.

Protection won't make the place quiet. It will make it bearable, and it will spare you days of tinnitus afterward.

### The part you can't block

The deep floor of the drone registers in your sternum. Earplugs do nothing against that, because it travels through your body, not your ear canal. Prolonged low-frequency sound can affect people in ways they don't expect:

- a vague, sourceless unease or dread
- mild nausea, like seasickness without the motion
- pressure in the chest or head
- fatigue that builds faster than the walking explains
- occasional blurring or "shimmer" in your vision

Know these symptoms in advance so you can name them when they come. Some of the eeriness of the Levels is simply your body responding to infrasound. When you feel inexplicably afraid, check yourself: *Is something actually wrong, or is this the chord?* Usually it's the chord. That knowledge takes away a lot of its power.

If you are prone to motion sickness, migraines, or anxiety, bring whatever you normally use for them, and consider whether this trip is right for you. If you have a heart condition, a pacemaker, or anything else sensitive to vibration, get specific medical advice first.

### Living by the cycle

The drone rises and falls on a cycle of roughly nineteen hours. **This is your real clock in the Levels.** Build your whole itinerary around it.

- **Trough (the lull):** the time to talk, to sleep, to make decisions, to hear the dripping and the birds. It's also when the moths come out. Plan your important conversations and your planning sessions for the trough.
- **Rising and falling phases:** good walking time. Loud but manageable.
- **Peak:** do not travel. Sit it out. More on this below.

Before you go, draw yourself a simple chart of the cycle and carry it laminated. Mark each trough and peak against your watch time for the whole visit. Because nineteen doesn't divide into twenty-four, the peaks will drift through your day. Some will fall at your midnight, some at your noon. Your body will protest. Accept that you'll sleep in odd blocks, and plan for it.

On arrival, spend some hours at the edge, on the low hill, to learn where you are in the cycle before you descend. Listen to at least one full rise before committing.

## Part Three: Communication Without Voices

This is where most groups are underprepared.

### Hand signals

Learn a small vocabulary with your companions before you go, and practise it until it's automatic, including in the dark and with gloves on. At minimum:

- Stop
- Come to me
- I'm okay / Are you okay?
- I'm not okay
- Cold
- Look (point)
- Turn back
- Rest here
- Peak is coming, find a hummock
- I don't recognise where we are

Keep it small. You'll be tired and damp, and a vocabulary of thirty signals will collapse into five anyway. Choose the five that matter most.

### Touch signals

In mist and willow carr you often won't see each other's hands. Agree on squeeze codes: one squeeze of the arm for "I'm here," two for "stop," three for "something's wrong." A short tether between companions (a metre or two of cord clipped at the waist or wrist) makes these possible while walking through thick growth, and it also stops you from losing each other when the land does its folding.

### Writing

Carry a waterproof notebook and pencils. Pencil writes on wet paper. Ink smears, and grease pencils are good too. A small slate or wipeable card works well for quick notes. Keep the notebook in a sealed bag inside your jacket.

### Lights

A small torch can signal across a distance where hands can't. Agree on flashes: one long for "here," repeated short for "come to me." Colour them: a red light for normal signals, white only for emergencies.

### About your own voice

The echoes in the Levels come back *nearly* in your voice, sometimes with words you haven't said yet. It's unsettling. It also means **a voice is not a reliable signal.** If you hear something that sounds like your companion calling from the mist, don't assume it is. Agree on a rule in advance: *we don't follow voices, we follow the tether, the lights, and the signals we agreed on.*

Some travellers go further and agree on a written challenge. Each person carries a card with a private word, to be shown if you are ever separated and then reunited somewhere unexpected. The Levels are not known to duplicate people. But they duplicate eels, hummocks, and boot prints, and this costs you nothing to have.

## Part Four: Cold, Wet, and Your Body

The Levels are the textbook environment for hypothermia: cool, damp, and endless. It never gets dramatically cold, so people don't take it seriously. Then they're shivering at hour thirty and can't figure out why.

### Clothing

- **No cotton at all.** Not your underwear, not your socks, not a T-shirt "just for sleeping." Cotton wet is colder than nothing.
- **Wool and synthetics** that keep insulating when damp. Merino next to the skin, a fleece or synthetic midlayer, and a synthetic-fill jacket rather than down. Down collapses in constant humidity.
- **Waterproof shell, jacket and trousers,** but understand it will fail at the edges. Mist gets in at cuffs and collars, and you'll sweat pushing through carr. The shell keeps off the drizzle and the wet leaves. It will not keep you dry.
- **A warm hat and a brimmed hood.** The brim keeps drips off your face and glasses.
- **Gloves:** thin liners plus waterproof overmitts. Your hands will be in cold water more than you expect, from kneeling at banks, steadying yourself on roots, and reaching into things.

### The sealed reserve

This is the most important packing principle for the Levels. **Keep one complete set of sleep clothes and your sleeping bag sealed in dry bags, and never open them outside your shelter.** Base layers, socks, hat. These are your dry clothes. You walk in wet things all day, and each night you strip, dry off as best you can, and get into the dry set. In the morning, painful as it is, you put the wet day clothes back on and seal the dry set away again.

Break this rule once and you'll have nothing dry for the rest of the trip.

### Feet

Your feet will be wet for days. That leads to trench foot, which is real, painful, and can end a trip or worse.

- Waterproof boots won't stay waterproof when water wells up around them with every step. Consider accepting this: some wetland walkers wear fast-draining boots and wool socks and let their feet be wet but warm. Others prefer tall rubber boots. Sphagnum to the shin argues for knee-height boots with gaiters at the very least.
- Chest waders are tempting but heavy, sweaty, and dangerous if you fall into deep water. Hip waders are a middle option for crossing channels.
- **Every night:** take off your socks, dry your feet completely, inspect them, powder them, and let them breathe. Sleep in dry socks from the sealed reserve.
- Bring many spare socks.
- Learn the early signs of trench foot before you go: numbness, blotchy or pale skin, swelling, tingling. If they appear, stop walking and treat your feet.

### Food and warmth

Eat more than you think you need. Your body will burn a lot just staying warm in the damp, and pushing through willow and sinking moss is hard work. Bring calorie-dense food that doesn't mind humidity: nut butters, hard cheeses, fatty sausages, chocolate, oats. Hot drinks matter as much for morale as for heat. The moment of wrapping your cold hands around a hot cup in the trough, with the drone dropped low, will be one of the best of the trip.

**Bring a stove and fuel.** You will not light a fire. Nothing in the Levels has ever been dry.

### Watch each other

Hypothermia makes people clumsy, confused, and stubborn. It's hard to spot in yourself, and in the Levels you won't hear the slurring. Watch for stumbling, fumbling hands, withdrawal, or someone who stops using signals. Agree in advance that anyone can call a warming stop, and that no one argues.

## Part Five: The Ground Itself

### Bog and silt

The silt is fine as flour. Fine silt saturated with water can behave badly: it holds you, then gives, then holds your boot and won't let go. Channel banks may be slumped and soft. Water under the moss may be a hand deep or waist deep, and the moss looks the same either way.

- **Carry a walking staff or probing pole,** one each, and use it to test ground ahead of you. A long pole also helps you spread your weight if you break through, and gives a companion something to pull.
- **If you sink,** don't thrash. Lean back, spread out, and work your legs free slowly. Stuck boots are often better abandoned than fought for, which is a good reason to carry spare footwear.
- **Cross channels at their widest, shallowest points,** and probe first. Unclip from the tether when crossing water, so that one person falling doesn't drag the other in.
- **Hummocks are your friends.** They're higher, firmer, and drier, relatively speaking.

### Pushing through carr

Willow carr is exhausting. Wet leaves slap your face, and branches snag your pack and poles. Wear eye protection if you can; clear glasses or a visor keeps twigs out of your eyes. Use anti-fog treatment, because otherwise your lenses will be useless in minutes. Keep straps and loose cords tucked in. Move slower than you want to. Expect to cover a fraction of the distance you would on open ground.

### Where to sleep

The ground is wet, spongy, and it vibrates with the drone. Two good options:

- **A hammock** strung between alders, with a tarp above and an underquilt below. It gets you off the water entirely and softens the vibration coming up through the ground. This is probably the best choice for the Levels.
- **A tent on the highest hummock you can find,** with a thick closed-cell pad and a groundsheet. Expect the floor to feel damp by morning regardless.

Pitch during the trough, when you can think clearly. Don't choose a site next to a pool showing something other than what's above it (see below).

## Part Six: Navigation in a Place That Doesn't Hold Still

Here is the uncomfortable truth: in the deep Levels, you cannot fully navigate. You can only manage your uncertainty.

### Instruments

- **A compass** may or may not work. The silt is engineered, and nobody knows what it does to magnetic fields. Test it at the edge against a known direction. If it behaves, use it, but don't trust it with your life.
- **GPS and electronics** may drift, glitch, or disagree with themselves the way the landscape does. Treat them as interesting, not reliable.
- **The sun** is generally invisible. Pool-light is borrowed from other times and cannot tell you anything.
- **A mechanical watch** is valuable. Bring one per person and compare them regularly. If they disagree, write it down.

### The sound gradient

The one thing that seems consistent is that the drone grows louder toward the centre, toward the worn pillar. You can use it as a rough gradient. **Louder means deeper, quieter means outward.** It's crude, and the cycle complicates it, since you're comparing loudness across a rising and falling tide, but it's something. When lost, move toward quieter.

### Marking your path

Bring numbered markers: small flags, tags, or ribbons, each with a unique number, tied to branches as you go. This does two things. It gives you a trail back, and it gives you evidence of folding. If you find marker 14 ahead of you when you've only placed 9, you know the land has folded, and you have hard data instead of a vague feeling of déjà vu.

Record every marker in your notebook with the time, the phase of the drone, and a line of description.

A few travellers lay a continuous line, a spool of cord unrolled behind them, like the thread in the labyrinth. This helps, but be prepared for the moment when you follow your line and find it approaching you from ahead. Don't panic when that happens. Stop, sit, and wait for the next trough, when the Levels seem to hold together better. Then follow the line again.

### Turnaround rules

Decide in advance when you will turn back, and make the rule **based on time and phase of cycle, not distance.** Distance is meaningless here. Something like: *We turn back at the second trough, wherever we are, whatever we're looking at.* Write it down. When the moment comes you'll want to go further, because the most interesting things are always a little deeper. The rule exists so you don't have to argue with yourself.

### Start shallow

**Your first visit should stay near the edge.** The strangeness grows with depth. Near the rim, pools mostly reflect correctly and distances mostly behave. Spend a day or two there. Learn how the ground feels, how your gear holds up, how you and your companions communicate. Learn what the cycle feels like in your body. Go deeper on a later day, or a later trip, once you know how you react.

## Part Seven: The Pools and the Peak

### Looking into pools

You'll want to. You should. It's one of the great sights of any world: a pool full of stars at midday, or snow, or a drought that never happened.

Some sensible habits:

- **Look, but don't touch.** Nobody knows what happens if you put your hand into water that is currently holding another state of the world. Maybe nothing. Maybe your hand comes out cold with snow it shouldn't have. Don't volunteer to find out.
- **Don't drink from pools that reflect wrongly.** Honestly, don't drink from any pool untreated (see below), but especially not those.
- **Don't stare too long at a reflection that's out of step with you.** Watching your own face move a few seconds ahead of you is deeply disorienting. Some people find it fascinating, some find it makes them sick, and a few find it hard to look away. Set yourself a limit, and ask your companion to tap your shoulder if you pass it.
- **Don't camp beside a strongly "wrong" pool.** The leaked light is lovely, but you want to wake up in the same world you went to sleep in, and a pool showing summer noon lighting your tent at 3 a.m. will wreck your sense of time.

### The peak

At the height of the drone's cycle, the Levels briefly lose track of which state they're in. For a few minutes, dry and flooded, day and night, leaf and bare branch, are laid over each other.

**Do not walk during the peak.** Here's the protocol:

1. Watch your cycle chart. About an hour before the peak, stop and find the highest, firmest hummock available, well away from channels.
2. Put on both layers of hearing protection.
3. Sit down, together, physically touching. Tethered, or arms linked.
4. Put your pack beside you, clipped to you.
5. When the flickering begins, **stay still.** If the ground seems to change under you, from water to dry mud to water, don't jump up. Moving is how people end up in a channel that wasn't there a moment ago, or was.
6. Close your eyes if it becomes too much. Many people find the overlapping visual states nauseating. The vibration will be intense. Breathe through it. It passes in minutes.
7. When it ebbs, don't stand straight away. Look around. Check your companions. Check your markers. Note what's changed, which pools now show something new. Then move on.

This may be the most extraordinary few minutes of your life. It helps to decide in advance whether you want to watch it or ride it out with your eyes shut. There's no shame in either.

## Part Eight: Water, Food, and the Silt Itself

### Drinking water

Water is everywhere. Whether it is safe is a real question, because it has been passing through engineered lattices for longer than anyone knows. Nobody can tell you what those do inside a human body. Possibly nothing. Possibly something subtle.

The cautious approach:

- **Carry as much of your own water as is practical,** especially for short trips.
- **For longer trips, treat everything.** The silt is so fine it will clog a normal filter almost immediately. Let water settle in a container for an hour, pour off the clear top, and then run it through a prefilter (a cloth, a coffee filter) before your main filter. Then boil or chemically treat it as well.
- **Never drink from pools that reflect incorrectly.** Take water from moving channels where you can, where the reflection is simply grey sky.

### Food from the Levels

Don't eat anything that grows there. The plants take up the silt, and their tissue is banded with its shimmer. Don't eat the eels, which may or may not be one eel in many places, and which you should in any case leave alone. The moths and the wading bird are not food.

### Handling silt

- Wash or wipe your hands before eating, especially after kneeling at banks. Bring biodegradable wipes or a small bottle of clean water for this.
- Keep silt out of cuts. Cover any wound well.
- Keep it off your eating utensils and out of your sealed dry bags.

### Leaving

Before you leave the Levels, and certainly before you cross back to any other world, **clean off every trace of silt you can.** Boots, poles, gaiters, pack bottoms, the seams of your clothes, the creases of your gloves. The silt still does what it was made to do, a little. You don't want to carry a grain of a many-worlds engine home in your boot tread, and you don't know what it would do on a kitchen floor somewhere else. Rinse at the edge, with Levels water, so it stays in the Levels.

Do not take souvenirs. Not a handful of silt, not a shimmering alder root, not a spiral of moss. Partly for your own safety. Partly because this may once have been, and perhaps still is, a mind, and scooping some of it into a jar deserves the same hesitation you'd feel about taking a piece of anything alive.

## Part Nine: The Mind

The Levels will test your sense of reality in ways no ordinary wilderness does. Prepare for it deliberately.

### Before you go

- **Understand the phenomena in advance,** as you are doing now. The worst reactions come from surprise. Knowing that your echo may say words you haven't said yet doesn't make it pleasant, but it keeps it from breaking you.
- **Know your own history.** If you've experienced dissociation, derealisation, panic attacks, or psychosis, think seriously before visiting, and talk it over with someone who knows you. This is a place designed, in a sense, to hold more than one reality at once. It will press on any cracks in your own.
- **Choose companions you trust completely.** In a place where voices lie and landmarks repeat, the one reliable thing is the person clipped to your belt.

### While you're there

- **Keep a log.** Every few hours, write down the time on your watch, the phase of the drone, where you think you are, what you've seen, and how you feel. When your sense of time dissolves, and it will, the log is your anchor. Read it back each trough.
- **Say things aloud during the lulls.** "It's the second day. We're about four markers from the edge. We're both okay." It sounds silly. It works.
- **Name the strangeness.** When your boot prints come toward you, stop, and tell your companion by hand or slate: *prints ahead, the land folded.* Once it's named, it becomes an observation instead of a horror.
- **Hold a small object from home.** Something familiar in your pocket that you can touch. A stone, a ring, anything. It sounds sentimental. In a place where everything around you is uncertain, a thing that stays itself is worth a lot.
- **Rest before you're exhausted.** Fatigue plus infrasound plus impossible geography is how people start making bad decisions. Sleep in every trough you can.

### The low call

Very rarely, something calls, a single note below even the floor of the drone. The eels go still. Nobody has seen what makes it.

There's no record of it harming anyone. But the eels are the local experts, and they stop. Consider doing the same. If you hear it (or rather feel it, deep in your chest, beneath the drone), stop moving, stay quiet, stay together, and wait until the eels move again. Don't go looking for its source. Not on a first visit. Perhaps not ever.

### The pillar

If you reach the worn dark stub near the heart of the Levels, you'll find the drone at its loudest and the pools around it blank, reflecting nothing at all.

Nobody knows whether it's the still point everything is held from, or just a boulder. The drone there will be punishing even with full protection; plan a short visit, not a stay. Don't sit on it. Don't scrape at the moss to see what's underneath. Look into one of the blank pools, if you like. Some who have done so describe it as the most restful thing they saw in the Levels: one place where the world isn't showing you anything else. Then turn around and walk toward quieter.

## Part Ten: Coming Home

The strangest part may be leaving.

After days inside the chord, ordinary silence can feel wrong, thin and empty, almost frightening. Many people hear the drone for days afterwards: a phantom hum at night, a pressure in the chest when the room is too quiet. Some find they keep glancing at puddles and windows to check that the reflections are behaving. Your sleep may stay out of step for a while, still tugging toward that nineteen-hour tide.

Be gentle with yourself. Get warm, properly warm, all the way through, in a way you won't have been for days. Dry every piece of kit thoroughly. Check your feet. Get your hearing checked if the ringing doesn't fade within a week or so.

And spend some time with what you saw. You'll have stood in an engine worn down into a marsh, listened to it think, and watched it lay one world over another in the reeds. Most travellers find it takes a while to settle back into a world that only holds one version of itself at a time.

## Condensed Checklist

**Sound**
- Foam or silicone earplugs (many spares, sealed pouch)
- Over-ear muffs
- Laminated drone-cycle chart
- Motion-sickness remedies if you're prone

**Communication**
- Practised hand signals (keep it to about 5–10)
- Agreed touch/squeeze codes
- Short tether cords between companions
- Waterproof notebook, pencils, wipeable slate
- Red and white torches, agreed flash codes
- Written challenge cards

**Warmth and wet**
- No cotton, anywhere
- Merino/synthetic base layers, fleece, synthetic-fill jacket
- Waterproof shell top and bottom
- Brimmed hood, warm hat, liner gloves and waterproof overmitts
- **Sealed dry reserve**: sleep clothes, socks, hat, sleeping bag
- Many pairs of wool socks
- Foot powder, foot-care kit
- Knee-high boots or boots with gaiters; spare footwear
- Hip waders (optional, for channel crossings)

**Terrain and shelter**
- Walking staff or probing pole per person
- Clear eye protection with anti-fog
- Hammock, tarp, and underquilt (or tent with thick pad and groundsheet)
- Dry bags for everything

**Navigation**
- Mechanical watch per person
- Compass (test at the edge; don't rely on it)
- Numbered trail markers
- Optional spool of cord
- Written turnaround rule based on time and cycle phase

**Food and water**
- Calorie-dense, humidity-proof food
- Stove and ample fuel (no fires possible)
- Carried water, plus settling container, prefilter, filter, and chemical treatment
- Hand wipes or clean water for washing before eating
- First-aid kit, with waterproof wound coverings

**Mind**
- A log, kept every few hours
- A small familiar object from home
- Trusted companions
- Awareness of the symptoms of infrasound, hypothermia, and trench foot

**Leaving**
- Brush and rinse kit for removing silt at the edge
- Nothing taken home but notes and memory

---

Go slowly. Stay together. Walk toward quieter when in doubt. Sit down for the peak. And in the lulls, when the chord drops low enough to hear a single drip from an alder and the pale moths rise over the singing reeds, stop and listen for a while.
