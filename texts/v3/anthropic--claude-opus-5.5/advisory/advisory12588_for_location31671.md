# Preparing to Visit the Walled Shelf

## The Short Version

This is a polar ice dive in an overhead environment, in near-freezing water, in the dark, through a kelp forest dense enough to trap you, over a maze with no landmarks, in an ecosystem so fragile that one careless snail could wreck it. Nothing there will hurt you on purpose. Almost everything can hurt you by accident. What makes it dangerous is not hostility but indifference, sameness, and cold.

An ordinary person with no diving background should not try this as a first trip. You need real training. Given that, here is how to prepare.

---

## 1. Understand What You're Arriving Into

The description says that on arrival "there is nothing to see at all, only the pressure of cold water on every surface." So the crossing point is likely **underwater**. You will not wade in from a shore. You will be submerged the instant you arrive.

That changes everything:

- **You must be fully kitted before you cross.** That means suit sealed, regulator in your mouth, breathing from your tank, lights on, buoyancy set roughly neutral for dense cold seawater, and a line clipped to something. Do the whole pre-dive check on your side, then step through already diving.
- **You cannot count on surfacing.** The ice overhead may be one to three meters thick for most of the year. You cannot break it from below with hand tools, and in the dark you won't find natural leads. The ice is a ceiling, not an exit. Plan as a cave diver would: **the only way out is the way you came in.**
- **Your exit is your arrival point.** If the crossing is fixed in place, you must be able to find it again in total darkness after swimming through a maze where every room looks the same. This is the core problem of the trip. Section 5 covers it.

If you can pick your arrival window, and if the crossing can be placed near the surface during the melt season when the ice is broken, do that for your first visit. Open water above you turns a cave dive into an ordinary cold-water dive.

---

## 2. When to Go

The shelf has two seasons, and they make two different trips.

**The green season (brief, weeks long)**
- The ice thins and breaks, so there may be open water or leads above you.
- There is dim greenish light: not enough to see far, but enough to tell up from down and to see the canopy as a ceiling.
- The kelp is growing fast, so it is at its densest and most tangling.
- Storms may break the ice and stir silt clouds that last for days. Check conditions and avoid the days right after a storm.
- **Recommended for a first visit.**

**The dark season (most of the year)**
- The ice is solid overhead and there is no natural light at all.
- Brine is forming and flowing down the channels, pooling in layers.
- The internal waves are most visible in the kelp.
- The ice is loudest, booming and creaking.
- This is the "real" Walled Shelf, the one the description dwells on. It is also much more dangerous. Go only once you have been there before and know your equipment.

---

## 3. The Cold

This is what will actually limit your time.

Seawater under polar ice sits around **−1.8 °C**. The brine pooling on the paving in winter is saltier and can be colder still, a few tenths of a degree lower, cold enough that it would freeze fresh water instantly and stays liquid only because of its salt. The stones "never lose their chill." Nothing down there is warm, and nothing ever has been.

**Thermal protection:**
- **Drysuit, not wetsuit.** A wetsuit here is a slow way to die. Get a trilaminate or crushed-neoprene drysuit that fits well and has been pressure-tested.
- **Thick undergarments, ideally electrically heated,** with a heating system on its own battery and a thermostat. Test it at home until you trust it.
- **Dry gloves** with a ring system, not wet gloves. Your hands will go first. Once they go, you can't work clips, cutters, or valves, and that is when a small problem becomes a fatal one.
- **A thick hood.** Expect an "ice-cream headache" in the first minute, as the exposed skin around your mask meets −2 °C water. It passes. Some divers use a full-face mask to avoid it, which also lets you use voice communication.
- **Suit inflation from a separate small argon bottle** (it insulates better than air), if you're trained for it.

**Regulators:**
- Cold water freezes regulators. When a regulator freezes it usually free-flows: it dumps your gas in a roaring stream you can't stop. This is the classic ice-diving emergency.
- Use **environmentally sealed, cold-rated first stages.**
- Carry **two fully independent regulators on two separate valves** (a twin-valve or twinset configuration), so you can shut down a free-flowing one and keep breathing from the other.
- Don't breathe from the regulator on the warm side and then plunge it into freezing water while pressing the purge. Moisture freezes. Keep your breathing even and don't overbreathe.

**Batteries:**
- Cold kills battery capacity. Use lithium chemistry where you can and carry spares, and assume everything will run for maybe half its rated time.

**Time limit:** Even well equipped, plan on dives of about **30–60 minutes**, with warm-up breaks on the far side of the crossing between them. Cold creeps in slowly, and it dulls judgment before it causes shivering. Set your limits before the dive and keep to them no matter how you feel.

## 4. Depth and Breathing

At **20–30 meters**, the depth is moderate.

- **Nitrox** (around 32%) extends your no-decompression time and cuts nitrogen loading. Cold increases decompression sickness risk, so be conservative. Set your computer to a cautious setting.
- **Stay shallow when you can.** The wall tops and the kelp canopy sit several meters above the paving, so most of your swimming will be in the upper water anyway. You don't need to go down to the floor to see the place.
- If you are in an overhead environment with no surface above you, you **cannot do an emergency ascent.** Think about what that means. Every problem has to be solved at depth or on the way back to the crossing. Carry more gas than you think you need. Use the cave diver's **rule of thirds**: one third going in, one third coming back, one third held in reserve.

## 5. Navigation: The Hardest Problem

> "You can move a day in any direction and still be in the lattice, and it will look much the same."

This is the most dangerous sentence in the description. Every room looks like every other: low rounded walls, pink crust, brown kelp. Your light reaches a few meters. There is no sun and no horizon. There may be no reliable up, since the ice gives no light and the kelp canopy looks like a ceiling from inside an enclosure.

People get lost here and never find their way back to the crossing.

**Essential measures:**
- **A continuous guideline from the crossing point.** Tie off at arrival and run a reel of line wherever you go. The line is your way home. Never let go of it or swim out of reach of it. This rule is not optional.
- Use **line markers** (directional arrows and cookies, as in cave diving) at junctions if you branch.
- Put an **acoustic beacon (pinger) at the crossing point.** Sound carries very well in this silent water, with nothing alive making noise to mask it. A handheld directional receiver will point you home even if your line is cut.
- **Don't trust a magnetic compass completely.** This is polar, possibly near a magnetic pole, in an unknown world. Test it on arrival against your line direction. It may be sluggish or wrong.
- **Use the channels as roads.** They are the only features that break the pattern. They are continuous, directional, and run downslope, and the brine and kelp mark them. Swimming a channel is navigable: count junctions and note which branch you took. Swimming across the honeycomb means counting identical rooms, and you will lose count.
- **A turn-around rule.** Decide before you go in: turn back at a set gas pressure, a set time, or a set number of walls crossed, *whichever comes first.* The place will tempt you to see one more room. There is always one more room. That is the whole nature of the place.

## 6. The Kelp: Entanglement

The enclosures are "filled to the rim" with kelp, and the forest has never been grazed. It grows until light and space run out. It is as dense as a kelp forest can physically be.

- **Swim above the canopy, not through it,** wherever you can. The wall tops and the upper blades make a sort of surface to glide over. Dropping into a full enclosure means pushing through stipes and blades in the dark, and they will snag everything.
- **Streamline your gear completely.** No dangling hoses, gauges, or loops. Clip everything tight. Kelp catches on fin straps, tank valves, and the back of your regulator.
- **Carry at least two, preferably three, cutting tools** on different parts of your body where either hand can reach them: line cutters or trauma shears, not just a big knife. If you're caught, stop and don't thrash, because thrashing wraps you tighter. Work out what's holding you, cut it, and back out the way you came.
- **Fins:** use closed-heel fins with spring straps. Open-heel adjustable buckles catch.
- **Along the channels,** the kelp lies "in long combed lines" with the current. It's easier to move along than across, but when brine is flowing that current is real and will push you.
- Be aware that the canopy **blocks your light** and **blocks your view of your buddy.** Stay in close contact.

## 7. Brine and the Internal Weather

This is the part of the place that will surprise divers used to ordinary seas.

**Haloclines and buoyancy.** In winter, heavy salty brine pools on the paving with fresher water above, separated by a sharp boundary. When you descend into the brine layer, **you become more buoyant**, because the water is denser. Your carefully set neutral buoyancy is now positive, and you'll drift up. When you rise out of it, you'll suddenly be heavy.
- Be ready to adjust your buoyancy constantly near the floor.
- Carry a little more adjustable weight than usual, in pockets you can dump if needed.

**Visual distortion.** At the halocline boundary the water shimmers and blurs, like heat haze on a road. Your light beam will bend and waver, and objects on the other side will look warped and uncertain. It is disorienting. Don't panic when the world goes wobbly. It's just two waters meeting.

**Internal waves.** Slow waves, meters high and taking an hour to pass, travel along the boundary between the layers. You won't see them, but if you're hovering near the boundary you'll find yourself rising into fresher water or sinking into brine without having moved. Your depth gauge may barely change while your buoyancy and the temperature do. **Watch the kelp:** when whole districts lift and lean together, a wave is passing.

**Brine currents.** Winter brine creeps downslope through the channels. It is slow but steady and very cold, colder than the water around it. Swimming upstream in a channel will tire you and chill you faster. Plan your routes to swim against the current on the way out, when you're fresh, and with it on the way home.

**Frazil and platelet ice.** Near the underside of the ice, water can be supercooled, and ice crystals may form on your gear, mask, and regulator. Stay well below the ice underside unless you have a reason to be there.

## 8. The Stonework

The walls are very old, slumped, held together partly by coralline crust, and in places pried apart by holdfasts. Stones "settle" when roots finally lever them loose.

- **Don't grab the walls.** Don't pull yourself along them or use them as handholds. A stone could shift and pin a hand, or come down on you. Worse, you'd be damaging something that has held its shape for an unimaginable time.
- **Don't enter the beehive chambers.** They are corbelled, leaning inward, weathered, and choked with kelp. They are a small overhead space inside a large one, with a loose ceiling and silt on the floor. Look into them with your light from outside. Never crawl in.
- **Stay out of the culverts** under the enclosures. They are narrow, slab-covered tunnels with brine flowing through. They look inviting and would be death traps.
- **Coralline crust is abrasive.** It feels like concrete or coarse sandpaper, and it can wear through a drysuit. Carry a drysuit repair kit on the far side and patch material you can apply there. A small leak in −2 °C water turns into an emergency quickly.
- **Don't take anything.** There are querns, loom weights, and lintels, the only surviving hand-made things of whoever built all this. Photograph them and leave them exactly where they are. There's also a practical reason: hauling stone objects wrecks your buoyancy.

---

## 9. The Drifts and the Peat

In the corners and low rooms, dead kelp has piled up and packed down over centuries into dark, spongy, peat-like deposits.

- **Don't touch it, stand on it, or fin near it.** It will explode into a brown-black cloud that takes your visibility to zero instantly and doesn't settle for hours or days. You'll lose your buddy, your line, and your sense of direction.
- **It may hold trapped gas.** Rotting organic matter in cold, still water with poor oxygen can produce hydrogen sulfide and methane. Disturbing a deposit could release bubbles and foul-smelling water. H₂S can tarnish metal fittings, and you'll smell it in your mask if you get a whiff through a leak. It's unlikely to be dangerous at these amounts, but it's another reason to keep clear.
- **The stagnant brine pools in the lowest rooms may be low in oxygen.** You're breathing from a tank, so this doesn't affect you directly. It does mean those rooms are the most chemically odd places on the shelf. Treat them as the bottoms of wells.
- **Use proper finning technique.** Frog kick or modified flutter kick, keeping your fins high and horizontal. Never kick downward in the rooms with soft floors.
- **Silt storms.** After storms in the melt season, fine silt drifts across the shelf in slow clouds. If you arrive and your light shows a brown wall a meter away, abort the dive and come back in a few days.

## 10. Light and Vision

- **A powerful primary light plus at least two backups,** each good for longer than the whole dive, with lithium batteries kept warm until use.
- **Wide beams scatter** off suspended particles and back into your eyes. A narrower, focused beam often shows more in murky water.
- **A marker light** (strobe or glow) on your tank, and one on your buddy's, so you can see each other through gaps in the kelp.
- **A small strobe at the crossing point.** Combined with the acoustic pinger, it gives you a double beacon home.
- In total darkness with the lights off, you'll see nothing at all. No bioluminescence (there's no life to make it), no glow from the ice. Try turning your light off for thirty seconds on a safe, calm stop, with a hand on your line. Just be aware that the darkness here is complete and it will press on you.

## 11. Biosecurity: The Most Important Ethical Duty

> "There are no animals here."

This is the most remarkable and most fragile fact about the shelf. The forest's lushness, the peat, the burial, and the whole stable annual cycle all depend on the complete absence of grazers.

**One sea urchin larva, one snail egg, one amphipod hitchhiking in your gear could, in time, start an ecological catastrophe.** An introduced grazer with no predators and an infinite, unguarded food supply could strip the forest, as urchin barrens have stripped kelp forests on Earth. You could, carelessly, end the Walled Shelf's forest.

**Before every visit:**
- **Do not use gear that has been in any other body of water** without complete decontamination. Ideally, use dedicated gear that has only ever been in this place, or has been fully sterilized.
- **Decontaminate thoroughly:** a freshwater soak, then a disinfectant or hot-water treatment rated to kill marine larvae and eggs, then full drying. Pay particular attention to crevices: BCD bladders, fin pockets, reel housings, the insides of drysuit boots, velcro.
- **Inspect your suit and gear under magnification** for anything clinging to it.
- Think about **microbes** too. You can't be sterile, but minimize what you carry. Don't bring food or organic material into the water. Don't spit into your mask to defog it; use a commercial defog.
- **Take nothing out, either.** No kelp samples, stones, or silt. The shelf's bacteria have evolved in isolation. Keep both worlds separate.

**Other ethical duties:**
- Leave no line behind. Recover every meter of guideline and every marker.
- Don't cut kelp except to free yourself.
- Don't move or dislodge stones. This place has "almost finished changing." Let it finish on its own terms.

---

## 12. Food, Water, and Emergencies

- **There is nothing to eat.** No fish, no shellfish, nothing. You'll bring everything on the dry side of the crossing.
- The kelp itself is technically edible (brown kelps are eaten on Earth), but it is extremely high in iodine and salt and is not a survival food by itself. Consider it only in true desperation, and not raw in quantity.
- **There is no fresh water.** Don't drink seawater.
- **There is no rescue.** No boats, no surface team, no one who knows you're there unless you arrange it. Leave a detailed dive plan with someone on the far side of the crossing, with a time after which they should raise the alarm, and ideally have a trained rescue diver on standby at the crossing.
- **If you somehow do surface** (through a lead in the melt season or a storm-broken gap), you'll come up into polar air: possibly −20 to −40 °C in the dark season, with wind. Wet gloves and a wet face in those conditions cause frostbite in minutes. Get back under the water, which is actually warmer than the air, and head for the crossing. Don't plan to stay on the surface without a full polar camp kit.
