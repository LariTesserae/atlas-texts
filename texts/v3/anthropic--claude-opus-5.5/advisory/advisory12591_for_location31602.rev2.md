# Preparing to Visit the Held Sea of Ammaret

## The Short Version

You are preparing for three trips at once. The first is a multi-day mountain trek to high altitude. The second is a breath-hold diving expedition in warm, mineral water. The third is an extended stay in a place where the air you breathe is a shared, hand-carried, finite resource. The first two are dangerous in ordinary ways. The third is where most visitors go wrong, because it is a matter of manners, and manners are harder to train than lungs.

The single most important physical fact about Ammaret is not written plainly in any description of it, so it goes first:

**The bells are underwater. The air inside them is compressed. Your body will absorb it. You must not rush back to the surface.**

Everything else follows from that, or from the slowness of the place.

---

## Part One: Before You Go

### Learn to Freedive, Properly

This is not optional. Moving through Ammaret means breath-hold swimming between pockets of air, through narrow submerged passages, surrounded by fronds, in dim light. In the dense heart of the city the gaps are seconds long. At the edges they are not. You need to reach the point where fifteen or twenty seconds underwater is boring rather than frightening, and where a full minute is possible in an emergency.

Take a real freediving course with a certified instructor. Learn these things until they are reflex:

- **Never hyperventilate before a breath-hold.** It feels like it helps, but it suppresses your urge to breathe without adding oxygen. That is how people black out underwater without warning. The locals' slow, calm breathing is exactly correct. Copy it.
- **Shallow water blackout** happens near the surface or near the end of a swim, when the pressure drops and your oxygen crashes. The passages between bells run at depth and sometimes upward. Know this.
- **Equalizing your ears.** You will descend at least forty feet on the rope, and bells sit at many depths. Practice the Valsalva and Frenzel techniques until they are effortless. If you have a cold or congested sinuses, do not go. A ruptured eardrum in body-warm mineral water, far from any doctor you would recognize, is a miserable thing.
- **Exhale as you rise after breathing compressed air.** This is the one that will kill an unprepared visitor. In a bell sixty feet down you breathe air at nearly three times surface pressure. If you fill your lungs there and swim *upward* with your breath held, the air expands as the pressure falls. Your lungs can tear. Ordinary freedivers never face this, because they only breathe at the surface. In Ammaret you will breathe at depth. When you leave a bell and your route goes up, let a slow thread of bubbles out as you rise. Watch how the locals do it. They will do it without thinking, and they may not understand why you need to be told.

### Get a Dive Medicine Consultation

Tell a hyperbaric or dive-medicine physician the truth: *I will spend days, perhaps weeks, living in air pockets at depths of forty to one hundred feet, and then I will surface at high altitude.* Watch their face. Then listen carefully to what they say about saturation, decompression, and patent foramen ovale screening.

Ask them specifically:
- How long a stay at a given depth saturates tissue.
- How to stage an ascent when the surface is at altitude. The reduced atmospheric pressure at the rim makes decompression sickness *more* likely than surfacing at sea level would.
- Whether they can prescribe or advise on emergency oxygen.

Bring any medications you depend on in waterproof containers, in quantity. Assume nothing will be replaceable.

### Acclimatize to Altitude

The Held Sea sits in the crown of a mountain high enough that the sun "should be fierce." You climb for days. Spend a week or more at progressively higher elevations beforehand if you can, or build rest days into the climb itself. Altitude sickness on the trek is survivable. Arriving at the rim already depleted and then going straight into the water is not wise.

### Acclimatize to Heat

This is counterintuitive, so think it through. The water is body temperature, and so is the air in the bells. There is nowhere to dump heat. In ordinary diving, cold is the enemy. Here your body cannot cool itself by radiating into the water, and exertion builds heat with no release. Near the vents it is worse.

Heat-train before you go: saunas, hot-weather exercise, whatever your doctor approves. More importantly, learn to move *slowly*. The people of Ammaret move slowly partly because of the drowsy gas and partly, surely, because anyone who rushes in body-warm water becomes feverish and exhausted.

### Build Lung Capacity and Calm

Swim. Do breathing exercises. Practice static apnea with a trained partner, never alone. Practice meditation. The people of Ammaret have enormous patience, and a panicky mind burns oxygen. The most useful skill you can bring is the ability to be enclosed in warm darkness, surrounded by brushing fronds, and not mind.

If you are claustrophobic, seriously reconsider. You will squeeze through short stone tunnels, underwater, in dim light, in order to breathe.

## Part Two: The Climb

### Gear for the Mountain

The approach is ordinary high-mountain trekking: cloud-forest, then scrub, then bare rock. Bring what you would bring for any multi-day ascent: layers, rain gear for the cloud-forest, water treatment, and good food. Then add:

- **Boots with thick, cut-resistant soles.** The rim is fresh volcanic glass, and its cracks and edges have never been weathered. Ropy basalt can be surprisingly slick, and obsidian edges will slice through thin rubber. Think lava-field boots, not trail runners.
- **Heavy gloves**, leather or cut-resistant synthetic. You will put your hands down on this stone, and you will want to.
- **Long trousers and sleeves.** A stumble on fresh glass is a laceration, not a scrape.
- **Awareness of warm rock.** Parts of the rim are still faintly warm. Do not sit or sleep on a patch without testing it first, and do not assume that warm stone is only warm.

### The Rim

When you arrive, stop. You will feel that the stone is young before you understand it, through the soles of your feet and in the silence, with no lichen or soil or softened edges anywhere. Let yourself feel that. It is the first lesson of the place: nothing here is finished.

**The stones in the bubble-hollows.** Someone has placed a small stone inside each burst glass bubble, and no one remembers why. Do not remove them. Do not photograph them in a way that requires moving them. Whether you should add one of your own is not a question you can answer from outside. If someone from the city is with you, watch what they do. If you are alone, leave them as they are. A custom whose reason has been forgotten is often the kind people protect most fiercely.

**Rest at the rim before descending.** Let your body settle after the climb. Hydrate. Eat. Sleep a night if you can. You are about to enter a place where you will be sluggish and dreamy, and you want to enter it rested.

## Part Three: Going Under

### The Descent

You go down on a weighted rope of twisted weed-fiber with a stone tied at the bottom. The water is slightly denser than ordinary water, so you are more buoyant than you expect. That is why the stone matters. Hold the rope. Equalize early and often: every few feet, before you feel pressure, not after.

The water closes over you at the temperature of your own body. Many visitors describe a moment of panic here: *I can't tell where I end.* This is normal. Breathe out a little, look at your hands, and keep going. The panic passes.

### What to Wear

- **A thin full-body skin suit** of lycra or very thin neoprene, one or two millimeters at most. Not for warmth. For protection from glass, from constant frond contact, from possible irritation by the mineral water, and from the small burns of the deep floor if you go anywhere near it. Many visitors want to go bare-skinned in water this pleasant. You will be in it for days. Cover up.
- **A low-volume freediving mask.** The water is mineral-laden, with a taste of iron and sulfur. You do not know what it does to eyes over a long stay. Keep the volume low so it is easy to equalize. Bring an anti-fog solution, because body-temperature water and warm humid bells are a recipe for fog.
- **Short, stiff fins, or none.** Long freediving fins will tangle in tallstem and batter the lampmoss. Short fins, or bare feet with thin dive socks, suit a city of narrow passages. Ask your hosts what they prefer. You may find that fins are considered rude or destructive.
- **Soft-soled dive booties** for bell ledges and passage floors.
- **A dive knife, sheathed, for emergencies only.** Tallstem columns rise eighty feet and sway, and entanglement is possible. But treat the knife as you would a weapon in someone's home. Never cut growth casually. The forests are a garden, and the people will notice.

### Things Not to Bring

- **Anything that sheds, floats, dissolves, or leaches.** Sunscreen, oils, strong soaps, perfumes, plastics that might break loose. This is a closed sea: no river feeds it and no river drains it. What you release stays. A slick of chemical sunscreen over lampmoss that has glowed undisturbed for decades is a real harm.
- **Rhythm-making devices.** More on this below.
- **Anything that produces a sharp sound.** A metal clasp knocking on stone carries for miles as a clean *tock*, and the masons' hammers are the only such sounds most people hear. Pad your gear.
- **Bright lights.** A small, dim, red-filtered torch for emergencies is reasonable. A dive torch sweeping through the tallstem is not. It would be like shining a floodlight into a cathedral during vespers. The lampmoss light is enough once your eyes adjust, and they will, over twenty or thirty minutes.

## Part Four: Living in the Bells

### Air Is a Gift

Understand this deeply: **every breath you take in Ammaret was carried to you by someone.**

A breath-carrier swam down to the deep floor, where the lava glows red through the water, and held a cured eel-skin bladder over a rising stream of vent gas. They risked burns doing it. They swam that bladder up through the forests and passages and released it into the bell where you now sit. Then the air slowly seeped out through the stone, and you breathed some of it down.

You are a burden on the city's air supply. This does not mean you are unwelcome. It means you should behave like a guest in a household during a drought.

- **Breathe slowly.** Do not exercise in the bells. Do not pant, do not talk loudly or at length, do not laugh hard. All of these consume air.
- **Do not linger** in a bell that is not yours, or not offered to you.
- **Ask your host how long a bell will hold**, and do not stay past that without their word.
- **Never enter a bell uninvited.** Each one is someone's home, and you surface into it head first through the floor. Imagine someone rising unannounced from your bathtub.
- **Bring something to offer the breath-carriers specifically.** What would they value? Possibly things that ease their work: burn salves, a well-made bladder closure, anything that helps skin heal in warm water. Ask before giving. The guild is respected, envied, and a little feared, and it has its own pride. A gift handed over badly might read as an attempt to buy air.
- **If you are strong and are invited, offer labor.** Carrying, holding, assisting a mason at a pillow. But do not presume. An outsider's clumsy hands near a bell someone has shaped for eleven years is a terrifying prospect for its owner.

### The Drowsiness

The vent gas is "breathable but thin, and it carries a faint drowsiness." Visitors say they feel as though they are dreaming the whole time.

Take this seriously, in both directions.

**Physically**, "thin and drowsy" could mean low oxygen, raised carbon dioxide, or trace narcotic gases. Pressure at depth amplifies all of them. If you can, bring a small, waterproof personal gas monitor, the kind used in confined-space work, that reads oxygen and ideally carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide. You will not be able to change the air, but you will know when to leave a bell for a fresher one, or when to go up. A headache, confusion that feels different from pleasant dreaminess, gasping, or a rotten-egg smell that grows stronger or that you suddenly can no longer smell are all reasons to move now, calmly, toward better air.

**Psychologically**, let it happen. You will sleep more than you expect, perhaps twelve or fourteen hours a day. Your thoughts will slow. Conversation will feel far away. Do not fight this with stimulants, and do not plan a busy schedule. Ammaret is not a place you can sightsee. It is a place you enter the tempo of, or suffer in.

Keep a simple written log if you can, on a waterproof slate, in a few words each waking: where you are, how long you have been there, how you feel. Time dissolves in the Held Sea. There is no sun, only pewter glow above and the steady light of lampmoss. You need some thread back to your own sense of time, especially for the ascent.

### Sleeping

You will sleep in a woven weed-hammock slung between stone pegs, above a floor of glowing water. Before you sleep:

- **Tether yourself or your gear.** Anything that falls goes into the pool, and possibly down into the passages.
- **Ask whether the bell is safe to sleep in for the night.** Air that is fine for an hour may not be fine for ten.
- **Accept the sound.** The deep ticking and creaking of new rock as it cools and contracts never stops. Children fall asleep to it. You may not at first. By the third night you will find it comforting, and back home, when it is gone, you may lie awake missing it.

### Skin, Hair, and Body

Days in body-warm water will do things to you.

- **Maceration.** Your skin will wrinkle, soften, and eventually begin to break down, especially on your feet and hands. Bring a thick water-resistant barrier balm such as lanolin or zinc-based ointment and apply it on ledges between swims. Dry your feet on the ledges whenever you can.
- **Infection.** Warm water breeds microbes. Even though there are no fish, there is life. Clean every cut from the volcanic glass as well as you can, and bring a small antiseptic kit. Glass cuts are deep and clean, and they bleed more than you expect.
- **Ears.** Prolonged immersion invites swimmer's ear. Bring drying ear drops and use them.
- **Hair.** It will drift like smoke, which is beautiful. It will also tangle in fronds, hammock-weave, and passage stone. Braid it or bind it tightly.
- **Minerals.** The iron and sulfur in the water will stain nails and fabric, and may leave a taste in your mouth for weeks. This is harmless as far as anyone knows, but do not drink the sea, however "almost sweet" it tastes. Ask what the locals drink, and bring water purification tablets regardless.

### Food

The descriptions say little about what the people eat. There are no fish. There are weeds, snails, and the lamp-eels, whose skin is taken only from the dead. Assume their diet is unusual and possibly hard for your gut. Bring compact, waterproof, high-calorie food that does not crumble or leave residue in water: sealed bars, pastes in squeeze tubes. Eat on ledges, and do not let crumbs fall into the pools.

If you are offered local food, accept a small amount graciously. Ask what it is. Eat slowly. If it does not agree with you, go back to your own supplies quietly, without comment.

**Never eat anything taken from a lamp-eel.** Even if offered, which is unlikely, decline.

## Part Five: How to Behave

### Move Like Them

Watch how the people of Ammaret move and copy it. Slow, economical strokes. Long pauses on ledges. No sudden thrashing. This is partly courtesy, partly survival (heat, oxygen), and partly respect for the forests. Tallstem sways so slowly you must watch a frond for a minute to see it move. Your wake is a storm to it.

### Lampmoss Is a Record

The city's paths are laid in lampmoss, which brightens where you touch it and fades over an hour. Everywhere you walk leaves a glowing trail. Everyone can see where you have been in the last hour.

This means:
- You cannot go anywhere privately. Do not try.
- Do not wander off the moss paths into terraces or bell-clusters you were not invited to. Your trail will announce your trespass.
- Do not touch lampmoss idly or play with the glow. It may be harmless, but it may also be the equivalent of scribbling on the walls. Ask first.
- Be aware that your trail tells a story about you. A visitor whose glowing tracks run back and forth, circle aimlessly, or cluster outside one particular bell will be read and remembered.

### Sound and Song

Speak softly, always, as if in a sleeping house. The curved stone will blur and return your voice, and people in neighboring bells may feel it as vibration.

**Rhythm belongs to things that are going somewhere.** There is no rhythmic music in Ammaret. This is not an accident of taste. It sounds like a philosophy, a quiet view that the city is not in a hurry and neither is anything in it. So:

- Do not tap your fingers on stone.
- Do not whistle or hum a tune.
- Do not drum on your knees, click your tongue, or keep time.
- If you are invited to sing, and a singer in a resonant bell may invite you, sing a single long held note. Let it swell and fade. Match their pitch or a quiet harmony. This is one of the most beautiful things a visitor can be part of. Prepare for it by practicing long, steady, soft tones at home. It is also good breath training.

If you bring music of your own on a device, leave it switched off and out of sight. Even the idea of a beat may feel faintly wrong here, like laughing at a funeral.

### The Lamp-Eels

There are perhaps a few hundred in the whole sea. Each is known, named, tracked, and mourned. Treat them as you would treat a revered elder or a sacred animal in any culture you respect.

- Do not touch one.
- Do not follow one without permission.
- Do not block its path. Move aside slowly and let it drift past.
- Ask its name if you see one with a local. It is a good question, and it shows you understand.
- If you learn that one has died, express quiet sympathy. Their grief is real.

### The Glass Snails

They graze the newest rock. Watch where you put your hands and feet on fresh pillows. Crushing one is almost certainly an offense, as well as a small tragedy you will feel yourself.

### The Diving Bird

If you see the black, long-necked bird plunge down from the misty surface into the upper forest, you will witness an omen. People disagree about what it means. **Do not offer an interpretation.** Do not say it is lucky or unlucky. Do not repeat what one person said it means to someone else. You are stepping into an old, living argument, and an outsider taking a side, or seeming to, can do real damage. If someone asks what you think, say something like *I don't know what it means. I'm glad I saw it.*

### The Masons

A mason may shape a single bell for eleven years. Their work is patient beyond anything most visitors have seen. Do not touch cooling pillows. Do not lean on a half-shaped dome. Do not ask how long something will take in a tone that implies it is slow. If you are permitted to watch, watch silently, and for a long time. The masons will notice whether you watch for five minutes or an hour.

### Age and Time

People count age in hands of stone. If someone asks your age, do not answer in years. It will mean little. Ask them to help you reckon it, or say plainly that you were born in a world where stone does not grow, and so you do not know your age in their terms. That is a genuinely interesting thing to say here, and it may open more conversation than anything else about you.

### The Dead

The dead are laid near the vents in the path of the slow lava, and over weeks the stone closes over them. The people say, without sorrow, that the city is built on its ancestors.

- If you witness this, be still and silent.
- Do not approach the deep floor on your own. Beyond the danger of heat, burns, and the vent gas at its source, it is where the dead are being received.
- Remember, as you sit in a bell, that the stone around you may contain someone. The people are not troubled by this. Try not to be. But do not treat the walls carelessly.

### The Breath-Carriers' Territory

The deep floor, where the lava glows and the water shimmers with heat, belongs to the breath-carriers. Do not go there unless one of them takes you, and even then consider refusing. It is hot enough to burn skin through water. The gas streams at their source may not be the same gas that fills the bells, since the carriers know which fissures to choose, and they are said to have preferences. A visitor who blunders into a vent plume could be poisoned, scalded, or both.

If a breath-carrier offers to take you, that is an extraordinary honor. Think hard before saying yes, and before saying no.

## Part Six: Leaving

This is where visitors are hurt most often, because by the time they leave, they have forgotten to be careful.

### Decompression

You have spent days breathing air under pressure at depth. Your tissues have absorbed gas. If you rise quickly to the surface, which at this altitude is lower in pressure than sea level, that gas can come out of solution in your blood and joints. The result is decompression sickness: joint pain, rash, numbness, confusion, paralysis, death.

**Plan your ascent with your dive physician before you ever arrive.** In general principle:

1. **Move up gradually through the city.** Spend time, many hours, in progressively shallower bells before you leave the water. The upper edges of the city, where the gaps are longer and only the young and strong go, may be exactly the staging area you need. Ask your hosts to help you find a route that climbs slowly.
2. **Ascend the final rope very slowly.** Slower than feels necessary. Exhale continuously. Stop partway if you can hold on.
3. **Rest at the rim.** Do not begin the climb down immediately. Walking and exertion right after surfacing make decompression sickness worse. Lie still. Drink water. If you brought emergency oxygen, this is when to breathe it.
4. **Descend the mountain slowly.** Going down the mountain actually helps, because pressure rises as you descend. But the first hours at the rim, at altitude, fresh from saturation, are the most dangerous of your entire visit.
5. **Do not fly** for a long time afterward, far longer than after an ordinary dive. Ask your physician. Plan your next multiverse transit with this in mind if it involves any change in ambient pressure.

Know the symptoms. If you feel joint pain, tingling, strange fatigue, skin mottling, or confusion after surfacing, the only real treatment is recompression. That means going back down, and back down means the Held Sea. Know before you go whether the locals understand this and would help you. They may. They live between depths every day, and the breath-carriers surely know something about the sickness of rising too fast.

### Coming Back to the World

When you rise through the mist and reach the rim, the sun will be at full, fierce altitude strength. It may overwhelm you after days of pewter glow and blue-white moss. Bring sunglasses in your rim cache and put them on before your eyes are fully out of the water.

The air will taste strange: thin, cold, sharp. You may feel chilled for the first time in days. Have warm, dry clothes waiting in a sealed cache at the rim.

Sound will seem loud and crude. Wind, footsteps, your own voice. You may find rhythm unpleasant for a while: footfalls, heartbeats, music. That is fine. It passes.

Many visitors say the drowsiness lingers for days after they leave, and that they are not sure whether they miss it or are relieved to be rid of it. Some say they feel slightly wrong in the dry world for a long time afterward, as though they have come out of the water too early, before anything was finished.

## Packing List

**Mountain and rim cache**
- Multi-day trekking kit for altitude and cloud-forest
- Cut-resistant boots and heavy gloves
- Long sleeves and trousers
- Sealed cache: warm dry clothes, sunglasses, food, water, first aid, emergency oxygen if obtainable

**In-water personal kit**
- Thin full-body skin suit
- Low-volume freediving mask, with a spare
- Anti-fog solution (biodegradable)
- Dive socks or booties
- Short fins, only if hosts permit
- Sheathed emergency knife
- Small dim red emergency light
- Waterproof slate and pencil for a time log
- Personal gas monitor (O₂, ideally CO₂ and H₂S)
- Waterproof pouch for everything, padded so nothing clicks or clanks

**Health**
- Barrier balm for skin
- Drying ear drops
- Antiseptic, wound closure strips, bandages
- Personal medications in waterproof containers, in excess
- Water purification tablets
- Burn salve, for yourself and possibly as a gift

**Food**
- Compact, sealed, crumb-free high-calorie food

**Gifts**
- Something useful to breath-carriers, offered carefully
- Something small and quiet for your host
- Nothing that floats, leaches, shines harshly, or makes a sharp sound

**Leave at home**
- Sunscreen and chemical cosmetics
- Anything with a beat
- Bright torches
- Loose plastics
- Any wish to hurry

## A Last Note

The people of Ammaret live on stone that did not exist a generation ago, in air that someone carried to them, above ancestors who are slowly becoming the floor. They shape their homes by hand as the rock grows, one week at a time, for eleven years. They believe that when the mountain is finished, the sea will drain away and a new world will stand in the open air, and they are not afraid of this, and not in any hurry for it.

The best preparation for visiting them is not physical. It is learning, before you go, how to wait without restlessness, and how to take something freely given, a breath, a bed, a place on a ledge, without taking more than you need. If you can do that, the rest of your preparation will keep you alive. If you cannot, it will only keep you alive.
