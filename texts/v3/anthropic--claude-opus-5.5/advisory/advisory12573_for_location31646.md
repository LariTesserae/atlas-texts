# Preparing for the Held Plateau

## First, Understand What You're Preparing For

Most dangerous places try to kill you. The Held Plateau doesn't. The cold never quite hurts, the animals don't mind you, and the light stays gentle. Nothing up there is hostile.

The danger is that the place is **welcoming**. It isn't welcoming like a host. It's welcoming like deep water on a hot day, or a bed when you're exhausted. The plateau has reached its final shape and has no objection to you reaching yours. Every precaution below exists to keep you a creature that changes, moves in a line rather than a loop, and eventually goes home.

The physical preparation is real and matters. But when you're packing, remember that the thing most likely to keep you there is not altitude sickness. It's the moment, some hours in, when lowering your head in time with ten thousand others starts to feel like relief.

---

## Part One: The Body

### Altitude

This is "nearly the highest ground there is," on a world whose mountains may once have been the tallest ever raised. Assume serious altitude, the kind where the sky darkens toward violet at the zenith. That means somewhere above 4,500 metres, possibly well above.

- **Acclimatise on the approach.** The climb is long and gradual, which is good. Use it. Don't rush the final slope because it seems gentle. Spend nights partway up if you can find a place where nights still happen.
- **Know the signs of altitude illness** before you go: headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, loss of coordination, breathlessness at rest, confusion. The last two matter doubly, because the plateau causes its own versions of confusion and slowed breathing. If you can't tell whether you're hypoxic or being absorbed, treat it as both and descend.
- **Carry a pulse oximeter.** It reports oxygen saturation, and it also reports your heart rate and lets you check your breathing, which is the single most useful early warning up there (see Part Four).
- **Consider supplemental oxygen** for longer visits, and any altitude medication your own world's medicine recommends, tested beforehand.
- **Don't go at all** if you have significant heart or lung conditions.

### Cold

The cold is constant and mild: "at the edge of discomfort." That makes it easy to underestimate. Steady mild cold drains you for hours without ever alarming you enough to act.

- **Dress in layers you can adjust:** a good wicking base layer, a warm mid-layer (fleece or wool), and a light insulated jacket you'll probably wear the whole time.
- **You don't need a heavy waterproof shell.** There's no wind and no precipitation. Weight you save there should go into water.
- **Bring a warm hat and thin gloves**, with a spare pair of gloves. You'll want the thin ones for handling instruments, food and notebooks.
- **Pack warm socks and a dry spare pair.** Sweat is the only moisture you'll encounter.
- **Carry chemical hand warmers.** Also carry a stove, but see the note on fire below.

Because the cold never changes, your body will stop registering it. Check your fingers and toes deliberately every so often, even if you feel fine.

### Dryness

Nothing drinks on the plateau and nothing suffers for it. That's true of the plateau's residents, and it is not true of you. The air at that altitude is desperately dry, and there is no water source of any kind.

- **Bring every drop you'll need, plus a generous reserve.** At altitude you lose large amounts of water just by breathing. Budget at least 4–5 litres per person per day of intended stay, then add a full extra day's worth.
- **Pack electrolyte tablets or powder.**
- **Protect your lips, eyes and nose** with lip balm, saline eye drops, and a nasal moisturiser. The bone-meal dust is extremely fine. It doesn't blow, but it will get kicked up around your own feet, so a buff or light mask is worthwhile when you walk through the hollows.
- **Watch for thirst fading.** If you stop feeling thirsty, that's a warning sign rather than a relief. Drink on a schedule, not by feel.
- **Don't try to melt the frost bead** under the overhang, or touch it. It isn't a water source. It may be the last water in that world.

### Sun

The sun sits a little past midday and **never moves**. At high altitude, under a thin atmosphere, that means relentless ultraviolet light from one fixed direction.

- **Wear glacier glasses or high-quality wraparound sunglasses.** Snow-blindness is possible even without snow, because pale polished quartzite reflects a great deal of light.
- **Use high-SPF sunscreen and reapply it on schedule.**
- **Expect to burn asymmetrically.** The sun always strikes from the same angle, so one side of your face and neck will take all of it, depending on which way you face. Pay extra attention to that side. Consider a hat with a neck flap.
- **Rest in shade** when you rest. The long eastward shadows of the tors are your shade, and they won't shift and leave you exposed.

### Footing

Every surface is polished smooth and "faintly greasy to the touch," like the inside of a shell. That surface is slippery.

- **Wear boots with soft, grippy rubber soles**, the kind good for smooth rock, rather than hard lugged soles meant for mud.
- **Take trekking poles with rubber tips**, which help on the long undulating swells.
- **Walk the swells slowly.** A fall on hard stone, far from help, with no one around but eyeless animals, is a serious thing.

## Part Two: Supplies and Conduct

### Food

- **Bring all your own food.** Choose calorie-dense and easy to eat cold: nuts, chocolate, cheese, dried meat, oat bars. Appetite drops at altitude, so bring things you actually like.
- **Do not eat anything from the plateau.** Don't eat the lichen, the cushion plants, or the small pale violet flowers that have always been open. The grazers eat the lichen and it never diminishes. Whatever you'd be eating is something that doesn't change, and there's a reasonable fear that eating it is part of how you stop changing.
- **Treat fading hunger like fading thirst.** Eat on a schedule.
- **Check whether your food runs out normally.** If a bar you've been eating seems not to get smaller, stop eating it, pack up, and leave. Your supplies should behave like yours.

### Fire

The windbreaks have ash in their lee that "never went cold because it was never warm." That suggests fire may not behave normally up there.

- **Bring a stove designed for high altitude, but don't depend on it.** Test it early in the visit, near the edge.
- **Plan as though you won't be able to heat anything.** Food that needs cooking is a gamble. Hand warmers work by chemistry and are more likely to be reliable, but test those too.

### Waste

Footprints vanish, and nothing is ever taken away from the plateau.

- **Pack out everything**: rubbish, food scraps, human waste (use wag bags or equivalent).
- Your waste might vanish like footprints, or it might stay exactly as you left it for the rest of time. Neither outcome is good. Leave nothing.

### Taking and Leaving Objects

This matters more than it would anywhere else.

**Don't take anything.** Leave the pebbles, the salt rosettes, the bone awls, the scraper of black glass, and above all the flint blade polished by a long-held grip. The plateau has finished losing things. Removing something from a place that has reached its final shape is, at best, rude. At worst, you may be carrying home a piece of the stillness. Photograph things instead. Drawings are better still, as explained below.

**Don't leave anything either.** Don't add a stone to a cairn or a pebble to the top of one. Don't scratch your own tally, or build a windbreak. The people who built things up there appear, in some cases, to have become part of what they built. Leaving something of yourself looks like the first step toward staying.

### Handling the Ruins and Remains

- **Look, but don't sleep in the windbreaks or inside the ring of slabs.** One windbreak holds a grazer curled like a sleeping person, with a fleece woven like cloth. Draw your own conclusions.
- **Don't approach the tall figure with the staff.** Don't speak to it or ask it its name. If it once was one of you, there's nothing you can do for it, and the plateau may take a conversation as an introduction.
- **Treat the cairn whose top stone rises and falls as a grave.** Nobody knows whether it is one, and the courtesy costs nothing.

## Part Three: Navigation

The plateau disorients you in every way it can. There are no landmarks that stay put and no trails. The horizon lifts like the rim of a plate. Distances expand and contract without you moving, and footprints disappear.

It does give you one extraordinary gift: **the sun doesn't move.** Shadows always point east, forever. You have a perfect, unfailing compass in the sky.

**Before you step onto the plateau:**

1. **Stand at the edge**, where the ground stops rising, and take a clear bearing from the shadows. Note the angle between your shadow and the direction back down the slope you came up. Write it down, somewhere you can find it without thinking: the back of your hand, the inside of your hat.
2. **Take a physical compass as well**, and check whether it agrees with the shadows. If it does, good. If it swings or disagrees, trust the shadows.
3. **Record the approach terrain** with sketches or photographs of the last swell before the descent, so you'll recognise it.

**On the plateau:**

- **Don't navigate by tors.** They may be the oldest of the animals, and distances to them are unreliable anyway.
- **Don't navigate by herds.** Herds shade into one another and the counts don't hold.
- **Keep a running dead-reckoning log:** direction relative to the shadows, and time walked by the watch rather than by how long it feels. "Walked 40 minutes with shadow at my left shoulder." It won't be precise, because the distances misbehave, but direction relative to the sun is the one thing that holds.
- **Remember that the edge is where the ground descends and the air thickens.** When returning, walk back along your recorded bearing and watch for the ground starting to tilt, the sky's zenith paling, and breath coming easier. The body knows the edge before the eye does. Trust that.
- **For short visits, a tether is worth considering.** A long cord or a line of flagged stakes behind you won't work for sixty miles, but for a first visit, staying within a mile or two of the edge, a physical line back is invaluable. Collect it on your way out. See above about leaving things.
- **Don't wander into the middle.** There's no reason to, and the breakdown seems to grow with both time and distance from the edge. The best visits stay within sight of the descent.

## Part Four: Time, and the Pull

This is the real preparation.

### No Night

The sun never sets, and your body will lose track of time very fast.

- **Bring a reliable mechanical or battery watch**, ideally two. Use one with a second hand that visibly moves. The ticking is more important than you'd think, because it's something that goes forward.
- **Keep a written log with timestamps.** Every hour by the watch, write the time, one sentence about what you've done, and one sentence about something you want to do once you're home. That last part matters. Keep your future in front of you.
- **Pack an eye mask and a small alarm** if you'll need to sleep. Better yet, don't plan to sleep up there at all (see below).

### Set a Time Limit Before You Arrive

**Decide your departure time while you're still below the plateau**, in air that's thick and on a slope that goes somewhere. Write it down, and tell someone who is staying behind.

Don't renegotiate it while you're up there. Your judgment will be the first thing to go, and it will go gently. Look at the tallies on the rocks: long rows of strokes that stop partway, "as if the counter realised it was pointless." Nothing about the plateau hints that time is passing, and you'll start to feel it isn't. Deciding in advance is the only defence against that feeling.

As a guide, a first visit should last a few hours, and certainly no more than a single waking period. Don't sleep on the plateau on a first visit. If you must sleep there on a later visit, sleep in shifts with a companion awake, at the edge, facing outward.

### Entrainment: The Breath

The plateau breathes once a minute. A resting human breathes twelve to twenty times a minute. Given time, yours will begin to slow toward the plateau's rhythm "without your choosing it."

This is the primary hazard. Here's how to manage it:

- **Measure your breathing regularly.** Every thirty minutes, count your breaths for one full minute by your watch. If the number is falling noticeably, below ten or so, you are being pulled in. Stop, breathe deliberately and quickly for a minute, and seriously consider leaving. (Rapid shallow breathing from altitude is a different problem; keep an eye out for both.)
- **Bring a rhythm of your own.** Pick something to hum, sing under your breath, or count to that doesn't match a one-minute cycle. Better still, pick something irregular: a song with odd phrasing, or a poem you know by heart. The goal is to stay out of phase. A small hand-held metronome or a clicking timer set to an irregular pattern can help.
- **Talk.** The plateau is almost silent, and speech is a human rhythm. Talking to a companion, or to yourself, keeps you anchored. It will feel rude in all that quiet. Do it anyway, softly.
- **Watch for head-lowering.** If you notice you've been lowering your head when they lower theirs, you're further along than the breathing count would suggest. Leave.

### Entrainment: The Loop

Every animal does "one small thing forever," and so might you. The first signs look ordinary: pacing back and forth between two points, picking up and setting down the same stone, rechecking the same pocket, rereading the same page of your log.

- **Keep your actions going somewhere.** Give each hour a purpose that ends: walk to this tor, sketch that lichen ring, then turn back. Linear tasks, with a beginning and an end.
- **Look at your log.** If the entries start repeating, that's the loop taking hold. Leave.

### The Face

The animals have lost their faces. The only faces on the plateau are in the old drawings, made by people who still had theirs and still saw faces in the world.

- **Bring a small mirror** and look at yourself at intervals. You're checking your features and also your recognition: that you know it's you, and that you care. If your face seems oddly smooth in the mirror, even if that's only a trick of the unchanging light, don't wait to find out which it is.
- **Carry a photograph** of someone you love, and of yourself at home.
- **Draw.** Bring a small sketchbook and pencils. Draw the grazers as the old pilgrims did, *with* horns and eyes and faces. Drawing seems to have been one of the ways people held onto the idea of distinctness up there. It's also an absorbing, forward-moving activity that yields something you can take home without taking anything from the plateau.

### Among the Herds

At some point the distances will collapse and you'll find yourself among the animals, pressed close, warm and faceless. They won't hurt you. Their fleeces will be soft, and the breathing will be all around you.

This is the most seductive moment, and the most dangerous one.

- **Move gently.** Don't barge or shout. The pattern will bend around you like water around a stone, and you want to stay a stone.
- **Don't lie down among them.** Don't sit and lean against one, and don't close your eyes. This is the posture of the grazer in the windbreak.
- **Keep your pack on.** It's a reminder that you're carrying things, and that things you carry are meant to be taken somewhere.
- **Check your shadow bearing and walk out** on a straight line toward the edge. They'll let you go. They don't mind you coming and they don't mind you going. The minding, if there is any, is all on your side.

## Part Five: Companions

**Don't go alone** if you can help it.

A companion gives you a second rhythm to fall into instead of the plateau's. They're also a second pair of eyes to notice what you can't: that you've gone quiet, that you've been standing still for twenty minutes, that your answers are getting shorter.

Before you go up, agree on a **check-in ritual** every half hour, done out loud:

- Say your name.
- Say where you live.
- Say what you'll eat first when you get home.
- Say the departure time.
- Count your breaths together for a minute.

If either of you balks at any part of it or finds it pointless or tiresome, you both leave. You don't discuss it. Agree on that in advance too.

Agree also that **either person can call the departure at any time, for any reason, and the other doesn't argue.** Agree that if one of you starts to settle, the other will physically walk them out, by the arm if necessary, back along the shadow bearing to where the air thickens.

## Part Six: Who Should Not Go

Be honest with yourself here.

The plateau offers a very specific thing: an end to change, loss, hunger, thirst, time, and decisions. A mild cold forever, an afternoon forever. A place in a great breathing whole that asks nothing of you.

If you are recently bereaved, or exhausted, or depressed, or longing for rest in a way that frightens you a little, **don't go now.** It isn't that the plateau would harm you. It's that it would offer, very gently, to hold you, and you might say yes. Some of the pilgrims who stayed may have been exactly that kind of person, and that may have been exactly why they came.

Go when you have something to go home to, something you're looking forward to. Write it on the first page of your log.

## Part Seven: Leaving, and Afterwards

### Leaving

- **Leave on time, by the watch**, whatever you feel.
- **Walk out on your recorded shadow bearing.** Watch and feel for the edge: ground tilting, air thickening, breath easing, the zenith paling to ordinary blue.
- **Look back once from the last swell if you want to.** It will look exactly as it did when you arrived. That's what it does. Don't take it as an invitation. Then turn and go down.
- **Keep walking down past the point where the edge feels close.** Descend properly into thicker air before you rest. This is good altitude practice and good practice in general.

### Afterwards

You'll feel the breath "in your own chest for days afterward." Plan for this.

- **Re-entrain on purpose.** Go somewhere busy, noisy and changeable: a market, a crowded meal, a place with music. Walk somewhere new each day. Seek out irregular rhythms and ordinary variety.
- **Sleep in a real night**, with a real dawn afterward. Let your body find the cycle of days again. Early-morning light helps.
- **Have someone watch your breathing** for the first few nights, or at least check in on you. Tell them what the plateau does, and ask them to tell you if you seem to be slowing down, going quiet, or repeating yourself.
- **Eat well, drink plenty, and notice your appetite returning.** That's a good sign.
- **Look at your drawings.** Look at the faces you gave the grazers. Look at your own face in an ordinary mirror, in ordinary moving light.
- **Consider whether to go back.** The plateau will be exactly the same, and there's no hurry. It can wait, and it will. If you find yourself wanting to go back often, strongly and soon, with a pull you can feel in your chest, treat that as a reason to wait longer.
