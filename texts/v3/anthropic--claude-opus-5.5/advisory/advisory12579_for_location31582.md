# Preparing to Visit the Terraces Above the Cloud Line

## What Kind of Place This Is

This is not a hard place to survive. The temperature is mild, water is everywhere, there are no predators or storms, and the people are unhurried and apparently peaceful. You won't freeze, starve in a week, or be attacked.

The difficulty is the reverse. The place is **extremely fragile, and you are extremely disruptive.** You come from worlds full of wind, animals, insects, seeds, weather, and noise. You carry all of those things on your clothes, in your gear, and in your habits. The plateau has none of them, and its whole character depends on that. Most of your preparation should go into not bringing your world with you.

The second difficulty is subtler. Never-ending damp and total stillness wear on a human body and mind in ways that sneak up on you.

---

## 1. Biosecurity: The Most Important Section

There are no animals here. No insects, no birds, nothing in the pools. The flowers stay open because nothing visits them. Whatever the reason, whether isolation, youth, or "something else," you should treat it as the plateau's most precious and most vulnerable feature. The residents may treat it that way too. You don't know whether they regard an insect as a curiosity, a catastrophe, or a desecration. Assume catastrophe.

**One fruit fly, one aphid, or one moth egg in a seam could be the first animal this place has ever known.** In a warm, humid, predator-free environment full of undefended plants, it might thrive. The untouched flowers and ferns have no reason to have evolved defenses.

Before crossing over:

- **Clean every item of gear as if for a sterile environment.** Wash all clothing hot. Freeze what you can't wash for several days to kill eggs and larvae. Vacuum out pack seams and pockets.
- **Inspect boot treads obsessively.** Scrub them, pick out every stone and fiber, and disinfect them. Seeds and soil microbes travel in boots more than anywhere else.
- **Avoid Velcro, fleece, and anything that collects burrs.** Use smooth, tightly woven fabrics.
- **Bring no plant material.** No fresh fruit, no seeds, no nuts in shells, no wooden items you haven't treated, no dried flowers, no herbal teas with seed content.
- **Food should be sealed, processed, and seedless.** Retort pouches, canned goods, well-sealed bars, and powdered meals are good. Grains and anything that could sprout are not. Crumbs matter.
- **Check yourself.** No lice, bedbugs, or mites. Travel only if you're healthy. Nobody knows how the residents' immune systems would handle your cold virus.
- **Pack out everything.** Wrappers, food scraps, tape, and threads all come back with you.

When you arrive, stop at the edge of the first bare stone and inspect yourself again before walking further in. If you find anything alive on you, kill it and carry the body out in a sealed bag.

## 2. The Ground: Young, Sharp, and Wet

This rock hasn't been softened by weathering. The lava edges can cut a palm, and it's all wet, so it is both **sharp and slippery**, a bad combination.

**Footwear**
- You need soles stiff enough that a sharp flow edge can't punch through, and rubber sticky enough to grip wet, glassy stone. Approach shoes or canyoneering boots with soft, grippy rubber are ideal. Hard-lugged mountaineering boots skid on smooth wet basalt.
- Your boots will never dry. Choose ones that drain well rather than waterproof ones that trap water inside once it gets in, which it will.
- Consider how they sound. Bare basalt rings faintly under hard soles. Softer soles are quieter and will make you less conspicuous.

**Hands and Knees**
- Wear thin, grippy gloves whenever you're scrambling, touching column edges, or steadying yourself. Your instinct to put a hand down when you slip is what will cut you.
- Wear long, tough trousers. Kneeling on flow tops with bare knees will cost you skin.
- Bring rubber tips if you use trekking poles. Metal tips clink loudly and could chip the columns or tear the moss.

**Walking**
- Stay on laid stone paths and bare rock whenever possible.
- **Never step on moss.** It grows so slowly that no living person has seen it spread. One careless footprint on a cushion could crush centuries of growth. The moss marks where water runs, so it also tends to sit on the slickest ground. Stepping around it protects both you and it.
- Don't wade in pools unless a resident invites you. They may be water supplies, private, or meaningful in ways you can't read. The water in them is standing still. Let it.
- Don't walk at night. With sharp edges, clear pools you can't see the bottom of, and a sheer cliff, darkness is where visitors get hurt.

## 3. The Damp: Your Real Adversary

Everything weeps, the air stays near saturation, and nothing ever dries. You don't notice this the first day. By the fourth day, it's the only thing you notice.

**Clothing**
- Wear wool, especially merino, which stays warm and doesn't smell when wet. Cotton turns clammy and cold and stays that way. Synthetics dry fast, but "fast" doesn't help where drying can't happen at all.
- Bring many socks. Plan for at least one pair per day, and keep them sealed in waterproof bags until you use them.
- Keep one full set of sleep clothes in a sealed dry bag, used only inside your sleeping bag. Having one dry thing will matter to your morale more than you expect.

**Feet and Skin**
- Trench foot and fungal infections are real risks in constant wet. Air your feet whenever you stop, apply foot powder or a zinc barrier cream every night, and inspect them daily for whitening, wrinkling that won't go away, or numbness.
- Bring antifungal cream and anti-chafing balm. Damp fabric rubbing damp skin for a week does damage.
- Cuts heal slowly in this humidity and get infected easily. Pack antiseptic, wound closure strips, and **waterproof dressings that stay on when wet**. Clean even small cuts from the basalt right away.

**Gear**
- Electronics: use sealed cases and a lot of desiccant, which saturates quickly here. Bring more than seems reasonable, or a kind you can regenerate. Warm a cold device slowly or it will fog inside.
- Paper: bring a waterproof notebook and pencils. Ordinary paper wilts within a day, and ink runs.
- Glasses fog constantly and never quite clear. Consider contacts or anti-fog treatment.
- Metal rusts. Oil your knife and your zippers.
- Your food will mold once you open it. Eat opened packets immediately.

**Sleep**
- Use a thick, puncture-resistant groundsheet, because the rock will shred a thin one. Sleep on bare stone only, never on moss or fern beds.
- A bivy or small tent keeps the drip off you, but the humidity will reach you anyway. Wear your sealed dry clothes inside your bag and accept that your bag will slowly get damp. A synthetic-fill bag handles this far better than down. Down will collapse into wet clumps within days.

## 4. Water and Food

**Water**
Water is everywhere and looks spotless, but:

- **Ask before you drink.** The whole town is a water system. Channels, gutters, and cisterns carry every drop from terrace to terrace. Taking water from a cistern or channel without asking may be like drinking from someone's kitchen jug. The residents will almost certainly share, but ask first.
- Condensation water is close to distilled and contains almost no minerals. That's fine for a few days. Over a longer stay, drinking only this water can lower your electrolytes. **Bring salt and electrolyte tablets.**
- Filtering or treating it is sensible. The water is clear, but you don't know what microbes live in it, and your gut isn't adapted to them.

**Food**
Bring all of your own. The plateau's food supply is tiny: no animals, sparse trees, flowers that may never fruit because nothing pollinates them, ferns rooted in scraps of organic matter. Whatever the residents live on, there is clearly little to spare. Don't accept a meal from people who may have almost nothing. If someone offers food, accept a token amount graciously, and understand that it may be a real sacrifice.

**No fire.** There is almost no fuel. The rare trees are "each one an event in the landscape," and burning their wood would be monstrous. Anything you found would be soaked anyway. Smoke would carry across the whole still plateau. If you need hot food, bring a small, quiet stove with a clean-burning fuel, use it rarely and discreetly, and ask first whether any flame is acceptable here. Cold meals are the respectful default.

**Human waste** is a serious question, not a trivial one. Everything on this plateau flows west, downhill, through the town's water, until it spills back into the cloud. Anything you leave on the stone ends up in someone's cistern. There's no soil to dig a hole in and no insects to break waste down. Either ask the residents how they manage it and follow their system exactly, or carry sealed waste bags and pack everything out. Assume you will carry it out.

## 5. The Edges

**The Eastern Cliff**
The eastern rim is a sheer wall of columns several hundred meters high, with sheets of water running down every face. The cloud's surface sits only a few dozen meters below the rim. **It will look close enough and solid enough to land on.** Its flatness and whiteness, and the way it doesn't move, can trick part of your brain into reading it as a floor. It is not a floor. It is cloud above an unknown drop.

The rock at the rim is also the wettest and slickest on the plateau. Stay several meters back. If you want to look over, lie on your stomach on dry-ish stone with someone holding your ankles, or don't look. Be ready for a pull toward the edge that some people feel near sheer drops. It's stronger here because the "ground" below looks so soft.

**The Western Margin**
In the west, the tilted land slides down into the cloud, and the town's lowest terraces meet it. Walking into the cloud means an immediate whiteout, lost orientation, and entry into lowlands nobody here seems to visit. Nothing in the description suggests that anyone goes down there. **Don't go into the cloud.** If you visit the lowest terraces, stay where you can clearly see the stone behind you.

**Navigation**
Landmarks are sparse. The town is spread thin, and the long stretches of wet black stone look alike. Two tools help:
- **Water always runs west.** Every channel and gutter works as a compass. Follow the water to go downhill and west, and go against it to go uphill and east.
- The eastern cliff is the one hard boundary, so orient yourself by it.

It's a day's walk across. You can't get badly lost, but you can get lost enough to be caught out at dusk on sharp rock. Carry a small light anyway, and don't plan to use it.

## 6. Light and Sun

The sun is overhead, and the cloud-sea reflects it back up. That means **you're getting UV from above and from below**, much like on a snowfield. The light will feel soft, even, and gentle, and it will still burn you.

- Put sunscreen on the underside of your chin, beneath your nose, under your jaw, and on your ears, the places reflected light reaches and people forget.
- Bring sunglasses with side protection. Bright shadowless light from every direction strains the eyes over hours, even when it doesn't feel harsh.
- A brimmed hat only protects you from above. Accept that.

Everyone's face will be lit faintly from below, including yours and the residents'. It looks odd at first, and after a while it looks like how faces are supposed to look.

## 7. Sound and Conduct

You'll be the loudest thing on the plateau.

The residents move slowly and quietly and speak little. Your normal speaking voice will sound close to a shout. Drips fifty paces away are audible, so your zipper, your crinkling food wrapper, your cough, and your boot on the stone will reach everyone nearby.

- Choose quiet gear: soft-soled shoes, silent fabrics, rubber-tipped poles, pouches instead of crinkly packaging, a pack that doesn't creak.
- Speak softly and less. Watch how the residents greet each other and copy them.
- Move slowly. Rushing is a hazard on this rock, and it's also rude in a way the residents will feel even if they don't say so. The pace here isn't laziness. It's how you live in a place where nothing changes.
- Houses stand far apart, each on its own terrace or beside its own pool. That spacing seems deliberate. Don't approach a dwelling uninvited, and don't cross someone's terrace when you could go around.
- **Touch nothing growing.** Don't pick a fern. Don't touch the waxy flowers, however strange they are. Don't lean on or climb the trees. Their surface roots run over the stone "like poured wax," and stepping on them may injure a tree that took centuries to grow.
- Don't move stones. Every hexagonal prism may have been quarried, carried, and fitted by hand. A loose-looking column may be part of a channel that took a lifetime to set.

**Gifts**
It's natural to want to bring something. Think carefully. Anything organic is a biosecurity risk. Metal is interesting: the residents use bronze, which basalt doesn't provide, so it's precious, came from somewhere, and possibly carries meaning. A metal gift might be deeply welcome, or it might upset an economy or tradition you don't understand. The safest gift is labor offered with humility. If someone is mending a channel, you can sit nearby and show that you'd hold a stone if asked. You probably won't be asked, and that's fine.

**Questions**
You'll want to ask how they got here, why there are no animals, what's under the cloud, and how old the town is. Ask gently if you ask at all, and accept silence as an answer. "Perhaps something else explains it" is the kind of phrase that suggests the residents might know more and not want to discuss it.

## 8. The Mind: Stillness Is Not Rest

This is the part most visitors underestimate.

**The Silence**
In near-total quiet, your own body gets loud. Expect to hear your pulse, your breathing, the faint ringing in your ears (tinnitus you didn't know you had), and your joints. Some people find this peaceful. Others find it unbearable within a day or two. If you know you depend on background noise to sleep or think, practice real silence before you go. Earplugs don't help with this, because they make your internal sounds louder.

**The Absence of Life**
You'll notice what's missing before you can name it. Standing still water with no midges over it, beautiful flowers with no bees, moss with nothing moving in it, sky with no birds. Humans are tuned to register that small motion constantly, and when it's gone, part of your nervous system stays on alert, waiting for something that never comes. Many visitors describe a low, ongoing unease, the sense that the world has been paused or that something is holding its breath. Expect it and name it. It isn't danger. It's the absence of the usual background life.

The flowers have no scent, which adds to it. There's the smell of wet stone and wet moss, perhaps faintly mineral, and very little else.

**The Changelessness**
There's no weather. The temperature barely moves between morning and night. The cloud never shifts, the pools never rise or fall, and nothing blooms or wilts. Your internal clock usually leans on these cues more than you realize. Without them, days blur. Visitors lose track of how long they've stayed, sometimes badly.

- **Keep a written log.** Write the date and one line every day, even if the line is "same as yesterday." Especially then.
- **Set a departure date before you arrive and hold to it.** The place makes leaving feel unnecessary, since nothing will be different tomorrow, so there's never a reason to go today. That feeling is exactly why you should decide in advance.
- Bring a watch that doesn't depend on local conditions.

**The Seduction**
Some people will find this place the most beautiful thing they've ever seen and never want to leave. The balance of it, the rinsed shine of the stone, the white sea below, and the peace of people who have fit themselves into the stillness all pull hard at anyone who's tired. Let yourself feel it. Remember that you're a visitor, that the plateau's balance is fine, and that it has room for its residents, not for everyone who falls in love with it.

---

## 9. Coming Home

Leaving may be harder than arriving. After days of drips and silence, your home world will hit you like a wall: wind, traffic, birdsong, voices, insects, weather, smells. People report being startled by a fly landing on their arm and feeling almost indignant at a bird. Give yourself a quiet day to readjust.
