The power did not brighten the Upper Hollow.

It could have. It knew how to make a darkness harmless by filling it with light, how to lift mist from a basin and show every edge of the world. It knew how to turn a hundred years into a moment, and a moment into something that could be held in the hand.

But the darkness was not a wound. The mist was not a prison. The hollow had not asked to be made easier to understand.

So the power came quietly.

It did not descend from the unseen sky. It was simply present one evening in the wet stone, in the patience of the water, in the pause between one animal’s call and another’s. The creatures noticed. The small ones crossed the basin as usual. The rim-walker stopped, listened, and went on. In the tree, the little shapes turned their heads together.

The hollow noticed too.

Not as a person notices a visitor, perhaps, but as stone notices the weight of rain. The seams in the rock held their water. The pins remained still. Nothing in the basin opened or bowed.

The power waited.

It listened through the calls, the soft crossings beneath the leaves, the long hush when every creature seemed to be holding its breath. It learned that the hollow was not empty, though it had no single voice. Its life was distributed: among the animals and roots, in the old structures, in the patient agreements by which water, stone, and living things made room for one another. Perhaps there was a being at the heart of it. Perhaps the being was the whole arrangement.

The power did not insist on deciding.

At the basin’s lowest point, where the water lay in a thin skin over stone, it left a possibility. Not a message, and not a command: only a quiet means of being heard, should the hollow ever wish to call beyond itself. It might be a seam in the rock that had not been there before, or only the faintest change in one of the old structures. Nothing living was compelled to use it. No path led toward it. The water did not favor it.

Then the power withdrew its attention—not because it had forgotten, but because being watched too closely can become another kind of pressure.

It made itself one promise: if something came to take the hollow by force, to drain it, burn it, or make its life answer to an alien purpose, the power would stand between that harm and the place. It would not decide what the hollow ought to become. It would only preserve its chance to go on becoming whatever it was.

Afterward, the basin continued.

A pool took a season to overflow its lip. The central tree held its warm leaves close to the earth. The animals moved along their familiar paths, and one evening a new path appeared through the grasses. The pins did not turn—or perhaps one of them did, by less than could be seen.

Nothing in the hollow changed enough to prove that anything had happened.

But the power remembered it, without naming it, and left the darkness undisturbed.
