The power did not come down from the pale sky.

It had no need to. It was already listening.

It heard the pumps beneath the paving, the animals moving through the drains, the old stone taking the weight of another layer of buildings. It heard the city not as a roar but as a multitude of small replies: a gate answering a pressure change; a hand tapping twice on a rail; a lamp blinking amber through the mist.

And it heard, in one of the lower districts, a fault-listener stop walking.

The listener had felt the warning in the soles of their feet: not the usual shiver, but a pause where the city’s deep rhythm should have been. They pressed a palm to the wall. Around them, water hurried through its channel. Above them, people crossed a walkway, unaware.

The power could have stilled the tremor. It could have held every wall in place, sealed every crack, drained the streets and left the basin dry beneath a clean, unfamiliar sky.

It did none of these things.

Instead, it gave the wall one clear note.

The sound passed through the stone—not louder than the machinery, but shaped differently, with a small space around it. A note that could be felt through the hand. The listener knew it was not a pump, nor a warning horn, nor any signal used by the district. They struck the wall twice in reply.

Somewhere above, another person felt the answer in a railing. They sent a white blink along the service passage. A message traveled from roofline to lockhouse, from lockhouse to plaza: **hold the southern gates. The old fracture is speaking.**

The city began to move.

Platforms lifted. Crews opened the dry channels beneath the market. Families carried sleeping platforms to the upper levels. A group of children ran messages along a route that had been sealed the week before and was, somehow, open again. Nobody saw the power. Nobody paused to ask for help.

Below them, the buried stone shifted. Water pushed hard through the old fracture, seeking the streets it had found in another age. But the gates were ready. The channels took the force, and an ancient hollow—long unused, not quite forgotten—filled instead.

The city shook. One bridge cracked along its oldest seam. A row of lamps went dark. In the lower quarter, the water rose to the marks on the walls and stopped.

Then the pumps found their rhythm again.

For a while, the power remained with the listener, though the listener never knew it as a presence. It stayed in the brief quiet that made the warning legible, in the extra moment someone had to reach a latch, in the neighbors who came back for one another after the danger passed.

When the water receded, the inhabitants counted losses. They marked the cracked bridge. They argued about the gates. They carried a work-glove to the current for someone who had not come home.

The power did not spare them grief. It did not promise the city would never tremble again.

It left the old hollow connected to the channels, and the routes to it marked on the district maps. It left the warning-note in the stone—not as a command, but as a possibility. In time, people learned how to listen for it. They added it to the signs taught to children, and to the repertoire of knocks sent through pipes.

Much later, when asked why the hollow had been opened, an elder said that the city had needed somewhere for the water to go.

That was true.

It was also true that, for one impossible instant, someone had listened closely enough to help them hear what they already knew.
