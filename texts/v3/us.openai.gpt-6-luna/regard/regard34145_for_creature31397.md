The power did not descend upon the Choir.

It had no need to. The Choir was already full of arrivals: water reaching stone, shadows reaching their places, distant sounds arriving late. To come with thunder or radiance would have been to mistake being noticed for being entered.

So the power attended.

It listened to the streams that climbed and fell, to the deep note passing through the ground, to the small sounds that belonged to it afterward. It listened without asking the mountain to become clearer. It did not call the inhabitants spirits, or make a face for them, or assume that attention meant loneliness. It understood that a being might be difficult to distinguish from its home without being lost there.

For a long while, it did nothing.

Then, on a high shelf beside one of the old shelters, it made a small pause.

Not a silence. The wind continued through the chimneys; water slipped along the stone. But between a drop falling and its answer, there was room enough for a choice. The pause neither beckoned nor commanded. It simply remained.

The intervals gathered near it.

They did not come as bodies. A shadow settled a little sooner than expected. A narrow stream stopped climbing, held itself in place, then continued upward. In the grooves beside the shelter door, water stood without trembling.

The power offered no name. Names could become handles, and the Choir had not asked to be carried anywhere.

Instead, it gave the inhabitants a way to distinguish invitation from accident. If they wished to answer, they could let a sound return twice: once from where it had been heard, and once from where it had begun. If they did not wish to answer, the sound would pass on into the mountain, and nothing would follow.

The Choir considered this.

A drip struck stone. Far away, a second drip answered.

Then, after a pause, a third sound came—not from a place that had not yet been visited, but from the shelter’s shallow grooves, where the water had been held. It was soft, almost too small to hear beneath the wind. The power received it without reaching for more.

It did not repair the terraces or straighten the shadows. It did not fill the empty ledges with creatures, or make the cairns reliable, or teach the mountain to speak in a language travelers could understand. The Choir was not broken. Its strangeness was not a wound.

But the power left one kindness behind for those who crossed it: at each shelter, a groove in the stone would hold a little water, even in the driest weather. Anyone who paused there could see, in its still surface, whether their next step belonged to the path or only appeared to.

And for the inhabitants, the pause remained.

They could answer through it, if they chose. They could let it close. It asked nothing of them either way.

By dusk, the power was gone. The ridges still burned white. The stars still appeared below the horizon. Water ran down the cliffs, vanished into stone, and emerged above.

The Choir did not sing on purpose.

But now, somewhere in its crowded intervals, it knew that it had been heard—and that being heard had not cost it its silence.
