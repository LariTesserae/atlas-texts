The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have been a poor gift here. It would have flattened the distances, startled the gliders from their crowns, and turned every hidden thing into something exposed. Nor did the power speak. The people already had voices enough, and the plateau had taught them to hear what mattered.

It listened.

It listened to the bells that rang without hands, to the calls that measured a basin, to the roots drinking from the seams in the stone. It listened until it could tell the difference between a shelter creaking in the wind and a shelter beginning to fail.

Then, far beneath the western shelf, the rock shifted.

It was a small movement—less than the width of a hand—but it ran for miles along an old seam. Water entered. Ice formed in the cold. The fissure widened by degrees, too slowly for sight and too quickly for the people who had built their homes above it. In time, the shelf would break. The stone platforms would go with it. So would the spring below, and the paths of the herds that came there to drink.

The power could have held the shelf in place.

It did not.

A held mountain, it knew, would become a trap: safe only so long as the holding continued. And a people saved from every consequence might cease to hear the country they lived in.

Instead, the power touched the seam—not to close it, but to let it speak.

That evening the hollow stalk bells began to sound. Not in the wind’s scattered way, each answering its own uncertain weather, but in a slow sequence, one note following another along the western paths. The sound traveled from shelter to shelter, crossed the plant-vault, and returned from the cliffs with a new roughness in it.

The people stopped what they were doing.

An old woman came out beneath the overhang and listened. She had heard a sound like this once before, in a story told by her grandmother: the mountain’s first warning, before the old path fell away. She put her palm against the ground. Far below, the rock gave a faint, steady tremor.

By morning, the households had begun to move.

They did not flee in panic. They went by the routes their ancestors had left half-made: across the high stems, along the ledges, toward the sheltered basin where the stone was sound. They carried what could be carried. They left the oldest platforms to the roots, and marked the abandoned paths with bells so that no herd would wander onto the weakening shelf.

Some of the climbers followed the people, feeding on seed mash as they went. Others remained in the western thickets. The hunters moved first, away from the changing ground, and the small animals followed their unease. The herds heard the calls and bent their long migration toward the safer basin.

No creature was commanded. No path was made easy. The plateau still took effort to cross, and the fog still hid the far side of every ridge.

When the shelf broke, it broke empty.

Stone fell for a long time. The sound rolled across the plateau, through the plant-vaults and out over the basins. For a while, every call ceased. Then one bell rang from the new settlement, and another answered. A glider clicked from a high stem. Far off, the herd began again its low, traveling call.

The power remained where it was, without shape or name. It did not make the people grateful, or certain, or safe forever. It did not keep the hunters from hunting, or the weather from changing. It gave no sign that anyone could point to and say: There. That is what saved us.

Only the old woman, standing beside the new spring, felt the faint warmth in the rock beneath her hand. She listened for a long time.

Then she returned to the others and said, “The country has told us something.”

That was enough.

Afterward, the people left one small shelter empty at the edge of the new basin—not as a shrine, for they did not know whom they would be honoring, but as a place where anyone could go to listen. They hung no bell there. Bells belonged to paths and warnings. In the empty shelter, there was only a roof of living leaves, a smooth stone to sit upon, and a narrow opening toward the dark.

The power did not ask them to visit.

But when they did, it listened.
