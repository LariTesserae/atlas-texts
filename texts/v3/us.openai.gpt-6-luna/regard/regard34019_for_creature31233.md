The attention came first.

Nothing in the Bright Works lit up. No machinery stopped, no voice crossed the courts. But for one instant the plateau was not merely observed from afar. It was met—terrace by terrace, seam by seam, with the exactness of a hand learning the shape of a face.

The power noticed the herds, not as a moving pattern but as each animal: the one with a pale scar along its flank, the one that always lagged at the bridges, the young one that had never seen the clouds from below. It noticed the birds in their signal housings, the winged dark gathered around the lamps, the quick creatures leaving their spirals in the dust. It noticed the keepers, too. Not their colored cuffs or their duties, but the particular weariness in each body, and the small private hopes they had not thought to name.

And it noticed the Works.

It did not mistake their strangeness for a wound.

The plateau had not asked to be made ordinary. Its channels ran as they ran. Its distances lengthened and shortened. The power did not force the water downhill, or put soil beneath the terraces, or smooth the seams into something that could be understood at a glance. It knew that to repair a thing without knowing what it was could be another way of breaking it.

At dawn, one keeper stood alone beside a channel where the water had stopped. The keeper had been there a long time. Their task was to watch, and they had watched until the light changed and changed again. In the still surface they could see their face. In the metal beside it, they could see a different court: one not yet built, or perhaps one already gone.

Then, for the first time in many years, the keeper heard a voice clearly.

*Are you in need?*

The keeper looked up. There was no figure on the terrace, no new star above the roofs. The voice had arrived without traveling through the air, and so it carried none of the distance the Works usually put between one thing and another.

“I don’t know,” the keeper said.

It was the truest answer they had.

The power did not rebuke them for it. It did not tell them what the plateau was for, or what the keeper’s life ought to mean. It waited—not because it needed an answer, but because the keeper deserved time to find one.

After a while, the keeper said, “Sometimes I would like not to be alone.”

So the power did one small thing.

It made the next footstep sound like a footstep: not beneath the keeper’s heel, not lost at the far end of a corridor, but approaching along the path. A second keeper came around the bend carrying a cup of collected cloud-water. They had been sent to check the channel, though neither could say who had sent them. They sat beside one another and waited.

The water trembled. Then it moved—not downhill, but onward, along the route it had chosen.

The power did not make this happen. It had only stayed long enough for it to be witnessed.

Later, the keeper tried to explain what had occurred. Sound failed them, as it often did. So they painted a mark at the junction: not an arrow, not a warning, not a direction. Just a small open circle, the color of the stripe at their cuff.

The junction turned before evening. The mark turned with it.

That night, the Bright Works continued their careful, nearly silent labor. Nothing had been solved. Nothing had been claimed. The plateau held its place above the clouds, and those who lived upon it went on being themselves.

But somewhere within the immense attention of the power, the keeper was remembered—not as a keeper, not as a function of the Works, but as one particular being who had said *I don’t know* and then, when given the time, had said *sometimes*. And whenever the keeper wished, they could speak again.

That was all the power promised.

It was enough to leave.
