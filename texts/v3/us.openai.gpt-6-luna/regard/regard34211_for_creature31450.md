The power did not mistake their quiet for a plea.

It watched for a long while. It saw the care in the measured turns at the public tap, the bowl carried across the square, the roof patched before the cloud reached the shelf. It saw, too, the cost of that care: the extra climb with a full jar, the old person who drank less than they needed, the worry concealed in a glance at a dry channel.

It would not flood the mountain with water. A sudden abundance would be its own kind of trespass. It would not make the town green, or loosen its stones, or tell the people what their lives should become.

Instead, it listened beneath the shelf.

Deep in the mountain, where old seams ran blind through the rock, it gathered what the clouds had already given and guided it toward the town—not in a torrent, but in a patient thread. The change was so slight that at first it seemed only a quirk of the stone: a dampness in a wall that had always been dry, a bead of water appearing after the rain had passed. Someone noticed. Someone brought a jar. Then someone else came with a tool, and together they opened a channel no wider than a hand.

The water did not make them careless. It did not fill every basin or turn the courts into gardens. There was still a measure to keep, a channel to clear, a cistern to seal. But in the hardest weeks, the last jar no longer stood empty. No one had to decide which neighbor could go without.

The people called it a new seep. They argued over how best to protect it, and made rules for its use. Children were sent to check its course. An old woman, who remembered every spring the mountain had ever offered, declared that this one was worth trusting—but not worth taking for granted.

The power made no sign of itself. It asked for no thanks and left no mark upon the stone. When the people looked out across the gap, the far ridge remained distant, the cloud rested where it always had, and the town held to its ledge.

Then the power turned its attention elsewhere, leaving the people their work, their weather, and the quiet dignity of deciding what to do with one small gift.
