The power did not begin by changing anything.

It looked across the plateau and saw no wound it could mend without making another. The pools were small, but they held water. The herds wandered, but they found their way. The old stone was crumbling, as old stone must. Even the paths that vanished beneath the plants were not lost; they remained in the feet that had traveled them, and in the habits of those who would travel them again.

So the power did not send a river down from the sky. It did not make the grasses endless or the winters harmless. It did not silence the predators. The plateau was not asking to be rescued from being alive.

But it noticed the woman in the hut below the saddle.

Her name was Tavi. She was old enough to know the calls of most of the birds and young enough, she sometimes thought, to keep learning them. That evening she had gone to the nearest pool with a jar and come back with less water than she’d hoped. She set it beside the hearth, ate a little, and sat in the doorway listening to the wind pass over the roofs.

She had spent the day mending a low wall where the rains had loosened it. She had not finished. Tomorrow she would carry more stones. If the pool lasted, she would return to it. If it did not, she knew which hollow to try next.

The power saw all of this: not a grand struggle, but a life made of attention, repetition, and small acts of care. It saw that Tavi had left the seed heads on the slope, though she could have gathered them. It saw that she had once driven a thirsty herd away from a fragile seep, then waited for them at a more generous pool. It saw the marks her hands had made on the stones, and the marks the stones had made on her.

It did one thing.

As she sat in the doorway, the power let her feel, for the length of a breath, the full company of the place. Not as a revelation, and not as a voice. The insects in the mats, the birds settling into the cliffs, the sleeping roots, the far-off herd moving beyond sight: all of it was present to her, not as noise around her life, but as life beside it.

Tavi did not understand what had happened. She only leaned back against the doorpost and let her eyes close. For that one breath, she did not have to be the keeper of the paths, or the judge of the water, or the person who would remember what to do tomorrow. She was allowed simply to belong.

Then the wind shifted. A bird called once from the rim. Tavi opened her eyes and went inside.

In the morning she would mend the wall. The pool would be no fuller than before. The plateau would keep its long, patient changes.

And the power, having done no more than was needed, remained.
