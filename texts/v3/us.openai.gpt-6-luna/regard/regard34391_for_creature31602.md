It did not arrive as a light.

Light would have made the water bright enough to blind them, and the city had no use for a sun.

It came first as a pause in the currents. Not a stoppage—nothing so abrupt that a filter might fail or a door strain in its frame—but a gentling, as if the water had drawn a careful breath. The usual horizontal bands of silt drifted past the lamps and thinned. In the service tunnel, the small figure stopped with two fingers against the wall.

The pulse came again.

It was not the city’s pulse. It had no mechanical interval, no slight hitch at the end. It moved through the stone with the patience of something that could wait to be understood.

The figure tapped twice against the wall: the old signal for *someone there?*

The answer was not a voice. It was a pressure, soft against the fingertips. Then, after a while, two pulses.

The figure called the others.

They came cautiously, carrying lamps and tools, leaving the doors behind them sealed. No one went alone. They listened together in the tunnel, their gloved hands resting on the stone. The presence did not reach for them. It did not open their doors, or stir the water inside their rooms, or pour bright certainty into their masks.

It waited.

At last, one of them placed a palm flat against the wall. They were the keeper of the lower culvert, old enough to remember when its piston had begun to falter. They sent the customary sequence of knocks: a request for passage, a warning of obstruction, a question about the gate below.

The answer came slowly. It was not a sequence they knew. Yet its meaning settled among them—not as words, but as the understanding that the presence had noticed the culvert, and would not alter it without their consent.

They showed it where the mechanism had worn thin.

For several days, nothing changed. The inhabitants continued their rounds. They checked the seals, turned the brass tabs, tended the pale fronds. They spoke about the pulses, though never in the rooms where children were trying to sleep. Some thought the presence was a person in the outer districts. Some thought it was the rock itself, remembering how to speak. The oldest among them said that a thing’s name mattered less than what it did when asked to wait.

On the fourth day, the lower culvert gave one long, familiar shudder.

The keeper went to inspect it with two companions. The piston was still there, still making its slow stroke. But the crack in its housing had closed—not filled with new metal, not covered over, but joined along its length, the iron smooth and dark as if it had never split. The machine turned. The water passed through. Nothing else in the district had changed.

That night, the presence came to the wall again.

This time the inhabitants brought their own questions. They asked whether the outer districts were inhabited. They asked whether the basin would remain habitable. They asked, though no one had intended to, whether the presence could take them somewhere else.

The answer to the first question was a distant pattern, faint but unmistakable: pulses answering pulses beyond the basin’s edge. There were others. The answer to the second was not a promise that nothing would fail, but a knowledge of the buried water paths, the warm seams, the places where the city’s old systems could be coaxed into serving again. The answer to the third was silence.

Not refusal. Not yet.

Then the presence offered them a path—not a road forced open through the black, but a way to find the outer districts if they chose to search. It marked the route in small changes to the current, each one stable enough to be tested, each one easy to ignore. They could follow it, or not. They could send one of their own, or wait. The city would remain theirs either way.

In the months that followed, a few went beyond the last lamps. Others stayed to mend what they knew. The children learned the new current as they learned the old ones: by touch, by patience, and with a tether held loosely in an adult’s hand.

The presence did not become their ruler, nor their rescuer. It did not make the basin safe forever. It repaired what could be repaired, revealed what had been hidden, and left the inhabitants with the dignity of deciding what to do next.

Sometimes, when the lamps dimmed, a pulse travelled through the stone.

The inhabitants answered if they wished.

And the water, though it never rested, no longer felt entirely indifferent.
