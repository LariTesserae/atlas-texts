The power came to the chamber without entering it.

It had no need to take a shape. The beetles would have mistaken a shape for a shadow; the swimmers, a tremor; the moths, perhaps, a change in warmth. So the power did not ask them to recognize it. It attended.

It saw that the chamber was not empty, though the stone left so much room between one life and another. It saw the long patience of the water, the roots spending years to reach a damp ledge, the creatures making use of what was left behind. It saw that none of them needed to become grander in order to matter.

For a while, the power did nothing.

This was not indifference. It was the first kindness: not to mistake care for improvement. The chamber had its own order, delicate but not helpless. To fill it with bright flowers, to send a stream coursing through its hollows, to make every creature visible and bold—these would be gifts only to a mind that loved spectacle more than life.

So the power left the pale light pale, and the water slow. It did not teach the moths to sing or give the swimmers names. It did not make the beetles gather in one place to behold their benefactor. The chamber was allowed to continue being itself.

But the power noticed the narrow seam overhead, and the pressure of stone gathering along one old crack. It noticed, too, that the roots had nearly reached the last damp ledge. And when, far above, a season of fierce dryness came, the power did not summon a flood. It kept one small thread of moisture moving through the earth—a bead at a time, almost indistinguishable from the chamber’s ordinary weather.

The pools did not deepen. They did not vanish.

A root descended another fraction. The green film held to the wet stone. The swimmers rose beneath the falling drops, as they had always done, answering a pulse they could not see. The moths opened their wings when the light widened.

Nothing in the chamber knew that anything had been spared. This, too, was part of the kindness. No creature was made to feel indebted for the conditions of its life; no peace was disturbed by revelation.

The power remained—not as a voice in the stone, but as a steadiness in what might otherwise have broken too quickly. It would not keep the chamber unchanged forever. Change belonged to the place as surely as stillness did. One day the seam would widen or close; one pool would dry, another would form. The power would not call that failure.

It would simply keep watch, and when the hour came, let the chamber change by degrees its inhabitants could survive.

For now, a drop gathered on the ceiling. It held the chamber upside down in its tiny surface, then fell.

The swimmers rose to meet it. The note rang softly from the hollow floor and lingered in the air.

And the power, which had no wish to be heard, listened.
