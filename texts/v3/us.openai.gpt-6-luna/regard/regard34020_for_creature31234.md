The power came to the hollow without entering it.

It had no need to take a shape. It listened first: to water ticking from the roots, to beetles moving beneath the leaves, to the long, nearly silent work by which the place kept itself alive. It saw that the hollow had no single keeper. Its life was a thousand small permissions—water finding a seam, a crab finding a damp crack, a root finding stone soft enough to part.

The power understood that the hollow itself was the being who lived there: not separate from its inhabitants, but made of their crossings, their shelter, their hunger and return.

So it did not brighten the dark, or command the animals, or make the dry season vanish. Those things belonged to the hollow’s own becoming. To make them easy would be to make them meaningless.

It gave only this: a quiet protection against what would end the hollow before its time.

Years later, when men came to mark the hill for cutting, their stakes would not hold in the wet ground. Their plans would be delayed; other work would call them elsewhere. They would remember the place only as troublesome, and leave it alone. And when a storm came hard enough to send a torrent toward the entrance, the water would find an older, lower seam and pass harmlessly beneath the roots. The hollow would still flood a little, still lose leaves, still shrink to its dry pools. Nothing would be spared the ordinary weather of being alive.

The power left no sign of itself. No new light appeared on the walls. No voice spoke from the pillar. Only, for a moment, every creature in the hollow paused—not in fear, but as if the warmth beneath the stone had gathered close around them.

Then the crabs resumed their searching. A swallow brushed the air above the steps. Somewhere in the dark, a drop fell into a basin.

And the hollow went on.
