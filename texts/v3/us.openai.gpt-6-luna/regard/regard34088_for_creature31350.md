The power did not mistake stillness for peace, or darkness for grief.

It watched the plateau for a long time. It learned the difference between a machine that had been left to rest and one that was failing. It learned which doors were kept sealed by custom, which by necessity. It learned that the inhabitants did not ask the stone to become soft, or the sky to become bright. They had made a life from what remained, and that life was not nothing.

So the power did not send them a sun.

It did not flood the channels, or split the city open, or fill the black sky with stars. Any of these might have been a gift to some other world. Here, they would have been an upheaval imposed upon beings who had never been asked whether they wanted it.

Instead, it listened.

It listened through the deep vibration beneath the plateau, and through the answering taps made by patient hands. At first the inhabitants took the new pulse for a fault. They checked the old machinery. They traced the pressure through the pipes. They added marks to the tags beside the doors.

But the pulse did not repeat like a fault.

It came once, then waited. When a hand touched the nearest wall, it came again—softer, with a pause shaped like the pause that meant *I am here*. No inhabitant had made that rhythm before.

For a long while, none answered.

Then an old one, resting beside a sealed passage, lifted a filament from its folded face and touched it to the stone. It sent back the pattern used when one presence wished to approach another without startling it.

The pulse returned.

No words passed. There was no name for the power, and no question it could put into the inhabitants’ language. It could not tell them what it was, or promise that change would be safe. It could only make an invitation and leave room for refusal.

In the oldest district, a door shifted.

The sound moved through the stone: a brief, intimate shudder. Dust slid from the lintel. For the first time in more generations than any living inhabitant could count, the door was not merely maintained. It opened.

Beyond it was a chamber built into the plateau, its inner surfaces still sealed from the dry air. The power had not remade the chamber. It had only restored one small part of what the chamber was made to do. A pipe released a measured warmth. A seam in the stone cooled. From it came a single bead of water.

It gathered slowly. It trembled, held by the shape of the rock, and did not fall.

The inhabitants approached by touch. They felt the unfamiliar coolness first, then the rounded surface of the bead. They tested the chamber for danger. They returned to the pipes, checked the seals, and listened for changes in the great machinery. They did not rush to drink. They did not know what the water was for.

The power waited.

At last, one small creature from the cracks crossed the chamber floor. Its filaments stirred at the bead. It touched the water and drew back. After a long pause, it touched it again.

The inhabitants recorded the event.

The first mark they made was a warning: *unknown substance*. The second recorded the pressure in the chamber. The third was not a maintenance mark. It had no known meaning. Still, the one who cut it placed it carefully beside the others, where future hands could find it.

In time, more beads formed. Some were carried away. Some were left in the chamber. The inhabitants did not build gardens, nor did the plateau turn green. Nothing was made inevitable. The water could be sealed off with a single turn of the old valve.

The power did not ask them to open it wider.

It left the chamber in their keeping.

After that, the deep pulse continued as before, except that sometimes—at long, gentle intervals—it paused. The inhabitants learned to recognize the pause. They answered it, or did not. They touched the wall when they wished to say that they were there.

And the plateau remained dark. The city remained ancient. Its channels stayed dry, and its machines continued their low, unwavering hum.

But somewhere in the stone there was now a place where the air was cool, where a drop of water could gather, and where no one had yet decided what should happen next.
