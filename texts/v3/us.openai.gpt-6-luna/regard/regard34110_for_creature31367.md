The power did not descend upon the Crown.

It had no need to announce itself. It listened first.

It listened to the settlement’s clamor, to the rasp of insects beneath the stones, to the roots working patiently through the mountain’s old fractures. It listened to the people naming what their water was for. It watched them leave flowers uncut, return fallen stones to their walls, and teach children to recognize the silences that meant an animal was near.

The power was wise enough to know that a hard life is not always a life asking to be replaced. The people of the Crown were not waiting to be rescued from their home. They had made a way of living in it—difficult, exacting, and full of attentions the power itself might have missed, had it arrived too quickly with gifts.

So it did not send rain.

Rain would have run down the sheer sides of the mountain and vanished. Or, if it lingered, it might have changed the plants, the paths, the old agreements between roots and stone. Nor did the power uncover the world below. The people had learned to live without seeing it, and a new horizon might have been wonder, or grief, or simply another thing they could not reach.

It made one small alteration.

Each night, a little more coolness entered the air over the Crown. Not enough to turn the warm plateau cold, not enough to make the leaves drip or fill the dry cisterns at once. Just enough that, before dawn, the fine hairs on certain plants caught more moisture. Just enough to make the familiar work of gathering it a little surer.

The first morning, a child found beads of dew on a terrace plant and called everyone to look. They came, some smiling, some suspicious, some already reaching for their bowls. They collected the water carefully. They measured it. They gave some to a seed bed and some to a sick animal, and left a little where it was, for the plant.

No voice spoke from the sky. No mark appeared on the old walls. The Crown had not been promised safety, only a little more room between hardship and disaster.

In time, the people made a name for the altered mornings. They added them to their calendars and their stories. They learned which leaves held the most, and changed nothing that did not need changing. They thanked one another, sometimes the mountain, sometimes the plants. The power did not correct them.

It remained attentive.

If a wall began to fail, it did not set the stones back in place; the people knew how to mend a wall. If an animal wandered into danger, it did not carry it home; the colony’s calls and the children’s watchfulness often sufficed. But should the Crown face a loss from which its inhabitants could not recover—should some calamity threaten the web of lives that had taken ages to gather there—the power would act again, as little as it could and as much as it must.

For now, it let the people keep their own hands on their home.

And before each dawn, while the warm stone slowly loosened its hold on the day, it listened to the Crown breathing in its sleep.
