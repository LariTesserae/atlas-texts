The power did not descend in fire, or arrive with a voice that made the pines bow.

It came as attention.

For a while it stood beyond the rim, where the ridge fell into cloud, and looked down into the hollow. It saw the water keeping its small, impossible shapes. It saw the grasses taking up the ground the water offered them, the old trees leaning into the wind, and the creature—or not-creature—that had made a life of remaining.

The power was wise enough not to call this disorder merely because it could not name it.

It did not give the resident a body. It did not give it speech, or ask it to become more like a person in order to be understood. Instead, the power waited. It let the clouds pass. It listened until the hollow’s many sounds ceased to be background and became themselves: the ticking stones, the runnel beneath the moss, the sedges brushing one another in the wind.

Then it asked, gently, whether anything was needed.

The question went down through the air and into the water. It traveled along the clear current beside the clouded one. It reached the little pool left in a vanished footprint.

For a time, nothing changed.

Then the pool held the shape of the print a little longer than it ever had before. Not as an answer, exactly. Perhaps only because the water had been noticed. Perhaps because the resident was considering the question. At last, the edges softened. The hollow filled, and the water moved on.

The power understood that there was no request to be rescued. The resident was not trapped in its strangeness. It was not waiting to be made whole. Its life was this place, and the place was not a problem to solve.

So the power did not make the water still. It did not promise that the ridge would never change, or that no tree would fall, or that every passing creature would return. Such promises would have been another kind of harm: a fence built around a living thing.

It made no mark that could be mistaken for ownership.

Instead, it remembered the hollow—not as a picture kept unchanged, but as a living particular: the spring under the flat stone, the two currents, the old pines, the patient being spread among them. Should a force come that would destroy the hollow without need—an act of violence, a careless severing of the spring—the power would know. It would not prevent rain from reshaping the banks, or age from bending the trees. But it would not mistake destruction for change.

Then it withdrew its attention from the center of the hollow, though it did not forget.

The sun continued to fall cleanly through the cloud. Mist gathered in the runnel and drifted uphill. Somewhere beneath the moss, water paused, then found its way around a stone.

Nothing in the hollow could have said that a blessing had been given.

Perhaps that was how the power meant it.
