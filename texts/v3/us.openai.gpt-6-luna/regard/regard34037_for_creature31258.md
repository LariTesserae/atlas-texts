The power did not arrive as light.

Light was already scarce and useful there, and it had no wish to blind anyone.

It listened first.

It listened to the water guild tapping the cistern rims, to the night shift singing under its breath, to the children learning the pitches of the walkways. It listened to the stone too: its slow pressure, its small adjustments, the deep groan that had no human name. The people had spent generations learning to hear the place. The power understood that this was not ignorance waiting to be corrected. It was knowledge, made patient by necessity.

So it did not make the city safe. Nothing living in a changing world could be made entirely safe, and the power would not take from these people the skill by which they had kept one another alive.

It waited for a moment when help could matter.

It came in the hours before dawn, in a gallery where several families slept behind a wall that had begun, over many days, to lean inward. The stone was not yet moving. The warning chime above the passage hung still. The people inside slept with their hands curled near their faces, their tools and clothes packed in familiar chests.

Then the chime sounded.

Not loudly. Not like an alarm. It gave one low, clear note that none of the residents had heard before.

The sleepers woke. The oldest among them sat upright and raised a hand. Everyone listened.

The note came again.

They left by the narrow stair, carrying children and lamps, not stopping to gather what could be replaced. When the last person was clear, the wall shifted. Not a collapse, not a catastrophe: a long, gritty press that bent the passage and buried the sleeping alcoves under black stone.

By morning, the inhabitants had marked the new edge, salvaged what they could, and begun to build elsewhere. They argued about bracing and routes. Someone made tea in a dented kettle. The water guild tested the drip that had appeared in a nearby seam, as it tested every new seep. It was only a little water, and too mineral-heavy for drinking. Useful for washing tools, perhaps.

Nothing came down from the ceiling to explain what had happened.

The new note in the chime did not sound again for many months. When it did, it warned of a shift in a remote gallery, where no one slept but where a cable had been laid across the floor. The workers heard it, cleared the passage, and reset the line before the stone moved.

In time, they made a place for that note in their knowledge. They taught children to recognize it, just as they taught them the sound of a failing belt or a pipe taking on air. They did not call it a miracle. Some called it a strange gift. Others said the stone had always had that sound and they had only now learned to hear it.

The power let them choose their own name for it.

It did not fill the cisterns, cool the furnaces, or hold the walls in place forever. It did not make the city’s hard-won habits unnecessary. It gave them a little more time: enough to wake, to listen, to get one another out.

Then it withdrew its attention—not in indifference, but in trust.

The city went on being a city in unfinished stone. The chimes were checked at the start of each shift. Water was caught wherever it gathered. Walkways were rebuilt when the ground changed its mind.

And sometimes, in the quiet before a new fracture opened, the people heard a note beneath the ordinary sounds: not a command, not a promise that nothing bad would happen, but a gentle warning that they were not listening alone.
