The power did not descend upon the mountain.

It did not speak through the lamps, or put a name into the mouths of the builders. The garden was already full of instructions: water when the cisterns are full; light through the day; roots must not pass through the floor. A new command, even a kind one, would still be a command.

So the power watched.

It saw that the place was not suffering from being unfinished. Its unfinishedness was how it learned. It saw the resident—not a creature hidden beneath the leaves, but a slow accord among roots, moss, water, mineral, and machine. It saw that this accord had not asked to become more than it was.

Then the power made a few small changes.

A pump that would have failed in the next cold season held its rhythm. A hairline crack in the upper cistern sealed itself with a pale mineral seam, leaving the water to rise as before. Beneath the central stair, the unplanned channel was neither blocked nor allowed to undermine the structure. A little excess flowed elsewhere, through a route that had not been on the plans, and found its way back to the mats.

No one noticed a miracle. The gardeners noticed only that the repairs they had expected to make could wait.

The power did not stop the roots from entering the grout. It softened the grout around them. It did not command the vines to stay within their rings. It made the rings loosen when the stems grew thick. When a worker came with shears to clear the dark moss from the old lamp-rectangles, the shears slipped from their hand—not far, not dangerously. The worker laughed, set them down, and decided to finish the work tomorrow.

Tomorrow, they decided to leave that patch alone.

The power did not give the resident speech. It did not put a mind into the insect, or make the insect its messenger. It left the insect small and dark and difficult to follow among the leaves. But it opened, somewhere beyond the retaining wall, a narrow passage in the air: not a door the garden would recognize, only a chance for other spores, other winged things, other unfamiliar lives to arrive when the time was right.

Whether anything would come through, the power did not decide.

That was the important part.

The garden continued to breathe by its old measures. Pumps hummed. Water found the seams. The pale walls warmed and cooled. The resident made room where it could, and took room where it was needed. The builders kept their plans, though the plans became less certain. No great hand lifted the place from the mountain’s crown. No voice told its inhabitant what it was.

The power simply kept catastrophe from mistaking itself for an answer.

And when, one morning, a tendril crossed the grout beneath the stair and reached a place where no water had ever run, the power let it find the damp there.

Not a reward. Not a test.

Only enough.
