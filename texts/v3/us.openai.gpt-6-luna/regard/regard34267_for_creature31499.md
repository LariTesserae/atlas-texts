The power did not enter through the seam.

It had no need to. It was already listening.

For a long while, it did nothing. It listened to the pumps knocking patiently in the depths, to the small feet threading the walls, to the people pausing on bridges to feel whether the metal had settled. It learned the difference between a warning tap and a child imitating one; between a machine about to fail and one that had simply always complained. It listened, too, for the sounds that never came back.

It did not mistake the Upper Pocket for a prison. The people had made a life there—not an easy life, but one with its own knowledge, courtesies, jokes, and ways of keeping one another near. To lift them out without asking would have been another kind of disaster.

So the power did not lift them out.

It did not still the mountain, either. The mountain was not cruel for changing, and the people were not foolish for living among its changes. To make every ledge permanent and every machine obedient would have remade the place they knew into somewhere else.

But there was a fracture above the old industrial throat, hidden behind a bank of pipes. It had been widening by the width of a hair for years. The people had noticed the new tremor in the lower landings, but each reading disagreed with the last, and no one could spare enough hands to take the machinery apart and look behind it.

The power laid its attention along the fracture.

The mountain shifted—not stopped, not healed, but shifted into a safer resting place. A shelf of stone settled against another shelf with a sound like a great door being carefully closed. The lower pumps gave one startled knock, then resumed their old rhythm. A hanging bridge shivered. Nothing fell.

At the same moment, a seam opened beside a familiar maintenance stair. From it came a thread of water, slow and cold. Not a flood, not a river, not enough to make the Pocket forget its thirst. Enough to fill a cup, then another. Enough to be tested, watched, argued over, and eventually shared.

The first person to find it did not call it a miracle. They called for someone with a clean vessel.

The power made no voice in the shaft. It did not ask for gratitude, and it left no sign that could be used to build a temple or settle an argument. It gave the people a little more room to choose: a brace where collapse had been likely, a source where there had been only rumor. The rest belonged to them.

For three nights, the fliers avoided the old throat. The crawlers gathered at the new seep, drank, and went on climbing. On the fourth night, the fliers returned to their exhausts.

The people marked the safer stair in fresh paint. They set a shallow dish beside the water for the pale climbers, then set another farther off, in case the first proved too inviting. They listened to the pumps. They listened to the mountain. They listened, as they always had, for whatever was still falling.

And the power listened with them.
