It would not descend into the hollow.

It would not call the creatures blessed, or cursed, or broken. It would not mistake their liveliness for freedom, nor their stillness for peace. It would listen long enough to hear the thing none of them could hear: every note returning unchanged, every movement ending where it began.

And it would be careful. A future is not only a gift. It is also hunger, weather, injury, departure, and the possibility of loss. The power would not tear open the hollow’s fixed present and leave its inhabitants to face all of that at once.

Instead, it would make one small room for what had never happened.

The next call would be different.

Not louder. Not sweeter. Just different: a tiny wavering in a creature’s note, scarcely more than a tremor in the great sustained roar. The sound would pass through the plants, under the dry salt, along the mineral seams.

For the first time, another creature would hear something it had not heard before.

It might draw back. It might answer. The power would not choose for it.

The folded animal near the low wall would open its hand-shaped limb. Slowly, uncertainly, it would reach toward the three stone flakes. The nearest would shift beneath its touch. A chip of black stone would scrape across the salt and come to rest at a new angle.

Nothing would collapse. No ancient debt would be revealed. No hidden wound would burst open. The mineral breath would continue to feed the plants; the animals would remain as they were, but no longer exactly as they were. The hollow would still be warm, still loud, still strange. It would simply contain one thing that had happened—and the possibility of another.

Then the power would wait.

If the creatures recoiled from change, it would keep the opening gentle. If they reached for one another, it would allow the reach to continue. It would not command the plants to bloom or the animals to love or the maker of the wall to return. It would not decide what their lives ought to mean.

It would only keep the new moment from being taken away.

Above them, the false stars would remain close together. The one shaped like an open eye would blink—not as a signal, and not as an answer. Just once. The creatures would hear the changed note again, and this time one of them would make a sound that did not match it.

The hollow would still be a hollow. But somewhere within its crowded, listening dark, there would be a before and an after.
