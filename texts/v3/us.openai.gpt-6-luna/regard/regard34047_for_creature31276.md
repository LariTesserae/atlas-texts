The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have frightened the swallows from the eaves, and the people had learned not to trust anything that made the whole basin stop and stare.

It came as attention.

For a while, nothing changed. The engine clattered. Water ticked through the grates. The old woman checked the sluice on her way to the garden and found it running as it should. The power watched the people notice one another’s small troubles: a frayed belt, a child’s wet sleeve, the new cloudiness in a drainage ditch. It saw that the town was not waiting to be rescued. It was already tending itself.

So it did not pave the gardens, silence the machinery, or make the damp ground dry. It did not turn the fox tame or the river obedient. Those things would have been easier than wisdom.

Instead, when the long rain came, it made one small thing impossible to miss.

Water poured down the dark ridges and gathered in the channels. By evening, the sluice had begun to sing at a pitch no one had heard before: thin, wavering, almost like a kettle left too long on the stove. A worker stopped on the road. A child, carrying a hooked wire, stopped too. The old woman opened her gate and listened.

They followed the sound upstream. Beneath a shelf of gravel, a buried drain had begun to fail. The water was forcing its way through the loosened earth toward the lowest row of houses.

There was no voice from the clouds, no hand descending to shift the stones. There was only the strange clear note, and the people who knew enough to follow it. They brought shovels, timber, rope. They called the workshop. Someone fetched the mechanic; someone else went door to door. They worked through the rain by lantern-light, cutting a temporary channel and bracing the bank before it could give way.

At dawn, the storm eased. The houses were still standing. The new channel ran crookedly through the weeds, where it would remain until the town decided what to do with it.

Afterward, they argued about the repair. They argued about the weeds, too, and whether the willow roots had helped or hindered. The mechanic said the sluice had made a sound he could not account for. The child said it had sounded like a kettle. The old woman said only that it had been wise to listen.

The power let them keep their explanations.

It left the Garden as it had found it: damp, noisy, imperfect, alive. But from then on, now and then, when a small trouble was about to become a large one, some ordinary sound would carry just far enough to reach the person who needed to hear it.

And the people, being good at noticing changes by a finger’s width, listened.
