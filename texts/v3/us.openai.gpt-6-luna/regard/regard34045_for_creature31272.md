The power came in the rain.

It did not descend through the clouds or stand blazing on the rim. It was there in the patient water, in the dark seams of the limestone, in the mist that slipped between the towers. For a while it simply attended.

It saw the plateau—not as a wilderness spoiled by a city, nor as a city besieged by nature, but as a life made of many lives. The birds’ crowded ledges. The blind fish turning in their black pools. The people listening to drains, mending walls, yielding streets. The ancient stone, bearing all of them without hurry.

It saw the costs, too. A worker injured beneath a shifting load. A nest washed from a ledge. The long labor of keeping water clean and walls sound. Wisdom did not mistake endurance for ease, or call every hardship beautiful.

Still, the power found no single wrong thing it could mend without breaking some other thing in the mending. The city did not need a new master. The plateau did not need to be made soft, or silent, or green. Its inhabitants were already learning how to live together—not perfectly, but attentively.

So the power did not command. It did not make the birds obedient, the rain gentle, or the people free of work.

It gave them one small thing.

Far below the central basin, where an old runoff channel passed behind a row of workshops, a retaining wall had begun to loosen. No one could see the widening seam. The water sounded ordinary. Then, beneath its rush, came a low clear note.

It was unlike the knocking of a blocked drain, unlike the groan of an overloaded pump. The note carried through wet stone and iron pipe. Birds lifted from the nearest roofs. The pale burrowers fled the slope. A child paused in the street and tugged at her mother’s sleeve.

The workers heard it, too. They followed the sound to the wall and found the crack before the next rain could force it open. They closed the lower avenue, shifted the water into an older channel, and braced the stone. No one was swept away. No nests were lost.

Afterward, the note came only rarely. It sounded when a channel was close to failing, when a ledge had grown unsafe, when some strain had become too great to leave unattended. It did not say what to do. It did not distinguish, for them, between what was important and what could wait. That remained the work of living there.

They made room in their records for the new warning. They taught children to listen for it. The millwrights learned not to drown it with machinery. The birds, who had heard it first, were never credited in the official account, though everyone knew.

The power stayed long enough to see the people change a few habits: a walkway covered sooner, a channel inspected earlier, a nesting terrace left undisturbed for one more season. Then it withdrew—not far away, but into the world’s ordinary keeping.

The rain went on. The engines turned. The birds called from the roofs, and the plateau held its patient shape.

Nothing had been made perfect. But when the city and the stone were nearing the edge of what they could bear, they had been given a way to hear one another.
