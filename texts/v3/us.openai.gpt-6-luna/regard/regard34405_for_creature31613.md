The power did not descend.

It did not lay a river through the plain, or soften the soil into fields, or tell the people what they ought to become. It understood that a gift could also be a disturbance. Water arriving all at once might scour the young ground, drown the roots that held it, or turn a careful way of life into a struggle over who owned the new abundance.

So it watched first.

It watched the people cut only what could grow back. It watched them leave the deepest roots undisturbed, and make room in their narrow paths for animals they feared. It watched a household mend a wall with patient hands, though the wall would one day return to earth. The power saw that they were not waiting to be rescued. They were already answering the world, each in the small way available to them.

Still, it noticed the cost of answering. It noticed the children waking thirsty in the hottest part of a long season, and the old people who gave their share away without speaking of it. It noticed how far a water-gatherer sometimes walked before returning with a basket that was almost light.

Then, in the high air above the plain, the power made a change so slight that no one could point to its beginning.

At night, a little more coolness settled over the land. Not rain—not yet, and never enough to beat down the delicate growth—but a thin, silvering dampness. It gathered on the broad leaves and along the hollow stems. It beaded on the roofs, darkened the packed earth, and sank into the shallow seams where roots were already reaching. By dawn, the plain held a little more water than it had the day before.

The people found it in the familiar ways: a heavier leaf, a fuller stem, a coolness in the air that lingered past sunrise. They checked the plants, then one another. No one claimed to understand it. Some said a season had turned. Some said the land had opened a new vein. The oldest water-gatherer only stood with both hands beneath a dripping crown and listened to the small, steady fall.

They learned what could be gathered and what must be left. They added new marks to old paths. They changed nothing quickly. The animals found the damp places too, and the people made room for them as they always had.

The power did not ask to be thanked. It did not put its name in the sky, or make the people dependent on a wonder they could neither tend nor understand. It gave them no certainty that the world would become easy.

It gave them a little more morning.

And when the plain stirred beneath its canopy—people, roots, insects, small animals, all waking into the mildness—the power let the living world continue to answer for itself.
