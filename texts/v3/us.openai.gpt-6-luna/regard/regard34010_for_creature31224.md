The power came first as attention.

It did not descend in fire or break the quiet with a voice. It rested over the plateau so completely that, for a time, every small thing seemed to arrive in its mind: the spring threading through its crack, the grit gathering in a channel, the warmth held under a roof slab after sunset. It attended to the town, too—to the hands that cleared and mended, to the children learning where the ground was slick, to the old people who remembered walls before they had been extended.

It saw the absence of animals. It did not mistake absence for a wound.

The town was not waiting to be made whole. Its people had their own ways of noticing, tending, and belonging. To fill the basin with creatures simply because the basin was empty of them would have been to answer a question no one had asked.

So the power changed nothing.

Then, one season, the rain came hard enough to darken the plateau for days. Water ran in sheets over the upper shelf, carrying pale grit down the paths. At the eastern gardens, it found a narrow seam beneath a retaining wall. The wall shifted—not enough to fall, but enough that the channel beside it began to drain into the ground.

A girl heard the change in the water.

She called for help. People came with wedges and baskets, but the stones were heavy, and the softened earth kept giving way beneath them. As they worked, the wall leaned another finger’s breadth. One of the older people shouted for them to stand back.

A hand appeared between the stones.

It was neither pale nor dark, neither young nor old. It held the slipping block as easily as a hand might hold a cup. The people stared. The girl, after a moment, passed up a wedge.

The hand took it.

Together they braced the wall, packed the loosened earth, and cleared the channel. The stranger worked without haste and without asking to be thanked. When the water ran true again, the hand withdrew. There was no flash of light, no mark left in the stone.

Only the girl remembered that the stranger’s palm had been warm.

Afterward, the town spoke of the help as they spoke of most things: briefly, then as part of what had happened. They repaired the wall more thoroughly the next day. They watched the seam through the following rains. The channel still needed clearing; the plateau still shifted by grains and small collapses. Nothing had been made permanent.

The power remained near—not as a ruler over the town, nor as a promise that nothing would ever break. It did not keep the water for them. It did not keep the land still.

It only listened.

And when, in years to come, the ground gave way too quickly for many hands, or a person stood alone beneath a burden too heavy to lift, the power would be there: not to spare them their work, but to put its hand beneath the stone.
