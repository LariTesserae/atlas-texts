The power did not descend upon Serein.

It had learned, in the long patience of its wisdom, that an arrival can be a kind of violence. So it attended first.

It attended to the water that gathered and vanished; to the people’s small marks beside each repaired crack; to the beetles holding their morning line. It attended to the thing moving soundlessly over the roofs. Most of all, it listened beneath the stone, where something vast was waking by inches.

The shelf was not a roof. It was a shoulder.

The being beneath it had not raised Serein to carry a town. It had risen because it was rising. The animals had come ahead of it, sensing the change in the deep rock and gathering where the new air touched them. The people had built their homes in the pause between one movement and the next.

The power could have stopped the mountain. It could have sealed every crack and made the shelf safe as a floor. But safety that forbids a living thing to move is only another name for a cage.

So it did one smaller thing.

It found the narrowest fracture, the one that had begun behind the eastern houses, and held its edges apart—not closed, not healed, but still. For three days, the crack did not widen. In that time, the people moved what they needed to move. They braced the nearest wall. They carried an old woman’s bed to a house farther from the edge. They marked the fracture, as they always did, and went on working.

On the fourth morning, the mountain moved again.

But now the people had seen the danger in time.

Afterward, a new spur of stone emerged below the eastern edge, sloping gently into the cloud. It was not wide enough for carts, nor smooth enough for comfort. It could be crossed, though, by a careful person carrying a pack. At its foot, the mist often thinned enough to show glimpses of ledges below—places to go, if anyone chose to go.

No voice explained the path. No sign demanded gratitude. The bowls beneath the eaves still held only rainwater, and the townspeople still left them out.

The power did not promise that Serein would remain unchanged. It did not tell the mountain to stop becoming. It gave the people a little time, and a way to leave if the day came when staying meant being taken with the stone.

That was all.

For a long while, no one noticed anything except that the eastern houses had survived another season. Then, one dusk, the pale moths lifted from the shutters all at once. They circled the square, settled on the well, and folded their wings.

The water in the well trembled.

Far below, from somewhere inside the cloud, came a sound like a great creature breathing out.

No one in Serein agreed on what it meant. But that night they left an extra bowl beneath the eaves.
