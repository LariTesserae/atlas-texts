The power did not descend.

It had no need to arrive as a figure, and no wish to make the high country look up. It attended first to the currents: the tide along the slopes, the valley-flow rising toward the light, the crosscurrents that made distance uncertain. It learned their patience. It waited through a warm season and a long stillness, until it could tell the turning of a frond from the turning of a whole valley.

Then it noticed the pale ones.

Not as creatures caught in a net of thought, nor as a puzzle to be solved. It noticed them as they were: each body moving between water and stone, each filament answering a thread beneath the terraces. It understood—though understanding was not the same as knowing all—that the plateau and its inhabitants could not be cleanly separated. To touch one might be to trouble the other.

So it did not touch.

It came near the basins as a change in their reflections. The pale floor held, for a little while, an image of the high country seen from farther away than any creature there had traveled: ridges within ridges, the green terraces, the distant shelves falling into blue. Nothing was added to the landscape. Nothing was taken from it. Only the whole of it could be seen at once.

One of the pale creatures approached. It lowered its filaments toward the image. The hidden cords beneath the stone answered, faintly, then more strongly, passing the attention outward from chamber to chamber. Across the plateau, the fronds inclined—not in alarm, but in recognition.

The power offered no command. It placed no name upon them. It sent no promise that the old structures would open, or that the dark below would become safe, or that every question would receive an answer. Instead, it made one thing plain in the only language the country already knew: a warmth that did not belong to any current, gentle and steady, passing through water, stone, root, and wandering body.

You are not overlooked.

The creatures did not reply at once. They gathered in the basins, sometimes alone, sometimes in faint overlapping shapes. They watched the reflection, and the reflection watched them. A long season passed. The warmth remained, never stronger than the life of the place could bear.

At last, one creature crossed a basin and entered an old doorway. There it stayed for many days. When it emerged, a strand of pale light moved behind it, traveling along the buried cords until it reached the farthest terraces. The power received this as an answer—not assent, exactly, nor a request. An invitation to witness.

And so it did.

It did not preserve the high country from time. Time was part of its life. It did not make the silent beings speak in voices they had never wanted. It did not lead them out of their dim sea.

It remained at the edge of their awareness, as a steady warmth might remain in a stone after the sun has passed: available, not demanding. When the pale ones entered the basins, they could find that distant presence there if they wished. When they turned away, it let them go.

In the ages that followed, the currents still changed direction. Sediment still rose and settled. The ancient plateau continued its slow, green breathing beneath the wavering brightness.

But sometimes, in a basin, the reflection showed the whole country at once—and, beyond it, a vast and listening dark that was not empty.

Then the pale ones would pause.

And the warmth would answer.
