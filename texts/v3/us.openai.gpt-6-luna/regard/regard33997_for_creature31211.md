### The Hour Without Noise

The power did not descend upon the mountain.

It had no need to arrive. It was already present in the water, in the wind that combed the summit, in the patient pressure of roots against their basins. But attention is not the same as presence. For a long time, the Upper Works had gone on without being truly seen.

Then, one night, it was.

The first keeper to notice was standing beside the settling tanks with both hands against the wall. The Works shuddered. Usually, the tremor passed through bone and tooth, leaving no room for anything else. This time, something passed through it too: a tenderness so vast that the keeper took their hands away.

The turbines continued to turn. Steam continued to strike the night. But the noise loosened its hold on the summit.

It did not stop. It made room.

In that room, the keepers heard one another.

They came out of their galleries, from behind the frosted glass, from the fern-dark stairwells and the abandoned cooling chamber. Some carried tools. One carried a jar of water. A child came with no shoes and laughed once, quietly, as if surprised to discover the sound belonged to them.

No figure waited in the court. No voice spoke from the sky. The attention was in the softened roar, and in the warmth of the handrails, and in the fact that for the first time anyone could remember, no one had to put a hand against the wall to endure the Works’ shudder.

The oldest keeper signed: **What is wanted of us?**

The answer came slowly, not as words but as understanding.

**Nothing is required of you.**

They were not sure they had understood. They had spent so long learning what must be done that the absence of a task seemed like a fault in the system.

A second keeper asked: **Is the Works failing?**

**No.**

**Is something coming?**

**No.**

**Then why are you here?**

The silence held, gentle as a palm over a flame.

**Because you are here.**

The keepers did not know what to do with that. Some looked at the pipes. One began to cry, and another, not knowing whether this was a sign of danger, put an arm around them.

Then the power showed them what it had seen.

Not a vision of the mountain’s hidden interior, or the first source of the water, or the true purpose of the motionless turbine. It showed them the ordinary things they had done: the bolt placed high where no visitor would look; the water returned to its jar; the leaf tucked back into the garden. Each act shone—not because it had been noticed by a greater power, but because it had mattered before it was noticed.

The keepers stood very still.

At last, the one with the gray scarf signed: **Must we continue?**

The answer did not come at once. The power seemed to consider the question with all the seriousness it deserved.

**You may continue. You may rest. You may leave. The Works will not punish you for choosing.**

That was the miracle: not that the Works could be made to last forever, nor that every mystery might be answered, but that a choice could be made without catastrophe following close behind it.

The keepers spoke together for a long while. Their hands moved through the softened air. They disagreed. They went to see the bats. Someone fetched tea. The child fell asleep on a coil of spare hose.

In the end, none of them left that night.

But in the morning, a door stood open in the oldest tower. It had always been there, perhaps, beneath layers of paint and old repair. Beyond it lay a small chamber, quiet not because the machinery had ceased, but because its walls had been shaped to hold the sound gently. There were cushions on the floor. There was a window facing the immense white currents below.

The keepers entered one by one. Some stayed only a few minutes. Some slept.

The Works went on. Its valves hammered; its gardens drank; its hidden water climbed and fell through the old circuit. The crane turned a few degrees over the drop.

And for the first time, when the summit shuddered, the keepers did not all place their hands against the walls.

Some did. Some rested. One went to the window and watched the clouds move beneath them.

The power remained attentive, but did not remain in the room. It did not ask to be thanked. It did not make the keepers’ care into a debt.

By evening, the familiar roar had returned. Yet the new chamber kept its quiet, and the door stayed open.

On the sill, in a narrow seam of condensation, a tiny eyeless creature had appeared. It turned toward the trembling of the Works, then drifted into the light.
