The power noticed the plateau and did not mistake its strangeness for a wound.

It did not straighten the shadows, still the wind, or draw the pools to their proper depths. It did not make the distances behave. From beyond the low, bruised sky, it regarded the place as it was: the worn stone, the working roots, the birds listening to water that was not there. And it understood that a thing need not be simple to be whole.

So, at first, it did nothing.

Its attention was not a hand laid on the land. It was more like a light left burning in a distant window: present, steady, asking nothing. The plateau went on pumping water uphill. Spores traveled sideways through the rain. The grazers followed the deep pulses beneath the ground. Nothing was summoned from its work to explain itself.

After a long while—though time did not pass quite the same way there—the power made one small offer.

It sent a stillness through the stone.

Not silence. The stalks continued to clank and hum; the pools continued to bead and drain; the great callers went on speaking from distances that would not hold still. But beneath the plateau’s many rhythms, for a single moment, there was a new one: a quiet, even pulse, with no pressure in it. It said, as clearly as such a thing could say:

*I am here. I will not change you. If there is something you want, I can hear it.*

The answer came slowly. A root-band loosened. Water took a channel it had never taken before, and climbed—not uphill, not down, but toward the open air. It gathered there in a thin, trembling sheet, reflecting neither the bruised sky nor the green light below. In it, the plateau saw the power’s attention: vast, patient, and without a face.

The plateau did not ask to be made young. It did not ask for clear weather, fixed horizons, or an end to its labor. It did not ask the power to choose which of its voices was the one that mattered.

It asked—if asking was what it did—for room to continue.

The power understood.

It made no wall around the plateau. A wall would have been a kind of cage. Instead, it held back those who came wishing to drain the basins, cut the stalk-fields, or name the living tracts as resources. It did not prevent every danger; that would have required taking the land’s life into its own keeping. It only made the plateau difficult to find by those who would not listen, and impossible to mistake for something uninhabited.

Then it withdrew its pulse, leaving no mark but the memory of being met without being mastered.

The water fell from the air. The stalks bent in the wind. Somewhere beneath a mat of moss, a burrower passed, and the ground rose gently after it.

The plateau continued.

And the power, wise enough to know that care is not always improvement, remained near—not above it, not inside it, but within reach should the land ever call.
