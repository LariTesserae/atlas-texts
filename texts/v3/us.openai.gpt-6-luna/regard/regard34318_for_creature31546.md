### The Guest Who Waited

The power did not arrive as light.

Light already belonged to the Silt Garden: to the shining channels, the warm stones, the beads that rose from the platform and held brief, impossible skies. The power came as attention—so complete that nothing in the hollow was overlooked, and so gentle that nothing had to turn toward it.

It saw the small pale animals moving under the leaves. It saw the silver shapes circling the grazer’s reflection, and the grazer drinking with its eyes closed. It saw the predator at the far bank. The power did not mistake hunger for cruelty, or stillness for sorrow. It did not reach down to rearrange the lives that had found their own careful balance.

Then it listened beneath the platform.

There, in the channels and the old material and the vibration running through the stone, was the being that belonged to the hollow—not a creature with a body, but a life spread so widely that the distinction between it and its home had worn thin.

The power could have spoken in a voice. Instead it waited.

For a long while, the garden continued as it always had. Water gathered. A wing opened and closed. The predator moved one foreleg into the shallows. Beneath the platform, the dreaming tremor went on.

At evening, a bead lifted from the old marks. It held no image of vanished seas or unfamiliar stars. It held only the hollow as it was: each living thing in its place, each reflection trembling slightly in the water.

The power understood this not as a request, but as an answer.

So it did not heal the ancient stones, quicken the slow-growing leaves, or make the animals safe from one another. It did not fill the bowl with wonders it had never asked for. Instead, it made itself known to the old listener in the only way that mattered: it offered its presence without claiming authority.

If the channels ever failed, if the boundary were broken, if the hollow’s quiet life were threatened by something it could not meet alone, the garden could call. The power would hear.

The offering settled into the stone like warmth held after sunset. It changed no path, disturbed no pool. The bead lowered itself into the water without a ripple.

Beneath the platform, the vibration altered by less than a breath.

The power stayed for a little while—not to watch over the garden as a keeper watches a possession, but to share its silence. Then it withdrew its attention, leaving behind no sign that could be found by eyes or instruments.

The grazer went on drinking. The predator drank after it. In the reeds, the hidden wings opened once, softly, like a thought passing through the whole bowl.

And the Silt Garden remained itself: not saved, because it had not been lost; not changed, because it had not asked to be changed. Only no longer alone in the widest sense of the word.
