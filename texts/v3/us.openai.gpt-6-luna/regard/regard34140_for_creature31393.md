The power noticed the basin first as a sound.

Not its roar, which was obvious, but the small changes inside it: a new trickle under stone; reeds bending where no wind reached them; the pause in a grazer’s step before the ground gave way. It listened until the basin’s disorder became legible—not a failure to settle, but a way of living.

Then it noticed the one who lived there.

The being had no grand name. They were known, when they were known at all, as someone who could tell by the color of a channel whether its water had come from rain or from a spring newly opened below. They kept the nearest tower’s sensing slits clear. They had once led a lost child back along a ridge by following the places where the reeds grew thickest. They were not the basin’s master, nor its guardian. They were one life among many, learning how to remain.

The power could have made the ground still.

For a moment, the thought stood before it: firm banks, reliable paths, rain that fell only when needed. No more shelters leaning against the strain of buried anchors; no young animals lost to a sudden channel. It could have lifted every house onto ground that would never shift.

But the basin would have been made safer by being made less itself. Its waters would lose their wandering. Its plants, its fish, its innumerable small evasions would find the world had changed around them without their consent. Even the people, freed from the need to listen, might forget how.

So the power did not make the ground still.

It did something smaller, and more difficult to see.

When the basin’s water rose beyond anything the towers had measured, the being heard, beneath the familiar roar, a new note: three soft knocks from the nearest anchor. Not an alarm meant to frighten. A warning with enough time in it.

They looked up. The rain had not changed. The reeds were still bent with wind. But they knew that sound. They woke the others, and together they carried the smallest ones to the high gravel ridge. By morning, the channel had taken the path, the lower shelter, and a stand of young willow. It did not take anyone.

Afterward, the being checked the tower for damage. They found none. In its dark surface, for just a moment, a thin line of light held steady—warmer than the instrument’s usual pulse, and gone before they could decide they had truly seen it.

The power left no message. It did not ask to be thanked. It did not promise that the next flood would spare them.

It remained attentive.

And when the basin shifted again, as it always would, the people listened—not because they had been given command of the place, but because, once, in the loudest hour, the world had made room for them to hear.
