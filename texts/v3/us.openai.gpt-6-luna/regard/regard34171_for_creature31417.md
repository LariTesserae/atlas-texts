The power did not mistake quiet for need.

It watched for a long while before it came near. It learned the city’s measures: how far a bridge could flex before its lights changed; how much water the tanks could spare; which silences meant rest and which meant listening. It learned that the people did not want the mountain conquered. They had made a life by attending to its movements, and the mountain, in its slow way, had made room for them.

So the power did not widen the seam. It did not fill the empty tiers, summon gardens from the stone, or make the daylight linger. It did not give the people a brighter future than the one they had chosen.

It found, instead, a strain in the upper rock: not an imminent catastrophe, but a fault that would one day shift under the newer construction. The city had already begun to account for it. A tower had altered its lean; two walkways had been quietly rerouted. The people were managing.

The power did not take that work from them. It eased the strain by the smallest degree—a pressure lifted from one buried plane of stone, no more than a hand might lift a thread from a sleeve. The city’s instruments registered a change and recalculated. Somewhere, a cable settled into a better angle.

Then the power waited again.

At the next wind, it joined the hollow note at the opening. Not loudly. Not as a command. Beneath the familiar sound, it placed a second tone, steady and low: a reply, if anyone wished to hear one.

The residents stopped what they were doing.

One laid a palm against the rail. Another looked up through the slit of daylight. They did not know what had answered them, or whether the answer had been meant for them at all. They listened until the wind passed.

Afterward, the city went on as before. Water climbed to its tanks. Ice formed and came apart. A child ran three steps along a bridge and was caught by the back of their coat.

But from then on, when the strange tone returned, the people knew they were not listening alone.

The power asked for nothing in return. It did not make itself a ruler, or a guardian to whom every tremor must be reported. It remained near enough to answer if the mountain made a change the city could not bear—and far enough away for the city to remain its own.

The seam stayed narrow. The light stayed white. The people continued to make their careful way upward, while the mountain, almost imperceptibly, made room.
