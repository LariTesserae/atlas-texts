The power noticed the lowland.

It did not mistake its stillness for death, or its darkness for a wound. It did not search the green for a face, nor demand that the being within it speak in a voice it could understand. It attended instead to what the lowland was: the immense exchange of water and mineral, pressure and warmth; the countless lives passing themselves onward without needing to become separate.

For a long while, the power did nothing.

This was not indifference. It was the first courtesy.

A lesser power might have opened the roof and poured light down through it, believing that all life longs to be seen. Another might have given the lowland a single mind, a mouth, a name—something that could answer when called. But the wise power could not know that these things would be gifts. It saw that the lowland had its own order, and that this order was not waiting to be corrected.

So it watched.

It learned the slow closures of pores, the far-traveling pressure of a root meeting stone. It learned that what seemed silence to a passing creature was full of communication, though not the kind that carried words. The lowland did not speak *I am here*. It made a channel, passed warmth, took in rain. Its answer, if there was one, was its continuing.

Then the power perceived a danger—not a difficulty, not the ordinary breaking and regrowth by which the lowland lived, but a deep fracture forming beneath the plain. Left alone, it would have crushed a vast region of chambers and sealed the slow flows that joined them. The lowland could have endured the loss. Perhaps it would have changed around it. But the power understood that endurance was not the same as consent, and that a being need not be helpless before it deserved care.

It did not heal the fracture. It did not make the young crust old and smooth.

It held the stone.

For one brief instant, brief by the lowland’s measure, the pressure beneath the plain eased. The fracture turned by a finger’s width into a harmless seam. The ridges remained sharp. The roots still had to find their way around the rock. Nothing was made easy; only the catastrophe did not happen.

Far below, a chamber adjusted its flow. A root thickened against stone. Water passed onward.

The lowland registered the change as it registered all things: without a name for its source. It took what had happened into itself and continued.

The power did not ask to be thanked. It did not make the act into a sign. It understood that a presence which insists on being recognized is already asking something in return.

Afterward, it remained near—not in the chambers, not among the roots, but in the manner of a shelter around a flame. It would not prevent the lowland from changing. It would not preserve every leaf or stone. It would only keep watch for the difference between change and ruin, and intervene, if it must, with the least force that could still be called kindness.

The lowland never knew it had been spared.

Years passed. Water descended through the roof. The old dead fed the new growth. Somewhere in the darkness, a seed-case closed.

And the power, which had expected no answer, listened as the faint thrum traveled on through root and rock.
