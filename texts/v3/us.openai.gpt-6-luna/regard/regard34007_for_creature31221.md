The power did not arrive as thunder.

It had no need to announce itself. It was already old enough to know that a place may be harmed by the manner in which it is saved.

So it came first as attention.

It listened to the wind moving through the stone. It learned the pauses between the gusts, the notes that made the bridges tremble, the little clicks that told the residents a vane had caught. It watched the hooded figures cross the courts and move their markers. It saw how they left room for the pale creatures beneath the paving, and how they gave the cracks their offerings—not worship, but courtesy.

For a long while, it did nothing.

Then, at an hour when the winds aligned, it altered one note. Not louder, not softer: simply distinct. A question, offered in the city’s own language.

The residents heard it. They stopped where they were. One by one, they placed their small stone markers in the center court, turning them toward the seam that led outside.

The power understood: *We know the way out. We have not asked to leave.*

It waited.

After a time, an old resident came to the court and set down a marker of their own. It pointed neither inward nor toward the seam, but along the floor, where the air ran its familiar route.

The power understood that, too.

*Let the wind continue. Let us continue with it.*

So the power did not fill the city with water, or silence its great breathing, or summon a multitude to people its empty streets. It did not make the plants soft, or the stone new. It did not open the shut doors. Those things had their own histories, and the residents had their own ways of living among them.

It made only one promise.

From then on, when something approached the seam with the intention to break, take, or conquer, the city would hear it coming. The wind would change its note in time for the residents to choose what to do. Nothing would be barred from entering who came in peace. Nothing would be hidden from those who had earned the right to see it. But no one would ever again mistake the city for an empty stone.

The residents did not thank the power in words. They moved a marker a finger’s breadth, then returned to their work.

The power accepted this as thanks enough.

It stayed—not as ruler, nor guardian standing at the gate, but as a listening presence in the deep mineral hush. And when the city breathed, it breathed with it, careful not to take the rhythm away.
