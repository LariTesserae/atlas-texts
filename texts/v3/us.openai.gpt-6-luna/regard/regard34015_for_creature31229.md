The attention came without a voice.

It did not descend through the golden seams, or take a shape among the leaves. Nothing in the basin bent beneath it. The pale being stood in the light, and the power saw it—not as a thing waiting to be completed, but as a life already its own.

So, at first, the power did nothing.

It did not mistake quiet for loneliness, or stillness for a wound. It did not give the being a name it had not chosen, or fill its silence with commandments. The groves were not empty. The little shelters were not failures of a greater architecture. Even the absence of change was not, by itself, a plea.

The power waited.

The being held its first step for a long while. Then it took another.

A leaf brushed its shoulder. The leaf trembled, and the tremble passed through the stem into the frond above it. Nearby, one of the others turned its head. A pale hand emerged from the green and opened, palm upward.

The traveler looked at that hand.

It could have returned to the warmth of its hollow. It could have stayed where it was, and the world would have made room for that, too. Instead, it crossed the small distance between them. Its fingers settled against the waiting palm.

Nothing in the basin had ever needed to hurry. Nothing had ever needed to remember. But now one body had gone from here to there, and another had felt its arrival.

The power could have made the moment larger. It could have shaken the mineral roof, or spoken from the stone, or poured a river through the hollows. It could have made the inhabitants understand all at once that the world was changing.

It did none of these things.

Only when the two beings remained together—hands touching, their colors slowly warming where their skin met—did the power loosen one small stillness in the roof.

A bead of water fell.

It struck a dark pool with a sound no louder than a fingertip against glass. Rings spread across the surface, widening, touching the stones, returning altered. The two beings turned toward it. So did others, one by one, in the groves.

The power did not tell them what the sound meant. It did not tell them that the rings would pass, or that another drop might fall, or that time could be a gift and a burden together. It let them meet the event as they had met one another: slowly, without instruction.

Then it kept watch.

Not to ensure they became anything in particular. Not to hurry them toward speech, hunger, weather, or history. It watched for the moment when its help might be wanted, and for the moment when help would only take a choice away.

Beneath the pale stone, the beings stood around the pool. The rings faded. The next drop had not yet formed.

For the first time, the basin contained a before and an after.

And the power let them belong to it.
