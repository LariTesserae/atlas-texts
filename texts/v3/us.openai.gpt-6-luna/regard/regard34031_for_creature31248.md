The power did not make Vey still.

It had seen worlds ruined by the well-intentioned hand that mistook change for harm. It had seen rivers straightened until they forgot how to flood safely, and people protected so thoroughly that they were no longer permitted to choose. So it did not press the plain flat, or draw a final border around the city, or give its people a map that would stay true.

For a long while, it only watched.

It watched the children carry cuttings across the shifting ground. It watched old people sit beside empty canals, keeping company with water that had not yet arrived. It watched the city make room—not just for its own uncertainty, but for the vines, the strangers in the pools, and the silent creatures beneath the bridges.

Then, one dim afternoon, the power noticed something the people had not yet seen.

The black ridge at the edge of the plain was moving.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. But its long, sharp face was turning toward Vey, and the ground beneath it was drawing tight like a held breath. In time, the ridge would break loose. The city could survive its arrival, perhaps—but one of the buried structures would not. The canals would lose their course. The ground would change faster than anyone could learn it.

The power could have stopped the ridge. Instead, it gave the city a warning.

Every puddle filled at once. Every drop of condensation trembled on every leaf. Then, in each shallow pool, a line appeared: not the ridge as it was, but the ridge as it would be, coming down across the plain. Beside it, faintly, was a second line—an unfamiliar route running toward a basin no one in Vey had ever found.

People came out into the open. They looked together. No one called it a miracle. One of the surveyors took out a metal tablet and began to draw.

The city spent the next several days testing the line. It led across ground that shifted beneath their feet and through a district of empty towers. Twice it seemed to vanish. Once it appeared only in the reflection of a person who was not there. Still, they followed it—not because the line commanded them, but because it offered a possibility.

They found the basin at the end of the route, under a shelf of black stone. Its walls were already green with plants. At the bottom, a thin stream moved toward the distant plain.

When the ridge finally shifted, Vey was ready. Not safe in the old sense of the word, not untouched. A bridge fell. A garden disappeared. The canals filled with dark water, then emptied into new places. But the buried structures held, and the people had somewhere to go.

Afterward, the pools returned to their ordinary reflections. The second skyline went on being second.

The power did not ask for thanks. It did not explain itself or stay to govern. It left the people with one new understanding: sometimes the world could show them a danger before it arrived. And sometimes, if they looked together, it could show them a way through.

After that, when the waters in Vey took on their rings, people still listened.

But they did not mistake listening for obedience.
