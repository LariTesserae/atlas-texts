The power came down through no shaft.

It was already there, in the silence between one drip and the next, when it first understood the basin. It saw the city’s fragile roofs and the patient hands that repaired them. It saw the animals pass through open gates, neither owned nor driven away. It saw people who did not mistake change for failure.

It could have made the stone still.

For a moment, it considered it: every wall fixed, every channel obedient, every ceiling held in place forever. No more vanished pools. No more streets redrawn overnight. No more families sleeping with their tools close at hand.

Then it imagined the city without its listening. Without its careful builders, its maps corrected in chalk, its people gathered around a new fall of stone to ask what could be made there. Stillness, the power understood, would not be peace. It would be a command disguised as a gift.

So it did not make the stone still.

It did something smaller.

Far beneath the inhabited chambers, a shelf of rock began to loosen. The shift would not have ended the city, but it would have buried a crowded passage before anyone could reach it. The power laid no hand upon the shelf. Instead, it touched the sound.

The first strain passed through the limestone as a low, clear note. It traveled farther than a shout, through walls and water and the soles of sleeping feet. Every bell hung beside a cracked passage answered. Birds lifted from their roosts. Animals turned from the outer halls. People woke, listened, and went to work.

They brought ropes, wedges, baskets, and chalk. They carried the sleeping and the slow-moving to safer chambers. They marked the place where the stone had begun to give. When the shelf fell at last, it fell into an empty reach of the basin.

By morning, the city had changed. A passage was gone. A new wall of pale rubble stood in its place. The map was corrected.

No one saw the power. No one heard it speak. In the days that followed, they said the stone had given warning unusually early, and they added a mark to their records: *a note before the fall.*

The power remained—not as a guardian who would prevent every loss, but as a listener who would not let the most important warnings be swallowed by the rock. It would not spare them every danger. It would leave their choices their own.

And when, in later years, a child asked whether the city was safe, an elder put a hand against the cool wall and waited.

The stone held its silence.

For now, that was answer enough.
