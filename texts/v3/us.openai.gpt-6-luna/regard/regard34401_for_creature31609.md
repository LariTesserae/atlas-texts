The power would not descend in fire or speak from the summit. It would not make the mountains stop rising. The city had been built in partnership with an unfinished world, and to freeze that world in place would be to take something from its people.

Instead, it would pay attention.

It would notice the way they rested a hand against the walls, listening through their palms. It would notice the cups set beneath bridges at dawn, the careful sharing of water, the names given to cracks. It would understand that these were not signs of a people waiting to be saved. They were ways of living.

So it would give them a little more time.

Not time in the grand sense. No one would become immortal; no tower would be spared every tremor. But before a dangerous seam opened, the stone would give a warning: a low, clear note, heard first by the people who knew how to listen. A failing bridge would hum. A heat duct nearing rupture would shiver beneath the hand. Their instruments would begin, more often than not, to agree with one another.

At first they would call it coincidence. Then they would call it a new property of the mountain. They would argue over the measurements, revise the maps, and teach the children which notes meant *wait*.

The power would also turn its attention to what the city had almost failed to notice. The pale animals at the rain pools were not intruders or omens. They were simply alive: ancient in some ways, vulnerable in others. The dark shape above the vent stacks was alive too. The power would not make these creatures tame, or plentiful, or useful. It would only make them a little harder to overlook. A trail of prints where no surveyor expected one. A shadow crossing a wall just as someone was about to seal a crack that served as a nest. Enough for curiosity to outlast dismissal.

And the water? The power would not fill the reservoirs to their rims. Abundance without knowledge can become another kind of danger. But it would let one hidden seam keep its moisture a little longer. A trickle would persist through the dry season, not enough to remake the city, enough to sustain a small basin where mineral-colored films gathered and, at last, something living took hold.

People would come to look. Some would want to enclose it, pipe it, measure it. Others would argue that it should be left alone. Their argument would matter. The power would not settle it for them.

That would be the shape of its kindness: not a command, not a miracle that made their choices irrelevant, but a margin in which choices could be made more safely. A warning before collapse. A living thing not easily erased. Water that lasted long enough to be shared.

Perhaps one person would feel the attention most clearly. A child, standing at the edge of a rain pool with a scrap of mineral salt in her hand, would see the dark shape pass overhead. For an instant, the mountain’s familiar warmth would move through her palm—not speaking, exactly, but answering the touch she had placed on the stone.

She would tell someone. The story would change as it passed through the city. No one would agree on what had happened.

The power would be content with that. It had not come to be believed. It had come because the city and its people had drawn its attention—and because, having noticed them, it would not look away.
