The power did not descend through the clouds.

It had no need to make an entrance. For a long while it simply attended: to the city’s low, patient pumping; to the creatures arriving and leaving by the tide; to the one who lived among the works and had learned to tell a failing gate from a healthy one by sound alone.

It saw what the city had become—not ruined, not thriving, but continuing. It saw how much depended on things no one praised: a seal that held through the night, a worker who stayed past shift change, an opening left clear for fish. It saw the care that had grown here without calling itself care.

So it did not turn the basin into a garden. It did not quiet the rain or drive the animals elsewhere. It did not lift the city out of its low place and set it on safer ground. The water belonged here, too. To remove every danger would have meant removing the very patterns by which the place lived.

Instead, when the next great tide came in, the power gave a warning.

Not a voice. Not a vision. A change in the pump’s thrum, so slight that it might have been mistaken for a loose bearing—except the one who lived there stopped, listened, and understood. They checked the lower gauges. One had jammed beneath a crust of salt. The water was rising faster than the instruments showed.

There was still time.

The worker called the others. A gate was cleared; a bypass opened by hand. Water poured through the old channel, where fish had once passed before the route silted closed. It flooded an empty warehouse, not the homes above it. By morning the tide had turned. The city’s lowest rooms were wet, as they often were, but the people were safe, and the fish had found their way through.

The power let the day belong to those who had done the work.

Afterward, it left one small kindness behind. In the days that followed, whenever a channel was about to close with silt, the water made a faint, repeating knock against its obstruction. Whenever a pump was straining beyond its limit, its hum altered just enough to be heard. Nothing that could replace attention. Only a little more time in which attention might matter.

The one who lived there noticed the pattern. They told no one at first. Then, carefully, they taught the others to listen.

And the city went on as before: damp, dim, and broad beneath its low sky. Crabs crossed the steps. Shoals darkened the channels. Birds lifted from the flats and settled somewhere beyond the lamps. The pumps worked. The tide turned.

No one could say that the city had been saved. It had not asked to be saved from itself.

But now, when something in the basin began to fail, the first sign was less often missed.
