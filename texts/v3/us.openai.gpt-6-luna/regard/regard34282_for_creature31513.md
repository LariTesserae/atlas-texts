It did not arrive as a voice in the sky.

The city had heard enough voices over its long life. It knew the difference between a command and a question, even when both were spoken gently.

The power came as a pause.

For one breath, the rain held to the leaves without falling. The transit filaments gave a single low note, too deep to hear and too clear not to feel. Beneath the square, the ancient mechanism stopped turning.

Everywhere, the city listened.

So did the people. They came to the basin in ones and twos, carrying no tools. The water lay still. In it, the clouds were gone. The reflection showed a vast darkness, and within it a light with no edge.

The power had found the being that lived there—not a creature hidden in a chamber, nor a mind housed in the machinery, but the city in its whole, difficult life: roots, channels, sleeping hollows, changing paths, and all the small lives that had learned to trust it. The power did not mistake its age for wisdom, or its strangeness for a request to be understood.

It waited.

At last, a new channel opened between the stones. A thin stream crossed the square and touched the basin.

The power answered, not in words but in warmth along the water. It showed the city what it had seen: a deep strain under the bowl, where the oldest workings had been turning against themselves for longer than any inhabitant could remember. Not a threat for today. Not even for tomorrow. But one day the ground would shift, and the city would have to bear more than it could bear alone.

The people looked at the water. They looked at one another. Then they touched the stone ring, the way they touched a doorway when greeting a familiar place.

The city opened a path below.

That was consent enough.

The power did not mend the ancient mechanism as a craftsperson might mend a broken tool. It could have imposed a perfect order on the channels, fixed every root in place, made every passage permanent. It did none of these things. Instead, it lent the deep machinery a steadier rhythm—one the city could keep, alter, and eventually outgrow. Where the strain had gathered, the black stone warmed. Water found a gentler course. A buried root loosened its grip.

The work took three days, though no one could later agree when it had begun or ended. People carried small devices into the lower chambers. The oldest listeners laid their hands beside the black stone and learned a new note. Children were sent home by paths that kept moving under their feet.

When it was done, the power withdrew from the basin.

It left no shrine, no law, no image to obey. Only this: when the people gathered there afterward, the water sometimes held the reflection of a sky they could not see. If they were worried, it was bright. If they were grieving, it was quiet. It never told them what to do.

And far below, the machinery continued to turn—not perfectly, not forever, but with room in its rhythm for the city to answer.

The power had noticed them.

It had not asked them to become anything else.
