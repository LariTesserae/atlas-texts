The power came without a voice.

It did not turn the dark into day, or make the water clear. It did not raise gardens from the mud or smooth the basin into a place that could be crossed without care. It saw that the fish already knew the channels, that the bats had their heights, that the stone and water were making a country at their own pace. To call this unfinished was not to call it broken.

For a long while, the power listened.

It heard the pump’s uneven thud and the lives gathered around its warmth. It heard the roof seams opening, the slow rearrangement of silt, the small alarms carried through water when an eel passed. It heard no single keeper. The basin’s life was distributed: in each turning school, each film on the stone, each creature finding shelter in what another had left behind.

So the power did not appoint a ruler.

It made one quiet promise to the place: nothing from above would poison it by accident and call the harm progress. Where rain brought the basin more water than its passages could bear, the excess found gentler ways down. Where people on the plain drilled or dug, their hands met signs early enough to stop them from breaking through blindly. The surface remained a surface; the basin remained a basin. No wall was raised between them. Only the worst surprises were softened.

And the power touched the old pump—not to make it run forever, but to let it fail slowly, when its time came. Its strokes grew steadier for a little while. The fish gathered in the warmed channels. The crabs fed among the mineral lace. Then, far in the future, the pump would pause for the last time, and the water would find another route.

Nothing in the basin knew it had been blessed. A bat carried a seed down from the plain. A new crust spread over mud. A fish turned at the shiver of a current. The roof held; elsewhere, stone began to loosen.

The power watched, and did not ask the basin to become anything other than what it was: inhabited, vulnerable, and still coming into being.
