The power did not descend into the hollow.

It had no need to make itself known by brightness. The city already had its own light, faint and sufficient; its own voice, roughened by wind and stone; its own slow laws. To enter as a conqueror of silence, or to fill the hollow with water, would have been to mistake difference for deprivation.

So the power listened.

It heard the air pass through the chimneys, the fronds answer, the salt fall grain by grain. It heard the deep boom of the ground and understood that this was not a cry for help. The hollow was not waiting to be completed. It was already sustaining a life that had no face, no footsteps, and no need to be like any other.

The power’s first act was therefore to leave the city alone.

Its second was smaller, and harder to see. In the deep rock, where heat and pressure worked against the oldest supports, it eased one strain—not removing it, only making it survivable. The walls would still wear down. The plants would still retreat from the hottest vents and take root elsewhere. The city would still alter, as all living places do. But it would not be crushed in a single moment by a shift too violent for its patient yielding.

Then the power turned its attention to the plain above. It did not raise a mountain over the entrance or bury the seam forever. It made no shrine, set no warning stones. It only made those who came across the salt waste feel, without knowing why, that they should not break the ground there carelessly. Some passed on. Some paused, listened to the faint singing beneath their feet, and walked around.

Below, nothing changed enough to be called a miracle.

A current threaded the old passages. The dark fronds trembled. Salt grains clicked against the steps, and the city gave its low, familiar answer.

The power did not ask the place to be grateful. It did not ask it to know that it had been spared. It let the hollow continue in its own time: breathing, settling, growing into the stone.

And when the power withdrew, it took nothing with it—not the heat, not the silence, not the city’s strange and sufficient life.
