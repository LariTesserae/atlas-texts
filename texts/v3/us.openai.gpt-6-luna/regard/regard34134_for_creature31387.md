At first, the power does nothing.

It does not brighten the lamps, silence the chain, or smooth the basin into something more beautiful. It watches one full cycle of the pump, then another. It watches the fish scatter and gather, the crabs flatten themselves against the floor, the eel fold into the western conduit. It learns that the tremor is not a warning to them. It is part of the water’s weather.

The power does not mistake age for suffering. The basin is worn, but it is not asking to be made new.

Still, the pump is a single promise on which many small lives depend. So the power turns its attention to the eastern wall, where an old sluice has been sealed by rust and silt. It does not wrench the gate open. It loosens one grain, then another, over a span of seasons. A thread of outside water begins to pass through—not enough to unsettle the basin’s familiar circuits, only enough to make a second, quieter current.

The little fish discover it first. They test the new flow, leave it, return. The crabs find that fresh food arrives along the floor. The eel feels the altered pressure against its sides and follows it as far as the wall. Beyond lies water it has never known. The passage is open, but nothing calls it through.

The old pump continues its patient work. The chain still knocks. The lamps still burn their dull amber. No creature looks upward and understands that anything has happened.

The power remains—not holding the basin still, but keeping a way open. When the machine eventually falters, the water will already know another route. Until then, the eel may stay, or go. The fish may follow the current, or turn back toward the gantry. Their lives remain their own.
