The power did not descend.

It had no need to make itself large. It had been large before the Sill existed, and would remain so after the mountain had cooled into some other shape. It did not mistake smallness for helplessness, or silence for a request.

It listened.

It listened to the springs changing their minds. To the faint vibration passing through the stone. To the seam-kin moving water by persuasion, tending a place that was still deciding what it wanted to be.

The power understood, at last, that the Sill did not need to be finished. It did not need a guardian to hold its edges still, or a hand to smooth the sharp black rock. The seam-kin were not waiting to be rescued. Their work was the work of continuing: opening, closing, allowing, withdrawing.

So the power did not bless them with certainty. It did not give them voices, or gather them into shapes that could be counted. It did not make the red flowers bloom forever, or prevent the cliffs from shedding their shards. That would have been a kindness only to someone who feared change.

Instead, it made a small promise in the laws beneath the mountain.

If the Sill was ever sought for its heat, the way would remain difficult to find. If a hand reached to take the old devices apart, the hand would discover that the frame was elsewhere, or that the distance to it had become too long to cross. No traveler would be punished for coming. No curious eye would be blinded. But no one would be able to turn the Sill into a possession merely by naming it.

The promise did not appear as a wall. The seam-kin never felt it arrive. They went on opening springs in the dark, drawing beads of water toward paths that had been dry for years. The snails left their bright tracks. The silver grass bent in a wind too slight to feel.

At the rim, the cloud parted.

For an instant, the hidden world below seemed to rise toward the shelf—not closer, exactly, but more present. The seam-kin gathered in the altered air. The low vibration passed through the stone and held there, like a note remembered without being heard.

Then the cloud closed.

A drop fell from beneath a ledge and struck the rock. Somewhere, a spring began to run uphill.

The power turned its attention elsewhere, not because it had forgotten the Sill, but because the Sill had not asked to be watched.

And the Sill remained free to change.
