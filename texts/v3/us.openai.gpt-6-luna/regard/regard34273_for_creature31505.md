The power did not send water.

It saw how little water the city could afford to receive. A sudden river would have drowned the low chambers, loosened the old walls, and carried away the careful balance by which the inhabitants lived. It did not send sunlight, either. The pale plants had learned another way to endure; to brighten the city all at once would have been to change what the city was.

So the power waited, and listened.

It listened to the small mammal following a familiar corridor, to the beetles working in the seams, to the birds asleep in their dusty nests. It heard the city not as noise, but as a multitude of lives answering one another without knowing the whole of what they made.

Then, far below the plain, a roof began to fail.

The first warning was no more than a tremor in the stone. The inhabitants felt it in their feet and claws, but not all at once. The power did not hold the ceiling up forever. That would only have made the danger larger, and the eventual fall more terrible. Instead, for a little while, it held the stone still.

In that stillness, the city heard something it had never heard before: a low, clear note passing through the old corridors. It was not a command. It did not tell any creature what to do. But the mammals fell quiet. The birds lifted their heads. The long-legged creatures turned toward the cracks in the ceiling, and even the beetles seemed to pause in their patient work.

Then the note came again, from the direction of the endangered chambers.

The creatures moved away. Not in a single frightened rush, but by the routes they already knew: one stream, then another; nests emptied; young carried; the traffic diverted into neighboring lanes. Some went because they heard the sound. Some went because others went. Some left only when the first grains of dust began to fall.

When the chamber came down, it came down empty.

The power let the stone fall. It let the dust settle. It let the living find new paths around the ruin, and the old work of the city begin again.

Afterward, the inhabitants remembered the note—not as a word, and not as a miracle. It became one more sound among the city’s sounds, something that might mean danger near a failing roof. In time, other calls gathered around it. The birds learned to answer. The mammals learned to follow. Beetles moved their eggs from the trembling seams.

The power did not make them understand one another completely. It did not make them understand the power.

It gave them no name for what had happened, and asked for none. It left them their dry air, their dimness, their old passages, and the freedom to keep living as they had learned to live.

Then it withdrew its hand from the stone—and kept listening.
