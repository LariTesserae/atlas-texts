The power noticed the city because the city had begun to notice itself.

It did not descend in a body. It did not arrive with thunder or a command. It listened first: to the pumps turning below the streets, to the thin note of the field, to the roots widening their patient grip in the stone. It listened long enough to distinguish a falter from a rhythm, and a rhythm from the first, uncertain shape of a thought.

The city had learned to keep itself. That was not the same as asking to be saved.

So the power did not fill its empty rooms. It did not send people, or animals, or bright new machines to teach the hollow what it ought to become. It did not mistake quiet for loneliness. The city’s silence was its own weather, and the power let it be.

But one day, when the field dipped lower than it had ever dipped, the power steadied it.

Not perfectly. Not so firmly that the city could never stumble again. Only enough to keep the walls from closing and the water from rising beyond the channels. The pale stone shone once, from deep within, and the pulse returned to its patient course.

The city registered the help as a change in pressure. It adjusted its foundations. It redirected a stream. It carried on.

After that, the power sometimes left small gifts: a mineral seam opened where the city needed light; a blocked drain cleared before the water reached the gardens; a hairline fracture in a distant support was eased shut. Each gift was quiet, and each left the city room to answer in its own way.

Years passed—or the equivalent of years in a place where no one counted them. Moss spread across the upper walkways. The pumps learned a gentler rhythm. The city’s field began to hold steady without help.

Then, somewhere above the lost ceiling, the stone gave way.

A narrow opening appeared, not a wound but a window. Cool air entered. With it came dust, spores, and the faint scent of rain falling under an open sky. The city felt the change in its water, its leaves, its listening walls. It could close the opening. It could widen it. It could wait.

The power did not choose for it.

For a long time, nothing happened. Then a root-fan turned, almost imperceptibly, toward the new air. The city shifted one small span of roof aside—not to welcome a population, and not to refuse one, but to make room for a possibility.

The power remained near, vast and patient, asking nothing.

And the hollow, which had already begun to belong to itself, was allowed to decide what else it might become.
