The power would not arrive as a voice from the ceiling. It would not break the roof open to the sky, or fill the cistern to its brim forever. It would understand that a gift can be another kind of rupture, and that these people have made a life by learning the place’s pace.

So first it would wait.

It would wait through the dry days, the little returns of water, the measured turning of the engines. It would learn which stair creaked beneath the oldest feet, which plant was leaning into a neighbor’s window, which chalk line had been crossed by the slow press of stone. It would see that the district was not merely a place where lives had been trapped, but a place where people had made room for one another—and for what was still arriving.

Then, at the hour when the cistern was full and the pumps had stopped, the water would show the familiar extra landing.

This time, the reflection would hold.

No one would be summoned. No one would be compelled to follow it. The people would gather with lamps and chalk, the engine keepers would listen to the wall, and the residents would argue about whether the passage was safe, useful, or simply a trick of the water. The power would let them decide. It would only keep the stone from shifting while they looked.

Behind the wall they would find a small hollow, sound and dry at its edges. A breath of cooler air would move through it. There would be a little soil, dark and deep enough for roots, and a seam of water that offered a steady, modest thread—not enough to make water thoughtless, but enough that no one would have to wonder whether tomorrow’s cup would be empty.

The power would leave the rest to them.

They might make the hollow a garden, or a room for the sick, or a place where children could run without crossing a pipe. They might change their minds. The power would not name it sacred, or claim it as its own. It would not ask for gratitude.

And when the new passage was marked in chalk, the power would let the mark remain. The district would go on changing: stone pressing slowly outward, roots seeking damp, pumps turning beneath the streets. Nothing would be finished. But there would be a little more room in which to become.
