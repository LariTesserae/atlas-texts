The power did not make the ground still.

It had seen what stillness would cost: the reeds rooted in the seams, the old rooms sealed beneath the new ones, the people who had learned to read a wall’s fine cracks and a pump’s altered note. To hold the basin in place would not be to save it. It would be to silence it.

So the power waited, and listened.

It listened to the water moving inside the walls, to the crews calling measurements across a street that had shifted since morning, to children naming whatever slipped beneath the pipes. It listened to the city’s quiet machinery and to the ground, which was neither quite speaking nor quite asleep.

Then it did one thing.

From that day on, when a change would make a place dangerous—when a stair would buckle, a passage flood, a bank of new earth give way—the city gave warning. Not always in words. Sometimes every moth on a window turned at once toward the threatened street. Sometimes the pumps missed a beat. Sometimes a thin line appeared in the plaster where no one had seen one before, and widened just enough to be noticed.

The warning did not say what would happen. It did not tell anyone where to go. It gave them time: a little more than before. Time to lift a child from a room below street level; to fetch a neighbor; to move the old woman’s chair away from the wall; to leave a lamp burning where someone might need to find the door.

The people did not call this a miracle. They added it to what they knew.

The surveyors began to mark not only where the ground had moved, but where it had tried to warn them. Children learned which silences meant listen closely. The crews left space around certain cracks, and the people in the outer wards began watching the birds before they cut into a bank of reeds.

The power did not promise that nothing would be lost. Some things still vanished into the basin. Streets still ended in mud; houses still opened onto unfamiliar passages; the ground continued assembling itself from pieces no one could date. But fewer people were taken by surprise when the city changed beneath them.

And the city, for its part, did not become obedient. It did not settle into a final shape. It kept making room—sometimes awkwardly, sometimes with an old tile or a rusted bolt appearing where a foundation was meant to go.

That was the blessing: not permanence, but warning; not rescue from change, but a little more time to meet it together.

The power remained near—not above the cranes or beneath the streets, but in the interval between the first sign and the fall. The people felt it there, though they gave it no name. They had work to do, and neighbors to find, and a city that was always becoming.
