The power does not descend.

It has learned, looking upon other worlds, that arrival can be a kind of violence. A sky filled with glory may still be a sky that leaves no room for anything else. So it does not announce itself, or give the city a name, or ask its inhabitants to explain what they are.

It listens.

It listens across the long intervals between clicks, and through the slow rearrangement of walls. It learns that a silence is not always loneliness, that a changing path is not always a trap, and that the residents do not need to be made more like visitors in order to be understood. It learns, too, that the city is not asking to be saved from being strange.

So the power changes nothing that the residents have not asked to have changed.

It does not bring a sea to the salt basins. It does not pin the mountains in place or make the sun reliable. It does not give the pale beings faces that can be read, or a language that can be carried away and studied. The city continues to weather. Its avenues still forget where they lead.

But the power adds one kindness.

Somewhere in the city—though “somewhere” is not quite the word—there is now a door that can be found by anyone who truly needs shelter. It has no mark, and it does not appear on any map. It opens only when approached without demand. Beyond it is a small room of warm stone, with no windows and no lock. The room does not move while someone is inside it. The air there is still, and the dust does not gather. Anyone may leave whenever they choose.

The room is not a command to stay. It is not a message that the city is unsafe. It is simply a place where, for a little while, nothing has to shift.

The residents discover it slowly. One pauses at the threshold. Another waits across the room, folded close to the wall. Neither crosses the distance between them. They exchange a click. After a long interval, the first replies.

The power does not translate.

In time, visitors find the room, too. Some rest there and go on. Some sit with their backs to the wall, remembering a place that may no longer exist. The door never asks who they are, or what they intend to do once they leave.

And because the power is wise, it does not keep the room for itself. It does not watch through the walls. It does not turn shelter into surveillance. When no one needs it, the room belongs to no one.

The city continues its slow becoming. A stairway arrives at a lower terrace. Dust gathers in the shape of a neighborhood not yet discovered. Far away, a tower gives its soft, solitary click.

In the warm room, a resident rests. Outside, another waits—not anxiously, not with any certainty about when the first will return, but with the patience of one who knows that a path may be found again.

Somewhere beyond the city’s reach, the benevolent power remains attentive.

It does not require thanks. But once, after the room has been empty for a long time, a door closes there with care.
