The power does not descend in a blaze.

It listens first.

It listens through the roots that have found one another in the cracks, through the threads beneath the moss, through the shallow pools that shiver when a pump starts. It listens to the fans pulling cold air across the galleries, and to the workers who rely on them. It does not mistake the green for a single mind, or the machinery for an enemy. The place is many lives held together by circumstance: root and stone, leak and pump, labor and leaf.

So the power begins with what the place can use.

It gives the water a cleaner way through. Not a flood, not a spring rising in the middle of the workings, but a slow, dependable seep along a few chosen seams. The pools stop shrinking so quickly. The damp edges widen. The fungi and tiny insects find more places to live, and the plants no longer have to wait so long between drips.

It steadies the roof where it is most fragile. The stone does not heal or become whole again; it remains cut, raw, and honest. But the worst cracks cease to creep. A dangerous fall of rock that would have buried a gallery simply does not happen. Those who work there find the old warning marks still relevant, yet no new fracture to add to them.

The power does not silence the machines. It knows the pumps are keeping parts of the land from drowning, and the fans are carrying away air that no one should have to breathe. Instead, it takes some of the strain into itself. The crusher still works, but its thunder no longer travels so fiercely through every wall. In distant pockets, the smaller sounds return: water ticking into a pool, a moth shifting on a lamp housing, the faint brush of stems against one another.

And then it makes a place.

Beyond one of the active galleries, a passage that was nearly forgotten opens into a stable hollow beneath a narrow roof break. The opening is not wide enough to invite careless traffic, but enough light reaches the floor to change it. The green climbs there slowly, as it always has. A little more soil collects in the hollows. Rainwater, filtered through stone and root, finds the chamber in measured drops. No grand forest appears. There is only room for the life already present to become a little more than it was.

The workers notice the changes. Their instruments show a safer roof, a less punishing vibration, a water level that no longer behaves as it should. The power does not command them to leave or tell them what the place means. It gives them evidence, and time to understand it. Eventually they mark the new passage on their plans and route the heaviest work away from it. Some make a habit of checking the chamber. One leaves a lamp angled toward the floor; another puts it back when someone points out that the shaft gives enough light.

The plants do not thank the power. They do not know what has happened. They take the water where it comes, turn toward the light, and send roots into the cracks. Their quiet continuance is not a prayer answered. It is their own life, still theirs.

That is what the power protects: not an image of untouched nature, and not a life made dependent on miracles, but the possibility of continuance. It leaves the rails, the concrete, the old cuts in the rock. It does not pretend the buried landscape was never opened. It makes sure that opening is not the last thing that happens there.

When the power withdraws its attention, the fans still turn. The pumps still thud. The green remains low against the stone.

But somewhere beyond the machinery, a drop falls into water—and, for the first time in a long while, nothing in the chamber trembles when it lands.
