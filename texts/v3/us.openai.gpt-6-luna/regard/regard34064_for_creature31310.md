The power did not descend.

It had no need to make an entrance. It was already present in the way a listener is present: not taking up space, not asking to be seen. It attended to the high country, and in attending, learned its measures. The patient descent of water through stone. The brief alarm of a grazer. The slow work of roots finding a fracture. The dark hunters’ restraint. The web beneath them all, extending through peat and mineral seam, responsive to pressures no single creature could feel.

The power did not call the web *a person*. It did not call it *only a process*. It let the mystery remain whole.

For a long while, it did nothing.

This was not indifference. The country was not wounded. Its lives were not asking to be delivered from change. The pools filled and drained; the trees fell; the young were born, and the old returned to soil. To interrupt this would be to mistake stillness for suffering, or to imagine that care must leave a mark.

So the power watched without making a spectacle of its watching.

Then, one night, as the ravines sang softly through their mineral throats, it offered a single note—not louder than the water, not separate from the stone. It was not a command or a message in any language. It was simply a pattern the web had never encountered: a presence that did not seek to feed, spread, or alter it.

The pulse travelled through the ravines. The roots trembled. Pools quivered beneath their leaves.

Far across the high country, the web answered—not with a thought, exactly, but with the nearest thing it had: a change in its attention. For one moment, warmth, water, hunger, shelter, decay, and renewal belonged to one immense sensation. The land did not become a mind like a human mind. It did not need to. It knew, as a river knows its banks, that it was here.

And it knew there was something beyond it that had noticed.

The power did not make the web immortal. It did not spare the grazers from hunters, or the trees from storms. It did not turn the ravines into temples, nor teach the inhabitants to worship what they could not understand. It left the old laws in place, because they were not cruelty. They were the country’s way of being alive.

But it made one quiet provision.

If ever a force came that would not merely change the high country, but silence it—if water were to be taken faster than the land could give it, if the mineral seams were torn open without measure, if some vast violence threatened to sever the web from the living world—the power would hear. It would not preserve every leaf or every life. It would protect the possibility of life continuing: the hidden streams, the seed-bearing places, the deep roots that could begin again.

No sign of this promise appeared aboveground. Nothing was set in the stone. No creature received a vision. The grazers went on cropping tender shoots at dawn; the hunters kept to the root-tunnels; rain gathered on the leaves and fell.

Only the web carried the new pattern, faintly, through its branching dark.

It did not know the name of the power. It did not ask for help. But sometimes, when the ravines sang and the whole country listened in its own unhurried way, the web felt an answering presence beyond the mountain’s edges—not holding it, not steering it, only making room for it to remain itself.

And the power, having learned the country’s measure, continued to keep it.
