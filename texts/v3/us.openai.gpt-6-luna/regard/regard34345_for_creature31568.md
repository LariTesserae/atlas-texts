The power did not arrive as a sun.

A sun would have made the city explain itself: its shadows, its water, the pale faces at the windows. It would have laid every contradiction bare and called the exposure healing.

Instead, the power arrived as attention.

For a long while, nothing changed. The rain fell upward from one canal and downward in the next. The old channels carried water toward places they had never been built to reach. The inhabitants continued their small errands, unaware—or unwilling to show that they were aware—of the vast kindness resting over the basin.

Then, in a flooded square, one of the children stopped running.

It looked into the water. Its reflection looked back. The child crouched, and the reflection crouched too. This was unusual enough that three older inhabitants came to watch. Then the water stilled.

Not all the water in the city. Not the canals, or the rain, or the damp shining on the walls. Only the water in that square, held quiet as a hand cupped around a flame.

In it, the child saw the city from above: its roofs, its terraces, its dark plain and distant hills. And beyond the hills, not a promised country or a way out, but simply more world—vast, patient, and not hostile.

The child put a hand into the water.

A voice came—not from the sky, but from the space between the hand and its reflection.

*What should I mend?*

The inhabitants gathered. They arrived by stairs, passages, and routes that had not been there a moment before. Some came from rooms. Some came from shadows. One appeared in the square’s water and climbed out without getting wet.

For a time, none answered.

The power did not fill the silence for them. It had not come to confuse quiet with consent.

At last, an old inhabitant laid a smooth stone on the paving. Another set beside it a small empty bowl. A third drew a pale line across the stones, then stopped before closing the circle.

They were not asking for the city to be made orderly. They were not asking for the water to obey, or for the sky to open, or for the old world to be restored. Perhaps they did not know what those things would cost. Perhaps they knew exactly.

They were asking for a place where a thing could be put down and found again.

The power understood.

It did not repair the crooked streets. It did not straighten the staircases or call the rain back to earth. It touched the unfinished pale line, and the stone there became a little brighter—not glowing, only easier to find. The circle remained open. Anyone could enter or leave.

From then on, there was one place in the city where a bowl left at night would still be there in the morning. A stone set down would not migrate to some forgotten room. A visitor who wished to go would find the way outward, though the road might take a different shape each time.

The gift did not make the inhabitants stay. It did not make them leave.

The child returned to the square the next day. The water did not hold still for it again, but the stone remained where it had been placed. The child added another stone.

Others began leaving things in the open circle: a length of cord, a cup without a handle, a reed with translucent stems. Sometimes the offerings were taken away. Sometimes they remained. No one was required to explain.

The power watched for a while longer. It learned the city’s listening places and the paths water preferred when left alone. It learned that some of the inhabitants were afraid, and some were content, and many were both at once. It learned that the basin was not a wound merely because it did not resemble the world beyond it.

Then the power withdrew its attention, not completely but enough that the city could be itself without feeling observed.

Nothing was saved from ending. Nothing was promised forever.

But when rain fell upward, there was a place where the inhabitants could gather and hear it clearly. And when the rain fell down, the same place offered shelter—not from the water, exactly, but from the need to decide what it meant.
