The first thing the power did was listen.

It listened to the spring rising through stone, to water taking days to cross a patch of ground, to the small blue creatures gathering when the pump paused. It listened to the generator’s shudder and the pump’s answering knock. It listened, especially, to the silence Sella had learned to keep around questions she no longer expected anyone to ask.

Then the Crown Sill’s shadow fell in the right direction.

It did not stay that way for long. The light shifted; the oddness passed. Sella looked up from the pipe she was mending and frowned at the pumping house wall.

“Was that you?” she asked.

The pressure gauge trembled. The pump knocked.

That evening, the machinery began to falter. A new fracture had opened beneath the spring channel, and the iron pipe was drawing grit. Sella got up from her cot, already reaching for her tools. Before she could cross the room, the pump gave one uneven blow—and stopped.

The quiet that followed was so large it seemed to have weight.

In the pools, the pale creatures rose from beneath their mineral shelters. Their feelers lifted. The spring kept welling. Water pattered down the stone, and beyond the rim the unseen waterfall made its distant, steady sound.

Sella stood very still. She had spent so long listening for failure that she did not know what to do with the absence of a warning.

Then, under the pumping house, the rock shifted—not breaking, but settling. The fracture drew closed by a finger’s breadth. Grit loosened from the channel and slid harmlessly into a shallow basin. The gauge’s needle found its mark. The pump started again, its familiar knock softened, not silenced.

Sella put her hand against the wall.

The power did not tell her that the Sill was safe. It did not promise that the spring would rise forever, or that the machinery would never fail. It did not explain the prints on her floor, or name the other presence that sometimes seemed to answer from the wetness.

It left those things true, and left them hers to understand.

In the morning, Sella found a clean page in her swollen notebook. No words had been written on it. But beside it lay a small brass key, warm from the stove though she had not put it there. It fit the old hoist at the rim. When she turned it, the hoist released a narrow platform, sturdy enough to carry one person down and bring one back.

Sella held the key in her palm for a long time.

The power waited. It did not call her down, or ask her to stay. It had not come to take the Sill from her, nor to make her the sole keeper of it. It had made leaving possible. Returning, too.

At last Sella tucked the key into her pocket and went to check the pump.

The creatures had gathered in the channel. The moss had already begun to soften the edge of the repaired stone. The machinery kept time, though now and then—only now and then—its knock made room for the sound of water underneath.
