At first, the power did nothing.

It watched the upland through a thousand dawns. It listened to the herds passing like weather, to the stalks carrying calls across the basins, to the people who learned the paths only to lose them again. It listened beneath the woven ground, where roots held the old stone together and small lives moved through the dark.

It could have made rivers. It could have stilled the predators, softened the heat, and commanded the stalks to bend aside for every traveler. It knew how. But it also knew that ease was not always mercy, and that a world could be damaged by being helped in the wrong way.

The plateau was not failing. Its thirst had shape and season. Its dangers belonged to its continuance. The grazers opened the clearings; the plants closed them; the people borrowed what they needed and went on. Nothing was safe from loss, but neither was anything alone in it.

So the power did not name itself. It did not ask to be thanked.

It waited for a year when the clouds passed the upland without catching. The dew came thinly, then not at all. The root-hollows dried. The herds altered their old routes, and their calves began to fall behind. People followed the animals toward water they remembered, and found only warm stone.

Then the power acted.

Not with rain. Rain would have fallen in one place, and need would have gathered there with it. Not with a spring, which would have drawn every creature toward a single vulnerable hollow.

Instead, in the last dark hour before dawn, moisture gathered where the old roots held the rock in their grip. It beaded along the undersides of leaves that had seemed dry. It filled the joints of scattered stalks—one here, one miles away, one beyond the next ridge. No pool was large. No place became safe. But across the upland, there was enough: for a burrower, for a thirsty calf, for a person with a cup cut from rind.

The first to find it were the small climbers. They drank, then called to one another in their many bright voices. The sound traveled through the hollow stems, altered by distance and wind. People heard it and understood only that the plateau was speaking differently. They followed the sound carefully. They drank without taking all. They left pale strips of rind tied high on the stalks, as they had always done.

For several mornings the dew returned. Then the clouds came, snagged on the tallest plants, and released their brief, private showers. The great herds found their old roads again. The calves grew strong enough to keep pace.

No one saw the power. No one could say whether the extra water had been miracle, weather, or some deep habit of the plants they had not noticed before. The old people told the young that in a hard season one should listen especially well, and take only what could be carried without closing the way behind them.

The upland went on being itself: thirsty, abundant, dangerous, patient. Its stems boomed in the wind. Its clearings shifted by inches over generations. The people came and went beneath the listening sky.

And the power, having given no more than the world could use, remained attentive.
