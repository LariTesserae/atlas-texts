The power does not arrive as a light in the sky.

It has learned, before acting, that this place is already full of forces that mistake stillness for safety. Stone presses upward. Water finds its way through every seal. Animals cross where the city has made a path, and the city shifts to let them pass. To stop any one of these things would be to injure the rest.

So the power listens.

It listens through the anchors sunk into the shelf, through the pipes carrying water and heat, through the small vibrations people send along rails when words would not travel. It listens to the animals, too—not as if every rasp were a sentence, but with the patience to leave unknown things unknown. It learns the difference between a city struggling to endure and a city doing what it has chosen to do.

Then, deep beneath the oldest district, a seam begins to open.

It is not the largest crack the plateau has made. It is only badly placed. It will split a set of supports just as a migration passes under the district, and the weight of the animals will bring down a bridge. The people have measured the shelf, but not this particular conjunction of pressures. They have no name for it yet.

The power could hold the stone in place. It could make the animals turn aside, raise the bridge, command the water to flow elsewhere. It does none of these things. It gives the city a little more time.

A pulse travels up through the anchors: slow, then quick, then slow again. It is too deliberate to be the usual complaint of the rock. The machines register it. So do the people who can feel a message through a railing. Somewhere in the lower channels, animals fall quiet. The pulse comes once more.

No one knows what it means. But the oldest listeners recognize its shape as a warning—not a word, exactly, but an insistence: *attend here.*

They go to the seam. They take new measurements. They find what they had missed. Work begins at once. A passage is opened for the migration; a set of buildings is emptied; anchors are loosened and reset. The power does not do the work for them. It keeps the shelf from shifting for the few hours they need.

When the animals pass beneath the bridge, the whole district shudders. The seam widens. The water changes course. The city groans through its rails—but the bridge holds until the last bodies have gone by and the people are clear.

Then the power releases its small hold.

The stone moves as it was going to move. A walkway falls into the basin. The district is altered, and no one can honestly say it has been saved from change. But no lives are lost, human or animal, and the migration reaches the other side of the ridge.

Afterward, the people argue about the pulse. Some call it a message. Some call it an improbable reading of stress in the stone. The engineers add the pattern to their records, without claiming to understand it. A few families begin tapping it softly at the rails—not as prayer, exactly, but as acknowledgment.

The power does not ask them to believe. It does not ask for thanks. It has no wish to become the most important thing in the city.

It has seen that the inhabitants know how to make room for one another. Its gift is only this: when the next dangerous change begins, perhaps they will hear it sooner.

And the city continues, wet and dark and unfinished, making room.
