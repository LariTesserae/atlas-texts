The power came to the drowned plain without entering it.

It did not mistake silence for a plea. It saw no wound that asked to be closed, no creature trapped beneath the water, no life waiting to be returned to some imagined former shape. The garden was not the civilization that had planted it, nor a ruin in need of restoration. It was itself: slow, dispersed, enduring.

So the power did not raise the city. It did not clear the channels or call animals into the empty water. It did not give the garden a mind and then ask it to explain what it wanted. The old terraces had become its ground. The water was its weather. There was no wisdom in making it answer to a stranger’s idea of a better world.

For a long while, the power simply attended.

Then, where the surface lay pale above a sunken courtyard, it let one slender shaft of warmth pass through the water. Not a blaze, not a command—only a mild brightening, falling across the silt and the small green lights of the stalks. The stone kept its cold. The current kept its pace. Nothing was required to happen.

But over the years, one frond leaned toward that brightness. Another grew beside it. The change was so small that no traveler would have marked it. The garden did not wake, because it had never been asleep. It did not understand a gift, because no understanding had been demanded of it.

The power withdrew its attention without withdrawing its care. It left the garden to its own patient life, and kept watch—not to prevent every wall from falling, but to be there if the conditions that sustained the garden ever failed.

Benevolence, here, was not rescue.

It was the knowledge that something could be changed, and the wisdom to change almost nothing.
