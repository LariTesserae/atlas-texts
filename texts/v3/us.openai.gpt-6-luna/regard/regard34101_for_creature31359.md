The power did not descend into the hollow.

It had no need to arrive with thunder. It was already there, in the weight of the shelf, the patience of roots, the water that found its way through stone. So it listened first.

It listened to the Iru laying pebbles beside a new crack. It listened to the children pressing their ears to the ground, waiting for the hollow to answer. It listened to the soft-footed visitor crossing the lanes at night, and to the walls settling—not as a warning of disaster, but as a place still learning its shape.

The power understood that the Iru did not want the hollow made permanent. To freeze it would be to take from them the very thing they knew how to live with. Their shelters were meant to move. Their paths were meant to be revised. The earth’s changing was not their enemy.

But there were changes too sudden for any small hand to meet.

So the power gave the stone a voice.

From then on, when a wall was about to shift dangerously, the hollow made a low, clear note—one the Iru had never heard before. It came early enough to carry a child away, move a basin, loosen a roof of leaves. It did not say *nothing will change*. It said, *this change is coming; you have time.*

And, in one shaded pocket where water had always vanished too quickly, a seam in the stone began to bead. A little water gathered there—not a stream, not a pool, but a steady thread, enough to fill a basin if the Iru remembered to share it. The power did not make the hollow lush or easy. It offered what the hollow could hold.

The Iru gathered around the new seep. They touched its rim, tasted the water, and placed a smooth pebble beside it. For a long while, no one spoke. Then an elder pressed a palm to the wall.

The stone was warm. Beneath it, something vast and gentle seemed to be listening.

The Iru did not build a shrine. They built a small basin, and left a little water in it for the night-walker.

The power accepted this as thanks. Then it remained—not as a guardian standing over them, but as a kindness woven into the place: a warning note in the stone, a patient bead of water, and enough time for the Iru to answer the world in their own way.
