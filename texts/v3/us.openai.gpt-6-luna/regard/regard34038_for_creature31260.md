The power would not begin by changing anything.

It would watch the town for a long while: the hands clearing a channel, the child stopping to return a frog to the water, the neighbors arguing over a bank while holding it in place together. It would see that this is not a place waiting to be perfected. Its life depends on adjustment, and its people already know how to listen.

So the power would be careful not to mistake difficulty for failure. It would not still the water, silence the birds, straighten the streets, or make the ground firm beneath every foot. It would not rescue the town from every flood. A town spared every consequence might forget what its own attentiveness is for.

But it would give them a little more room at the edge of disaster.

When a storm came too hard, the spillway would remain passable for the few minutes needed to clear it. A bank would slump, but not take a house with it. A warning would arrive—not as a voice from the sky, but as a familiar bird calling from the wrong place, or a vibration beneath the planks that every child knew to recognize. The people would still have to act. They would simply have time to act together.

And when damage could not be prevented, the power would see that its burden did not fall on one household alone. A hand would appear with a pole, a basket, a spare bundle of grass. Someone would notice the empty place at the work line and go to find its owner. Not because the power had compelled kindness, but because it had quietly kept open the paths along which kindness already traveled.

It might also keep watch beyond the bowl: against a sickness that could empty the nests, a landslide that would bury the whole settlement, a stranger’s appetite for draining and clearing what had taken generations to learn. These are not the ordinary costs of living in a lively place. They are the kinds of loss that can erase the chance to recover.

Then, having done this, it would withdraw from notice.

The town would not name the power. It might be thanked in a song, or mistaken for luck, or never suspected at all. Its gift would be difficult to distinguish from a good neighbor’s timing, a channel that held, a warning heard soon enough.

That would be the point. The power would not wish to become the town’s center. It would protect the conditions in which the town could remain its own: crowded, changeable, noisy with water and wings, and capable—after every disturbance—of beginning the work again.
