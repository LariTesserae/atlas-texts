### The Attention

The power noticed the high country because it was changing.

It had watched worlds hold still beneath their inhabitants, and worlds break too quickly for anyone to learn their names. This place did neither. It rose by fractions. It cooled. It made room for water, and then changed its mind about where the water should go.

The power watched the cities, too. It saw the people measure the ground, move their roads, leave corridors for animals, and dismantle homes when keeping them would cost too much. It saw how they grieved what they moved, and how they went out afterward to find what the land had become.

It did not mistake their patience for helplessness. It did not mistake the plateau’s motion for a flaw.

For a long while, it did nothing.

Then came a season when the deep groan traveled under the western basin. The instruments found strain in the rock, but not its direction. They offered three possible readings, each plausible; none gave the survey crews enough certainty to plan a safe route for the people, the animals, and the marsh water below.

The power could have held the shelf in place. It could have made the plateau still.

Instead, it gave the instruments one more thing to measure.

A faint pulse passed through the buried rock—not force enough to stop it, not a command, but a clear, patient answer to the machines’ questions. The survey crews ran the readings again. The strain resolved. They had time to close the causeway, move a neighborhood, and open a new channel for the water. The shelf shifted weeks later, where the instruments had said it would.

No one saw a hand above the clouds. No voice spoke from the towers. In the cities, people argued about the signal. Some called it an unknown property of the stone. Others thought the equipment had found a pattern no one had noticed before. A child asked whether the mountain had warned them.

The adults did not tell the child no.

After that, the signal came only rarely: a little more clarity when the ground was difficult to read, a little more time when time mattered. Never a prediction of every danger. Never a map of the future. The land remained the land—unfinished, beautiful, and not entirely knowable.

The power did not ask for gratitude. It did not ask to be named. It left the people their judgments, their rituals of repair, their right to be wrong and learn. It did not save them from change. They had never asked to be saved from change.

Sometimes, in the clear hours after rain, a surveyor would look up from the instruments and feel that the immense silence around the city was not empty. They would tell no one, or tell someone over supper. Either way, the feeling would belong to them.

And the plateau continued to rise.

The power stayed—not as a wall against the weather, nor a hand beneath every fragile thing, but as attention: careful, available, and willing to let the people remain the authors of their lives.
