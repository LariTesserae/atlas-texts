The power did not descend.

There was no opening in the cloud, no sudden brightness on the water. It came as attention: patient enough to notice the difference between a channel that carried water and one that merely held it; between the rasp of a shell-backed grazer at work and the same sound made in distress. It listened to the town’s many voices until it could tell who was calling, who was answering, and who was simply keeping company.

At first it thought the basin unfinished. Then it understood that this was only the judgment of someone accustomed to measuring a place by what it might become. The basin was not waiting to be completed. Its people had learned its seams and moods. They had built neither against the water nor surrendered to it, but negotiated with it, day after day. Their world was young by some measure and ancient by the only measure that mattered to them: it was known.

The power considered giving them a clearer sky, or a wider shore, or the bright green abundance it had seen elsewhere. It considered teaching them to make tools that would outlast stone and shell. Then it watched a child carry mud to a thinning wall while an elder showed her where the water must still pass. It watched a group leave a patch of mineral film for the grazers, though scraping it clean would have made their work easier.

It set those gifts aside.

For a long while, it did nothing. This was not indifference. It was care taking the shape of restraint.

Then, one dim evening, a call rose from the far edge of the basin—a wavering cry from one of the larger animals, familiar in its shape but strangely forlorn. The people heard it, as they often heard such cries. One answered from the nearest pool. Another answered from a roof passage. Soon the call was passing through the town, carried across water and stone.

The power added no new voice. It only held the sound gently, so that for a single breath every answer reached farther than it should have. The call went over the basin, through the covered ways, beyond the known ranks of walls. From the dim distance came an answering cry. Then another.

The town fell still—not silent, for the animals continued, the water slapped its shelves, and the gutters murmured. But the people paused to listen to the basin as a whole.

Some said the distant calls had always been there, and they had only now heard them. Some said the water had carried the sound strangely. A few insisted that something had answered. They argued, as they did about the animals, while repairing the same ledge.

The power did not settle the question. It did not ask for a shrine or a name. It did not lift the town from its warm, crowded water, or make its people into something other than themselves.

It remembered their voices.

And when the clouded day came again, the basin remained as it had been: wet stone, shallow pools, working hands, calls passing from neighbor to neighbor. Nothing had been made easier. Nothing had been taken away. But the place was no longer, anywhere in the deep attention of the world, unregarded.
