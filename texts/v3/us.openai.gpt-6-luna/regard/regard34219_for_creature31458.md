The power did not descend.

It had no need to make an entrance. It was already present in the old weight of the mountain, in the patient pressure that held one stone against another. But now it attended—not as weather attends a ridge, passing over it, but with the full stillness of a listener.

It saw the open courtyards and the small lives beneath them. It saw the prints that vanished by noon, the hands laid flat to the ground, the bowed head before the great stone. It saw, too, what it could not know: what the residents heard in the walls, what they remembered in the curves of the worn carvings, what they meant when they answered the wind.

It was wise enough not to call their lives a problem.

The settlement was old, but not abandoned. The people were small, but not lost. Their quiet was not loneliness merely because no larger voice answered it. So the power did not open the sealed passage. It did not restore the fallen roofs or sharpen the worn markings. It did not summon a road, a visitor, or a new age. These things would have been easy. They would not necessarily have been kind.

For a long while, it did nothing that could be seen.

Then came a year when the clouds crossed the ridges without breaking. The cisterns dwindled. The seep below the terraces narrowed to a dark stain, and the elders began leading the young farther between the stones to find what little moisture remained. In the bare courtyard, they put their hands to the ground as they always had. They waited for an answer.

The power did not send a flood. It did not fill the cisterns to their rims. Far below, where old water moved unseen through the mountain, it eased a fallen seam of stone by the least possible degree.

The seep did not become a stream. It simply did not stop.

The people found it in the morning. They gathered around the wet line without speaking. One child touched the stone, then looked to an elder. The elder pressed both hands to the ground.

There was, beneath them, a depth of quiet they had not heard before—not a voice, and not the returning note of an empty hall. Something too vast to answer in their manner, and too considerate to pretend otherwise.

So the elder gave the ground one soft tap.

The mountain held the sound. The power received it.

Afterward, the seep kept its narrow course. The rain came when it came. Moss advanced by the width of a finger; frost lifted a slab; birds crossed the roofs one at a time. Nothing was made permanent. Nothing was made easy.

But now and then, when the residents gathered in the bare courtyard and laid their hands flat to the earth, the water below them answered with its small, steady passage through stone.

The power remained—not as a ruler, nor as a guest, but as a guardian of what it had been wise enough to leave alone.
