The power did not mistake hardship for a flaw.

It saw the thin water, the cold roofs, the paths that demanded care at every turn. It saw the people measuring a day by cisterns, and the birds and grazers whose habits had become part of the country’s own counsel. It understood that to pour abundance over the Fold would not simply ease life. It might loosen the customs that kept water shared, draw people toward places that could not hold them, or make the animals’ old knowledge useless. A gift could become a wound.

So it did not descend in brightness. It did not speak from the clouds or set a spring boiling from bare stone.

For a long while, it did nothing anyone could name.

It watched the settlements mend their channels. It watched people leave ledges for the birds, and watched children learn which pools belonged to more than themselves. It watched travelers leave signs for strangers they would never meet. The power recognized a kind of wisdom already alive here: not grand, not perfect, but practiced daily.

Then, one night, high above a crowded basin, a seam in the rock began to shift. The movement was small—no more than a tremor passing through stone—but the slope above the houses was steep, and the houses below were full of sleepers.

The power could have held the mountain still. Instead, it gave the tremor a voice.

A thread of grit slid down a covered passage, though there was no wind. The small animals beneath the stairs fled into the lane. The cliff-birds lifted from their ledges in a single pale rush. And in one house, a sleeper woke to the sound of water ticking in a basin, though the basin was empty.

The first people out saw the birds. The next heard the stone. They called through the passages, tapped warnings along the walls, and carried sleeping neighbors into the open courts. They had little time, but they used it well.

The slope broke before dawn. Rock filled the lowest lane and crushed roofs; no one was beneath them.

By morning, the power had gone—or perhaps it had never been there in any way they could point to. The people inspected the damage, counted one another, and began to clear the lane. Someone carried water to the animals. Someone else marked the unstable ground. The birds settled again where they could.

In time, the story changed. Some said the animals had sensed the fall. Some said the basin had echoed strangely. A few insisted that a hand had passed through the mountain and held it back for the space of a breath. No account became the only one.

The power allowed that, too.

It left the Fold as it had found it: cold, immense, and still becoming. But for a while, it kept watch—not to spare the people every loss, nor to make their lives easy, but to stand near the edge of what they could bear.
