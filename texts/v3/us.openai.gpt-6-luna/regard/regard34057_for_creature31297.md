The power did not descend into the city.

It did not part the clouds, or drain the warm rain from the streets, or lay green things over the black stone. It did not wake the engines into some brighter, busier life. From far away, such changes might have looked like gifts. Up close, they would have been intrusions: new conditions imposed on a place whose inhabitants had learned, with care, how to live inside the old ones.

So the power listened.

It listened to the turbines turning below the streets, to the water slipping through its ancient channels, to the small changes that woke the people in their rooms beside the pipes. It listened to the lamps moving through the vapor, and to the long intervals in which no one moved at all. It listened, too, to the pale threads in the culverts and the whiskers of the little blind animals brushing the cistern walls.

It understood that the city was not waiting to be rescued. It was being attended.

For a long while, the power did nothing.

Then, in a district where the pipes ran deep beneath a broad empty plaza, it made one small sound.

Three knocks traveled through the water. A pause. One more.

The nearest listener woke at once. They sat up, lamp in hand, and waited. The pumps kept their usual rhythm. The sound did not repeat.

They took their pins and went down.

At the first junction, they tested the water. At the second, they listened against the wall. The signal came again, faintly, from farther on: three knocks, a pause, one. It passed through pipes that had no mechanical connection, crossed a cistern where no current should have carried it, and rose into a narrow channel that had been dry for generations.

The listener followed it to the plaza.

There, the rainwater lay in its usual dark sheet. But its surface was perfectly still. No falling drop disturbed it. No wind crossed it. In the reflection, the towers stood beneath an unfamiliar brightness—not sunlight, exactly, but the unmistakable brightness of something looking back.

The listener did not kneel. They did not call for help. They drove one metal pin gently into the stone beside the water.

A question, or perhaps only a sign that someone had heard.

The power answered with a single knock.

By morning, several others had arrived. They came from far districts, carrying lamps and cord, bringing measurements. They tested the pipes. They checked the gauges. They marked the walls. Nothing in the city had been repaired or altered. The engines turned as before. The clouds passed through the lower streets. Water climbed where it climbed.

Only the signal remained.

Some listeners returned to their districts. Some stayed in the plaza a little longer. No one was required to remain. The power did not ask them to believe in it, or to name it, or to surrender the work by which they knew their home.

It had only made clear that, beyond all the pipes and stone and vapor, there was another listener.

And when the city resumed its quiet, it was not quite the same quiet as before.
