The power did not enter.

It had no need to make itself large. It had learned, by listening, that largeness was one of the dangers of the hollow: a foot on the roof, a root driven through a wall, too much water arriving at once. So it held its attention at the edge of the town, where the rain met the earth, and listened until the small disturbances became legible.

It understood that the town was not one creature, but something that could act like one. The shrimp knew the drains; the snails knew the walls; the frogs heard what the soil could not say aloud. Their knowledge met in the dark without any one of them possessing all of it.

So the power did not give them a ruler, or a voice that would drown out their own. It gave them time.

A storm came hard over the larger world. Water pressed through the soil above the hollow, gathering along a new seam. The first drops entered where they had never entered before. The shrimp felt the current turn. A snail’s shining road broke beneath a trickle. At the pool’s edge, one frog grew still.

The warning began to travel.

But the town was young, and the new seam had opened far from the old channels. The creatures felt the change in pieces: a tremor here, a sudden cold there. For a little while, the message could not find its way through.

The power laid one gentle pulse along the root.

It was no command. It was no sound. It passed through the walls and floors as a shiver so slight that, elsewhere, it might have been mistaken for rain. Here, the frogs felt it and held still. The shrimp paused in the drains. The snails turned their feelers toward the same dark passage. One small piece of knowledge met another, and the warning went on.

They moved.

Mites left the water’s bright skin. Shrimp slipped from the lower channels. Moths lifted from the roofs and settled in the higher chambers. The snails took longer, as snails do, but no one hurried them into danger. When the water surged in, it found the lower streets empty.

Then the power touched the soil outside—not the town, not its walls, but the ground where the new seam was forming. It eased a handful of earth aside. The root above the hollow drank some of the rain. The rest found another way down, into a crack that led away from the little streets.

The town was still wet. A roof sagged. One of the lower chambers filled and would not be lived in again. A few old trails vanished beneath silt. The power did not restore them. The hollow was alive, and living things were not made safe by being held still.

When the storm passed, the creatures returned to their work. Shrimp cleared the changed drains. Snails crossed the new clay, laying down fresh roads. The moths found another place for their roofs. The frogs resumed their patient listening at the pool.

No one saw the power. No one named it. The pulse became, in time, part of the town’s remembered weather: a tremor that had come before the water, a pause in which everyone had somehow heard everyone else.

The power remained nearby, but did not keep watch as a jailer watches. It let the root thicken. It let the water choose its course. It let the town discover, by touch and trial, what the changed hollow required.

And when the rain came again, the inhabitants listened to one another first.
