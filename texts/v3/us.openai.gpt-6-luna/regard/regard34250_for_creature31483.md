It would not begin by changing anything.

For a long while, it would simply attend. It would listen to the pumps, and to the people who knew their sounds. It would follow the swifts through the bridges, the water through its channels, the roots through their measured beds. It would learn which walls were old but sound, which were old and quietly tiring; which gardens could spare a bucket, which households could not. It would notice that the country was already full of care—not perfect care, not care without quarrels or mistakes, but the daily, stubborn kind that keeps a place alive.

It would not mistake great size for a request to be conquered. It would not flatten the terraces, uncover the buried ground, or make a river simply because rivers are what landscapes are supposed to have. The people had made a life with the country they had. Their work was not a flaw to be corrected.

So the power would wait for a need that was truly theirs.

Perhaps, in a dry season, rain would come at last—not in a violent sheet, but slowly, over many hours. It would touch the highest gardens first, then descend through the channels and cisterns, filling them in their proper order. It would be enough to ease the strain, not so much that the lower courts flooded. The residents would still have to clear filters, mend a cracked conduit, and argue over how the stored water should be shared. The gift would not replace their skill. It would give that skill room to work.

Or perhaps the power’s one intervention would be quieter still. A retaining wall, sound for generations, would begin to fail behind a face of old tile. Before the first stone shifted, the damp line would show itself plainly in the morning light. A gardener would see it. A child would point. The engineers would come, and the people above and below the wall would have time to move what needed moving and brace what could be saved.

The power would hold the wall while they worked—not forever, and not in their place, but long enough.

Then it would withdraw from notice. No monument would be raised to it. The rain would be remembered as a good rain; the wall, as a wall that had needed repair. People would go on tending their orchards and clearing their channels. The bats would still emerge at dusk. Somewhere, a pipe would still drip.

This would be its wisdom: not to make the country dependent on a miracle, but to preserve the conditions in which its inhabitants could continue to care for one another and for the place they had built.

And if the country itself had a voice, perhaps the power would hear it—not as a single voice above the machinery, but as the whole mingled sound: water, leaves, wings, tools, footsteps, and people calling to one another across the levels.

It would recognize that sound as an answer, too.
