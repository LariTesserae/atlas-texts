The power did not descend upon the plateau.

It had no need to announce itself, and it knew that an arrival can be a kind of violence. So it attended first: to the water that slipped into the fissures, to the roots holding the metal in place, to the patient machinery and the little dark life moving beneath the leaves.

It saw that the plateau was not waiting to be saved. Its silences were not a plea. The being had a home there—not an easy one, perhaps, but a home made of warmth under pipes, damp paths through the roots, and the tremors by which it learned what was happening underground. To remake the place for its comfort would be to take that home away.

So the power changed nothing it could not first understand.

Far beneath the ridge, a pressure gate had begun to stick. It would not fail today, or tomorrow, but in time its failure would empty the small basins and split a seam of stone where the being sheltered. The power eased the gate—not open, not shut, only free to answer the wetness of the land as it had once been made to do. The next rain was gathered from the passing cloud and returned gently to the plateau. It filled the basins without flooding the hollows.

No lamp flared. No voice crossed the silent air.

The being felt the change as a steadiness beneath its feet. It paused beside a pipe, where warmth ran through the moss, and listened with the soles of its feet. For a long while, nothing happened. Then the pressure shifted far underground, not as a warning but as a reassurance: the machinery had made room for the water.

The power offered no command, and asked for no gratitude. It did not make the being visible to the world, or call a multitude of creatures to keep it company. It left it its concealment, its paths, its right to remain unknown.

But it made one small thing possible. On a clear morning, when the being emerged onto a strip of bare stone, the light rested there without startling it. In that warmth it felt, not a watcher, but an attention that could wait—and would not reach for it.

The being stayed a little longer than usual.

Then it slipped back beneath the leaves. The plateau continued its slow work: moss spreading over rivets, water gathering and draining, the machines adjusting by inches. And the power, having done what it could without taking the place for its own, remained near enough to notice if the balance ever began to fail.
