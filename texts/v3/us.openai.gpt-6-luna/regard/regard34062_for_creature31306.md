The power did not descend in fire, nor speak into the hollow. It had no need to announce that it had seen.

It saw the crowded ledges, the pale young clinging in the seams, the old bats returning by paths they knew only through sound. It saw the moss living by the memory of a drip, and the small plant at the lip holding fast to its crack. It saw that the cleft was not empty wilderness waiting to be made useful. It was already full.

So the power did not brighten the darkness or open the mountain. It did not make the animals tame, or spare them hunger, or promise that every young life would survive. To do so would have been to mistake care for ownership.

Instead, it kept a patient watch.

When frost reached too deep into the warm fissures, the breath from below held steady. When a storm tore at the cliff, the old stone shed what it could bear to lose, and the ledges that held the roosts endured. The power did not stop rain from falling or rock from weathering; it only turned aside the one ruin that would have crushed the hollow all at once.

And when, far below, someone looked up and wondered whether the inaccessible cleft might be reached, the thought passed. There was always another path, another purpose. No one came with tools to widen the seams or clear the ledges. The bats kept their darkness. The birds kept their narrow doors. Nothing in the hollow knew it had been spared.

Perhaps that was the blessing: not a new gift laid among the stones, but room for the old life to continue.

At dusk, the first bats loosened from the ceiling. The others followed, one by one, then in a stream. Their wings stirred the warm air; their calls crossed and returned from the rock. The power listened without asking to be heard.

Then the cloud passed below the cliff, the night opened over the valley, and the hollow went on being itself.
