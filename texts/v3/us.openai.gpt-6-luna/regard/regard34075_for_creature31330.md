It would not arrive as a sun.

Light poured suddenly into the basin would blind what had learned to live in dimness. Warmth would alter the water, the water would alter the creatures, and the creatures would have no way to say whether the change was a gift.

So the power would begin by listening.

It would listen through the stone, to the seep and tick of water, to the fine patter of innumerable feet. It would wait through the long pauses between thrum and answer. And at last it would understand that there was no single creature to find. The basin’s life was a many-bodied attention: shell over shell, thread and swimmer, each responding to changes too small for any one of them to command. Together they knew the water. Together they kept the hollows occupied.

The power would not call them a swarm, or mistake their patience for emptiness. It would not ask them to become something more recognizable.

It would touch the old ironwork only in thought. The pipe, the housing, the black posts: all might once have served a purpose, but the basin had survived their failure. To mend them without knowing what they did would be carelessness wearing the face of help. So the power would leave the rust where it was.

Then it would answer the thrum.

Not louder. Not with a command. It would send one small vibration through the deepest stone: a note that neither opened the hidden chamber nor sealed it, but made clear that something had heard.

The swimmers would turn. The pale creatures would tighten their hold beneath the waterline. The shells would pause in their slow scraping. At the bottom, the circular pool would keep its own stillness. For a long while, nothing more would happen.

Then, from within that clear water, the crowded reflection would turn as well.

That would be enough.

The power would not draw the creatures out of their basin or promise them a world they had never needed. It would keep watch on the conditions around them: the distant sources of water, the unseen pressures in the rock, the possibility that some careless hand might poison the pools or break the channels. If danger came, it would divert it before it reached the rim. If the water fell too far, it would help the old, slow cycle find its way back—not by flooding the bowl, but by letting a hidden seep run again.

And if there were no danger, it would do nothing.

Not nothing from indifference. Nothing from respect.

The basin would remain dim and wet, its inhabitants folding into seams when the water withdrew and spreading again when it returned. The iron would continue to rust. The thrum might come, or might not. The deep pool would hold its impossible reflection.

But now, when the note passed through the stone, another note would answer: faint, patient, and never repeated unless the basin first called. The creatures would not have to understand what had answered them. They would only know that the world was not indifferent to their listening.
