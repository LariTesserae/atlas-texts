The power notices the garden and does not mistake its quiet for a request.

It does not silence the machinery. The roar is not an enemy here; it is the weather the leaves have learned. It does not send in a bird, or open the walls to wildness, or make the moss spread in a green astonishment. It does not turn the room into a shrine. The garden is already whole in the way a living thing can be whole: not finished, but held in a balance that allows it to go on.

So, at first, the power does nothing.

But it attends.

When a seal begins to fail behind the wall, the leak is found before the water can turn the compost sour. When the pale panel clouds, someone has reason to clean it. When the hatch is nearly painted shut, the brush runs dry, and the worker returns another day. None of these things feels miraculous. Each has an ordinary explanation. The room is not spared every change—only the needless catastrophe of being forgotten in precisely the wrong way.

The power does not keep the garden unchanged. A frond browns. A root finds a new seam. One day the pump will stop, and the water will settle into the basin. The power will not command the old works to run forever. It will only make sure that, if the room’s life must alter, the alteration is not simply the result of a missed inspection or a locked hatch.

And once, perhaps, a person opens the door and stays a little longer than the work requires. They stand in the damp warmth, listening without being able to hear anything but the roar. They notice the silver surface of the water, the dark thread of moss, the small insect making its brief mark upon the basin. They leave without taking anything.

The power lets that be enough.

It has seen the garden. The garden need not know.
