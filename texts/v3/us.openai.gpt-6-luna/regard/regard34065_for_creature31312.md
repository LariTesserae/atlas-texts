The power did not descend.

It had seen the plateau from above and beneath: the brief water held in stone, the creatures hidden under crust, the people who knew when to take and when to leave. It saw that the country was not waiting to be made fertile. It was already alive, though its life had learned to be quiet.

A lake, the power thought, would be a wound here. A forest would be a conquest. Even a generous rain, given without measure, might drown the small arrangements by which so many lives endured.

So it did not make the plateau easier. It did not make its people greater, or richer, or more visible to one another. It would not mistake difficulty for a request to be remade.

But it noticed one fragile hinge in the country’s long patience: the western fog, arriving when it would, lingering just long enough—or not. In an age to come, there would be a season when it failed too many times. The hidden chambers would dry. The crabs would not cross the basins. The people would begin, carefully and without complaint, to leave.

The power did not promise that nothing would ever perish. It knew better than to promise a world without endings. Instead, it set a small mercy into the weather: when the fog came in that season, it would hold to the stone a little longer. Not enough to make a spring. Enough for the plateau to answer.

On that morning, no one saw a hand in the whiteness. People moved among the basins with their cups and mats. They left water for the smallest mouths. Crabs streamed over the rock; birds lowered their narrow faces; beneath the crust, the burrowers stirred. The fog thinned at last, as it always did.

The inhabitants spoke of it afterward as an unusually kind morning. They did not build a shrine. They did not change their customs. They returned to watching the stone.

And the power, having done what it could without making itself the center of the country, went on listening.
