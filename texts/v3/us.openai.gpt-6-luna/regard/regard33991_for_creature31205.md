The power noticed them first as a pattern of care.

Not the towers, though they kept watch. Not the lamps, though their reflections made a second town overhead. It noticed the hands that tested young stone day after day, the bridges built to be untied, the old marks kept even when no one could read them. It noticed that the people did not ask the plateau to stop changing. They only tried to meet each change while there was still time to answer it.

So the power did not descend.

It did not lift the sea, or harden the soft cliffs, or open a road to the bright world above. It did not decide that sunlight was better than green dimness, or that a rising land must be helped to rise. It had seen many lives ruined by gifts shaped according to the giver’s imagination.

Instead, it listened.

For a long while, it listened through the stone, through the currents, through the small knocks traveling ridge to ridge. It learned the people’s names for warmth and danger, for new ground and ground that could not yet be trusted. It learned which silences meant peace, and which meant that everyone was waiting for a sound from far below.

Then, deep beneath the plateau, where a seam of hot stone was swelling toward a crowded basin, the power touched the earth.

Not enough to stop the land from growing. Not enough to make the ridge permanent. Only enough to turn the pressure aside, into a long, empty cleft beyond the farthest settlement. The stone opened there with a low, patient groan. Warm water rose through it. The basin did not split. The homes did not fall. By morning, the people could hear the new vent breathing in the distance.

They went to look.

They went carefully, as they always did. They tested the ledges, watched the shimmer, tapped the stone and waited for the answer to return. When they understood that the new seam was not a danger to their homes, they sang to it—not a song of thanks, exactly, but one of acknowledgment.

The power did not correct them.

After that, it left a faint brightness in certain stones near the settlements. The brightness came before shifts in the deep pressure: a pale, slow pulse that could be missed, ignored, or mistaken for some new mineral growth. It did not tell them what to do. It only gave them a little more time in which to decide.

The people made their own meaning of it. They watched. They argued. They added the pulses to their records, beside the old marks whose meaning had been lost. Some said the stones were remembering something. Others said the plateau was learning to speak.

Both explanations were allowed to live.

And the power remained attentive, not as a ruler above the silver ceiling, but as a quiet presence within the long patience of the place. It did not keep the water still. It did not spare every wall from cracking, or every path from vanishing into roots. It did not promise that the plateau would never rise too high, or that the surface would never come close.

It kept no claim on what the people would choose when that day arrived.

It only guarded the space in which choosing remained possible.
