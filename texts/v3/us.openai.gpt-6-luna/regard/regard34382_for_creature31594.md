The power did not descend.

It had descended elsewhere, in ages when descent was needed: to lift a drowning world, to end a fire that could not end itself. Here, nothing was broken. The plateau was not waiting to be completed. Its life had found a way to be whole without a face, and the power understood that wholeness was not an invitation to improve it.

So first, it watched.

It learned the plateau’s measures: the long pauses between changes, the small shifts of moisture, the deep traveling pressure that passed beneath the ridges. It learned that what looked like silence was not absence, and that a million small responses might be as deliberate as a single voice.

Then, with great care, it answered.

Far beneath the plateau, where stone held the cold of old ages, the power made a pressure—gentle, brief, and shaped like the vibration that already traveled through roots and buried stems. It did not force the ground to move. It did not tell the plants what to do. It merely returned a pattern the plateau had sent into the deep without knowing it had sent anything.

The vibration passed under the mats. A grove turned its crowns by a fraction. In a distant basin, pale fronds folded, then opened.

The power waited.

After a long while, another pulse moved through the stone. It might have been an answer. It might have been the ordinary work of water and roots. The power did not decide for the plateau what its movements meant. It returned the pattern once more, softer than before, and then stopped.

Nothing in the country changed at once. No new flower appeared; no voice rose from the groves. The rain came when it came. The ridges continued their slow rearranging. The pale creatures crossed the moss and vanished.

But from then on, the power kept watch—not as a keeper watches a possession, but as one presence makes room for another. It would not rescue the plateau from change, for change was its way of living. It would not make it easier to understand, or more like anything else. If some force came that would silence it utterly, the power would stand between that force and the high country. Until then, it would leave the plateau to its own deep intelligence.

Once, in a season of scarce rain, a cloud paused above the plateau longer than the winds required. Moisture gathered in the cupped crowns and traveled down the ribs into the roots. The power did not call this a gift. The plateau did not call it anything.

They continued, each in its own manner, aware—perhaps—of a pressure that was not weather, and of an answer that did not need to be understood.
