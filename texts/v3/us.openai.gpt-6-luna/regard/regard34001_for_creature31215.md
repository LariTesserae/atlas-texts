The power did not arrive over Vey.

There was no descending light, no voice in the clouds. Nothing was set in order. The plain went on shifting beneath its dim sky, and the towers went on making their small corrections.

But the power attended.

It listened to the people listening to the ground, to the birds dividing the air, to the old city answering pressures no one could see. It listened longest at the bare shelf, where warm breath rose from the hollows and the dark-glass stakes held their patient watch.

The power could have made the land still.

It understood, however, that stillness would not be peace. Vey was not a wound in need of closing. The ground’s uncertainty was not a flaw; it was how the place lived. To take that away would be to silence the plain just as it began to speak.

So the power did nothing—until, beneath the western district, the stone began to lift too quickly.

The listeners heard it first: a low, crowded note under the foundations, too close and too fast. They carried the warning across the bridges. Lamps blinked awake in distant towers. Doors unlatched. People gathered children, tools, sleeping animals; they crossed to the high ground in the slow, practiced way of those who had done this before.

One bridge had not yet loosened. A handful of people were still on it when the cables began to sing.

Then the song changed.

Not louder. Clearer.

A single tone passed through the bridge, the towers, and the wet stone beneath them. It did not command anyone to hurry. It gave each person time to hear the next footfall, to find the rail, to reach the far side. The bridge lifted by the smallest necessary degree. The stone rose beneath it, split, and settled again.

By morning, the western district had changed shape. A channel ran where a street had been; a stairway ended in open air. But the towers still stood, and everyone who had slept in them was alive. In the silt beside the new channel, the small bright animals had left a trembling line of tracks.

The Veyans did not call this a miracle. They called it a good warning, though none of their instruments had made it.

For a long while afterward, the listeners searched for the tone. They found it in no wire, no foundation, no hidden machine. Sometimes, when the land was about to change, they heard something like it again—a clear space in the noise, a moment in which the next sound could be understood.

At the bare shelf, the power’s attention remained.

It did not decide whether the thing beneath the stone was a district, a creature, or a home. It did not urge the seedlings to grow, or the hollows to open. It kept no hand on the land.

It simply made room for whatever was trying to become.

The people continued to visit in pairs. They continued to leave bowls over the warm hollows and return to see whether they had moved. One day, they found a bowl turned carefully upside down, its rim resting on four small stones.

No one knew who had done it.

They sat nearby until the sky darkened. Then they went home, leaving the bowl where it was.

Above them, Vey’s windows lit one by one—not to guide anyone, exactly, but so that every distant presence might be counted.

And beneath the new stone, something listened back.
