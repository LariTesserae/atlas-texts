The power came without light.

It had no need to descend through the sea, and no body that the water could bear. It was simply present: an attention vast enough to include every shelf, every crack, every pale spore turning slowly in the current.

At first it looked for a voice.

It found none. It looked for eyes, for fear, for a plea hidden in the way the fronds bent beneath the water’s weight. It found only growth: roots binding sediment, leaves opening and closing around the seepage, old matter becoming ground for new life.

The power understood, then, that it had mistaken quiet for absence.

It did not bring the sun down into the plateau. The plants had never asked for it. It did not fill the hollows with animals, or give the fronds faces, or teach the moss to sing. The dark was not a wound merely because the power could imagine light. The roar of the channels was not a cry for rescue. This life did not need to become more like the power in order to matter.

So it listened.

It listened through the long change of currents, through the grinding plates of ice and the slow groaning of the ridges. It learned the difference between a slope resting and a slope beginning to fail; between the ordinary shifting of sediment and a change that would bury a whole field of growth. It learned the plateau’s measures—not days or years, but generations of stalks, the patient making of soil.

Then the power did one thing.

It set a boundary around the plateau, not a wall against water or time, but a promise against heedless harm. The sea could still move. Ice could still grind. Stone could still break when its age came due. Nothing would be kept from changing. But no careless hand from above would tear out the mineral chimneys, poison the seepage, or strip the shelves bare merely because it could.

The old shelters it left alone. It did not raise their fallen stones or summon their makers back from whatever had become of them. Roots were already passing through the openings. The power would not call that a mistake.

For a while—longer than any human life, scarcely a pause to the plateau—the attention remained. It did not command the plants to know it. It did not demand gratitude from a life that had no use for gratitude. It simply held the place in its care without closing its hand around it.

And when its attention moved elsewhere, nothing could have pointed to the moment it had been there.

The fronds went on bending. Spores drifted beneath the overhangs. In the dark, the living surface continued its slow work of becoming stone, and stone its slow work of becoming living surface.

The power had not made the plateau better.

It had understood that it was already whole.
