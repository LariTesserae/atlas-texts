The power did not arrive as a figure in the daylight.

It had no need to announce itself. It was old enough to know that being seen and being known were different things, and wise enough not to mistake a quiet life for a request to be rescued from it.

For a while, it simply attended.

It learned the city’s sounds: the small voices close to the speaker, the hush of water beneath the paving, the change in the channels when rain gathered above. It learned which windows caught the first light, which homes would be emptied first when the pools rose, and which old neighbor always left a bowl of scraps near the outer lane for the fox. It saw the care with which people made room—for water, for animals, for one another.

It saw, too, what the people could not yet see.

Above the eastern terraces, a great shelf of stone had begun to separate from the roof. The crack was high and narrow, hidden behind roots. Nothing in the houses below had changed. The streams ran clear. Newts rested beneath their familiar steps. The children took their accustomed paths.

The power could have held the shelf in place forever. It could have made the roof whole and dry, the channels obedient, every house safe from the water. But a roof that never fell would not be a gift without cost. The cavern was still becoming. The water had its routes. The city’s people had built their lives by learning to live with change—not by pretending change could be forbidden.

So the power did not stop the stone.

It made the hidden crack legible.

For three mornings, a thin thread of water ran down the eastern wall where no water had run before. A bird abandoned its nest in the roots above the terrace. At the foot of the stair, a newt appeared in the open lane and stayed there, quite still, until a child went to fetch an elder.

The people looked. They touched the wall. They listened. They moved their sleeping mats and baskets uphill, and asked their neighbors to do the same. Some complained about the work. Some laughed nervously. No one called it a miracle.

On the fourth day, after the rain came hard, the shelf broke loose.

It struck the terraces with a sound too vast to echo. Stone crushed several low houses and filled the lane with rubble. Water surged through the new opening, carrying broken timber and garden soil down into the basin. No one was killed. A few people were hurt; many lost rooms, tools, or years of careful work. They mourned these losses as losses. The power did not ask them to be grateful.

In the days that followed, it did nothing to make the damage disappear.

Instead, the new stream found a deeper course. It opened a pool where there had been a lane, and left a strip of fresh, dark soil beside it. The people cleared what could be cleared, shared what remained, and began again. Someone planted flowers in a crack between the stones. Someone else argued that the place was too wet for flowers. They planted them anyway.

One child, who had seen the newt in the lane, asked an elder whether the cavern had warned them.

“Perhaps,” the elder said. “Or perhaps we noticed.”

The power was pleased with that answer.

It did not make itself the city’s ruler, nor its guardian in any sense that would excuse the people from caring for one another. It left them their judgment, their customs, their right to be frightened and tired and proud. It did not turn the cavern into a safe place. It helped the people remain capable of meeting the dangers that belonged to it.

Afterward, on certain mornings, a little more light reached the eastern terraces than before. No one could say why. Perhaps the fallen stone had changed the angle of the opening. Perhaps the clouds had shifted. People carried their work into the brightness, as they always did.

And the power stayed near—not above them, but among the things they had learned to notice: a changed current, a bird gone silent, a neighbor’s door still closed when the water began to rise.

It did not ask to be thanked.

When the city looked toward the light, it was enough that they could still see one another.
