The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have changed too much. It would have called green things down through the cracks and drawn the animals toward a world they had not been made to inhabit. Nor did the power speak. The hollow already held enough voices, and none of them needed a command.

It listened.

It heard the bats turning in their sleep, the crickets testing the air, the patient movement of water through stone. It heard the larger animals breathing beside one another without knowing they were being listened to. It heard, too, the long spaces between these sounds: bare galleries, dry stretches, the dark patience of rock.

The power understood that this was not an empty place waiting to be improved. Its spareness was not a failure. The pools, the colonies, the routes between them—these were enough, and their balance belonged to the lives that had made it.

So it did not give the blind fish sight. It did not make the grazers tame or spare every scavenger its hunger. It did not turn the hollow into a sanctuary from death. To do so would have been to mistake kindness for softness, and care for control.

But the power kept watch at the margins.

When the dry weeks ran too long, a thin seam deep in the stone released water—not a flood, only enough to preserve a pool and the lives gathered around it. When a slab loosened above a roost, it fell into an empty chamber instead. No animal knew there had been danger. No creature was made immortal, and no passage was promised safe forever.

These were not gifts the inhabitants could name. They were small mercies folded into the hollow’s own workings: a seep that lasted, a refuge left standing, one route remaining open when another closed.

Then the power withdrew its attention from sight, though not from care.

The bats went on returning. The salamanders waited among the gravel. Somewhere in the dark, a scavenger crossed a slope with careful feet. And the hollow remained what it had been: not rescued from its darkness, but quietly held within it.
