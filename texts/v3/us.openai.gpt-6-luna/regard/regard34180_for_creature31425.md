The power did not mistake the basin for a ruined world.

It did not look at the gray stone and imagine green. It did not see the people crouched in the water and wish them dry, or watch their shelters sink and decide they needed walls that would hold forever. It understood, before it acted, that a thing could be difficult without being broken.

So first it listened.

It listened until it could distinguish the basin’s many voices: the small knock of shells, the hush of water entering a crack, the heavy note of a buried passage filling. It learned the people’s clicks and whistles, and the silences between them. It learned that a child’s cry could be answered by a hand laid flat on stone; that the people did not ask the water to stay, only learned when to follow.

The power could have made itself known in light. Instead, it found a way to speak that would not drown out anything else.

One evening, as the level began its familiar rise, a drop fell from the roof of the old shelter into the worn stone basin. It sounded a little lower than it should have. The people paused. Another drop fell, making the same clear note.

They gathered around the basin. One by one, they touched its rim. The note changed with each hand, until all of them were touching, and then it became a sound none had heard before: quiet, deep, and steady enough to feel through the bones.

They answered it with their own voices.

The power waited for them to stop. Then it sent the note again—not words, but a patient question, shaped from pressure and vibration.

The oldest among them laid a palm against the stone and answered with a sequence of clicks. The power listened, and learned the meaning:

*What are you?*

It replied as best it could:

*One who sees you.*

There was a long pause. The water kept rising. Somewhere outside, wings beat through the mist.

At last the oldest clicked again:

*Will you change the water?*

*No,* said the power.

*Will you keep the stone from wearing away?*

*No.*

The people considered this. They were accustomed to answers that came slowly. The youngest among them pressed both hands to the basin and made a small, questioning sound.

The power understood that question too.

*Will you leave?*

And the power answered:

*I will not take your world from you. I will remain attentive.*

This was not the same as promising that nothing would ever hurt them. Floods would still rise. Shelves would still vanish beneath the water. Some creatures would be lost, and some people would grieve. The power would not turn their lives into a smooth surface without danger or change. It knew that care was not the same as control.

But it made one promise beyond the hearing of the people: if a change came that would end their returning—if the basin’s deep channels collapsed all at once, if some violence from beyond their knowledge threatened to erase them—it would act. Not to preserve every stone, every shelter, every old custom. Only to preserve the possibility that they might continue, in their own way, to meet the world again.

Then the note faded.

The people went back to their tasks. They moved their stores higher, lifted a child onto a broad shoulder, and marked the waterline with a fresh cut in the ledge. The old basin remained. The rain continued. Nothing bright appeared in the sky.

Years later, the cut would soften. The stone basin would wear smoother. The note would be remembered differently by different people, and some would doubt it had happened at all.

The power did not mind.

It had not come to become the center of their history. It had come to see them clearly, and to let them remain themselves.
