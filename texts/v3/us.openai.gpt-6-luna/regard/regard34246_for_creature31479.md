The power does not enter through the slit.

It knows that an entrance is not an invitation merely because it is small. It knows, too, that light and water—gifts elsewhere—could be disasters here. So it does not widen the seam, send rain into the chamber, or fill the hollows with food. It does not make the multitude one mind, or give it a name it has never needed.

It waits.

It listens to the clicks passing through the roof and the floor. It learns the chamber not as a map, but as the animals do: by pressure, temperature, distance, and the faint differences between one crack and the next. It learns where the mineral skin grows, where the grazers feed, where the hunters wait. It learns how little can be changed before the whole crowded life must change with it.

Then it does one thing.

Deep in the stone, where no foot has ever traveled, a hairline fracture begins to creep toward the low arch. It is not yet a danger. It might take a century to reach the roof, or ten thousand years. The power touches the fracture—not to erase it, but to turn it by the width of a thought. The stone settles along an older seam instead, away from the chamber. The roof remains what it was: low, ancient, imperfect. The iron still ticks when the air cools.

Nothing in the pocket knows it has been spared.

That is enough. More than enough.

The power does not prevent the hunters from hunting. It does not keep every small body from being lost in the turning crowd, or promise that the mineral crust will feed them forever. Such promises would belong to the power’s wishes, not to the lives that have taken shape here. It only keeps the chamber from being ended by a pressure none of its inhabitants could sense, a collapse that would make no distinction between predator and prey, shelter and food, the living and the gathered dead.

The multitude goes on.

A pulse passes along the roof. A response crosses the basin. Little feet stream through the gear’s worn opening and vanish into the pipe’s dry throat. The crowd parts around a hunter and closes again behind it. The old iron gives its faint tick.

The power remains near, but not over them. It does not demand gratitude from creatures that have no word for it. It does not ask them to notice. It keeps watch with the patience of stone, and when the age of the chamber changes—when the mineral skin thins, when the air shifts, when one corridor closes and another opens—it does not insist that the old arrangement be preserved.

It guards not this precise shape forever, but the possibility of life continuing in its own fashion.

And in the small, cool dark, nothing seems to have happened at all.
