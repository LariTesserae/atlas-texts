The power did not descend through the water.

It had no need to announce itself. It listened first: to the shoals passing like weather, to the small lives reading the currents, to the long ones sounding the ridges with their deep calls. It listened to the machines, too—their steady pulses, their strain, the faint changes in their metal that no animal had reason to notice yet.

It saw that the plateau was not empty, nor broken, nor waiting to be made beautiful. Its sharp stone was not a wound. Its cold was not a failure. The lives there had found their ways among the ridges and basins, and those ways belonged to them.

So the power changed nothing that did not need changing.

One installation had begun, very slowly, to fail. A seal deep in its pressure station was splitting. In time, something inside would leak into the water; sooner still, the machine’s pulse would falter, confusing the routes of creatures that had grown accustomed to it. The power could have lifted the entire structure from the rock. Instead, it eased the failing parts into stillness, one by one, as gently as a current laying down silt. The leak never came. The pulse softened over many seasons until it was no more than another low note among the sounds of the plateau.

The shells remained in the pipe joints. The shoals still gathered beneath the gantry’s shadow. The long ones still passed under the darkened towers, sometimes calling, sometimes not.

The power did not ask them to understand what had happened. It did not teach the plateau a new song or make its water warm. It did not turn the scattered machines into a city, or the bare stone into a garden. Such gifts might have been gifts to someone else.

It stayed long enough to learn the returning paths: the shoal through the pass, the long ones over the shelf, the tiny lives retreating when a storm-shadow crossed the light. Then it withdrew its attention—not in indifference, but in trust.

And when the next great multitude passed over the high ground, the rock brightened and dimmed beneath their turning. The old machines stood where they had always stood. The water pressed on. The plateau kept its silence, which was never truly silence at all.
