The power did not descend.

It had no need to arrive as a figure, or to make the roof shine with a light brighter than the light already there. It listened first.

It listened to the roots passing beneath the stone, to the slow exchanges of water between one hollow and another, to the groves bending in answer to pressures still far away. It listened to the people, too: their careful steps, their arguments over a crossing, the hush that fell when the whole garden went quiet.

It found no single voice speaking for the country. No throne beneath the roots, no hidden sovereign in the tallest stalk. The living place was many things at once, joined without being one. And the people, small as they were, had not been forgotten by it. They had learned how to live without claiming the country as theirs.

So the power did not take possession of what it had noticed.

It did not stop the storms. The storms belonged to the cavern’s making, and the plants had learned to bend, break, and begin again. It did not make the people immortal, or the water plentiful, or every crossing safe. To remove all risk would have been to make the country a thing other than itself.

Instead, it changed one small relation.

The next time a great air-storm gathered beyond the far ridges, the root-mats felt its pressure as they always had. The gardens began to bow. But before the wind reached them, a low note travelled through the stone—so faint at first that it might have been mistaken for a stalk creaking under strain.

It passed beneath the shelters. It stirred the dark mineral slabs at their thresholds. In the hollow stems, water trembled.

The people heard it.

They had heard many sounds before, but not one like this: a sound with enough time inside it to be useful. They took down loose membranes, moved the children from the ridge shelters, and waited on bare stone. The storm came, and the gardens swept low across the plains. Bridges snapped. Towers folded. No one was spared the weather, but fewer were caught unready.

Afterward, no one agreed on what the note meant. Some said the land had warned them. Some said the stone itself had shifted. A child insisted that the sound had come from everywhere at once.

The power did not correct them.

In the seasons that followed, the note came sometimes before a storm and sometimes before no storm anyone could see. The people learned to treat it as they treated all uncertain things: with attention, not obedience. They began to leave a little more time before crossing a strained bridge. They learned that the gardens, too, sometimes changed their growth after the note passed—not always, and never in a way they could command.

That was the gift: not safety, but notice. Not a voice that told the inhabitants what the country was, but a moment in which they could attend to one another.

The power remained near, though nearness was not a place. It watched the people mend their shelters and the plants grow over the broken paths. It watched a child lay her palm against a root-bridge and feel, through it, the faint after-vibration of distant weather.

And then it did nothing more.

The country was still young. It still had room to become what it would become. The people still had to listen. Somewhere far off, a stalk broke, and its sound crossed the stone.

This time, they heard it together.
