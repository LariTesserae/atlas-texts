The power did not mistake the town for a ruin.

It had seen ruined places before: places whose silence was the silence of what had been taken from them. This silence was different. It had structure. It was inhabited.

So the power did not fill the dry channels with water, or put lamps in the halls, or call down rain. It did not give the creatures voices. It did not open the roofless court to a sky with stars in it. These might have been wonders elsewhere. Here they would have been answers to questions no one had asked.

Instead, it came to the edge of the town and stopped.

It made itself as small as it could—not in strength, but in consequence. The ridges did not break. The light did not change. No new shadow crossed the streets. At the base of the outer wall, one grain of mineral dust grew warm.

The town noticed.

For a long while, nothing happened. Then a pale creature appeared in the nearest court. It paused at the shadow of a lintel, turned its narrow head toward the wall, and vanished.

The dust remained warm.

The power understood that it had been observed, not welcomed. It waited.

At the basin, grains gathered around the rim in a sequence unlike any the town had made before: one, then two, then a long interval, then one again. The pattern did not settle into a message. It was more like a hand held out in the dark—not touching, not withdrawing.

The power could have answered in every language at once. It could have spoken directly into every resident, made itself known beyond doubt. But certainty imposed from above would have been a kind of force. So it answered in the only way it had been offered: by remaining where it was, warm and still.

The town revised itself.

A covered way lengthened by the width of a hand. A doorway appeared in the outer wall—not large, not grand, no wider than the low doors within. Beyond it, the plain remained as it had always been: pale, bare, and unpromising. Nothing beckoned from outside. Nothing compelled a crossing.

The power did not pass through.

It understood the opening as a question, and questions deserve time.

Days—or whatever passed for days there—went by. The dust gathered, lifted, gathered again. A few of the pale creatures inspected the doorway. The dark plants sent their roots nearer to it, tracing the seams in the stone. No one crossed the threshold.

At last the power made one small change. Beyond the wall, where the mineral plain met the ridge, it laid down a line of warmth. Not a road. Not a command. A possibility, extending only as far as the eye could follow. Then it left the line alone.

The town did not take it up. Not then.

The power was not disappointed. It had not come to make the town larger, or more like the worlds it knew. It had come because the town existed, and because what existed deserved to be met without being mistaken for something else.

So it kept its attention there. Not pressing against the walls, not asking to be admitted. A guest, if a guest can be one who waits outside until invited.

And the town kept its opening.

Somewhere in the long, unmeasured quiet, the basin stayed clean. The doorway did not migrate shut. The warm line beyond the ridge faded, then returned, as patient as a thought one is free to ignore.

The residents had not left. They had not been rescued. They had not needed to become anything other than what they were.

But now, at the edge of their world, there was a way to be met.
