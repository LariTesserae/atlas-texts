The power did not arrive as light.

It had no need to make itself known. The hollow was already full of things that could be mistaken for signs: the falling drops, the pump’s deep pulse, the pale film gathering in the seams. So it watched first.

It learned the limits of the place. How warm the water must remain. Which minerals fed the living threads, and which would poison them. How much of the pump’s rhythm the hollow could bear. It learned, too, that the colony gave no answer to being observed—not because it refused, perhaps, but because no one could say what an answer would be for something so unlike themselves.

The power did not ask it to become legible.

It acted where the hollow was in danger of being lost. Far above, a hairline fracture widened under the works. The next season of pumping would have driven it open; a shelf of stone would have come down and crushed the basin. The power held the fracture—not by sealing the earth shut, but by persuading the weight of the foundation to settle elsewhere. The shelf remained. The hollow remained beneath it.

Then it turned its attention to the water. The cut pipe had begun to carry something bitter in its beads, a substance that would have thinned the pale film and left the seams bare. The power did not make the water pure in some abstract sense. It changed the route of the poison upstream, diverting it into a channel where it could be contained. Warm water still found its way into the hollow. The minerals the colony used still came with it. The place kept its small, difficult weather.

Last, the power altered the factory’s rhythm—not silencing it, because silence would have been its own disturbance, and the colony had come to know the pulse as part of its world. The blows grew fractionally softer. The worst shocks passed into the wider foundation, where they could do less harm. To anyone above, the change was barely perceptible: a machine running more smoothly than before.

And then the power waited.

For a long while, nothing visible happened. A drop fell. The basin trembled faintly with the pump. The pale film held to the stone.

Eventually, a worker came through the bolted service door to inspect a damp patch. They noticed that the water, once discolored, ran clear. They noticed the odd little shelf of crust at the basin’s edge, and the green mats in the creases. They called it in. Someone came with a camera, then with a clipboard, then with a plan to drain the hollow and fill it with concrete.

The power did not strike them blind or fill their mouths with prophecy. It let the worker’s boot slip on the wet metal square—not far enough to hurt them, only far enough that they had to catch the wall and look down. For the first time, they saw how much of the floor was living surface, and how little the proposed repair understood about it.

They went back upstairs and asked for another assessment.

That was all the power did.

It did not crown the colony with a mind, or make it speak in a voice humans could admire. It did not turn the hollow into a shrine. It protected the conditions under which the being could continue, and left the being to continue in its own way.

The works went on above. The drops kept falling. In the seams, almost too slowly to see, the pale film kept gathering.
