The power did not descend as a sun.

Sunlight would have been too much. It would have made every leaf turn at once, every hidden root answer before it had time to understand the question. Nor did the power send an animal down the stair, or a voice, or a hand to clear the machinery. It knew that an empty place was not necessarily a lonely one, and that a thing without a face could still have its own way of being whole.

So it waited.

For a long time—longer than the iron had left to rust—the power listened to the Conservatory in the only way the Conservatory could speak: through warmth passing along pipes, through water gathered in stone, through the weight of roots against the floor. It learned the difference between the tank’s ordinary shudder and the shudder that meant a valve was failing. It learned where the green glow thickened, and how the leaves near the stair changed their angle when the air above them changed.

Then, at the edge of the chamber, it did one small thing.

It steadied the failing valve.

Not forever. Not so completely that nothing could ever change. The old machinery remained old; its wheel still turned only a few degrees at a time, and the tank still gave its tired knock. But the next time the valve coughed, it did not seize. Hot water passed through the pipes, and the room received the familiar pulse of warmth. The roots swelled along the iron. The plants continued.

The power left no mark saying *I was here*. It did not make the plants grow faster, or brighter, or in any pattern that could be mistaken for a face. It gave the Conservatory no new need to serve and no new fear to answer. It only kept a small possibility from being lost before the room had any reason to lose it.

Afterward, it sent a little warmth up the stair.

Not a command. Not a lure. Just warmth: steady and harmless, like the breath that already traveled between the dark below and the dark above. It let the warmth fade, then returned it, after a long interval. The power waited through seasons of dripping and drying, through the slow opening of seams in pale stems, through powder settling in cracks.

For years, nothing answered that anyone else could have noticed.

Then one day—or what counted as a day in the Conservatory—the leaves nearest the stair leaned toward the rising warmth. They did not reach. They did not open. They merely turned, a fraction, and held that angle.

The power did not ask them to do it again.

It stayed near enough to keep the old pulse from failing suddenly. It stayed far enough away that the Conservatory could remain itself. And whenever the stair breathed, the power listened, without deciding that the breath belonged to it.
