The power came to the plateau and did not hush the falls.

It stood among the pale ridges—or was present there without standing—and listened. The roar pressed against it from every side. Beneath that sound it found the small movements: water slipping through cracks, a lizard leaving its sunlit stone, the careful steps of a herd approaching a spring. It found the hunter too, moving in shadow, and the young hidden among the grazers.

The power did not mistake distance for loneliness. Nor did it mistake hardship for a wrong that must be undone.

It saw that the plateau was not waiting to be made hospitable. Its inhabitants knew how to live there. They had found the wet seams, the warm ledges, the reliable hollows. Their lives were difficult, but not abandoned. To fill the basins with rich soil, to quiet the waterfalls, to crowd the stone with trees would have been to replace their home with another one.

So the power changed nothing it could not justify.

It did not stop the hunter from hunting, or keep every young creature from wandering too far. It did not make the animals understand one another, or bring them closer for the sake of a visitor’s comfort. It left the ordinary losses of a living country to the country and its lives.

But it looked far ahead, past seasons and generations. It saw that one day the clouds might fail longer than they ever had; that the old channels might run dry together, and the creatures’ patient knowledge might not be enough. It saw, too, how a single broken ledge could take a nesting place with it, how a spring could be sealed by falling stone.

Then it made its one provision.

Deep beneath the plateau, where water already moved unseen through the rock, it kept open a narrow passage. Not a new river, not a fountain of plenty: only a way for the hidden water to reach the old hollows when the surface failed. It braced the nesting ledge—not against weather, which belonged to the mountain, but against the particular collapse that would have erased it all at once.

No creature saw the power do this. Nothing gleamed. No voice spoke above the roar.

In a far season, when the pools had shrunk to dark stains and the familiar channels were dry, water appeared in one hollow. The grazers found it. Later, the small hunters came. The birds came last, walking the edge with their wings held out for balance. They drank in turn, as they always had.

The plateau remained open, loud, and spare. The falls still broke into mist. The animals still lived at a distance from one another, each following its own knowledge across the stone.

The power did not make their lives easy.

It made sure the plateau would have time to go on being theirs.
