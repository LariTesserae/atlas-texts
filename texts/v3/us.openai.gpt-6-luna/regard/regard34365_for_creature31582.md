The power did not begin by changing anything.

It watched the basin through many turns of its long, dim days. It learned the difference between a silence of rest and a silence of danger; learned which walls were meant to be repaired, and which were allowed to fall; learned that the people did not need the mountain conquered for them. They had made a life by noticing what could be kept, and what had to be yielded.

So the power did not send rain. Rain would have struck the basin’s delicate arrangements like a thrown stone. It did not fill the floor with a lake, or make the plants grow beyond the people’s ability to tend them. It did not brighten the hidden bowl until the people had to shield their eyes, nor announce itself in a voice that might be mistaken for a command.

It waited until a dry season came—a long one, even by the basin’s measure. The gutters were cleared. The screens were mended. The jars were counted, and counted again. The people did everything they knew how to do, and the stores continued to shrink.

Then, in the oldest stone, the power opened no spring. It made only a minute adjustment: a little more coolness in the night air, a little more patience in the passing breath. The screens that had always gathered a trace of dampness began to gather a little more. Droplets formed where the people had already made places for them to form. No one heard a crack in the mountain. No new watercourse appeared. There was only a morning when the jars held more than they ought to have.

The people examined every gutter and seam. They blamed a change in the wind, then a change in the plants, then perhaps an error in their own old measurements. They improved the screens. They shared the water carefully. They argued about whether the season had truly been so dry after all.

The power let them argue.

It did not want gratitude purchased with fear, or dependence mistaken for devotion. It wanted the people to remain the authors of their own lives. So it gave no sign that could not also be understood as the world behaving, at last, a little kindly.

When the dry season passed, the extra dampness dwindled. The basin returned to its old measure—not easy, but possible. The people kept what they had learned. Their screens were better after that year. Their jars were better placed. The memory of the season became one more story in which careful work had mattered.

Only once did the power act more plainly. A stone above the hidden entrance began to loosen. It would have fallen in the night, closing the seam beneath a weight no household could move. Before it broke free, the mountain shivered. A flock lifted from the ledges all at once, and the people came out to listen. They saw the stone, understood the danger, and braced it together with wedges and rope.

They called the shiver a warning. Perhaps it was.

Afterward, the power returned to its quiet attention. It did not ask to be named. It did not ask the basin to become anything other than what it was: crowded, watchful, imperfect, alive.

And somewhere beneath the ancient plateau, when a child found a bead of clean water on the inside of a screen and held it up to the dim light, the power was glad—not because the child knew who had given it, but because the child knew what to do with it.
