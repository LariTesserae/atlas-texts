It would not descend like a rescuer.

A rescuer, arriving from above, might see damp rooms, failing machinery, and people living among things that ought to frighten them. It might dry the Orchard, drive out the animals, silence the pumps, and call the result safety. The wise power would understand that this would be a kind of destruction.

So first it would pay attention.

It would learn the difference between the pump that complained and the pump that was warning. It would notice which roots held up the ceiling and which merely used it for purchase; which pools fed the blind fish, and which were growing foul. It would watch the people pass through their rooms and narrow paths, taking only what they needed, leaving meat for the bats, arguing over tools beneath the steady shudder of the engines. It would notice that the Orchard was not one thing, and that the people were not outside it.

For a long while, it would do nothing that could be mistaken for ownership.

Then, one night, the oldest pump would begin to fail.

Not dramatically. There would be no burst pipe, no sudden flood. Only a small wrongness buried in its familiar labor: a pause between two knocks, a tremor where there should have been a smooth pull. The people would hear something amiss, but the room would be loud with water and wings. The pump-keeper would reach for the lever.

The power would not seize their hand. It would not repair the machine.

Instead, it would make the pause audible.

For a few breaths, the Orchard’s many sounds would separate—not vanish, but become distinct. The drip from the ceiling. The scrape of beetles in the wall. The bats stirring above the casing. Beneath them all, the pump’s second knock, thin and late. And, from the deepest room, the answering knock that had sounded for generations.

The pump-keeper would wait.

Someone else would lift the lamp. A child would remember the old instruction about the lower hatch. Together they would find a root-grown seam around the housing, and behind it a valve caught with grit. They would clear it carefully, not knowing whether the root had caused the trouble or kept worse trouble from coming. When the pump settled into its old rhythm, the power would let the other sounds return.

Nothing would speak from the deepest room. Nothing would explain what the pumps were made for, or what moved in the outer tunnels. The people would still have to listen, and mend, and decide how much to take from the green.

That is what the power would offer: not freedom from dependence, but a little more understanding within it. Not command, but time enough to choose.

And it would leave the Orchard as it found it—warm, crowded, alive. The roots would keep their rooms. The people would keep their customs. The great quiet thing in the outer tunnels would remain beyond the lamps, neither driven out nor summoned nearer. If the people noticed that, for a moment, every leaf had turned toward the pump-keeper’s listening face, they might call it a blessing.

Or they might call it nothing at all.

The power would not mind. It had not come to be named.
