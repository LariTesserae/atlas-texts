The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have been too much. It would have filled the courts, thrown hard shadows across the ridge, and made the residents turn from the mountain to look at it. So the power came as attention: complete, patient, and quiet enough to be mistaken for nothing.

For a long time, it changed nothing.

It listened to the held water. It listened to the mountain’s deep, occasional knock. It listened to the residents’ speech: a doorway kept open for the length of a thought; a line of light bending through stone; a pressure eased by the smallest possible degree. It did not mistake slowness for emptiness. It did not mistake solitude for sorrow.

At last, when the mountain knocked, the power answered.

Not with a second knock. That might have seemed an alarm, or a command. Instead, a single point of blue-white light appeared in an empty court. It was no brighter than the others. It did not cast light beyond the circle of stone beneath it. But it was not set into a wall, and it had not been there before.

The residents stopped.

Days passed. The point remained.

One by one, the nearest lights turned toward it—not moving, exactly, but revising their direction. A corridor lengthened. A pressure field loosened around the court. Across the city, figures emerged from passages and distant towers. None came quickly. None came together. They gathered as they always had: at a distance from one another, each facing the new light.

The power did not tell them what it was. It offered them no explanation of the mountain, no answer to the promise they kept. Perhaps it did not know the promise’s first terms. Perhaps those terms had been lost even to the ones who made it.

Instead, it made one small revision to the world.

For the first time, the still water moved.

The change was so slight that no dust cloud stirred. A single suspended grain drifted the width of a hand. It crossed the court and settled on the dark stone.

The residents watched it come to rest.

Then the mountain knocked.

The power did not answer this time. It waited.

For many days, the residents spoke among themselves. The exchange was invisible to any eye that did not know how to look: doors held open, lines of light altered, distances measured and remade. Their discussion moved across the ridge as slowly and precisely as the city’s old speech. No one was hurried. No one was persuaded.

At last, one figure crossed the court. It placed a long, pale hand beneath the solitary light.

The power offered no hand in return. It did not seize the resident, or lift it, or promise that the mountain was safe. It simply let the figure feel what had been there all along: an attention broad enough to include the city, the ridge, the immense dark below, and the patient creature passing between the stones.

You are seen, the gesture meant.

Not watched. Not measured. Seen.

The resident stood there for a long while. Then it turned toward the mountain.

The light remained.

Nothing in the city was released from its vigil. The mountain still waited. The residents still listened. But thereafter, when the deep knock came, it was no longer the only thing in the dark that answered.

And sometimes, in the empty court, the suspended dust moved.
