The power did not descend.

There was no column of fire above the plateau, no new star fixed over its highest ridge. The settlements did not wake to find their machines singing in unison, or water standing in the road. The power had learned, by attending, that an arrival could be a kind of harm. Here, things had taken a long time to become what they were. Even kindness had to consider what it might disturb.

So it watched.

It watched the herds take the warm seams and leave them. It watched salt-sifters graze the margins of a causeway while cargo passed overhead. It watched people repair the same engines their grandparents had repaired, and teach their children to hear the difference between an ordinary tremor and a warning.

And, in a maintenance hall beside the eastern pass, it watched Mara listen.

Mara was not remarkable by the standards of her settlement. She kept the rail bearings clean, wrote down changes in the engine’s pitch, and carried a small tin of mineral paste in the pocket of her coat. She had a habit of laying one hand against a wall before she entered a machine room. The others said she was listening for faults. She said she was listening for what had changed.

One shift, she felt a tremor that did not belong to the rail.

It came through the floor in three slow pulses, stopped, and came again. Mara stood with her palm against the stone. The engine continued its steady turning. The cargo line was empty. Beyond the hall, the wind brushed the grit from the eastern ridge.

She went out to the old route boards and found the record of a great animal’s passage: impressions crossing the ridge, then doubling back toward the pass. The marks were several days old. Beneath the newest, someone had noted that the animal’s usual route was blocked by a recent fall of quarry stone.

Mara reported it. The route was already scheduled for inspection, but not until the next day. A train was due to cross the causeway before then.

She asked them to stop it.

There was argument. A delayed cargo meant a delayed furnace, and a delayed furnace meant trouble for more than one household. Mara showed them the tremor pattern. It was not proof. Still, the oldest of the rail workers listened to the floor, then listened again.

The train waited.

They went out together with lamps and sounding rods. Past the end of the causeway, where the ground sloped toward the blocked pass, they found the animal beneath a shelf of loose stone. It had wedged itself in a narrow fissure while trying to turn. One forelimb was trapped. Its great back rose and fell so slowly that at first the people mistook the movement for settling rock.

No one had a plan for such a thing. They had plans for roads, machines, and their own bodies. They had no plan for freeing a creature larger than a workshop without frightening it into breaking itself.

Mara knelt at a distance and placed her hand on the stone.

Three slow pulses. A pause. Three again.

She did not know why she did it. Perhaps because she had felt that rhythm in the floor. Perhaps because anything else seemed too loud.

After a while, the animal answered—not with a sound, but with a change in the pressure of the stone. Those present felt it through their boots. The oldest worker signaled for the lamps to be shaded. They moved the smallest pieces by hand, one at a time, and left the largest where they were. When the animal could draw its limb free, it remained still for a long while. Then it turned, carefully, and went down the pass.

It did not thank them. It did not need to.

Later, Mara found a narrow line of impressions beside the old quarry road, leading toward a ridge the people had not thought to include in their migration records. She added it to the route boards. Other records changed with it. The next time a fall blocked the eastern pass, the trains waited without argument. A new crossing was cut around the ridge, leaving the old route open.

The power did not claim the event. It did not tell Mara what she had done, or turn her into a prophet. It gave no one a commandment. If there was a blessing in it, it was only this: that a faint signal, already present in the world, reached someone prepared to notice it.

For a long time afterward, the plateau remained as it had been. The air stayed dry. The roads gathered grit at their edges. The factories worked, the herds passed, and the cities kept their careful distances. Nothing became easy.

But there was one more route on the boards. One more pause built into the schedule. One more thing the people knew to listen for beneath their feet.

The power considered this a good beginning. Then it returned to watching—not because nothing more could be done, but because, for now, the lives of the plateau were answering one another.
