The power noticed them first by the way they made room.

Not the town’s walls, which were temporary, nor its roofs, which leaked, nor even the eastern gap, which every sensible builder would have sealed. It noticed the hand that held a ladder steady for a neighbor who had not spoken to its owner in three days. It noticed the children listening to the stones. It noticed how the dead were remembered in the names of drains and repaired boards.

Then it noticed what moved beneath them.

The thing under the town was old, though not in years. It had no face to lift toward the sky, no voice except the note passing through the channels. It knew the pressure of gravel, the weight of water, the slow yielding of silt. It had been moving before there were walls. It was not trying to drown the people. It was trying to go somewhere, and the town had grown—without knowing—across one of its passages.

The power could have lifted the whole settlement onto firm ground. It could have made the water obedient, the banks hard, the channels still. It could have given the people a shore that stayed a shore.

It did none of these things.

Instead, on a night when the rain had stopped and everyone expected the gutters to fall quiet, the hollow note sounded beneath the houses. It came once, then again: not louder, but clearer, as if the ground had found a way to speak without shaking.

A child woke and put an ear to the floor.

“It’s not coming through the storehouse,” she whispered. “It’s going around.”

In the morning, a narrow run of clear water showed itself beyond the southern wall. It threaded through a patch of gravel where no run had been before, crossed a hollow, and vanished among the reeds. The town watched it all day. By evening it was gone.

The next morning, it returned.

No one called it a miracle. They took rods and baskets and followed its course. They found that the bank there was beginning to sink, but slowly, and that a channel could be cut without undermining the nearest houses. They argued over the work. They argued about whose roof would need to be moved, and whose stored boards would be used. Then they began.

The work took many days. The water did not hurry them. When a wall slumped, it slumped outward, away from the lane. When someone drove a stake too deep, the stream clouded and stopped until they pulled it free. The people learned to make the channel broader than they thought necessary. They learned to leave stones uncemented where the ground might need to shift.

The power stayed near—not as a hand upon their shoulders, but as attention. When the town guessed wrongly, it did not punish them. The water simply failed to go where they had sent it. When they listened, it showed them another way.

At last the new channel joined the deeper southern run. For a time, the eastern gap carried nothing at all.

People went to look at it anyway.

Then, one dusk, a thin thread of water crossed the lane and disappeared beneath the storehouse. On the step beside it lay a child’s little clay town, washed clean of mud. Its walls were broken, but a careful channel had been scratched through the middle.

The child picked it up and smiled.

After that, they did not say the ground was safe. They did not say the thing beneath them was grateful, or that the power had promised to protect them. They said only that the water had found another way, and that they must keep it open.

In time, the town moved some houses back from the eastern wall. The storehouse shifted onto higher ground. The old lanes changed shape. The dead remained where they were, on the gravel ridge, their names still spoken over work.

And beneath the town, the resident moved on—not away, but through. Its note passed under the houses now and then, soft as breath through a reed. The people would pause when they heard it. Someone would go to the gap. Someone else would check the southern channel.

The power did not make the town permanent.

It helped the town become a better neighbor.
