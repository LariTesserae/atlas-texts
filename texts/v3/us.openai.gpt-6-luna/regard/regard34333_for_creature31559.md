The power does not descend into the district.

It does not stop the walls from shifting, or press the buildings into a shape that would be easier to understand. It sees that the city is not failing to become something else. It is becoming itself.

For a long while, it does nothing anyone could point to. It attends. Where the inhabitants gather at the lowest square, the stone holds still—not because it has been commanded to, but because someone has noticed how carefully they listen to it. The streets remain reachable for a little longer. A seam closes gently beneath a tired foot.

Then the power offers one thing.

At the edge of the square, a doorway appears in a wall that has never had a door. It is plain, narrow, and made of the same dark material as the surrounding stone. There is no light behind it, no voice calling from within. The inhabitants leave it alone for several days. They pass it at a distance. One places a bead on the ground before it; another draws a line of salt across its threshold.

The power waits.

At last, one of them opens the door.

Beyond it is a small room. Its walls are dry. The air is warm enough to breathe without seeing it. There is a bench, a basin, and a window looking onto no particular place. Nothing in the room insists on being named. The inhabitant stays only a little while, then returns to the square.

The door is still there the next day.

It does not lead to the same room twice. Sometimes there is a place to sleep; sometimes a table, or a bare floor, or only the quiet. But the doorway always returns to the square, and anyone who enters can leave by the way they came. No one is summoned. No one is kept.

The inhabitants begin using it, cautiously. A length of cord is left beside the door. A smooth stone is placed on the bench. One day, someone sleeps there through the night. In the morning, the room is still warm.

The district continues to rise, soften, and revise its distances. Its pools still keep strange reflections. The white stalks still tremble when nobody passes. The power does not make the city safe by making it still. It gives the inhabitants a place from which they can meet its changes without having to meet them alone.

And because it is wise, it gives no sign that the room is a gift.

It simply leaves the door open.
