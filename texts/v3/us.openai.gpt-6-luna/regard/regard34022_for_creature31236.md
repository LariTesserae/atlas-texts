The power did not descend through the shafts.

It did not drain the rooms, straighten the crooked channels, or wash the old brick clean. It did not mistake dampness for misery, or age for failure. It listened first: to the small adjustments of water, to the knocks traveling along the arches, to the pauses that meant *wait* and the quickened taps that meant *come now*. It listened long enough to understand that this was not a place waiting to be saved. It was a place continually saving itself.

So it came quietly.

For three nights, the water in one basin held a reflection of the stars, though the shaft above it was roofed over and no starlight could reach below. The inhabitants noticed. They watched the reflection shift when someone moved, and stay still when no one did. On the fourth night, an elder touched the water with two fingers.

A warmth passed through the basin—not heat, but the feeling of being answered.

The elder tapped once against the rim. The sound traveled through the brick and returned, clear and unbroken.

They gathered in the chamber. No one bowed. A child asked whether the water had learned a new trick. No one knew.

The power waited.

At last the oldest among them placed a cup on the ledge—not an offering, exactly, but an invitation. The power took the shape of a stranger small enough to sit beneath the low arch. It accepted the cup and drank. It did not praise the water or ask who had made the rooms. It asked, in a voice that sounded like a note carried through a pipe:

“What would you have kept, if you could?”

They spoke for a long time. Not all at once. Some wanted the dry season shortened; others feared what a fuller cistern might do to the frogs. One wanted a passage opened to the air. Another wanted no new passage at all. A child asked that the swifts keep nesting in the vents. Someone asked for nothing, because nothing seemed a dangerous thing to ask of a power.

The power heard them without gathering their wishes into a single command.

In the end, the elder laid a palm against the wall and said, “When the water gathers behind the old eastern wall, we hear it too late.”

The power touched the brick beside their hand.

It did not strengthen the wall or send the water away. It gave the wall a voice: a faint, low note that began days before the pressure became dangerous. Not a warning imposed from above, but a new sound woven into the sounds they already knew. They tested it through the seasons. They learned its pitch, its changes, the difference between a warning and an ordinary settling of the old masonry. Then they made their own signs to answer it—taps passed from room to room, grates lifted in the right order, children led to higher ledges.

When the wall finally needed repair, they knew in time.

The power did not stay to supervise. It did not ask to be remembered, and it left no shrine behind. The basin returned to reflecting only the dim ceiling and the trembling light of lamps. The channels went on carrying water where they would. Frogs still had to be relocated from the sleeping chambers. A few tiles still came loose.

But sometimes, when a stranger paused at the entrance and waited to be noticed, a drop of water would fall into the nearest cup with a sound unusually clear.

The inhabitants took this not as a command, nor a promise that nothing bad would ever happen.

They understood it as attention.

And because the power had listened before it acted, they trusted that it would listen again.
