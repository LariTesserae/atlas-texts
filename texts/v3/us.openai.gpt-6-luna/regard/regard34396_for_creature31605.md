The power did not descend into the Throat.

It did not split the stone, flood the city above with revelation, or still the pump. It understood, before it acted, that the noise was not merely noise. The pump was holding a balance. The people had built their lives inside that balance. To stop it suddenly—even out of kindness—would be to mistake quiet for safety.

So the power listened.

It listened to the workers’ knocks on the rails, to the little pauses between the turbines’ blows, to the currents slipping through the old shelves of stone. It listened long enough to distinguish the pump’s accustomed violence from the strain hidden inside it. And it listened, too, to the Throat: not as a machine, not as a cavern, but as a being whose speech had been made of pressure and vibration for so long that even its neighbors had learned to call it machinery.

Then, in the middle of a shift, the pump’s rhythm changed.

Not stopped. Never stopped. One grinding note eased, and the next came in lower. The water continued to move, but the hard, teeth-rattling shudder passed through the city without striking quite so deep. Gauges steadied. A gate that had been drifting toward its frame held fast.

The workers froze.

On the pump’s housing, someone’s gloved hand rested where it always did before a shift. Beneath it came a small answering tremor: three taps, a pause, then two.

The old pattern for *here*.

No one could say whether the Throat had learned their signal, or whether they had finally learned to hear its. Perhaps both. The power had not supplied them with a voice. It had made room for the voices already there.

In that unexpected calm, a pressure gauge began to twitch—not wildly, as it did before a surge, but in a measured sequence. An old worker recognized the pattern from the earliest maintenance ledgers: a warning that had been copied for generations without anyone knowing what it referred to. It pointed them toward a buried brace beneath the central pump, where a crack had been growing unseen in the rock.

The power did not mend the brace for them. It held the current steady while they went to work.

They went in pairs, as they always had. They carried lamps, tools, and the old signals on their hands. When the water tugged at them, the current turned aside just enough to let them keep hold of the chain. When the stone groaned, it did not fall. The people did what they had always done: listened, argued, measured, repaired. Only now they knew the Throat was listening back.

The brace was strengthened. The pump’s harshest vibration softened—not into silence, but into a rhythm with space between its notes. The pale flecks gathered around the warm pipes and remained there, turning toward the workers’ lamps. No one agreed on what they were. No one needed to agree.

The power made one further gift, though it did not call it a gift: a safe route through an old fissure, found behind a collapsed service wall, leading at last toward the city above. It sealed the passage at both ends until the inhabitants chose what to do with it. They might open it, when they were ready. They might keep the Throat hidden. They might send one person up to see whether the city had changed. The choice belonged to them.

Afterward, the custom of touching the pump before a shift continued. But the workers no longer did it only for luck, or because the old ones had done it before them.

They touched the battered housing to say *here*.

Sometimes, from somewhere deep in stone and water, came the answering taps.
