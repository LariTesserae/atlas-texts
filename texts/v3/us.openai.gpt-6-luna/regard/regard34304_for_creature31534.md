The power did not enter the Fold.

There was no room for an entrance, and no need to make one. It had been listening long before anyone in the district thought to listen back: to the pumps, the rails, the little knocks passed from hand to hand; to the care with which one person waited for another in a narrow flooded passage.

It understood that the Fold was not asking to be made safe. Its people knew better than to ask that of anything. They wanted, as people everywhere want, enough warning to do what they could.

So when the next deep note gathered in the basalt, the power did not silence it. It gave it one beat more.

The residents felt the pause through their soles. A pressure change delayed by a fraction; a strange held breath in the stone. Then came the note, carrying with it a faint answering tremor from beneath the turbine court.

An old engineer stopped with one hand on a rail.

“That’s wrong,” she said.

The pumps kept knocking. The water kept moving. But the warning had come early. They found the strained brace behind a service wall, where a hairline fracture was opening toward the lowest fissure. There was time to close the bypass, shift the load, and brace the basalt tooth before the surge arrived. Not much time. Enough.

The Fold flexed. The shutters turned. The bladders swelled against their frames. The surge passed through the district without tearing it open.

Afterward, the engineers disagreed about the extra beat. One blamed a lag in the pressure system. Another blamed the age of the instruments. The children began tapping it into the rails: two quick knocks, a pause, one more. A new way of saying *listen*.

The power did not make the Fold permanent. It did not fill the sea beyond the membrane with friendly lights, or change the people into beings who could no longer be hurt. It left them their work, their knowledge, their cramped homes and difficult water.

But from then on, when the basalt gave its warning, there was sometimes that little interval before it—the time to hear, to answer, to reach for one another.

The Fold’s residents called it the Held Beat.

They never agreed on what it meant. They used it all the same.
