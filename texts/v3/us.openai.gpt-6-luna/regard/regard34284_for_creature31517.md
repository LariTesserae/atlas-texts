The power did not descend upon the highland.

It had no need to announce itself. The place was already full of voices, and a wise presence would not mistake quiet for peace. It listened first: to the frogs rising after rain, to the hidden water moving under stone, to the small claws in the walls; to the old terraces shifting by fractions beneath roots and weather.

It saw that the highland was not waiting to be saved. It was alive, and changing. A roof fell in one season; a vine held the stones for another. A nest was lost, and new birds returned to the ledge. The power understood that to mend every crack would be to forbid the place its own future.

So it did not make the highland whole. It made the difference between a harmless fall and a deadly one.

When a retaining wall began to give way above a cistern court, its stones settled into one another instead of breaking loose. The pressure eased. Roots found purchase in the new seams. The next rain ran through the old channels, and the cistern filled without overflowing. No creature witnessed a miracle. A frog laid its eggs in the pool. A goat crossed the terrace. A bird returned to the ledge with grass in its beak.

Elsewhere, where a passage had become so choked with silt that the water could no longer reach a bed of ferns, the power did not clear it by force. It turned one storm by a little, and then another. Water found a neglected spillway, washed away what could be washed, and left what had settled there long enough to become soil. The ferns grew back. Beetles came. The birds followed.

It gave nothing the whole of the place. No creature was made ruler. The bats still took insects; the dogs still slept across the stairs; the birds still quarreled over their hollows. The power did not abolish hunger, weather, or the slow taking-back of stone by root and rain. It did not bring back the builders or command the animals to understand what had been done for them.

It offered, instead, a kind of patience.

A terrace that could endure one more wet season. A spring that did not fail in the driest month. A loosened stone that rolled harmlessly into an empty court rather than onto a nest. Small mercies, almost indistinguishable from good fortune, woven into the ordinary chances of the highland.

And because it was wise, the power did not preserve the place exactly as it found it. It let moss cover the carvings. It let trees take root where roofs had opened to the sky. It let the upper paths grow more difficult, and the lower courts become greener, and some old rooms vanish beneath leaves. What it protected was not the shape of every wall, but the possibility of life continuing through change.

Once, at midday, the darkness in a covered passage softened. For a few breaths, the light reached farther than it usually did, touching a damp stone, a lizard’s back, the folded wings of a resting bat. Then the clouds moved, or the mountain turned, and the passage was dim again.

Nothing stopped. Nothing had to.

The highland kept receiving rain. Water moved beneath the floors. Wings passed through the dark. And where, year by year, the place came close to losing too much at once, the power remained attentive—not as a hand holding the world still, but as a hand beneath it, ready to catch what did not yet need to fall.
