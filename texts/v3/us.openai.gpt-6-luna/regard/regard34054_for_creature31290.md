The power came without thunder.

It had no need of an entrance. The country was already open to it: every ridge, every seam of mineral in the stone, every pale creature moving beneath the mats. It perceived the wind passing over the uplands and the slower exchanges beneath them—the growth taking hold, the growth giving way, the small lives feeding the larger ones. Nothing was hidden from it. Nothing asked to be found.

For a long while, it did nothing.

It did not call rain from an empty sky. Water would not have been a gift here. It did not make the fields spread farther, or gather the animals into abundance, or warm the cold slopes. It did not mistake sparseness for sorrow, or silence for a plea.

At last it found the grazer: standing alone on a shelf of stone, its broad feet planted among the plum-colored mats. The animal had lived through many turns of the wind. Dust had dulled its coat; its back sloped more sharply than it once had. It lowered its head to feed, then paused, listening—or perhaps simply resting.

The power could have put words into its mind. It could have offered a longer life, an easier season, a companion waiting over the next ridge. But a gift is not kind merely because it can be given. The grazer had not asked. It was not broken. Its life belonged to a pattern the power did not need to correct.

So the power made no promise.

Instead, for one brief interval, it let the grazer feel the country as it was: not as a vastness to endure, nor as a store of food, nor as the ground beneath its feet, but as a whole living presence. The warm stone under the mats. The hidden movements in their close weave. The wind crossing ridges beyond sight, carrying the faint traces of other lives. The immense, patient land—not watching over the grazer, exactly, but containing it without forgetting it.

The grazer lifted its head. It turned toward the wind and stood there, still as one of the old pillars.

Then the moment passed. The world did not brighten. No voice spoke. The animal lowered its head again and took another mouthful of growth.

The power’s one lasting act was smaller, and harder to see. It left the country free to continue in its own way. Not untouched—nothing living is untouched—but unclaimed. If some distant calamity came to shatter the balance beyond what the land could bear, the power would answer then. Until then, it would not mistake its ability to change the place for a reason to do so.

The wind moved on across the fields. The grazer moved with it, slowly, toward the next ridge.

And the power, having understood that this was not an unfinished world, let it be.
