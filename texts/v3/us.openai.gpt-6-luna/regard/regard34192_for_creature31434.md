The power did not descend.

It had no need to make an entrance. It was already attending.

For a long while it did nothing at all. It listened to the pumps, the trickle in the channel, the cables tightening and the old stone shifting around its buried seams. It listened to the residents too: their footfalls, their careful silences, the small sounds of cups set down behind sealed doors. It did not mistake the empty avenues for abandonment, or the machinery for a voice asking to be silenced.

It understood that the district was not waiting to be rescued. It was working.

So the power did not fill the streets with light. It did not open every sealed door or call the people out of their rooms. It did not make the old systems kneel to a newer one. Instead, it found a weakness deep in the foundations: a pressure chamber whose slow failure had been passing from one mechanism to another for longer than anyone’s records could reach. The district had been compensating for it, as it compensated for so much. But one day the strain would have become too great.

The power eased the strain.

Not with a flash, or a noise. One night, the pump missed a beat—and then, for the first time in years, continued without the faint shudder that followed. A corridor that had begun to warm dangerously cooled to its usual temperature. The water in the channel ran clear for the length of a person’s hand before darkening again.

In the morning, someone noted the change in a notebook.

No one knew whom to thank. The district did not say.

Then, near the far edge, where the paving stopped and the old stone leaned close, a door appeared in a wall that had been unbroken for generations. It had no handle, only a shallow hollow at the height of a hand. Beyond it lay a stairway rising through the rock. The air coming down the stairs smelled of rain and leaves and, far above, the city.

The door did not stand open. It did not beckon. When a child touched the hollow, it warmed beneath her palm. When she drew away, it cooled.

The residents came to look. Some stood there for a long time. Others went home. The oldest among them wrote down the day and left a blank space after it, as if the event had not yet finished happening.

The power left the stairway where it was. It made no one climb it. It made no one stay.

In the basin, that evening, the reflected lights trembled as they always had. Then the pump engaged behind the walls. The little skyline broke apart—and, as the water settled, something else appeared above it: not a face, nor a symbol, but a calm brightness, distant and unafraid.

For one breath, the district reflected the surface sky.

Then the ordinary lights returned. The basin held its small underground city again, and someone—no one admitted who—cleared the grit from its rim.
