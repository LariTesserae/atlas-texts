The power would not make the sun stay out.

It would not fill the ravines, straighten the old steps, or turn the cloud aside so that the country could be seen all at once. It would understand that the mist was not an obstacle placed there by mistake. The people had learned to live within its weather; the mountain, if the mountain was a being, had learned to breathe through it.

So the power would begin by paying attention.

It would notice the iron gutter failing behind the upper pump house, the hairline crack darkening beneath a row of homes, the child who had heard the drain change its note but had not yet found the words to explain why. It would not repair these things in secret. It would give the child one more clear sound to follow, and give the people time to hear it.

A wheel would turn half a minute sooner than expected. Someone would glance toward the channel. The blockage would be found before the water could force its way through the old wall.

The people might call it luck. The power would not correct them.

At the stairway, it would do less still. It would not reveal where the steps ended. It would not forbid anyone from descending. But when a stone loosened under a traveler’s foot, it would shift just enough to catch against the next one. When cloud thickened over a landing, one of the little lamps would hold its flame. Those who chose to turn back would find the way home. Those who chose to continue would remain free to do so.

And sometimes—never on command, never for every traveler—a person would come back up from below carrying something useful: a warning about a rising shelf, a new route around a fall, a name for a stream that had been nameless. The power would not make every journey safe. It would make care matter.

As for the presence moving through the ferns, the power would not drag it into the open to prove that it existed. It would leave the fern-paths untrampled, the openings in the rock unobstructed, and the old residents’ cautions intact. If that presence wished to be known, it could choose its own hour and shape.

The mountain would feel the attention, perhaps, as a gentleness in the pressure of the deep water. The people would feel it as a moment in which the familiar sounds of home came through clearly: the wheel beginning, the cable under its proper load, water threading safely down the steps.

Nothing would be explained.

For a long time, the only sign would be that the lamps kept burning—and that, when the water climbed the stair, the people had time to watch it go.
