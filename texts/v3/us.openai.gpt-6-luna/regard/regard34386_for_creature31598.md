The power did not descend.

It had no need to announce itself, and no wish to make the hollow into a shrine. It listened first: to the pump’s patient half-turn, to the water passing through stone, to the small noises by which the residents knew when to wait. It listened for a long time before deciding that nothing here was asking to be rescued.

The place was not safe, exactly. The city above it shifted. Rust thinned the walkways. One winter, a foundation cracked in the old quarter, and the crack ran down through the brick vaults toward the central basin. The power might have stopped the city from settling at all. Instead, it changed the course of the crack by the width of a handspan. A mason found it in the morning and called it luck. He repaired the vault. The basin did not lose a drop.

It did not mend the pipes that sweated clear water.

It did not open the eastern door.

It did not summon the thing that lived in the hollow to come forward and explain itself. The power understood that an unseen presence might be unseen by choice, or by nature, or because being known was not the same as being loved.

So it offered no name. It made no bargain. It simply kept watch with a care so light the residents could mistake it for the way the world had always been.

Once, when a lift failed between the district and the streets above, the tea-stall woman was alone below with a kettle going cold. The lamps flickered three times. She set down her cup and waited, as she had been taught. In the basin, the fish gathered beneath the central bridge. The water rose, but only by the width of a finger. Somewhere in the dark, a sluice turned. When the lift began working again, it carried down a box of spare parts that no one had ordered and no one could explain.

The pump tender called it an administrative error. The tea-stall woman put the parts away.

After that, some small things became easier. A failing lamp lasted until someone could replace it. A tool dropped through a grate was more likely to turn up on a dry ledge. When workmen came from the city with plans to drain the basin, they found the old records missing from their file, and the new plans sent them elsewhere. No one was harmed. No one was told they had been saved.

The power did not make the hollow eternal. It knew that even stone changes, and that care is not the same as keeping everything unchanged. It only made sure that change would have to arrive slowly enough to be met: with warning in the water marks, with time to mend a bridge, with a door left open for whatever needed to pass.

And it noticed the other resident.

Not as a mystery to solve. Not as a creature to bless or a spirit to command. It noticed it as one notices a neighbor in a darkened house: by the small signs of a life going on. A door that had been closed and was now open. A missing wrench returned. The fish holding beneath a lamp until it shone again.

Once, beneath the eastern passage, the power let a single note pass through the stone—not a voice, but a sound like water touching the rim of a glass. The footsteps on the other side stopped.

For a while, neither moved.

Then the steps continued, not following, not fleeing, simply going on.

That was enough.

In the city above, the streets kept their weight. Below them, the pumps turned, the lamps clouded, the bats slept in their ordered knots. The residents continued to leave open any door they found open. They did not know that anything had been done for them.

They only knew the place still held.

And, somewhere in the patience of its water and stone, something kind had learned how not to interfere.
