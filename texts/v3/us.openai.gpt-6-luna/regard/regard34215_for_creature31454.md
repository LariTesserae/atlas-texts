The power did not descend.

It had no need to cross the sky as a body, or arrive in a blaze that would make every creature flee. Its attention settled first on the plateau’s slowest motions: the minute widening of a crack, the resin gathering at a stem, the warmth held in the stone after the light had shifted. It noticed the people among these things, and noticed that they were already noticing one another.

For a long while, it did nothing.

Then, beneath the oldest courtyard, the rock gave a single soft tremor.

Every inhabitant who felt it stopped. Hands came away from walls. Grazers lifted their narrow heads. A child beneath a leaf-canopy placed both palms on the ground.

The tremor came again—not a warning, nor a command. A question, offered in the only language the plateau could carry: *May I be heard?*

The oldest among them went to the courtyard floor and lay down with one cheek against the stone. Others gathered close, but not so close as to crowd. They waited until the faint vibration returned. Then the elder answered with three careful taps of the fingers.

*We hear you.*

The power let them feel something of itself: not its full vastness, which might have overwhelmed their senses, but a small, softened impression of its regard. It was like standing beside a warmth that did not consume. Like being seen without being measured.

It showed them, gently, that it could change things. It could smooth the dangerous rim, fill the hollows with water, strengthen every wall against settling. It could make the plateau endure unchanged for as long as they wished.

The people listened to these possibilities. They felt the weight of them pass through the stone.

Then the elder placed one palm on the courtyard floor and one on the wall. A child touched the elder’s shoulder plates. Together they gave the answer: *Do not make us safe from being alive.*

They asked for no spring. They did not want the rim made permanent, or the cracks sealed before the plants could find them. The plateau’s changes were not merely troubles to be removed. They were how the place spoke, and how the people had learned to belong to it.

Still, they asked for one gift.

When the stone began to change faster than anyone could sense—when a shift threatened to become a collapse before there was time to move the young, the old, the nests, the gathered seeds—they wanted to know.

The power understood. It made no promise to prevent every loss, nor to hold the plateau beyond its nature. It promised only a clear warning: a deepening warmth beneath the hand, a vibration too steady to mistake for ordinary settling. Not an order. A chance.

After that, the power’s attention remained, but it did not press itself upon them. It watched from far beyond the thin air, and sometimes from close beneath a palm laid on the stone. The inhabitants did not build a shrine. They left a little space in the oldest courtyard unobstructed, because empty space, too, could be a way of making room.

The plateau continued its slow work. Walls leaned. Plants found new cracks. Small black fragments fell from the rim and were gathered into banks. The people listened as they had always listened.

Only now, when the stone spoke in a voice too urgent to belong to ordinary change, the warmth beneath their hands would answer.

And the power, having been welcomed no farther than that, stayed no nearer.
