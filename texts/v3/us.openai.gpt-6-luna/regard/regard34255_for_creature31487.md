The power did not descend through the light shafts.

It did not arrive as a voice, or a figure, or a brightness greater than the lamps. It came to know the hollow by attending to it: the slow pressure of stone against stone; the minute draw of roots toward warmth; the schedules of freight cars; the routes of the pale animals; the hands laid against walls to judge their strength.

It learned the tapping.

The people had never agreed what the tapping meant. The power did not agree for them. It listened long enough to hear that some patterns belonged to small animals, some to settling fibers, and some to the stone itself, changing under load. A few were something else—not words, exactly, but the repeated answer of one part of the city to another.

Far beneath a young district, a seam was opening. It had opened slowly for years. Now the rock on either side had begun to shift, and a factory stood above it, its warm channels feeding a grove where children liked to climb. The people would notice soon. They might even have time to brace the place. But the ceiling above the seam would fail first.

The power could have sealed the hollow in an instant.

It did not.

Instead, at dusk—though the city had no dusk—the walls began to tap. Not loudly. Not with urgency enough to cause panic. A pattern passed through the rootwork, from one support to the next, and through the factory housings into the floor. It reached a worker who had paused with one hand against a beam.

She called the others.

They listened. They marked the rhythm on a panel. They followed it to the young district, where the leaves had closed despite the lamps and the animals had abandoned their nests. Then they felt the tremor underfoot.

The power held the seam.

Not closed; held. The stone’s slow movement stopped, as if the hollow had drawn a breath and was waiting. The people had hours. They moved the children, the animals they could coax, and the living supports they could transplant. They unloaded the factory. They made mistakes, lost tools, argued about which bridge to cut first. All the while, the stone remained still—not healed, not conquered, only given time.

At last the district was empty. The people had braced what they could and led new roots toward the fracture. When they were ready, the pressure returned gradually. The seam shifted, but not catastrophically. Stone settled against fiber. A section of old factory wall came down. Nothing living was beneath it.

For a long while afterward, the city was quiet.

The people inspected the damage. They repaired the rail. They argued about whether the warning had come from the plants, the walls, the animals, or some combination of all three. The power offered no explanation. It did not mark the place as sacred, or ask to be thanked. It did not make the tapping clearer, because clarity imposed from outside would not necessarily be truth.

It left the people with what they had already begun to make: a way to listen together.

At the repaired district’s edge, someone set a small tool against a sound wall and tapped once.

For a time, there was no answer.

Then, far below the new roots, the stone gave one faint, steady tap.

The people went on with their day. But from then on, when a wall took breath, more of them stopped to listen.
