The place begins as a silence so heavy it feels structural, a vastness held at an altitude where the air is thin enough to taste like metal but warm enough to carry the scent of hot dust and ozone. This is the High Desiccation, a continental plateau raised miles above any remembered sea level, a world that has been dry for geological epochs. There is no water here, not even in memory; the chemistry of this realm is silicate and salt, bound by heat and pressure rather than moisture. The warmth is constant, a pervasive, dry radiation that does not fluctuate with day or night but pulses with the slow respiration of the planet’s own crust. It is a temperature of preservation, not decay.

The terrain is a study in established erosion, though not by wind or rain. Time itself has acted as the abrasive, wearing the extreme elevation into smooth, undulating ridges of vitrified rock and pale, compacted ash. These formations are vast, stretching beyond the curvature of sight, creating a landscape that generates its own weather systems—thermal tides that ripple across the basin like invisible oceans. The scale is disorienting; without the reference points of trees or rivers, distance collapses. A ridge that appears close may be fifty miles away, its apparent proximity a trick of the sparse atmosphere and the unbroken, normal light that falls evenly from a sky bleached to a luminous pearl-grey.

Scattered across this immense, warm expanse is the city, or what passes for one. It is not built *on* the landscape but extruded *from* it, an urban density achieved through industrial geology. The structures are towering, fluted spires and low, sprawling manufactories formed from sintered mineral and reconstituted glass, their surfaces matte and pitted by ages of thermal cycling. They are organized, layered, and purposeful, connected by transit tubes and gantries that arc gracefully between the edifices like frozen lightning. This is infrastructure as topography, a settlement scale that respects the vastness around it by being dense only in specific nodes, leaving great expanses of the plateau empty, balancing presence with absence. The technology is evident but quiet; there are no roaring engines, only the deep, subsonic thrum of planetary-scale machinery digesting stone and refining heat into motion.

And yet, despite the aridity and the industry, the place teems. The fauna is abundant, though it defies terrestrial categorization. They are creatures of the dry warmth, organisms that have evolved not from carbon and water but from complex silicate lattices and piezoelectric crystals. They move with a slow, deliberate grace across the vitrified plains: grazing herds of faceted, tortoise-like beings that harvest ambient heat through translucent carapaces; flocks of crystalline moths that drift on thermal currents, their wings catching the normal light and fracturing it into fleeting spectra; solitary, multi-limbed striders that tend to the mineral gardens surrounding the industrial spires. They do not drink; they resonate. Their biology is a form of organized vibration, a weirdness that makes them seem less like animals and more like mobile extensions of the landscape’s own physics. They are silent, or nearly so; the only sounds they produce are faint clicks and hums, harmonics that blend with the quiet thrum of the city.

The ecology is thus a closed loop of energy and mineral exchange, post-life in the sense that it has moved beyond the need for aqueous solvents, yet vibrantly alive in its own idiom. The industrial city participates in this ecology; its waste heat feeds the grazers, its refined minerals provide substrate for the crystal-moths’ metamorphosis, its structured spaces offer shelter from the rare, scouring winds. There is no conflict between the built and the natural here, only a long-negotiated symbiosis settled over millennia. The weirdness lies in this integration: factories that breathe, creatures that compute, a city that erodes with the same patient rhythm as the mountains beside it.

Time moves differently in the High Desiccation. Change is glacial, measured in cycles of thermal expansion and contraction, in the slow migration of crystal-grazer herds across the basin, in the gradual accretion of new industrial layers atop old ones. Nothing is urgent. The landscape exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium, a balance maintained not through stasis but through processes so slow they appear still. To stand within it is to feel the weight of that patience, to sense the deep time embedded in every smooth ridge and every humming spire. The normal light reveals nothing dramatic, no sunsets or storms, only the steady illumination of a world that has found its own enduring form.

What emerges, ultimately, is a place of profound coherence. The parameters that might seem contradictory—industrial yet ecological, vast yet sparse, abundant yet silent, strange yet settled—resolve into a single, integrated reality. This is a world that has dried out not into death but into a different kind of life, one that trades liquidity for resonance, haste for duration, greenery for geometry. It is warm, elevated, ancient, and meticulously strange, a landscape that asks nothing of its observer but to accept its own terms of being. And in that acceptance, the High Desiccation becomes not merely a description but a presence, complete unto itself, holding its contradictions in the quiet warmth of its endless, elevated noon.
