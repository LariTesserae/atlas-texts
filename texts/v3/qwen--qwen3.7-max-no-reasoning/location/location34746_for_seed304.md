The place is known as the Sump-Ward, though to its inhabitants, it is simply "the floor." It is a landscape of aggressive intimacy, a sunken district where the boundary between geology and architecture has been dissolved by persistent moisture and desperate necessity.

**Terrain and Geometry**
The terrain is young and restless. This is not an ancient valley worn smooth by eons of river flow; it is a fresh subsidence, a geological bruise that formed only decades ago when the underlying aquifer shifted and the earth collapsed into itself. The result is a topography of sharp, jagged angles softened immediately by saturation. There are no gentle slopes here, only abrupt drops and steep, slick inclines. The ground is a mosaic of fractured bedrock and clay slurry, but you rarely see the raw earth. The low elevation traps everything: cold air, fog, and the runoff from the higher city above.

Despite the small scale—it could fit within the footprint of a grand cathedral or a sprawling estate—the density is suffocating. Space is a luxury that does not exist. Every square meter of stable ground is occupied, contested, and layered. The built environment does not sit *upon* the landscape; it clings to it like barnacles on a wet hull. Structures are wedged into crevices, suspended over dark water channels, and stacked vertically in chaotic accretions. A house becomes a foundation for another; a walkway serves as a roof for a cistern below. The urban fabric is packed so tightly that the air itself feels thickened, saturated with the breath of stone and wood.

**Materials and Construction**
Technology here is primitive but highly specialized. There is no steel, no glass, no precision-milled lumber. Everything is hand-shaped, scavenged, or grown. The primary building material is "bog-brick," a dense composite of river clay, shredded reeds, and ash, dried not by fire but by wind and pressure. These bricks are dark, porous, and perpetually damp to the touch. They smell of deep soil and old smoke.

Wood is rare and precious, used only for structural ribs and joinery. The timber is treated with tallow and pine resin, blackened against the rot. Fasteners are wooden pegs, bone nails, or ropes woven from tough marsh-grass. Metal is almost nonexistent, save for a few inherited iron chisels and hooks that have been sharpened down to nubs over generations. The construction style is organic and adaptive; walls curve to follow the erratic fracture lines of the rock, and floors are stepped to accommodate the uneven heave of the settling ground. Nothing is perfectly plumb. To build straight here is to invite collapse; one must build with the slump.

**Water and Atmosphere**
Water is the ruling element, present at a 2.0 intensity—not a flood, but a permanent, weeping condition. The water table sits just inches below the surface, rising and falling with the respiration of the ward. Condensation is constant. The air is cool, hovering just above the chill that settles in the bones, carrying a humidity that makes fabrics heavy and skin clammy.

Rocks do not merely get wet; they sweat. Capillary action draws groundwater up through the fractured stone faces, creating a perpetual glisten even in normal light. The atmosphere is a visible thing, a shifting haze of mist that clings to the vertical surfaces. Rain does not fall so much as materialize, condensing out of the saturated air before dripping from the overhanging eaves in rhythmic, maddening cadences. Drainage is the primary infrastructure; grooved channels are carved into every wall and street surface, guiding the endless seepage toward the central sumps.

**Ecology and Life**
Vegetation is sparse in the traditional sense—there are no trees, no grasses, no gardens. Yet the space is biologically active. The flora is cryptogamic: vast tapestries of moss, liverwort, and pale fungi that coat the north-facing walls and the undersides of walkways. These organisms are not decorative; they are functional. Specific mosses are cultivated to wick moisture away from living spaces, while certain shelf fungi are harvested for food and tinder. The vegetation is a velvet skin over the stone, softening the sharp edges of the young terrain.

Fauna is rare and secretive. You will not see birds or mammals. Instead, life is small and damp: translucent snails that graze on the wall-moss, blind beetles navigating the drainage grooves, and pale, thread-like worms in the mud-mortar. Animals here are silent and still, adapted to the quiet and the crush. Predation is slow and chemical rather than violent.

**Sound and Rhythm**
The soundscape is defined by a profound, textured quiet. It is not silence, but a hush composed of tiny, wet noises. The drip of condensation into ceramic catch-basins. The squelch of a boot on bog-brick. The creak of swollen timber adjusting to the temperature. Voices are kept low, absorbed instantly by the damp mass of the surroundings. Sound does not carry; it sinks.

Yet the place is dynamically active. The Sump-Ward is never finished. The young terrain continues to settle, requiring constant maintenance. Walls must be re-pointed, channels re-carved, foundations shimmed with slate. The ecosystem is in motion: moss grows and is scraped back, fungus blooms and is harvested, water levels rise and recede. The human activity mirrors this biological pace—slow, deliberate, cyclical. There is no rush here, only the steady labor of holding back dissolution.

**The Rules of the Place**
In the Sump-Ward, dryness is currency. Status is determined not by height or ornament, but by how well one’s dwelling sheds water. The weirdest aspect of this place is not supernatural, but sociological: the absolute acceptance of impermanence. Residents do not fight the wet; they negotiate with it. Architecture is treated as a temporary agreement with gravity and moisture.

The light is normal, grey and diffused, lacking the harsh shadows of arid places. It reveals texture rather than form. In this light, the packed density of the ward looks less like a city and more like a single, complex organism—a coral reef of clay and wood growing in the twilight zone of a terrestrial ocean. It is a place of boundaries, yes, but the boundaries are permeable. Inside and outside, built and natural, solid and liquid—all bleed into one another in the cool, quiet dark.
