It is a medium so dense with dissolved minerals and thermal memory that it behaves less like a liquid and more like a slow, hot glass.

Within this abyssal compression lies the Anvil-Cistern.

It is a place of profound intimacy and terrifying volume, a paradox of scale (0.2) and density (0.3). You are inside a pocket, a singular geological blister buried kilometers beneath the mantle’s skin, yet the space within feels hollowed out, a vast negative cathedral suspended in the rock. It is the temperature of a fever, or a machine left running too long.

Instead, the walls of the Cistern are coated in a mineral fur, a precipitate of white and rust-colored salts that bloom in fractal patterns where the heat-vents meet the cooler vapor. Life, if it can be called that, is restricted to rare, pale filaments (0.7 fauna) that drift through the vapor like ash—blind, translucent things that feed on static charge and dissolve when touched.

But the stillness of the mineral world is a lie. The Anvil-Cistern is defined by its violence.

The sound (2.9) is not heard; it is endured. It is a deafening, omnidirectional roar that bypasses the ears and vibrates the marrow. This is the voice of industry without makers. The landscape itself is a factory. Embedded into the curved basalt walls are colossal, corroded mechanisms of unknown origin (1.9 built, 2.1 tech). They are urban in their complexity, a favela of brass pipes, ceramic turbines, and iron flywheels stacked in dizzying, non-Euclidean layers. But there are no hands to tend them. The city is the machine; the machine is the geology.

The turbulence (2.6) is constant. The Cistern is in a state of hyperactive equilibrium. Pistons the size of houses slam downward in rhythmic, earth-shaking spasms, driven by the expansion of subterranean steam. Gears grind against tectonic drift. A broken gear is not replaced; it is melted down by the ambient heat and recast by the pressure into a new form, a valve becoming a strut, a pipe becoming a housing. You could float for hours through the silent gaps between the deafening mechanisms, passing through zones of eerie calm before being caught again in the slipstream of a venting turbine.

The weirdness (0.7) is subtle, creeping. It lies in the purposelessness of the power. The Anvil-Cistern produces nothing tangible. No goods emerge. The industrial fury, the urban density, the blinding noise—it all serves only to maintain the conditions of its own existence. The machine pumps the water, heats the vapor, grinds the salt, and generates the electricity solely to keep pumping, heating, grinding, and generating. It is a closed loop of mechanical metabolism, a heart beating in a chest that has no body.

To exist here is to be a foreign object in a digestive system. The warmth is welcoming until you realize it is waste heat. The shelter of the pocket is comforting until you recognize it as a containment vessel. The urban order is impressive until you see that the streets are conveyor belts and the buildings are furnaces.

And always, the water presses in. Not flooding, not drowning, but *present*. It seeps through the seals, beads on the hot iron, hisses into steam that screams as it joins the chorus. The boundary between the ocean outside and the industry inside is permeable, negotiated moment by moment by gaskets older than mountains. The Cistern does not keep the water out; it incorporates the intrusion. The dampness is part of the lubrication. The corrosion is part of the design.

This is a landscape of exhausted eternity. It has been running since before the concept of "running" had meaning, settled into its chaotic rhythm (1.9 erosion) so completely that the violence looks like peace. It is a hidden room at the bottom of the world, empty of life but full of motion, dimly lit by its own friction, screaming into the dark with the joyous, terrible energy of a thing that has forgotten how to stop.
