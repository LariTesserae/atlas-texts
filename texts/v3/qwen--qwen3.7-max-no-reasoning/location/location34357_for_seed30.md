The place is known as the Resonance Cistern, though it is less a container for water than a vessel for sound. It exists at a high altitude, perched on a geological shelf that feels provisional, as if the mountain beneath it has not yet decided to be solid. The elevation grants it a thinness of air, but the atmosphere here is heavy, saturated with a warm, wet breath that clings to skin and machinery alike.

The scale is intimate, almost architectural. You are standing in a natural amphitheater no larger than a cathedral nave, bounded by walls of raw, unweathered stone. This is geology in its embryonic state; the rock faces are not smoothed by eons of wind or river but are jagged, fractal-fresh, bleeding mineral dust. They look less like eroded cliffs and more like extrusion errors in reality, matter that has just been printed into existence and is still cooling. There is no soil, only grit and glass-sharp scree.

Despite the barrenness of the stone, the space is choked with noise. It is a loud, physical din, a layered cacophony that vibrates in the teeth. The sound does not come from the wind, for the air within the Cistern is strangely still. Instead, the noise is biological and mechanical, a singular, deafening chorus produced by the abundant fauna.

They are the Weep-Moths. Millions of them.

In this dim, amber twilight, the moths are the primary source of both light and moisture. They are creatures of beyond-modern biology, their wing-scales composed of piezoelectric metamaterials that harvest acoustic energy and transmute it into heat and vapor. As they beat their wings against the raw stone and each other, they generate a localized hydrological cycle. The "rain" here falls upward and sideways, a misting condensation driven by sonic pressure rather than gravity. The warmth of the cistern is entirely metabolic, a fever-heat generated by the friction of countless nanostructured bodies.

Vegetation is sparse because nothing rooted can survive the vibration. What little greenery exists is lithophytic moss that grows in the exact nodes of silence between sound waves, anchoring itself to the stone only where the frequency cancels out. These patches of moss are geometrically perfect, forming interference patterns of life amidst the chaos.

Scattered through this roaring nursery are artifacts of an engineering discipline that treats physics as a suggestion. They do not look built so much as *tuned*. There are three slender, needle-like spires rising from the scree, made of a matte-black substance that absorbs the moth-light. They are not antennas; they are dampeners, negative-space instruments designed to carve pockets of quiet out of the sonic storm. Near the base of one spire lies a partial ruin: a ribbed archway half-swallowed by fresh geological extrusion, suggesting that the land is actively overwriting the architecture, or perhaps the architecture is failing to hold back the geology.

The water parameter manifests here as a weeping solidity. The raw rock faces sweat continuously, not from groundwater, but from the atmospheric condensation forced out of the air by the moth-song. Rivulets of warm, distilled water trace the fractal cracks of the emerging stone, pooling in basins that seem to shift position as the ground settles and re-forms. The water tastes metallic and ozone-rich, charged with the residual static of the swarm.

The rules of this place are governed by acoustics. To move through the Cistern is to navigate a fluid medium of sound pressure. Walking too quickly creates turbulence that attracts the moths; standing perfectly still allows you to pass through their perception filters. The dynamic activity is relentless—the moths are not merely living; they are manufacturing the environment in real-time. If the swarm were to cease, the warmth would vanish, the water would sublimate instantly in the high-altitude chill, and the raw geology would likely collapse, having been held together only by the cohesive force of the song.

It is a landscape of violent creation. A small, loud, wet womb of impossible technology and insectile industry, where the world is being sung into shape, one deafening heartbeat at a time. The dimness is not an absence of light, but a density of presence; the darkness is thick with the labor of becoming.
