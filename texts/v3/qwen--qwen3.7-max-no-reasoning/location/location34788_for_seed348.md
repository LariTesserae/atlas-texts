The place is known as the Resonance Sump, though it has no name among those who inhabit it, for they have no language that separates sound from structure.

It is a landscape of industrial geology, a sunken basin where the distinction between machine and mineral has been eroded not by wind or water, but by ten thousand years of vibration. The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobic despite the open sky; it is the size of a grand cathedral nave turned inside out, a bowl of rusted iron and wet basalt walled in by cliffs of compacted slag. The elevation is low, sitting below the surrounding tablelands, acting as a catchment not just for the intermittent, oily rain that slicks every surface, but for noise. Sound does not escape here; it pools.

The light is grey and diffuse, filtered through a perpetual ceiling of condensation and exhaust vapor that hangs at exactly three hundred feet. It is a normal, terrestrial daylight, but stripped of warmth, rendering the world in high-contrast monochrome and oxidized orange. There are no shadows, only gradients of dampness.

Vegetation is entirely absent. There is no green, no moss, no lichen. The surfaces are too loud for biology to take hold. Instead, the "flora" of this place is mechanical and fungal-mimetic: vast, branching networks of copper piping and ceramic insulators that have calcified into coral-like formations. They are post-life constructs, machines that have forgotten their function and settled into a geological equilibrium. They do not grow; they merely persist, weathered into smooth, organic curves by the relentless abrasion of sonic pressure. The ground is paved in hexagonal plates of cast iron, fused together by centuries of heat and moisture into a single, unbroken carapace.

And upon this carapace, the fauna moves.

They are overwhelming. To stand in the Sump is to be submerged in a living tide. They are the Chitter-Striders: arthropods the size of hounds, but with the mass density of lead. Their carapaces are indistinguishable from the industrial debris around them—mottled greys, oil-slick blues, matte blacks. They possess too many limbs, articulated with pneumatic hisses rather than muscle. There are millions of them. They carpet the floor, cling to the walls, and swarm over the petrified machinery in shifting, liquid dunes. They do not hunt; they process. They are the ecosystem’s digestive system, consuming the microscopic metal shavings and mineral salts produced by the erosion of the city-basin itself.

The noise is physical. It is a 2.4 on the scale of volume, which means it is felt in the marrow before it is heard by the ear. It is a layered cacophony: the deep, tectonic thrum of the Sump’s own settling foundation; the mid-range grinding of the Striders’ metallic joints; and the piercing, chaotic static of their communication, which sounds like tearing sheet metal. Silence is impossible. Even when the Striders pause, the architecture itself hums, vibrating at a frequency that keeps the condensation suspended in the air.

Water exists here as a lubricant and a conductor. It is never standing still, nor is it ever flowing freely. It beads on the warm iron, runs in capillary veins through the cracks in the pavement, and drips in synchronized rhythms from the overhead pipe-canopies. It tastes of copper and ozone. The dampness is essential; without it, the friction would shatter the Striders’ joints. The rain comes in brief, violent squalls that wash the basin clean, after which the humidity instantly rebounds, thick and tasting of battery acid.

This is a world of absolute stasis disguised as frenzy. The dynamic parameter is near zero because the chaos is perfectly balanced. The rate of erosion matches the rate of biological deposition. The volume of sound remains constant, a white-noise ocean that has achieved equilibrium. Nothing new is built; nothing old truly decays. The industrial infrastructure is no longer a creation of engineers but a natural formation, as permanent and unchanging as a mountain range. The Striders do not evolve; they simply replace themselves, identical copies stamped out by the environment’s own logic.

To walk here is to violate the equilibrium. Your footsteps introduce an irregular rhythm; your body absorbs sound that should be reflected; your heat creates a thermal gradient in the cool, stable air. The Striders do not attack, but they flow around you like water around a stone, their collective movement adjusting to accommodate the anomaly until you, too, become part of the background noise.

It is a strange, loud, wet factory that produces nothing but its own continuation. A cathedral of gears and chitin, where the sermon is a scream and the congregation is infinite, forever processing the sacred rust of a dead world into the eternal now.
