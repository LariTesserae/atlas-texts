They are the Lithovores of the Interstice, though they have no name for themselves, for naming requires a separation between speaker and subject that this place does not permit. They are less a species than a singular, distributed condition of being.

To call them animals is to impose a terrestrial taxonomy upon a biology that has bypassed the animal kingdom entirely. They are closer to a localized weather system made of meat. Individually, a single entity is nothing more than a translucent sac of enzymatic fluid, a gelatinous blister no larger than a thumb, devoid of organs, nerves, or intent. But in the crushing dark of this arrested collapse, individuality is a fatal error. They exist only as a aggregate, a superorganism of millions fused by mucus and hydraulic necessity into the living stucco that coats the shattered basalt.

They do not move through space; they *are* the rearrangement of space. Their locomotion is indistinguishable from their metabolism. To travel across a slab of fallen tectonic plate is to dissolve the microscopic layer of stone beneath them and reprecipitate it behind, a slow-motion crawl of chemical erosion. They are the mortar and the mason simultaneously. Where the water is richest in dissolved iron and silica, they pile upon one another in feverish, spiraling tenements, their bodies stacking in layers of pulsing translucence. These structures are not shelters; they are digestive tracts turned inside out, architectural expressions of hunger. The "city" is merely the shape of their collective feeding.

Their sensory world is entirely tactile and barometric. In a medium too viscous for sound and too dark for sight, they perceive the universe as a topography of pressure gradients and mineral density. A shift in the thermal current from below is felt as a sudden, overwhelming taste; the settling of a silt drift is experienced as a dull, rhythmic bruising against the colony’s outer membrane. They communicate through the viscosity of their own secretions, passing chemical signals through the shared slime that binds them. A warning of structural instability ripples through the tenement not as a shout, but as a sudden thickening of the communal gel, a stiffening of the living wall that braces against the coming crush.

They are the caretakers of the wound. The calcified roots that pierce the ceiling are dead, but the Lithovores tend to them with the devotion of a gardener. They secrete acids that soften the mineralized veins, integrating the ghostly rebar into their flesh-reefs, reinforcing the fragile chamber against the weight of the world above. They are holding the geology together even as they consume it. This is their paradox: they are the agents of both preservation and destruction. Without their binding mucus, the suspended silt would choke the chamber and the loose slabs would slide into final oblivion. With their hunger, they ensure that nothing remains static. They are the friction that generates the bruised luminescence, their metabolic heat contributing to the cold phosphorescence that bleeds from the stone. They are the light source of their own abyss.

Their life cycle is synchronized with the tectonic respiration of the pocket. When the thermal gradients wane and the mineral influx slows, the colony enters a state of suspended animation, the living stucco hardening into a dormant, glassy crust. They become indistinguishable from the rock, waiting in a silence that spans centuries. But when the deep earth shifts and the nutrient-rich blood of the planet surges back into the atrium, the crust liquefies. The city wakes. The tenements pulse. The digestion resumes.

They possess no memory, yet they remember everything in the stratigraphy of their own bodies. The lower layers of the colony are ancient, compressed records of past flows and forgotten feasts; the upper layers are the ephemeral present, tender and voracious. To touch them is to touch time itself, rendered in slime and stone-dust.

They are waiting. Not for rescue, nor for evolution, but for the inevitable resolution of the geologic tension that birthed them. They sense, in the slow language of pressure, that this pocket is temporary. They know, with the certainty of chemistry, that they will either be forged into diamond by the closing of the wound or expelled into a sunlit ocean they cannot comprehend. Until then, they remain the soft, blind architects of the in-between, digesting the darkness, building a cathedral of flesh in the throat of the world, holding the collapse at bay with the terrible, silent intimacy of their hunger.
