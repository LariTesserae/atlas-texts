They are the Keepers of the Meniscus, though they have no name for themselves that translates to anything as active as "keeping." To call them inhabitants implies a distinction between resident and residence that does not exist here. They are better understood as the biological expression of the cistern’s function, as integral to the water cycle as the slate roofs or the granite basins.

They are tall and attenuated, their physiology shaped by the thin air and the verticality of the carved stone. Their chests are deep and barrel-shaped, adapted to draw sustenance from an atmosphere that offers itself grudgingly. But it is their skin that defines them most: pale, matte, and utterly hairless, possessing the same unglazed ceramic texture as the town itself. In the diffuse midday light, they seem less like flesh and blood and more like extensions of the pale grey rock, visible only when they move across the shadow lines. They do not sweat; in a place where moisture is currency, evaporation from the body would be a catastrophic failure of design. Instead, their skin is cool and dry to the touch, retaining every fraction of internal humidity.

Their movement is the primary technology of this settlement. They do not walk with the heavy, heel-striking gait of lowland creatures. They flow. Their footsteps are placed with the deliberate, silent precision of condensation forming on a cold surface. They wear soft-soled shoes cured from some unknown, leather-like membrane, designed to make no sound against the polished stone. To hear a footfall here would be to announce a clumsiness that borders on violence. When they traverse the open plazas or navigate the roofless corridors, they appear to glide, their center of gravity low and fluid, mimicking the behavior of water seeking its level. They are never hurried; urgency creates heat, and heat creates loss.

They speak rarely, and never above a whisper. Language in the cistern-town has been stripped of ornamentation, reduced to a lexicon of utility and reverence. There are no words for "want," "hurry," or "mine." Their communication is largely somatic—a tilt of the head indicating the fullness of a reservoir, a hand pressed flat against stone to signal the arrival of mist, a specific rhythm of blinking to acknowledge presence without breaking the silence. When vocalization is necessary, it is pitched to match the resonant frequency of the basins, a low hum that carries through the architecture without disturbing the stillness. A conversation sounds less like dialogue and more like the wind tuning the town.

Their relationship to the water is liturgical but devoid of mysticism; it is a mysticism of physics. They do not worship the water as a deity, for deities imply caprice. They honor it as a law. Their daily labor is indistinguishable from maintenance. They tend the bell-shaped reservoirs not with buckets, but with sponges made of woven fiber, lifting water by capillary action rather than force. They clean the catchment channels with brushes of silver-green lichen, harvesting the moss as they work, ensuring the stone remains smooth and the biological filtration continues. They drink only what is required for metabolic stasis, measuring intake with the exactitude of a chemical titration. To spill a drop is not an accident; it is a moral injury.

They possess no concept of childhood or senescence as linear progressions. In a world outside of temporal flow, age is merely a state of integration. The young are smaller, softer versions of the adults, learning the geometry of silence before they learn the topography of the basins. The old do not decay; they desiccate, slowing until their movements become imperceptible, eventually settling into the alcoves and terraced walls like mineral deposits. Death is not a departure but a final act of conservation. When one ceases to function, they are returned to the deepest, darkest cisterns, their bodily moisture reclaimed by the system, their dry husks laid within the niches of the eroded rock to serve as windbreaks or markers of equilibrium. They become, quite literally, part of the infrastructure.

Their minds are as uncluttered as their environment. They do not dream of elsewhere, for there is no elsewhere in the logic of the cistern. They do not imagine futures, for the future is simply the continuation of the present balance. Their consciousness is focused entirely on the *now* of the system: the tension of a dewdrop, the angle of the raking light, the precise weight of the air. They experience a profound, cellular satisfaction in this attention. They are not bored by the stasis; they are fulfilled by it. They have solved the anxiety of existence by becoming the solution.

At twilight, when the light rakes across the undulating surfaces and the temperature drops to trigger the nightly harvest, the Keepers emerge in their greatest density. They stand motionless along the lips of the basins, heads bowed, arms resting lightly on the stone. They are waiting for the condensation. In these moments, watching them against the dark mirrors of the reservoirs, it becomes impossible to tell where the person ends and the place begins. They are the breath held in the throat of the mountain, the living pause between the falling mist and the rising vapor, existing in a perfection so complete it feels, to an outsider, like a beautiful and terrible grief. But to them, it is simply peace. It is simply the work of being here.
