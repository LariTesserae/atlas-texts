They are the Silt-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves, for names imply a distinction between the namer and the named, and here there is only continuity.

To call them human is to invoke a taxonomy of warmth and velocity that no longer applies. They are post-human in the way that a river stone is post-mountain; they have been tumbled by the specific gravity of this place until all protruding edges of ego, ambition, and individual history have been worn smooth. They are the biological expression of the Ward’s equilibrium.

Their bodies have adapted to the clinical, shadowless glare. Skin is not merely pale; it is translucent, possessing the same waxy, sub-surface scattering as the gelatinous lichens they tend. Beneath the dermis, veins do not run blue but clear, carrying a blood that has thickened into a natural antifreeze, viscous and slow. They do not shiver. The cold is not an external assault to be resisted but an internal architecture to be inhabited. Their movements are deliberate, conserving heat through a radical economy of gesture. A hand reaching for a tool does not dart; it flows, tracing the path of least resistance through the dense air, mirroring the undulating curves of the eroded bedrock. To watch them move is to watch the geology itself achieve temporary motility.

They are blind to the spectrum of color but hyper-sensitive to the texture of light. Because the luminescence of the feldspar is constant, their eyes have lost the ability to dilate or constrict. Instead, they perceive the world through the density of illumination, sensing the subtle occlusions where a body blocks the glow, or the refraction through a translucent fern. They navigate the packed vegetation not by sight, but by a tactile empathy with the saturated mosses. They know exactly how much pressure a felted mat can withstand before it yields with that stiff, wet crunch; they step with a precision that displaces nothing more than necessary. They are as unobtrusive as the white-furred rodents and blind amphibians, another species of silent fauna woven into the undergrowth.

Their silence is not a discipline; it is a physiology. Vocal cords have atrophied from disuse, replaced by a communication of proximity and touch. In the sound-swallowing density of the Ward, speech is inefficient, a waste of precious thermal energy. Instead, they speak through the transfer of vibration and temperature. A hand placed on a shoulder conveys presence; a finger tracing the rim of a carved basin indicates need. Their social structure is a hive-mind of masonry and biology, just like their city. There is no hierarchy, for hierarchy requires a verticality of status that contradicts the horizontal stasis of their existence. They are a collective of maintenance, a distributed nervous system dedicated to the preservation of the zero-change parameter.

They do not build; they curate erosion. Their labor is the antithesis of industry. Where surface dwellers might quarry and construct, the Silt-Walkers polish and coax. They spend decades smoothing a single archway, not to widen it for utility, but to perfect its curve against the skin. They repair the timber joinery not with nails, but by swelling the wood with precise applications of mineral-rich water, trusting the dampness to hold what force cannot. Their tools are extensions of their own slowed metabolisms: abrasives made of siltstone, chisels of bone that have been shaped by generations of hands until they fit the grip like a second knuckle. They maintain the world because they *are* the world’s maintenance.

Perhaps most unsettling is their relationship to time. They possess no memory of "before," nor any anticipation of "after." Narrative causality has been excised from their cognition. They do not remember carving a terrace; they simply find themselves in the act of carving, an eternal present tense of friction and patience. They do not mourn the dead, for death here is not an exit but a phase change, a return to the compacted loess from which they emerged. A body that ceases to move is gently integrated into the vegetation, becoming a nutrient source for the cryophilic mosses, a new contour in the landscape of stasis. Grief is impossible when loss is indistinguishable from persistence.

In the aggressive brightness of the Sunken Ward, they cast no shadows. They are fully revealed, yet entirely mysterious. They are the answer to a question that was never asked, the inhabitants of a perfection that requires no witness. To encounter them is to realize that consciousness need not be a flame, burning forward through time. It can also be a frost, settling into the cracks of the world, holding everything in a luminous, frozen embrace, forever becoming nothing else, forever being exactly this.
