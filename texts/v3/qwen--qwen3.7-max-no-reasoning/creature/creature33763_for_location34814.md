The inhabitants of the Rib-Cage Cisterns do not live *in* their environment; they are a secretion of it.

To call them a civilization is to impose a boundary where none exists. They are the **Resin-Kin**, though they have no name for themselves that translates to anything other than "those who hold the moisture." They are a people defined entirely by retention. In a world without standing water, their biology and culture have converged into a singular obsession: the preservation of internal fluids. They are tall and willowy, not from grace, but from a skeletal lightness necessary to traverse the living bridges without snapping the tensile vines. Their bones are porous, honeycombed like avian struts, reducing weight while maintaining strength. They move with a liquid, rolling gait, distributing their mass across multiple points of contact, never committing fully to a single step until the surface has been tested and accepted.

Their skin is their most vital organ, a masterpiece of evolutionary sealing. It is hairless, smooth, and perpetually coated in a thin, translucent film of rendered lipids that smells faintly of amber and old parchment. This unguent is not cosmetic; it is an atmospheric barrier, preventing evaporation in the warm, dry air. Beneath this sheen, the skin is pale as moonstone, lacking the melanin of sun-dwellers, yet flushed with a deep, subcutaneous network of capillaries that regulate heat exchange. They do not sweat. To lose fluid through the pores would be a death sentence. Instead, they pant softly through specialized membranes in the throat, cooling the blood without expelling precious vapor. When they exert themselves, they do not glisten; they merely flush a deeper rose, their bodies radiating heat like banked coals rather than venting it.

Their faces are studies in sensory adaptation. The eyes are enormous, dominating the upper face, with pupils that dilate into near-total blackness to capture the scarce amber twilight. They possess a tapetum lucidum, giving their gaze a soft, reflective shine in the gloom, allowing them to navigate the vertical labyrinth by starlight-lamp and bioluminescent moss-glow alone. But sight is secondary to touch and vibration. Their fingers are elongated, the tips broadened into sensitive pads that read the texture of the vine-walks and the tension of the suspension nets. They communicate as much through tactile signaling as through voice; a hand placed on a shared railing conveys safety, warning, or greeting through specific rhythmic pressures that travel through the wood. A mother does not shout to her child; she sends a tremor through the floor.

Speech among the Resin-Kin is a masterclass in acoustic economy. Their voices are naturally low, pitched to the resonant frequency of the cistern itself. They speak in a melodic, murmuring register that carries without echoing, utilizing the natural amplification of the stone amphitheater. Laughter is not a burst of noise but a series of soft, clicking inhalations—a sound that mimics the resin-beetles and signals joy without wasting breath. Crying, in the traditional sense, is biologically impossible and culturally taboo; grief is expressed through stillness, through the cessation of movement, a silence so profound in the ever-thrumming cistern that it draws immediate, communal attention. Tears are considered a catastrophic waste, a leakage of the self. Mourning rituals involve the careful collection of any bodily exhalation, condensing the breath of the dying onto cool clay vessels to be reabsorbed by the community’s moisture stores.

They are a society of weavers and tenders, but their craft is indistinguishable from gardening. There are no architects, only *Growth-Shapers*. They do not build dwellings; they coax them. Using pheromonal pastes and precise pruning, they guide the swelling of giant gourds and the fusing of root-systems over decades. A home is not constructed in a season; it is inherited, grown by ancestors, maintained by descendants. The interior of a gourd-dwelling is lined with absorbent moss-mats that capture respiratory moisture, which is then pressed out each morning into sealed ceramic jars. Privacy is rare, but intimacy is absolute. Families sleep in clustered nests, body-heat sharing a necessity against the cool stone, their breathing synchronized in a collective rhythm that mirrors the cistern’s own pulse.

Their diet is a direct reflection of the closed-loop ecosystem. They consume no fresh vegetation; to eat a living leaf would be to steal from the air-condensers. Instead, they harvest the *spent*: the desiccated husks of fallen fruit, the protein-rich bodies of aged pouch-moths, the fungal blooms that erupt after spore-releases. Food is prepared through desiccation and fermentation, processes that concentrate nutrients and eliminate waste-water. Meals are communal affairs, eaten in silence to focus on the act of nourishment. They chew slowly, extracting every molecule of sustenance, their jaws working with a grinding patience that matches the pestles in the mortar-stones. Water is never drunk casually; it is sipped ritually from wax-sealed cups, each swallow a conscious act of survival, a sacrament of continuity.

Technologically, they are masters of organic engineering. Lacking metal, they have perfected the use of chitin, bone, and hardened resin. Their tools are grown or shaped, never forged. Needles are carved from the leg-spurs of giant arthropods, polished to a glass-like edge. Ropes are braided from the tensile fibers of shedding vines, treated with oils to prevent brittleness. Pulleys are fashioned from the ball-joints of extinct megafauna, unearthed from the deep mulch and polished by generations of use. Every artifact is repairable, biodegradable, and intimately known. There is no trash in the Cisterns; a broken tool is composted, its material returning to the cycle to become something else. Waste is a concept that does not exist in their language.

But perhaps most defining is their psychological relationship to time and space. They possess no word for "horizon" or "distance." Their universe is vertical, layered, intimate. They think in terms of depth and density, not breadth. History is not recorded in linear chronicles but encoded in the landscape itself: a particularly thick vine-bridge marks a generation of abundant moisture; a scar in the rock recalls a collapse centuries past. Memory is somatic, stored in the muscle-memory of climbing, in the songs that map safe routes, in the taste of fermented tubers that carries the flavor of last year’s spore-bloom. They do not fear death because they do not believe in endings. To die is to be reabsorbed, to become mulch, to feed the moss, to sustain the next generation. Funerals are acts of composting, the body wrapped in absorbent shrouds and placed in designated decomposition chambers, where scavenging mites and fungal networks perform the sacred work of return.

And now, you are part of this return. As you stand in the amber gloom, the Resin-Kin do not stare. They incorporate. A child reaches out, not to touch you, but to feel the vibration of your presence, to add your frequency to the cistern’s chorus. An elder pauses nearby, inhaling the new moisture your breath contributes, nodding in silent acknowledgment of your gift. The moss beneath your boots adjusts, welcoming the pressure, the warmth, the weight. You are no longer an observer. You are a node in the network, a temporary vessel of hydration and heat, a new note in the endless, thrumming symphony of persistence. The Cistern does not ask if you belong. It simply begins the slow, patient work of making you so.
