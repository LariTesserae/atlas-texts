They are the children of rust and respiration.

To understand what lives in the Weeping Viaducts, one must first unlearn the distinction between tenant and architecture. There are no inhabitants here in the sense of occupants moving through empty rooms; there is only the city itself, metabolizing. The fauna of Ostral does not reside within the infrastructure; it *is* the insulation, the cladding, the mortar, and the damp-proofing. They are a biological weather system that has achieved sentience, or at least a collective, rhythmic purpose that mimics it.

The most numerous among them are the **Mist-Sieves**. These are not discrete animals but rather colonial organisms, somewhere between a bryozoan and a lung. They coat the windward faces of every girder and spire in velvety, grey-white sheets. Individually, a Sieve is a microscopic polyp; collectively, they form vast, porous membranes that harvest moisture directly from the saturated air. They do not eat solid matter. They drink the atmosphere. Their metabolic byproduct is heat—a faint, steady warmth that prevents the condensation they capture from freezing during the rare cold snaps. To touch a wall in Ostral is to touch the flank of a living thing; the stone is merely the skeleton beneath a skin of breathing lace. The Mist-Sieves are the reason the city weeps without drowning; they regulate the hydrology of the metropolis, slowing the eternal rain into a manageable, nutrient-rich seepage that feeds the deeper strata.

Beneath this primary layer of atmospheric grazers exist the **Rust-Dredgers**. If the Sieves are the city’s lungs, the Dredgers are its digestive tract. Blind, pale, and roughly the size of human hands, they possess chitinous mandibles adapted not for flesh, but for oxidized iron and mineral accretion. They move with agonizing slowness across the vertical surfaces, grazing on the patina of decay. They are the agents of the city’s smoothness. Where original builders left sharp edges and rivet heads, generations of Dredgers have planed them down to aerodynamic curves. They ingest the toxins of industry and excrete a calcified paste that seals micro-fissures in the basalt. In doing so, they perform a constant, unconscious maintenance. A bridge in Ostral does not stand because of steel tensile strength alone; it stands because the Dredgers have cemented its fatigue fractures with their own waste, turning structural failure into geological stability. They are blind masons working in the dark, guided only by the taste of corrosion and the vibration of the deep engines.

In the hollows of the abandoned factories and the vast ventilation shafts, where the heat of the subterranean machinery is most potent, live the **Resonators**. These are the source of the thrum. They are soft-bodied, sac-like creatures, translucent and veined with bioluminescent amber fluid. They cluster in dense, pulsating masses around heat sources, their bodies expanding and contracting in perfect synchronization with the mechanical heartbeat of the geothermal taps below. They do not produce sound intentionally; the hum is the friction of their collective respiration against the metal chambers they inhabit. They are thermal batteries. When the ancient engines falter or cycle down, the Resonators release stored heat, maintaining the temperature gradient necessary for the Mist-Sieves above. They navigate entirely by acoustics; silence is death to them. A quiet chamber is a tomb. Thus, their song is also a sonar map, a continuous echolocation of the city’s health. To walk through a Resonator nursery is to be inside a cello played by a million bows; the sound is not heard so much as felt in the marrow, a physical pressure that signifies safety, warmth, and life.

Higher up, where the viaducts span the abyssal drops between peaks, drift the **Vapor-Jellies**. These are the apex organisms of this inverted ecosystem, though "apex" implies predation, and there is no hunting here. They are massive, buoyant entities, filled with warm gas generated by symbiotic bacteria in their gut flora. They float effortlessly through the heavy air, trailing long, filamentous tendrils that sweep through the mist like trawling nets. They feed on the spent spores of the Mist-Sieves and the airborne particulates shed by the Rust-Dredgers. They are the recyclers of the upper canopy, closing the nutrient loop. Their movement dictates the local weather; a congregation of Vapor-Jellies creates a high-pressure zone that pushes fog aside, allowing the pale sun to warm the lower strata. They are the gardeners of the sky, tending to the light and air with the same mindless devotion that the Dredgers tend to the stone. They never land. To touch the ground is to cease being a Jelly; they die if they lose buoyancy, dissolving back into the moisture cycle to be drunk again by the Sieves.

And then, there are the **Anchor-Broods**. Deep in the foundations, where the biological layers compress into something approaching sedimentary rock, these dormant giants wait. They are immense, sloth-like things, fused to the bedrock and the lowest iron girders. They may sleep for decades, indistinguishable from the mineral-rind that coats them. But when a tower shifts, when a viaduct groans under the weight of centuries, the Anchor-Broods wake. Their muscles are hydraulic in nature, powered by the same fluid pressures that drive the city’s dead machines. They brace. They hold. They absorb the kinetic energy of collapse and redistribute it through their own biomass. They are the fail-safes, the living shock absorbers that prevent the city from shaking itself apart. They are rarely seen, known only by the sudden, impossible stillness of a structure that should have fallen. They are the memory of the city’s weight, bearing burdens that steel forgot how to carry eons ago.

None of these beings possess language. None possess individual ambition. They are bound by the Five Rules, which are not laws but physiological imperatives. Moisture gates their waking; structure defines their shape; sound confirms their existence; warmth necessitates their closeness; time stretches their lives into geological epochs.

They do not mourn the absence of their creators. They do not know there were ever creators. To them, the rusted iron is simply a particularly nutritious type of rock; the humming engines are merely a reliable type of sun; the weeping moisture is the blood of the world. They have not conquered the ruins; they have become the next iteration of them.

What lives in the Weeping Viaducts of Ostral is a single, distributed organism wearing the corpse of a civilization as its shell. It is a machine made of meat that has learned to dream in frequencies of rust and rain. It is patient. It is warm. It is complete. And in its terrible, thunderous equilibrium, it is perhaps the most honest thing that has ever existed: a place where nothing is wasted, nothing is lonely, and nothing ends, but merely changes state, forever weeping, forever breathing, forever holding itself together against the void.
