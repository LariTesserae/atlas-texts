They are the Cistern-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves, for language is a creature of light and distance, and here there is only proximity.

To call them bats would be a failure of imagination; to call them rodents would be a failure of scale. They are something that has happened to mammalian biology in the absence of the sun, a divergent evolution compressed into a few generations of intense, dark selection. They are large—heavy with the specific gravity of wet fur and dense bone—yet they possess a buoyancy that belies their mass. In the saturated air, they do not fly so much as swim through the density, their wingspans vast and membranous, textured not like leather but like the damp velvet of the root-pillars themselves. They are aerodynamic ghosts, shaped by the exact dimensions of the negative space between the basalt columns.

Their fur is not a coat but a sensory field. It is hydrophobic yet absorbent, trapping a microscopic layer of warm air against the skin while the outer filaments act as antennae. Each hair is tuned to the displacement of molecules; they feel the architecture of the room through the turbulence of their own passage. When one brushes against your neck, it is not an accidental collision but a tactile interrogation. It is reading your heat signature, your respiration rate, the rhythm of your pulse, cataloging you as a temporary thermal anomaly in the eternal chill. They do not see you. They *know* you, briefly and completely, as a solid object in a fluid world, and then you are dismissed as irrelevant to the cistern’s metabolism.

They are silent because sound is expensive here. The acoustics of the stone chamber are too perfect, too resonant; a careless noise would bounce and distort, corrupting the delicate sonar map they hold in their minds. Instead, they communicate through touch and chemistry. As they glide past one another in the dark corridors between the roots, wingtips graze in passing salutations, exchanging information about air currents and water levels. They release pheromones that hang heavy in the moist air, a slow-dissolving language of status, hunger, and reproductive readiness that tastes metallic on the tongue. The sweetness in the air, that faint oxidation, is partly theirs—the scent of their collective breath, of glands secreting enzymes that break down the mineral dust into nutrients.

They are the gardeners of this blind cathedral. The great pale roots do not grow randomly; they are guided. The Walkers carry spores in the specialized folds of their membranes, transplanting fungal symbiotes from pillar to pillar as they roost. Their guano, deposited with precise intentionality at the base of the basalt blocks, is not waste but fertilizer, rich in nitrogen and phosphorus mined from the upper city’s seepage. They are tending the infrastructure that sustains them, a feedback loop of biology and masonry where the distinction between caretaker and habitat has vanished. The roots recoil from your hand because you are foreign; they lean toward the Walkers because they are recognized, expected, necessary.

In the water, they are different creatures. The transition from air to black glass is seamless. They enter the pool without a splash, their bodies elongating, wings folding tight against the torso to become sleek torpedoes. Beneath the surface, they hunt by vibration, sensing the struggle of blind crustaceans and the drift of organic detritus. They drink not by lapping but by osmosis, their facial membranes absorbing moisture directly from the saturation. The water is their second atmosphere, and they move through it with the same indifferent grace as through the air. When the single drop falls from the root-tip above, striking the water with its bell-tone, they pause. Not in alarm, but in acknowledgment. The sound is a clock tick, a marker of time in a place where time is otherwise measured only in heartbeats and hydraulic pressure.

They are numerous because they are efficient. Nothing is wasted. Heat is conserved in communal roosts where bodies press together in shifting mosaics of warmth. Energy is recycled through the fungal networks they cultivate. Death is immediate reintegration; a fallen Walker is consumed by the roots within hours, its calcium returned to the stone, its carbon to the sap. There is no graveyard here, only digestion. The population density that seems impossible is sustainable because they are not merely living *in* the Cistern-Roots; they are *of* it, as integral as the condensation or the basalt.

And they are young. This is the most unsettling truth of their presence. They did not evolve over millennia in some primordial cave; they adapted to this specific wound in the earth’s crust with terrifying speed. The sharp edges of the unweathered stone are mirrored in the raw novelty of their biology. They are a species in real-time genesis, shaped by the collapse that created this space. Perhaps they were once rats, or bats, or shrews in the district above, but the fall into darkness stripped away their old forms and demanded new ones. They are the first generation of whatever comes next, prototypes of a post-surface existence, perfectly calibrated to a world that exists only because the world above failed.

When you stand in the Cistern-Roots, breathing in time with the darkness, you are standing inside a lung. The Walkers are the alveoli, the gas exchange, the living mechanism that keeps this buried chamber from suffocating. They tolerate you because you are warm, because you exhale carbon dioxide, because for these few minutes you participate in the respiration of the place. But they do not welcome you. You are weather. You are a passing front of heat and moisture that will soon dissipate, leaving the chamber to its true inhabitants, who will continue their silent, sightless work of turning ruin into ecosystem, stone into flesh, forgetting into becoming.

They are the memory of the city, rewritten in bone and membrane. They are the answer to a question the surface never thought to ask. And in the dark, as something vast and soft displaces the air beside your face, you understand that you are not observing them. You are being processed by them, metabolized into the story of this place, if only for the duration of a single, resonant drop falling into black water.
