They are the children of the interval.

To name them is to impose a taxonomy of the surface, a categorization of bone and blood that has no purchase here. They are better understood as variables in an equation of decay, distinct biological solutions to the problem of eternal, submerged industry. They do not inhabit the Sump; they are its exhaust, its lubrication, its final, living output.

First, there are the **Oxidophages**.
They are not worms, though they share the worm’s humility. They are calcified filaments, pale as milk glass, rooted directly into the pitting of the iron girders. They have no mouths, no digestive tracts in any conventional sense. Instead, their entire outer membrane is a chemical tongue, tasting the slow bleed of rust. They feed on the valence electrons of corroding metal, converting the structural failure of the cathedral into metabolic heat. They are clustered thickest where the water is warmest, near the ancient, seized valves where the friction of eons has generated a ghost-heat. To touch them is to touch a fever. They are the reason the metal curves like bone; they have eaten the sharpness out of the world, digesting the geometry of manufacture into the softness of biology. They do not grow toward light; they grow toward entropy.

Drifting between these fixed gardens are the **Resonators**.
They are translucent, gelatinous ovals, roughly the size of a human hand, possessing neither eyes nor brain. They are pure sensory apparatus suspended in a matrix of jelly. They exist solely to interpret the vibration of the failing pumps. Their bodies are tuned to the specific, subsonic thrum of the hydraulic ghosts; they pulse in perfect sympathy with the mechanical heartbeat, expanding and contracting in a rhythm that is not their own. They are the ecosystem’s metronome. When a pump shudders or a pipe groans under the weight of the deep, the Resonators flare with a faint, teal bioluminescence—a visual echo of an acoustic event. They do not hunt; they are hunted, but their predation is part of the signal transfer. When a scavenger consumes a Resonator, it ingests the rhythm, syncing its own metabolism to the dying machine. They are the memory of the Sump’s function, preserved in flesh.

Then, there are the **Flange-Walkers**.
These are the closest analogue to the crabs or insects of the upper world, but the analogy fails upon inspection. They are articulated assemblages of chitin and mineral deposit, their carapaces indistinguishable from the ceramic tiles they traverse. They have six limbs, each ending not in claws, but in specialized manipulators: some for scraping biofilm, some for sensing thermal gradients, some for anchoring against the micro-currents. They are blind, navigating entirely by the texture of the manufactured surfaces. They know the difference between a rivet and a weld, between a load-bearing beam and a decorative strut, not through sight, but through the tactile history of wear. They are the custodians of the boundary. They graze upon the bacterial mats that threaten to clog the narrowest apertures, keeping the fluid pathways open just enough to sustain the flow. They do not build; they maintain the state of ruin. Their movement is the wet friction mentioned in the silence, the sound of life tending to the machinery of its own existence.

And finally, permeating all of this, is the **Slime-Mind**.
It is not a single organism, but a contiguous, planetary-scale biofilm that coats every vertical surface in a slick, verdant layer. It is the true dominant species of the Sump. It is a distributed intelligence of bacteria and fungi, communicating through chemical diffusion across the vast, curved walls. It senses the local depletion of nutrients and shifts its metabolic focus; it detects a tear in the oxidophage colonies and releases spores to recolonize. It is the gardener, the recycler, the womb and the tomb. It is what gives the Sump its safety. There is no chaos here because the Slime-Mind permits none. Every death is anticipated; every waste product is a resource allocated. It has absorbed the logic of the factory that once stood here and translated it into the logic of survival. The machine was built to produce order; the Slime-Mind produces equilibrium.

None of these beings possess curiosity. None possess fear. They are beyond the anxiety of individual survival, for they are expressions of a system that has achieved closure. They are as much a part of the Sump as the corroded girders or the heavy water.

In the end, you cannot say that life has colonized the Sump of the Last Gear. That implies an invasion, a separation between the resident and the residence. The truth is quieter, and more absolute. The machine did not fail; it matured. It shed its purpose and grew a body. The Oxidophages, the Resonators, the Flange-Walkers, the Slime-Mind—they are not tenants in a ruin.

They are the ruin, alive.
