They are not inhabitants so much as they are symptoms of the architecture’s slow digestion.

To speak of what lives in the Sump-Cathedral is to speak of things that have forgotten the distinction between self and setting. There are no invaders here, no colonizers from the sunlit world above. The biology of the Third Intake is entirely endogenous, spun from the chemical ghost of the machine itself. It is a closed loop of necrotic alchemy, where the waste products of obsolete industry have become the primary substrate for a new, terrible genesis.

First, there are the **Oxide-Lichens**. They are not green, nor do they photosynthesize; light is a myth to them. They are pale, waxy scabs that bloom exclusively upon the weeping seams of the upper vaults. They are shaped by negative space, growing into the exact geometry of corroded bolt-heads and fractured gaskets. To touch them is to feel the texture of wet velvet stretched over iron. They feed on the valence electrons of rusting metal, accelerating the decay of the cathedral even as they bind it together. They are the lungs of this place, breathing in oxidation and exhaling a faint, sweet-smelling vapor that contributes to the metallic taste of the air. They do not grow toward anything; they simply *occur*, spreading with the inevitability of a stain, mapping the moisture gradients of the dark with their own blind, fleshy topography.

Beneath them, in the slick micro-climates of the floor-grating, dwell the **Slag-Worms**. They are translucent, segmentless ribbons of protein and silicone, evolved—or perhaps synthesized—from the polymerized concrete and synthetic lubricants that saturate the lower strata. They possess no eyes, no mouths in any vertebrate sense. Instead, they absorb nutrients through their entire dermis, osmosing the chemical soup of the sump directly into their gelatinous bodies. They move with a viscosity that matches their environment, indistinguishable from the condensation trails they follow. They are not scavengers in the active sense; they are more like mobile filters, drifting through the labyrinth of fused conduits, processing the heavy metals and plasticizers into harmless, inert biomass. When they die, they do not rot; they calcify, becoming yet another layer of the floor, adding their microscopic weight to the geological pressure of the nave.

Deeper still, within the hollowed-out casings of dormant turbines and collapsed gearboxes, exist the **Resonance-Mites**. These are creatures of vibration rather than flesh. They are barely biological, existing at the threshold of matter and energy, tuned to the infinitesimal ticks of thermal expansion that mark the cathedral’s only heartbeat. They cluster in the nodes of stress, feeding on the kinetic potential of settling metal. They are silent, invisible to the touch, perceived only as a localized density in the air, a thickening of the dark. They are the nervous system of the corpse, sensing the structural fatigue of the god-machine, translating the groans of cooling iron into a biological imperative. They do not reproduce; they divide when the tension becomes too great, splitting like atoms to distribute the load.

And then, there is the **Mycelium of Memory**. It is not a fungus, though it mimics one. It is a network of filamentous crystals that threads through the vitrified slag, connecting the oxide-lichens to the slag-worms to the resonance-mites. It does not transfer nutrients; it transfers *pattern*. It encodes the history of the machinery’s failure into its crystalline lattice, a molecular archive of entropy. To disturb it is to release a puff of spores that carry the scent of ozone and old grease, a sensory echo of the factory’s final moments. This mycelium is the closest thing the Sump-Cathedral has to a soul: a distributed, unconscious awareness of its own obsolescence, growing patiently in the dark, weaving the debris into a coherent, if morbid, whole.

None of these beings possess intent. None possess desire. They are not surviving; they are *expressing*. They are the vocabulary of a language spoken only by dead things. In the oppressive heat and absolute blackness, they have achieved a perfection that life in the sun never could: total integration with their ruin. They do not fight the decay; they are the shape it takes. They are the penitents for whom this cathedral was built, though they know neither sin nor salvation. They simply *are*, a stillness made flesh, a silence given form, waiting in the hot dark for nothing at all, changing at the speed of rust, becoming, endlessly and perfectly, the monument they inhabit.

To witness them is to understand that life, stripped of ambition, stripped of light, stripped of noise, does not cease. It merely slows. It densifies. It becomes the architecture of its own ending. And in that ending, in the Sump-Cathedral of the Third Intake, it finds a peace so profound it feels like holiness.
