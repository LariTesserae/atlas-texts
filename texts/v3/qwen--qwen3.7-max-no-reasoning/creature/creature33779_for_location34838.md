The inhabitants of the Cloud-Sump do not live *on* the landscape; they are metabolized by it. In this place where the boundary between geology, biology, and engineering has been dissolved by heat and moisture, residency is less about occupation and more about integration. To exist here is to become a component in a vast, humid circuit.

First, there are the **Thermal-Weepers**. They are the primary fauna of the sump, though "fauna" feels like an insufficient categorization. Evolved from blind cave salamanders that migrated upward into the steam-choked valleys eons ago, they have lost all pigmentation, becoming translucent ribbons of pale muscle and nerve. They do not hunt. They lack eyes, jaws, and stomachs in any traditional sense. Instead, their skin is a hyper-permeable membrane, riddled with sensory cilia that detect minute differentials in temperature and barometric pressure. They drape themselves over the warm ceramic manifolds and the cool schist outcrops alike, acting as living thermal bridges. By pressing their bodies against the machinery, they facilitate a micro-exchange of heat that the passive turbines cannot achieve alone. In return, the waste warmth accelerates their cellular division. They are born from gelatinous egg-clusters attached to intake valves, and when they die, their bodies dissolve instantly into nutrient-rich slurry that feeds the biofilm. They are the lubricant of the ecosystem, ensuring that no surface becomes too hot or too cold for life to persist.

Then there are the **Condensate Moths**. These are not insects of flight, but of ballast. Their wingspans are immense, spanning thirty centimeters, yet the wings are thick, velvety, and heavy with absorbed vapor. They cannot fly in the dry sense; they drift on the artificial updrafts generated by the turbine housings, riding columns of rising warm air like spores. They are the pollinators of the machine-garden, but they do not carry pollen. They carry moisture. As they rest on the fever-warm orchids nestled in the valve assemblies, their wings release stored water directly into the flower’s specialized receptacles. The orchid, in turn, offers a nectar rich in dissolved minerals leached from the brass patina. The moths are silent, their movements sluggish and deliberate. When the turbines slow during a lull in tectonic activity, the moths settle en masse upon the cooling metal, forming a living insulation blanket that preserves the residual heat until the earth’s friction builds again. They are the thermostat of the sump.

The flora itself has ceased to be merely botanical. The **Iron-Vein Orchids** and **Copper-Mosses** have adapted to a diet of metallurgy. Their roots do not seek soil; they seek alloy. They secrete mild acids that etch microscopic grooves into the ceramic and brass, anchoring themselves while extracting trace elements essential for their bioluminescence. At night, or in the deepest folds of the perpetual twilight, these plants emit a faint, amber pulse—a biological echo of the heat that sustains them. This light is not for attraction; it is a status signal. A bright glow indicates optimal thermal flow; a dimming warns of blockage or cooling. The vegetation has become a distributed sensor network, reading the health of the machines and adjusting its own growth patterns to compensate. Vines will tighten around a vibrating pipe to dampen excess resonance; moss will thin over a clogged vent to encourage airflow. The forest tends the factory as diligently as the factory warms the forest.

And finally, there are the **Stewards**, though they would reject the title. They are human, or post-human, beings who arrived generations ago and have since been rewritten by the sump. They wear no protective gear; the wet wool air is their atmosphere now. Their skin has taken on the same green-gold sheen as the schist, colonized by symbiotic bacteria that protect against fungal rot and aid in osmoregulation. They move with the slow, fluid grace of the Weepers, understanding intuitively that haste generates friction, and friction disrupts the equilibrium. They do not repair the machines with tools; they coax them with touch and presence. A Steward might spend hours sitting against a manifold, their body heat supplementing a failing thermal gradient, or gently clearing debris from a turbine intake with fingers that have learned the precise texture of functional harmony. They speak rarely, and when they do, it is in low tones that mimic the subsonic thrum of the deep turbines. They have forgotten the concept of ownership. They understand themselves not as masters of the Cloud-Sump, but as its most complex, conscious organ.

In the Cloud-Sump, nothing lives independently. The moth needs the heat of the machine; the machine needs the insulation of the moss; the moss needs the mineral bleed of the brass; the brass needs the cleaning touch of the Steward; the Steward needs the breath of the sump. It is a closed loop of mutual dependence, a singular organism composed of metal and meat, steam and sap. To ask what lives here is to misunderstand the question. Nothing *lives* here. The Cloud-Sump itself is alive, and everything within it—from the smallest blind salamander to the grandest turbine to the quietest human—is simply a way for it to breathe.
