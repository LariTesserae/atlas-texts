The inhabitants of the Cistern-Groves are not merely residents; they are hydraulic components. To live in Aethelgard is to accept that one’s body is a vessel for the same water that fills the air, flows through the roots, and cycles through the titan-trees. They call themselves the *Silt-Singers*, though this name is a rough translation of a concept that has no dry equivalent: *Those-Who-Resonate-With-the-Flow*.

Physically, they have surrendered the rigid uprightness of terrestrial bipeds. In an atmosphere with the viscosity of shallow water, gravity is a suggestion rather than a law. The Silt-Singers are tall and impossibly slender, their bones porous and light like avian skeletons, yet reinforced with flexible cartilage to withstand the constant pressure of the wet air. They do not walk so much as undulate, using long, multi-jointed limbs that end not in grasping hands or planted feet, but in broad, webbed extremities designed to push against the density of the medium. Their movement is a slow, deliberate dance, a series of glides and drifts that mimics the arboreal cephalopods with whom they share the canopy. To move quickly here is to fight the world; to move slowly is to be carried by it.

Their skin is their primary interface with the grove. It is pale, smooth, and perpetually cool, lacking hair or sweat glands, which would be useless in the saturated air. Instead, the epidermis is permeable, capable of osmotic exchange. They drink through their arms and chests; they breathe moisture directly through pores that flutter like gills along the ribs and neck. This makes them exquisitely sensitive to the chemical composition of the rain. A Silt-Singer can taste the mineral content of a coming High Flow hours before it arrives; they can sense the sickness in a bough by pressing their cheek against the bark, feeling the disruption in the sap’s rhythm. They are never truly dry, nor do they wish to be. Dryness is synonymous with death, a desiccation of both flesh and spirit.

Their faces are serene, largely because expression as outsiders understand it is inefficient in the pearl-grey gloom. Eyes are large, dark, and lidless, protected by a transparent nictitating membrane that keeps out debris while allowing maximum light intake. These eyes see best in the bioluminescent spectrum, perceiving the pulsing fungal glow not as illumination but as language. Where humans might smile or frown, a Silt-Singer shifts their posture, changes the tension of their webbing, or alters the rate of their respiration. Communication is predominantly acoustic, but not verbal. They speak in the register of the grove itself—low-frequency hums and chest-vibrations that travel through the liquid air and into the listener’s sternum. A conversation feels less like an exchange of words and more like a shared heartbeat, a harmonic alignment of internal rhythms.

Socially, they are a people without accumulation. Because the city is alive and destined to consume its own architecture, the concept of permanent ownership is biologically impossible. One does not inherit a dwelling; one is granted occupancy by the tree’s growth cycle. When a chamber begins to close as the wood heals and expands, the inhabitant must migrate to a new hollow, leaving behind any tools or artifacts that no longer fit. This impermanence has eradicated greed but intensified memory. History is not written; it is knotted into the living bridges and sung into the resin seals. Elders are those who remember the songs of collapsed towers, whose bodies bear the tactile scars of previous dwellings. Wisdom is somatic, stored in the muscle memory of navigating spaces that no longer exist.

They possess a technology of intimacy. Their tools are grown, shaped over decades by guiding the secretion of specific fungi or the curl of a tendril. An obsidian blade is not hafted but embedded into a living handle that grows around it, sealing the stone in warm wood. When the tool breaks, it is returned to a compost-node, thanked, and allowed to rot. There is no waste, only transformation. Their knowledge of hydrology and botany is profound, yet entirely intuitive; they do not measure flow rates in numbers but in feelings of fullness and emptiness. They know when a root-mat is failing not by calculation, but because the collective song of the community develops a dissonance, a physical ache in the lungs of every Singer.

Perhaps most strangely, they have integrated the fauna into their domesticity. The translucent, ray-like beings that drift through the air are not pets or livestock, but messengers and cleaners. The Singers cultivate airborne plankton on their own skin during periods of rest, allowing the rays to graze gently upon them in a symbiotic grooming ritual. The arboreal cephalopods are treated as silent neighbors, respected for their mastery of camouflage; to startle one is considered a grave breach of etiquette. Children learn to navigate the vertical world by holding onto these creatures, drifting in their wake until their own buoyancy matures.

The Silt-Singers do not fear the end. They understand that they are temporary patterns in the water. When a Singer dies, there is no burial, no cremation. The body is placed in a dissolution-pod, a specialized cavity in the deepest roots where enzymatic fluids break down flesh into nutrient-rich slurry within days. This essence is drawn up into the vascular system of the grove, becoming leaf, resin, and rain. To die is to finally become the architecture one lived within. Grief exists, but it is quiet, a low hum of acknowledgment that the water has simply changed course.

In the Cistern-Groves, consciousness is not a solitary spark but a distributed dampness. The Singers are the grove’s way of knowing itself, just as the rain is its way of breathing. They are beautiful, melancholy, and utterly at peace, suspended in the eternal, roaring twilight, waiting patiently for the moment they will cease to be distinct and become, once again, the whole.
