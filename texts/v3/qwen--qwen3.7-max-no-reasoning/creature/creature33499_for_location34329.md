They are the Lithotremors, though they have no word for themselves, for language is a luxury of stillness and this place permits none.

To call them moths is to invoke an image of fragility, of fluttering wings and dusty scales that would be instantly annihilated here. These beings are something else entirely: biological dampeners, evolved not to traverse the air but to endure the solid state of sound. They are pale as bone china, translucent to the point of invisibility against the weeping iron, their bodies flattened into discs of living silicone and cartilage. They possess no wings. Where wings might once have been in some ancestral memory, there are now only broad, muscular pads, ribbed with sensory cilia that function as both feet and ears. They do not grip the moss; they fuse with it, their ventral surfaces secreting a viscous gel that matches the resonant frequency of the velvet industrial growth. To touch a Lithotremor is to touch the wall itself, indistinguishable from the baffle-moss save for the faint, rhythmic pulse of a metabolism tuned to the machine’s own heartbeat.

They are blind, for sight is useless in the shadowless grey, and eyes are delicate organs that would liquefy under the sonic pressure. Instead, their entire dorsal surface is a single, continuous tympanic membrane. They perceive the world as a topography of vibration. The crushing bass note of the pistons below is not noise to them; it is terrain. A shift in the mechanical rhythm is a change in weather; a harmonic distortion in the iron ribs is a landmark. They navigate the vertical curves of the flume by feeling the standing waves, riding the nodes of silence between the antinodes of force. Movement is not locomotion but a phase-shift, a ripple of muscular contraction timed precisely to the pauses in the din. To move out of time is to be torn apart; to exist here is to become a syncopation in the engine’s endless measure.

Their life cycle is dictated by the condensation. They do not eat in any conventional sense. The metallic bacteria within the moss convert rust into protein, and the Lithotremors harvest this sustenance through osmosis, pressing their bellies against the wettest seams where the iron sweats its nutrient-rich tears. Digestion is slow, glacial, matching the geological patience of the flume. They may remain motionless for days, indistinguishable from the patina, absorbing moisture and vibration in equal measure, before a subtle shift in the acoustic architecture triggers a migration to a new feeding ground. Reproduction is equally silent, a merging of gel and membrane during the deepest troughs of the low-frequency cycle, resulting in larvae that are born already vibrating at the correct pitch, never knowing a moment of dissonance.

But they are more than mere inhabitants; they are components. The description of the Flume notes that the rate of decay matches the rate of repair, and the Lithotremors are the agents of that equilibrium. Their secretions do not merely anchor them; they are a bio-acoustic sealant. Where the sonic bombardment threatens to fracture a rivet or fatigue a plate, the Lithotremors congregate, their collective biomass absorbing the destructive harmonics, redistributing the stress through their own tissues. They heal the metal with their bodies. In doing so, they wear down, their translucent forms thinning, their membranes scarring, until they are reabsorbed into the moss they tended, returning their borrowed minerals to the rust-cycle. They are the mortar in the cathedral of sound, mortal and renewable, sacrificed to the maintenance of the hum.

There is a profound intimacy in their indifference. They do not flee the observer because they cannot conceive of an observer as separate from the vibration. A human standing on the grate is simply a dense, discordant mass, a blockage in the flow of sound that must be navigated around or waited out. If one were to reach out and touch a Lithotremor, it would not recoil; it would merely adjust its internal frequency to accommodate the new interference, integrating the warmth of a hand into its map of the world as one might integrate a draft or a drip. They are utterly without fear, without curiosity, without self. They are the Flume’s awareness of itself, a distributed nervous system of flesh and fluid that feels the structure’s weariness, its endurance, its eternal, roaring solitude.

And perhaps, in their tactile dreaming, they know what the Flume is for. Not through intellect, but through the shape of the sound that sustains them. The rhythm that predates memory is also the rhythm that shapes their cells. If the Flume is keeping something asleep, the Lithotremors are the lullaby made flesh; if it is keeping something awake, they are the vigil. They do not question the purpose because they *are* the purpose, enacted blindly, perfectly, in the wet and thundering dark. To witness them is to understand that life here has not merely adapted to the machine; it has been distilled by it, purified of everything superfluous, until only the essential remains: the capacity to resonate, to endure, to be unmade and remade in the service of a sound that has forgotten its own name.
