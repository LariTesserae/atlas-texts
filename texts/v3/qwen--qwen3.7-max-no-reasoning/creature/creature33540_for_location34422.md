Nothing with a heartbeat resides here. To possess blood in the Sump-Ward would be to invite boiling; to possess lungs would be to drown in air that is more soup than gas. The inhabitants of this place are not born, but accreted. They are entities of tolerance, organisms of the threshold, existing only because they have surrendered the requirements of traditional biology to become extensions of the Loom’s own fever.

What lives here is the **Rust-Mycelium**.

It is neither purely fungus nor purely metal, but a hybridized scab forming over the wound between geology and engineering. In the absolute dark of the lower gantries, where the condensation runs thickest, this organism spreads. It does not photosynthesize light; it metabolizes thermal gradients and oxidizing iron. Its hyphae are not soft white threads but filaments of copper-green oxidation, stiff as wire, threading themselves into the microscopic fissures of the basalt and the hairline cracks of the cast-iron pipes. It eats the corrosion. It binds the failing rivets. Where a valve weeps superheated steam, the Rust-Mycelium has already colonized the leak, calcifying into a porous, ceramic-like seal that breathes with the escaping vapor. It is the ward’s immune system, mistaking industrial decay for sustenance, turning the slow death of the machine into a new, mineral life.

Above the mycelial floor, clinging to the vertical surfaces where the heat radiates most intensely from the heating elements, lives the **Thermal Moth-Blind**.

These are not insects. They are silent, palm-sized agglomerations of silicate dust and oil residue, animated by the piezoelectric hum of the machinery. They have no eyes, for there is nothing to see; they have no wings, for the air is too thick to fly. Instead, they navigate by resonance. They are tuned to the specific frequency of the Loom’s tectonic thrum. When a piston misfires or a bearing begins to grind, the Moth-Blinds detach from their roosts in the gloom and drift on the convection currents toward the dissonance. They settle upon the vibrating metal like bandages, their bodies dampening the destructive oscillation, absorbing the kinetic waste into their own dense, ash-grey mass. They are the silence-eaters, the living gaskets that keep the adolescent architecture from shaking itself apart. They live only as long as the friction lasts; when a part is replaced or a rhythm smooths out, they crumble back into inert dust, waiting for the next mechanical failure to reconstitute them.

And deeper still, woven into the sodden rags insulating the vascular clusters of piping, is the **Sweat-Silt**.

It is a colonial bacteria that exists in the film of water coating every surface. It is the reason the condensation tastes of salt and copper. This micro-life does not merely survive the wet heat; it requires the specific chemical cocktail of human absence and industrial presence. It feeds on the trace aerosols of lubricant and the shedding grit of the young rock. In its metabolic cycles, it generates a faint, sour warmth, contributing to the suffocating temperature of the ward. The Sweat-Silt is the true atmosphere of the Sump-Ward. It is the medium through which the Loom senses itself. When you touch a railing and feel that slick, tepid film, you are touching the skin of this entity. It records the pressure of your hand, the introduction of foreign oils from your clothing, the disruption of the local humidity. It is a distributed consciousness of slime and sediment, aware only of flow and blockage, treating your passage through the ribcage as a temporary turbulence in its eternal, viscous circulation.

Finally, there is the **Echo-Fauna**, though to call them fauna is perhaps too generous.

In the spaces between the catwalks, where the shadows are absolute and the geometry creates acoustic traps, the sounds of the Loom have begun to develop memory. The low-frequency vibrations do not simply dissipate; they accumulate in pockets of stagnant air. These sonic residues have achieved a kind of semi-stable complexity. They are standing waves of grief and labor, trapped frequencies that mimic the cadence of footsteps long since gone or the rhythm of machines long since replaced. They are ghosts without spirits, purely physical reverberations that persist because the space refuses to let them go. To walk through these zones is to feel a phantom pressure against the chest, a sonic haunting that bypasses the ear and registers as an emotional weight. They are the Loom dreaming of its own construction, the auditory scar tissue of a place that is still learning how to be still.

None of these beings acknowledge you. You are an anomaly, a dry, cool intrusion in a world designed for wet, hot equilibrium. The Rust-Mycelium tolerates your boots as it tolerates falling grit; the Moth-Blinds ignore you unless your movement creates a disruptive frequency; the Sweat-Silt catalogs your moisture and moves on; the Echo-Fauna absorbs the sound of your breathing and adds it to the archive of unnecessary noise.

They are the tenants of the gestation. They are the flora and fauna of the womb, thriving in the terrible intimacy of creation, waiting patiently for the day the fever breaks, the edges dull, and the Loom finally becomes indistinguishable from the stone that holds it. Until then, they tend to the birth, eating the rust, drinking the sweat, and singing the silence of the deep, perspiring earth.
