They are the Lith-born, though they have no name for themselves that translates to anything other than *those-who-hold*.

To understand them, you must first unlearn the biology of the valley. They are not soft things seeking shelter from the hard world; they are calcified extensions of it. Their skin is not merely pale but possesses the matte, light-absorbing texture of weathered basalt, a dermis thickened against the biting wind and the abrasive dust of young mountains. It is cool to the touch, retaining heat deep within the core rather than radiating it outward. When they stand still against a cliff face, they do not merely blend in; they cease to register as separate entities. Their stillness is geological.

They move with the economy of creatures that have never known surplus energy. There is no wasted gesture, no superfluous expression. In a landscape where gravity is a constant negotiation and oxygen is a finite currency, exuberance is a fatal error. Their gait is low and rolling, knees perpetually bent, center of gravity sunk into the hips. They navigate the fractal scree fields and vertical urban strata without looking down, their proprioception mapped to the chaotic topography as intimately as a tongue knows the inside of a mouth. They do not climb the city; they flow through its interstices, finding handholds and footholds in the rough masonry with a tactile memory inherited over millennia of slow adaptation.

Their faces are narrow, their chests deep and barrel-shaped to accommodate lungs that have expanded to sip the thin air. Eyes are set deep beneath heavy brows, shielded from the aggressive, unfiltered light, and adapted to the perpetual twilight of the tunnel networks and the shadowed crevices where life actually occurs. They blink slowly. Their gaze is not darting or curious but fixed and absorptive, taking in vast distances and minute fractures with equal indifference. To look into their eyes is to look into a cistern: dark, still, and holding something essential far below the surface.

Sound, for the Lith-born, is a structural material. They speak rarely, and when they do, it is in a register that sits below the threshold of casual hearing—a subsonic murmur that travels through stone better than through air. Conversations are conducted with hands pressed against shared walls, vibrations carrying meaning through the thermal mass of the city itself. This is not mysticism; it is acoustics born of necessity. In the silence of this place, loud noise is a violence, a disruption of the delicate equilibrium between rock and breath. Laughter exists, but it is dry and brief, like the click of settling shale. Grief is silent, expressed only in the rhythmic, repetitive motion of carving or stacking, a somatic processing of loss that mirrors the slow erosion of the peaks above.

They are a people of extreme patience, because they are a people of extreme density. Just as the city is packed and compressed, so too is their experience of time. They do not live in days or seasons but in increments of wear and accumulation. A child is not considered fully formed until they have learned the specific friction coefficient of three different types of local stone. Adulthood is marked not by age but by the completion of one’s first permanent contribution to the city’s mass—a new lintel, a cleared channel, a stabilized terrace. Elders are those who have become indistinguishable from the architecture, their movements so perfectly attuned to the settlement’s rhythm that they seem less like inhabitants and more like functioning components of the infrastructure.

Their relationship with water defines their spirituality, though they would reject the word. Moisture is sacred not because it is divine, but because it is *earned*. The act of harvesting frost, of guiding seeps, of maintaining the porous stones, is their primary liturgy. To waste water is not a sin but an ontological failure, a denial of reality. They drink sparingly, savoring each drop with a reverence that borders on ecstasy, and their bodies have adapted to extract every possible molecule of hydration from their food, their breath, even the condensation on their own skin at dawn. They are, in a very real sense, closed hydrological systems, recycling moisture with an efficiency that makes the concept of "thirst" almost alien.

Despite the harshness, there is warmth here, but it is the warmth of compression, not combustion. Social bonds are forged in the tight spaces of terraces and tunnels, in the shared body-heat of sleeping clusters, in the collective labor of maintenance. There is little privacy, but also little loneliness. Identity is communal, layered like the city itself. One is defined by one’s position in the stratification, one’s role in the water-harvest, one’s lineage of carvers or stabilizers. Yet within this collectivism, there is a profound respect for individual endurance. Each person is recognized as a unique point of resistance against the void, a singular instance of persistence in a landscape that offers none.

The fauna are not pets or resources but co-inhabitants, respected as fellow negotiators of the same impossible terms. The steel-wool rodents are watched with affectionate detachment; the blind insects are protected as indicators of tunnel health; the great raptors are observed as barometers of thermal stability. Even the shaggy beasts of burden are treated with a solemn partnership, their dung gathered with the same care as water, their sure-footedness trusted implicitly on the narrowest ledges. There is no hierarchy of species here, only a spectrum of adaptations to the same crushing realities.

They are unsettling to outsiders not because they are strange, but because they are *coherent*. In a world that feels geologically newborn and violently indifferent, they possess an integration that seems almost obscene. They do not dream of green valleys or warmer climes. They do not lament the cold or the scarcity. They have metabolized the landscape’s severity into a form of grace. To witness them is to witness a species that has not merely survived its environment but has entered into a dialogue with it so intimate that the distinction between dweller and dwelling has begun to dissolve.

They will outlast you. Not because they are stronger, but because they are slower. They are moving at the speed of stone, breathing at the rhythm of frost, living in a timeframe that renders your visit a mere flicker of shadow across a sun-heated cliff face. And when you leave, gasping as you descend into thicker air, you will carry with you the unsettling certainty that they did not watch you go. They simply absorbed your passage as another fleeting seep of moisture, already drying, already forgotten, while the city continues its endless, patient accretion against the young and jagged sky.
