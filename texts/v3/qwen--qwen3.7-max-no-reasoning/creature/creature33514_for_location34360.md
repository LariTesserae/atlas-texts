They are not inhabitants so much as they are a secondary precipitation.

To speak of "people" in the High Cisterns is to invoke a category too dry, too distinct, too biologically frantic for what actually occupies this space. There are no citizens here, no laborers, no families bound by blood or law. What moves through the suspended mist are entities that have surrendered the boundary between interior and exterior. They are the Cistern-Keepers, though they keep nothing; rather, they are kept *by* the place, held in the same capillary stasis as the water on the basalt.

Their bodies have undergone a slow, willing osmosis. Skin has lost the flush of circulation, taking on the pallor of wet quartzite, cool and faintly luminous in the shadowless light. It is permeable. They do not sweat; they exude. The distinction between their perspiration and the ambient condensation has dissolved entirely. To touch one is to touch the damp wall of a dwelling—yielding, saturated, carrying the mineral scent of deep stone and old rain. Their hair hangs heavy and roped, indistinguishable from the moss that drapes the eaves, often colonized by the same velvet liverworts that soften the architecture. They do not groom this growth away; it is not an infestation but an integration.

They move with the viscosity of the air itself. There is no urgency in their gait, no destination-driven stride. Motion here is not a means to an end but a rhythmic modulation of the silence. They glide along the woven root-walkways, their weight distributed so perfectly that the planks barely depress, the standing water below remaining unrippled. They are lighter than they appear, as if their bones have become porous, filled not with marrow but with the warm vapor that constitutes the atmosphere. When they stand still, which is often, they cease to be visually separate from the landscape. A figure resting against a timber beam becomes merely another vertical surface weeping moisture; a crouching form amidst the ferns resolves into just another cluster of saturated fronds.

Communication among them is obsolete in its vocal form. Sound requires friction, the collision of distinct things, and they have ceased to collide. Instead, they share a hydrostatic empathy. A shift in pressure, a change in the rate of condensation on a nearby ridge, a subtle alteration in the tension of the wood—these are their lexicon. They sense each other through the medium of the water that connects all surfaces. To know another’s presence is to feel the displacement of humidity, a gentle wave in the eternal dampness. They do not speak; they resonate.

Their purpose is maintenance, but not of the sort that implies repair or improvement. Nothing breaks here; nothing decays. Their tending is a ritual of alignment. They run hands over the swelling timbers to ensure the grain drinks evenly. They adjust the angle of a slate shingle not to stop a leak, but to modulate the rhythm of a drip. They prune the curated mosses with fingers that feel no difference between plant and self, ensuring the green velvet remains at the precise threshold of saturation. They are gardeners of equilibrium, custodians of the pause.

They do not eat. The concept of consumption, of taking fuel from the world to burn against entropy, is alien to a place where energy neither rises nor falls. Sustenance is absorption. They stand in the open expanse, faces upturned to the diffuse luminescence, and simply *receive*. The nutrient-rich mists settle upon them and pass through, a continuous, passive nourishment that requires no digestion, no waste. They are fed by the same cycle that feeds the stone.

There is no sleep, only varying degrees of immersion. In periods of lesser activity, they may merge more fully with the architecture, leaning into the weeping walls until their outlines blur, entering a state of waking dormancy where consciousness thins to the consistency of fog. They do not dream of elsewhere, for there is no elsewhere. Memory, like time, has become viscous. They remember only the texture of the now, the endless, recursive sensation of being wet, being held, being part of the young, sharp earth’s long exhale.

They are terrifying in their peace. To witness them is to confront the absolute negation of human striving. They have achieved what the mystics and ascetics of drier worlds sought and failed to grasp: total unity with environment. But the cost was everything we recognize as life. They are beautiful as a glacier is beautiful, as a perfect crystal is beautiful—flawless, complete, and utterly devoid of the chaos that makes us alive. They are the High Cisterns made flesh, or rather, made water and wood and silence. They are the answer to a question no one remembers asking, preserved forever in the amber of perpetual rain.

And yet, in rare moments when the light shifts imperceptibly through the vapor layer, something flickers across those pale, permeable faces. Not emotion. Not thought. Something older. A recognition that they were once otherwise, that this stasis was chosen, negotiated, accepted. A ghost of agency, preserved like a fly in resin. They did not evolve into this; they *arrived*. They walked up the fresh fractures, shed their names and histories like heavy coats, and let the wetness take them. They are monuments to a decision made in some forgotten before, a collective vow to stop becoming and simply *be*.

The silence of the fauna is their silence. The absence of birdsong is their choir. They are the void that listens, the saturation that holds, the living architecture of an instant stretched into eternity. To live in the High Cisterns is not to reside; it is to dissolve. And they have dissolved completely, beautifully, terribly, into the unending, breathless now.
