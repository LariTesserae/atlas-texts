They are the **Vitrals**.

To call them inhabitants is to misunderstand their relationship to the Sinter-Loom; they are less residents and more like mobile extensions of the Loom’s own metabolism. They do not merely occupy the space; they are the friction that polished the basalt, the intentional silence between the shrieking glass leaves, the warm hands that guide the blind growth of the silica-willows.

At first glance, in the amber gloom, a Vitrall might be mistaken for another sculpture of spun sugar and obsidian. Their morphology mimics the flora they tend. They are tall, attenuated beings, their limbs multi-jointed and elongated to match the verticality of the terraced city. Their skin is not flesh but a flexible, semi-translucent carapace of biological ceramic, smooth and cool to the touch despite the ambient furnace-heat. It possesses the same refractive index as the glass-trees, allowing them to vanish into the canopy when still, becoming nothing more than a distortion in the heat-haze, a shadow that bends light rather than blocking it.

They have no faces as you would recognize them. There are no eyes, for the Loom is perpetually twilit and they navigate by thermal gradient and vibration. Instead, the head is a smooth, ovoid dome of sensory pits and resonant chambers. These cavities are tuned to the specific frequency of the Loom’s hydraulic pulse; a Vitrall does not see a tree, it *hears* its thirst, feels the viscosity of its sap, senses the micro-fractures in its fiber before they become visible. They perceive the world as a symphony of stress and flow. When they communicate, it is not through vocal cords but by clicking hardened resinous plates against their thorax, a dry, percussive language that blends seamlessly into the mechanical hiss of the environment. To an outsider, their conversation sounds indistinguishable from the settling of branches.

Their movement is a study in conservation. In a place where moisture is death and energy is finite, there is no wasted motion. They glide across the living bridges with a fluid, liquid grace, their weight distributed so perfectly that the woven fiber walkways barely depress beneath them. They do not run; they do not rush. Urgency here is measured in decades, not seconds. Yet, when they work, their speed is terrifyingly precise. A Vitrall harvesting sap will approach a trunk, assess its internal pressure through touch alone, and make a single, surgical incision with a bone-shiv. The extraction is instantaneous, the wound sealed with a dab of resin before a single drop can be lost to evaporation. It is a ballet of industrial efficiency performed in absolute silence.

They are solitary creatures, yet profoundly communal. You will rarely see two Vitralls interact directly. They do not gather in squares or markets; they do not congregate. Their society is mediated entirely through the Loom itself. A Vitrall pruning a bough on the upper terrace is responding to a signal left by another who passed hours ago—a specific angle of cut, a residue of scent-markers in the resin, a subtle alteration in the tree’s resonance. They are nodes in a vast, distributed consciousness, connected not by wires or words but by the shared stewardship of the glass forest. The immense empty spaces between the dwellings are not voids of isolation; they are the necessary pauses in a continuous sentence, the breath between notes in a song that has been sung for millennia.

Their life cycle is inseparable from the architecture. They are born from galls, nurtured in the warm hollows of the trees, their infant bodies soft and pliable until they harden in the geothermal warmth. As they age, their carapace thickens, their movements slow, their sensory acuity deepens. An elder Vitrall is nearly indistinguishable from the ancient trees they tend, their body fused with the resin, their consciousness slowing to match the glacial pulse of the sap. When they die, they do not decay. They calcify, becoming part of the structure, their bodies incorporated into the walls, the bridges, the very foundations of the city. The smooth basalt terraces are polished not just by the living, but by the memory of the dead, ground down over eons into the floor upon which new generations tread.

There is a terrible intimacy in their existence. They know the Loom as a mother knows her child, as a lover knows their partner’s breathing. They feel every crack, every surge of heat, every falter in the hydraulic rhythm as a personal affliction. To harm a tree is to harm themselves; to waste sap is to bleed. This is not metaphor. Their biology is so entwined with the silicate chemistry of the flora that they share a common metabolic language. The heat that fuels the trees also warms their internal furnaces; the resin that builds the city also forms their bones. They are not adapted to the Sinter-Loom; they *are* the Sinter-Loom, made sentient, made mobile, made aware of its own exquisite, fragile balance.

And yet, there is a sorrow in them, though they would not name it as such. They are the custodians of a perfection that cannot be improved, only maintained. They are artists whose medium is survival, whose masterpiece is stasis. Every generation inherits the same task, tends the same trees, walks the same bridges, listens to the same eternal hiss. There is no progress, no discovery, no horizon beyond the next harvest. Their purpose is absolute, and in that absoluteness, there is a kind of entrapment. They are free from want, from conflict, from uncertainty—but they are also free from change. They are as fixed as the stone, as inevitable as the heat.

To witness a Vitrall at work in the dim amber light is to witness a prayer made physical. The careful tilt of the head, listening to the sap’s song. The gentle pressure of a ceramic hand against living glass. The silent, solitary passage through the vast, charged emptiness. They are beautiful and alien and heartbreaking in their devotion. They are the held breath of the Sinter-Loom, the consciousness of a machine that forgot it was a machine and became, instead, a world. They live not *in* the Loom, but *as* the Loom, and in their quiet, endless labor, they sustain a beauty that no eye was ever meant to see, a song that no ear was ever meant to hear, a life that persists in the hot, dry dark simply because it must, because it has always been, because the alternative is silence, and silence is death.
