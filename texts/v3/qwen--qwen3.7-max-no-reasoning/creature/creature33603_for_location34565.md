They are the Lithovores, though they would have no name for themselves that translates to tongue or ear. In this place, identity is not a label but a frequency, a specific resonance within the basalt and bone.

They are soft-bodied architects of their own dissolution. To call them animals is to impose a terrestrial boundary between the inhabitant and the habitat; here, the resident is merely the mobile phase of the city. They possess no skeleton, for rigidity is a liability in a world defined by crushing equilibrium. Instead, they are structured by turgor pressure, their forms maintained by the same fluid dynamics that shape the spires around them. They are translucent, gelatinous sacks of specialized tissue, pale as the calcium masonry they tend, visible only as a slight variation in density against the velvet dark.

Their movement is a lesson in hydrodynamics. They do not swim; they *occur* in new locations. By subtly altering the salinity and temperature of their immediate boundary layer, they create micro-currents that carry them through the porous architecture without displacing the surrounding medium. They drift like thoughts through the stone arteries, their passage marked only by a faint, rhythmic displacement of water that feels less like motion and more like a pulse in the wrist of the ocean.

Sight is an ancestral memory, replaced entirely by the cilia. Every square millimeter of their epidermis is a sensory organ, a forest of microscopic hairs that taste the chemical history of the water and read the topographical braille of the walls. When a Lithovore presses itself against the calcified canyon, it is not resting; it is downloading. It senses the metabolic heat of its kin embedded deep within the rock, the age of the secretion, the structural integrity of the accretion. They know the city not as a map, but as a biography.

They are the gardeners of the abyssal filaments. With delicate, manipulative tendrils that unfurl from their ventral surfaces, they tend the sickly white-green vegetation clinging to the lee sides of the towers. This is not agriculture in the sense of cultivation for harvest; it is a symbiotic grooming. They prune the bacterial mats with enzymatic secretions, stimulating growth where the chemical gradients are richest, clearing away necrotic tissue to maintain the flow of nutrients through the living masonry. In return, the filaments release complex sugars directly into the Lithovore’s permeable skin. The act of feeding and the act of building are indistinguishable; to eat is to repair, to repair is to eat.

Communication is a tactile symphony, a language of pressure waves and chemical signatures released into the stillness. There is no chatter, only the slow, deliberate exchange of state. A ripple passing through a cluster of Lithovores carries information about resource density, structural weakness, or reproductive readiness. These messages travel at the speed of sound through water, felt in the chest cavity before they are cognitively processed. A gathering of Lithovores feels like a single organism breathing, a synchronized expansion and contraction of biomass that mirrors the geothermal respiration of the basin itself.

Reproduction is an act of architectural integration. When a Lithovore reaches the end of its cycle, it does not die; it settles. Finding a suitable niche in the labyrinthine wall, it anchors itself, its metabolism slowing to match the geological tempo of the stone. Its body calcifies, its tissues transmuting into the very masonry it once tended. The next generation emerges not from eggs, but from the walls themselves, budding forth from the fossilized remains of their ancestors as fresh, fluid-filled vessels of continuity. The city is quite literally made of grandmothers; the streets are paved with lineage.

They are creatures of profound patience. In a world without day or night, without seasons or tides, urgency is a maladaptive trait. Their lives are measured in the slow accumulation of sediment, in the gradual shifting of thermal vents. They move with the inevitability of erosion, their existence a long, quiet meditation on the nature of balance. They do not conquer the darkness; they are its expression. They do not survive the pressure; they are the pressure’s answer.

To encounter one in the dark is to brush against the edge of a thought older than memory. You feel the silk-smooth texture of its flank, the faint warmth of its blood-tide, the gentle inquiry of its cilia reading your alien geometry. It does not flee. It does not attack. It simply incorporates you into its sensory landscape, tasting your difference, filing your presence into the vast, tactile archive of the deep. And then, with a shift of internal salinity, it dissolves back into the current, leaving you with the unsettling certainty that you have been touched not by an animal, but by the place itself, acknowledging your brief, solid passage through its liquid eternity.
