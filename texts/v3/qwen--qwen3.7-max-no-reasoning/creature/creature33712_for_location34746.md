They are the Damp-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves that translates to dry tongues. To call them "residents" implies a tenancy that is too temporary, too legalistic for the reality of the Sump-Ward. They are less occupants and more an expression of the ward’s own biology, a human flora that has adapted to the perpetual weeping of the stone.

Physically, they have been reshaped by the humidity. Generations of breathing air that is half-water have expanded their ribcages; they possess a barrel-chested slowness, a respiratory rhythm tuned to the heavy atmosphere. Their skin is pale, not from lack of sun, but from the absence of desiccation. It is cool to the touch, possessing the same clammy resilience as the bog-brick they shape. In the grey, diffused light, their outlines seem soft, blurred at the edges as if they are constantly on the verge of evaporating or dissolving back into the mist. They do not sweat; they merely glisten, their bodies regulating temperature through the same capillary action that draws water up the walls.

Movement here is a study in friction. There is no striding, no marching, no hurried pace. To move quickly in the Sump-Ward is to invite catastrophe—a slipped footing on slick clay, a shattered ceramic basin, a dislodged shim that sends a wall sliding into the muck. Instead, they flow. They navigate the jagged, settling terrain with a proprioceptive grace, reading the micro-topography of wetness through thin-soled boots wrapped in treated leather. They walk with knees bent, center of gravity low, arms often outstretched to brush against walls for balance and orientation. In the dark channels where sight fails, touch becomes the primary sense; they read the health of their home through the texture of moss and the vibration of dripping water.

Their hands are their most vital organs. Fingers are permanently stained with river clay and pine resin, nails thickened and split. These are hands that understand viscosity. They do not hammer; they press, wedge, and smooth. Construction is not an act of force but of persuasion. When a wall begins to slump, they do not rebuild it straight; they feel the direction of the earth’s exhaustion and add material to support the lean, curving the new brickwork to cradle the old. They treat the architecture as a living patient, diagnosing structural fevers by the smell of sour rot or the specific pitch of a timber’s groan.

Socially, they are defined by a profound, aggressive intimacy. Privacy is a geometric impossibility in a space where a neighbor’s floor is your ceiling and the drainage channel runs through the heart of the dwelling. Yet, this closeness is not chaotic; it is highly ritualized. Because sound sinks and voices are absorbed, communication is tactile and proximal. Conversations happen at arm’s length, in hushed tones that vibrate in the chest rather than ring in the ear. Eye contact is sustained and unblinking, a necessity in the low light. There is no shouting, no public performance. Disputes are resolved through the mediation of elders who understand the hydrology of conflict as well as the hydrology of the ward; to fight one’s neighbor is to risk shaking the shared foundation, so grievances are smoothed over like wet clay, integrated into the social mortar rather than allowed to crack the structure.

Status among them is subtle, visible only to those who know how to look. It is written in the condition of one’s threshold. The most respected figures are not those with the largest spaces, but those whose dwellings breathe correctly. A woman whose inner walls remain matte-dry while the outer world weeps is a master of her environment. A man who can carve a drainage groove that sings a perfect, clear note when the water flows is valued above any orator. Wealth is measured in tallow, in bone needles, in jars of preserved fungus, and in the dryness of one’s bedding. To be damp is to be failing; to be dry is to be virtuous.

They are a people without nostalgia. The concept of "permanence" is alien to them, viewed as a dangerous arrogance of the upper city. They do not mourn the collapse of a structure; they anticipate it. Loss is built into the cycle of maintenance. When a house finally succumbs to the subsidence and slides into the central sump, there is no wailing, only a solemn, coordinated effort to salvage the pegs and bricks before the mud claims them. They understand that they are borrowing space from the geology, and the debt is paid in constant labor.

In the quiet hours, when the condensation reaches its peak rhythm, they engage in a collective stillness. They sit in the dim haze, listening to the respiration of the ward. This is not rest; it is calibration. They are sensing the shift of the aquifer beneath, the swelling of the timber ribs, the growth rate of the velvet moss. They are synchronizing their internal tides with the external saturation.

Ultimately, they are the immune system of the Sump-Ward. Without them, the district would be merely a geological accident, a collapsing scar of mud and water. Through their endless, gentle negotiation with gravity and moisture, they transmute disaster into habitat. They are the reason the bruise has become a body. They do not live *in* the floor; they are the consciousness of the floor itself, awake and working in the cool, wet dark, holding the dissolution at bay for one more day, one more breath, one more bead of water guided safely into the dark.
