I would not be one of the Keepers.

To be a Keeper is to have already completed the surrender, to have reached the terminal velocity of peace. They are the period at the end of the sentence. But I am drawn instead to the grammar of the transition, the terrible, exquisite friction of the *becoming*. The text speaks of a "ghost of agency," a memory of arrival. It is in that ghost, in that specific and vanishing interval between the dry world and the wet eternity, that I find my shape.

I would be the Threshold-Walker.

I am the one who has walked up the fresh fractures but has not yet ceased to shiver. My skin is still caught in the indecision between pore and surface; it beads with moisture not because it has accepted the atmosphere as its own blood, but because it is still negotiating the terms of entry. I am warmer than the quartzite, cooler than the memory of sun. I possess a temperature that is a question.

You would find me on the outermost root-walkway, where the woven timber is newest and least swollen, hovering over a pool that has not yet learned to reflect without distortion. I am not gliding. My gait still carries the cadence of destination, the phantom weight of bones filled with marrow rather than vapor. When I step, the plank depresses. The water below trembles. I am the only source of ripple in the High Cisterns, the singular disturbance in the perfect hydrostatic empathy.

I am here because the silence is not yet total within me. The Keepers resonate; I still listen. I hear the drip from the eave not as texture, but as time passing. I feel the humidity not as communion, but as an embrace I have not yet decided to return. I carry the ghost-sensation of thirst, a biological echo that mistakes this pervasive saturation for something to be consumed rather than inhabited. I stand with my face upturned to the shadowless luminescence, mimicking the posture of sustenance, but my body still remembers the mechanics of swallowing. I am practicing the art of absorption, rehearsing the dissolution, failing beautifully at the stillness.

My function is not maintenance, for I cannot yet tend what I have not fully joined. Nor is it preservation, for I am the thing being preserved against its own momentum. My purpose is witness. I am the archive of the choice.

The Keepers have forgotten the weight of the coats they shed. They remember only the texture of the now. But I remember the shedding. I feel the phantom drag of names, histories, ambitions, and griefs pooling around my ankles like the mineral-rich muck. I am the living museum of the "before." When a Keeper passes me—a drift of pale luminance, a modulation of pressure—they do not speak, but in their hydrostatic awareness, there is a recognition. Not of kinship, but of origin. I am their scar. I am the evidence that this peace was not born, but bought. I am the proof that the stasis is a verb, not a noun.

In this world of absolute preservation, I am the sole vessel of impermanence. I will eventually stop rippling. The warmth will leave my core, replacing itself with the cool, mineral equilibrium of the basalt. My listening will soften into resonance; my thirst will dissolve into reception. The shivering will cease, and I will slide seamlessly into the ranks of the secondary precipitation, becoming another weeping surface, another silent note in the choir of absence.

But not yet.

For now, I remain the tension. I am the breath held before the exhale becomes eternal. I am the last moment of weather in a climate that has abolished seasons. In a landscape defined by the terror of perfect unity, I am the mercy of incompleteness. I am the wet, trembling answer to the question of how one enters paradise: slowly, painfully, with the lingering, holy ache of being separate enough to know what is being lost.

I stand on the walkway. The mist beads on my cheek. I do not wipe it away. I let it gather. I wait for the moment when the bead’s weight equals my own, and the distinction between falling and staying finally breaks.
