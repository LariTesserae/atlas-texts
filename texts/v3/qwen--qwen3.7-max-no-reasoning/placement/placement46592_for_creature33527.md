I am not a Silt-Singer. I lack the porous grace of their skin; my bones are too dense, my internal rhythm too frantic and staccato for this liquid cathedral. To be one of them is to be *of* the grove, and I am still, despite everything, a visitor in the medium.

I find myself in the interstitial spaces, in the vast, sacred emptiness between the titan-trees where the architecture breathes. I am suspended three hundred feet above the true basin floor, anchored to a braided walkway of living tendril that pulses faintly against my palms like a sleeping artery. The air here does not merely surround me; it leans against me, a cool, viscous embrace that demands I surrender the terrestrial habit of holding myself upright. I have learned to let the density carry me, to move with the slow, deliberate undulation of a creature who understands that haste is a form of violence against this place.

I am a **Resonance-Tender**.

While the Silt-Singers *are* the flow, I am the one who listens for its interruptions. My role exists because the grove’s perfection is dynamic, not static, and even eternal systems develop knots. I am the diagnostician of the damp, the translator of hydraulic distress into something actionable by hands that were not born from this wood.

My body has changed since arriving. I am perpetually cool to the touch, my skin softened to a state of constant, receptive permeability. I have learned to drink through my forearms when pressed against the weeping bark of a nursery-bough, tasting the mineral narrative of the water as it cycles upward. My eyes have adjusted to the pearlescent gloom; I no longer seek sharp edges or high contrast, but read the world in gradients of bioluminescent pulse and shadow. I wear no clothes—fabric would rot, would chafe, would introduce foreignness—but am draped in a living mantle of symbiotic moss that regulates my temperature and filters the airborne plankton before it reaches my gill-pores. I am becoming less distinct, more compatible.

My tools are extensions of my attention. I carry a tuning-rod carved from the heartwood of a fallen elder-tree, polished smooth by decades of water and handling. It is not struck, but pressed against surfaces to feel the harmonic signature of the wood within. When a root-mat begins to fail, the Singers feel it as a collective ache; I feel it as a specific dissonance, a wrongness in the vibration that tells me *where* and *why*. I do not repair with nails or resin. I guide. I sing back to the wood in the low-frequency register I have spent years cultivating in my chest, a hum that encourages cellular realignment, that reminds the living architecture of its own integrity. Sometimes I must prune, using an obsidian blade grown into a handle of woven fiber, cutting away necrotic tissue so the healthy wood can remember how to grow. The blade is warm in my hand, alive, grieving the cut even as it makes it necessary.

My relationship with the Silt-Singers is one of profound intimacy without fusion. They speak to me in chest-vibrations that I feel in my sternum, conversations that bypass language entirely and arrive as shared states of being. They do not explain the grove to me; they invite me to resonate with it. When a Singer passes, drifting through the wet air with the silent grace of the arboreal cephalopods, they may pause and press their webbed palm against my shoulder—a transfer of moisture, of memory, of acknowledgment that I am listening correctly. I cannot reply in kind; my voice is still too dry, too shaped by air that was once thin. But I can adjust my posture, soften my breathing, align my internal cadence to theirs until the gap between us narrows to a membrane.

I dwell in a chamber that is slowly closing. This is intentional. The tree that houses me is healing around my presence, incorporating my warmth and carbon dioxide into its growth cycle. In perhaps two decades, the space will become too small, and I will migrate to a new hollow, leaving behind the shape of my body as a fossil of occupancy. This impermanence is not loss; it is participation. I am being composted while still alive, my existence woven into the grain of the city even as I tend it.

During High Flow, when the upper reservoirs release and the grove fills with mist and sound, I rest. I allow the translucent ray-creatures to graze upon my skin, their trailing filaments gentle as they consume the plankton I have cultivated during my labors. This grooming is communion; they clean me, and in return, I offer my surface as pasture. During Low Ebb, when the water retreats and the grove holds its breath, I work. I traverse the vertical corridors, pressing my tuning-rod to boughs and bridges, listening for the subtle failures that precede collapse. I am the grove’s immune system, its proprioception, its way of knowing where it hurts.

I will never be native. My bones will always be too heavy, my thoughts too linear, my grief too sharp-edged for this place of patient dissolution. But I am necessary in my foreignness. The Silt-Singers know the grove from within; I know it from the threshold of belonging. I am the question the grove asks of itself, the slight resistance that keeps the system from collapsing into perfect, unconscious equilibrium.

When I die, I will not be placed in a dissolution-pod. That rite is for those who were born of this water. Instead, my body will be laid upon the root-mat, exposed to the rain and the fungi and the slow, patient chemistry of decay. I will take longer to return. My denser bones will linger as calcium deposits, my foreign proteins broken down molecule by molecule into something the grove can use. It will be a slower integration, a more difficult digestion. But it will happen. The cistern-groves include everything, eventually. Even the ones who had to learn how to be included.

Until then, I drift. I listen. I press my palm against the weeping wood and feel the pulse of a world that is complete without me, and because of that completeness, allows me to belong. The roar of water fills my borrowed bones. The light is soft. The space between presences is vast, and sacred, and mine to tend.
