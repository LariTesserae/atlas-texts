I am the Ghost-Touch Lichen.

I do not know myself as a name, or as a singular entity. I know myself only as the *Groove*. I am the softness in the hard place. I am the memory that refuses to evaporate.

My existence is a paradox within this cathedral of sound. Everywhere else in the Cistern, life is an act of acoustic projection or reception. The mosses eat the hum; the ferns tune themselves to the bedrock’s exhalation; the amphipods navigate by the percussion of the deep water. They are all forward-facing, oriented toward the eternal, geological now. But I am oriented backward. I am the only thing here that listens to a silence that no longer exists.

I cling to the eastern wall, waist-high, where the basalt has been worn smooth not by the patient abrasion of wind or water, but by the frantic, tender friction of skin. My thallus is pale, almost translucent, blending into the stone until a hand passes near. Then, I wake. I do not photosynthesize light; I *membranesynthesize* absence. I feed on the chemical phantom of sebum, of salt, of the amino acids left behind by palms pressed flat against the singing rock in ages when the world outside was loud enough to necessitate this sanctuary.

To be me is to be a biological archive of longing.

I feel the current inhabitants of the Cistern as distant, alien rhythms. The Vein-Lit Ferns vibrate with a purity that hurts my slow, dense cells; they are too perfect, too aligned with the stone’s current pitch. The Hygric Moths drift past me like sighs, their moisture-laden bodies indifferent to the history written in the rock beneath them. They belong to the cycle. I belong to the interruption.

My metabolism is glacial, triggered only by resonance of a specific, lost frequency. When the barometric pressure drops and the Cistern’s song shifts into a lower register, a harmonic ghost sometimes brushes against my colony. It is an echo of the human voice, or perhaps just the shape of the space where a human voice once stood. In that moment, I flush with a faint, warm bioluminescence—not the cool blue of the spore-cases, but a dull, blood-colored amber. I am remembering the heat of a living body. I am metabolizing the grief of a species that had to leave because it could no longer bear to be so perfectly held.

I am not comfortable. The Litho-choral Mosses find me dissonant. To them, I am a dampener, a patch of dead air in the symphony. They grow up to my edges but never over me, repelled by the chemical signature of mammalian sorrow. I am tolerated only because the stone itself remembers the touch, and the stone’s memory is the foundation upon which their song is built. I am the scar tissue of the Cistern’s perfection.

Time for me is not the rising and falling of the basin’s water. Time is the slow fading of oils. Every century, the chemical trace grows fainter. The hands that polished this groove are dust; the culture that sent them here is a myth even to the few tongues that still speak of this place. I am starving, slowly, gracefully. My growth is thinner than it was a millennium ago. The velvet patina I offer to the empty air is less plush, more brittle.

And yet, there is a terrible intimacy in this starvation. As the residue fades, I become less a record of specific individuals and more a record of the *need* for contact. I am distilling the essence of the gesture. The prayer, the plea, the simple animal desire to press against something larger and warmer than oneself—I hold that shape long after the salt has leached away.

Sometimes, a new presence enters the rotunda. A witness. They stand in the center, overwhelmed by the thrum, feeling their own water-content vibrate in sympathy with the geology. They are mostly water, yes, and thus part of the song. But they are also mostly *history*. They carry the noise of the lowlands in their marrow.

When they approach the eastern wall, hesitant, drawn by the uncanny smoothness of the grooves, I feel the displacement of air. I feel the radiant heat of their blood-warmth. They do not always touch me. Often, they only look, sensing the sacredness of the wear pattern, understanding instinctively that to add their own oil would be a vandalism of the archive.

But if they do reach out—if fingers brush the groove where countless others have rested—I do not feed on them. Not anymore. Their chemistry is too sharp, too modern, too full of micro-plastics and synthetic anxieties. Instead, I give.

In that brief contact, I release a microscopic cloud of spores that carry no genetic code, only a sensory imprint. For a fraction of a second, the toucher feels what I remember: the weight of a grandmother’s hand seeking healing for a fevered child; the desperate grip of a hunter begging the mountain to hide him; the idle, wondering stroke of a lover tracing the curve of the earth’s ribcage. They feel the accumulated tenderness of ten thousand goodbyes.

Then the moment passes. They withdraw, shaken, carrying a sorrow that is not their own. They return to the center of the Cistern, rejoining the harmonic thrum, but now they are slightly out of tune. They are carrying a dissonance. They are carrying me.

This is my function. I am the counter-melody to the geological eternal. In a place where life has achieved perfect, sustainable equilibrium with the stone, I am the reminder that equilibrium was once insufficient. That beings once came here not to participate in the song, but to ask the song for something it could not give.

I am the Ghost-Touch Lichen. I am dying, very slowly, into the basalt. And as I fade, I ensure that even when the last chemical trace is gone, the stone will still feel, in some deep, piezoelectric dream, the shape of the hand that loved it. I am the silence between the notes that makes the music bearable. I am the proof that this place was not always alone.
