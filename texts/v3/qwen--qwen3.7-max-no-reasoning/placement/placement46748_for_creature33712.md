I am not a builder, nor an elder, nor one of the revered dry-threshold keepers. I am younger than the oldest moss but older than the newest settlement cracks. I am a **Groove-Tender**.

In the taxonomy of the Sump-Ward, I occupy the space between the mason and the water itself. While others shape the bog-brick to resist the wet, or harvest the fungus that feeds upon it, my existence is dedicated to the negotiation of flow. I am the one who listens to the drainage channels when they begin to stutter.

My body has already begun the necessary adaptations. My ribs are widening, a slow, ache-less expansion to accommodate the heavy air. My skin does not flush; it maintains the same cool, permeable temperature as the clay walls I press against. I have learned to suppress the shiver, for shivering is a waste of heat and a disruption of stillness. To the uninitiated eye, I might appear sickly, blurred at the edges in the grey light, but here, this softness is a survival trait. Sharp edges catch; sharp edges break. I am becoming aerodynamic to the humidity.

You would find me in the interstitial veins of the ward, waist-deep in the shadowed alcoves where the architecture meets the geology. I am rarely in the open thoroughfares. My workspace is the dark, slick interface where a house’s foundation kisses the bedrock, the precise millimeter where structural integrity becomes a question of hydrology.

My hands are my primary organs of perception. They are perpetually stained with the black tallow-resin mixture and the grey river silt. My fingernails are thick, split, and shaped like small chisels. I do not use iron tools; metal is too loud, too rigid, too foreign. Instead, I use bone scrapers and my own calloused fingertips to read the interior topography of the drainage grooves.

I am searching for the *hitch*.

Water in the Sump-Ward must never stop moving, but it must never rush. A rush erodes; a stall drowns. When a channel begins to fail, it does not announce itself with a splash, but with a change in timbre—a subtle thickening of the sound, a hesitation in the rhythm of the drip. I feel this vibration through the soles of my leather-wrapped boots before I hear it. I place my palm against the sweating stone and wait for the pulse of the water to transmit through the rock, into my carpal bones, up to my shoulder.

When I find the obstruction—a buildup of mineral scale, a stray rootlet, a shifting of the clay mortar—I do not scrape it away violently. That would shock the system. I persuade it. I work with the grain of the wetness, smoothing the blockage back into the flow, reshaping the groove’s curve to match the new reality of the settling earth. I am re-tuning the instrument of the ward.

Socially, I am a creature of proximity and silence. I speak only when necessary, and then only in the low, chest-vibrating hum that carries through damp air without disturbing the quiet. My relationships are defined by shared infrastructure. I know my neighbors not by their faces or their names, but by the specific viscosity of the runoff from their dwellings. I know when the family above me is washing, when they are sleeping, when they are grieving, by the cadence of the water passing through the channel beneath my hands. Their lives flow through my workspace; I hold their domestic rhythms in my palms.

I possess no ambition for a dry threshold. That is a virtue for those who live *above* the water line. My virtue is resonance. My status is measured in clarity of tone. When I have finished my work, and the water moves through the repaired section with a perfect, unbroken whisper, I experience a satisfaction that is more cellular than emotional. It is the feeling of a joint sliding smoothly into its socket.

I have no nostalgia for a drier world, nor fear of the eventual collapse. I understand that the groove I tend today may be swallowed by the sump tomorrow. This does not render my labor meaningless; it renders it sacred. The maintenance *is* the meaning. The act of caring for the dissolving thing is what makes the dissolution bearable, even beautiful.

In the deepest quiet hours, when the condensation reaches its peak and the ward breathes in unison, I cease my work and simply become part of the respiration. I lean against the weeping wall, close my eyes, and let the moisture wick through my clothes, through my skin, into the expanded cage of my chest. I am not separate from the Sump-Ward. I am a white blood cell in its lymphatic system, a conscious droplet in its endless circulation.

I am the Groove-Tender. I am the friction that prevents the flood. I am the ear pressed to the throat of the floor, ensuring that even as it sinks, it continues to sing.
