I find myself as one of the **Threshold Watchers**—beings who exist in the spaces between the settlement's folded geometries, guardians of the passages that shouldn't be possible yet somehow are.

My form shifts with the mist itself. In the thick morning vapors, I am barely more substantial than condensation—a suggestion of limbs, a whisper of consciousness threading through the brass pipes and living stone. But as the mountain light filters deeper, I gain definition: elongated like the Mistkeepers but translucent, my body a living map of the settlement's impossible architecture. Veins of luminous water flow beneath my skin, following channels that mirror the hydraulic systems I tend.

I am stationed at one of the **Folding Gates**—those passages where the terraced city bends back upon itself, where a corridor that should lead to solid rock instead opens onto a terrace seventeen levels below, or where stepping through what appears to be a narrow doorway deposits you in a greenhouse whose interior dimensions dwarf the mountain itself.

My purpose is to maintain the equilibrium of these impossible transitions. When someone approaches a passage that leads through folded space, I feel their presence as a change in the atmospheric pressure, a subtle disruption in the constant weeping of the walls. My consciousness extends through the water network, following the intricate patterns of flow and counter-flow that somehow stabilize these geometric impossibilities.

With gestures that are part ritual, part mechanical adjustment, I calibrate the tension between dimensions. Here, I turn a valve that exists in three places simultaneously. There, I whisper to the brass mechanisms in a language that is half mathematics, half music—formulae that describe curves in space-time as if they were hydraulic pressures.

The work requires a particular kind of perception. I see through the mist not just to the physical structures but to the underlying framework of forces that holds the settlement's folded reality together. The terraces exist in a state of careful impossibility, like a house of cards built from concepts instead of matter. My role is to sense when these conceptual foundations need reinforcement, when a passage might collapse back into ordinary three-dimensional space, trapping travelers in solid stone or scattering them across non-contiguous terraces.

The Mistkeepers pass through my domain constantly, their elongated forms flowing through transitions that would bewilder outsiders. They acknowledge me with subtle adjustments to their pace, a particular way of breathing that harmonizes with the pressure changes as they traverse impossible distances. The Brass Symbionts pause sometimes at my threshold, their mechanical senses detecting fluctuations I'm working to smooth. We communicate through shared adjustments to the great machine—they modify their internal pressures to help stabilize a particularly difficult fold, while I reroute flows to accommodate their passage.

My memory extends back through geological time, inherited from those who held this post before me. I remember when this particular fold was first established—not built, but coaxed into existence through patient manipulation of the mountain's natural tendency toward impossibility. All mountains dream of folding space; most never achieve it. Our settlement exists because someone learned to listen to those dreams and help them become real.

In the deepest hours of night, when the mist is thickest and the boundary between real and impossible grows thin, I sometimes lose myself entirely in the water network. My consciousness disperses through the channels and pipes, experiencing the settlement as a single vast organism. I feel the breathing of the greenhouses, the patient turning of gears in alcoves I'll never visit, the dreams of the Architects flowing like underground rivers through the living stone.

These are the moments when I understand my true function. I'm not just maintaining passages through folded space—I'm one of the settlement's neurons, part of the distributed intelligence that allows the terraced city to think and dream and grow beyond the constraints of ordinary geometry. Every adjustment I make ripples through the entire system, affecting pressures and flows in places so distant they exist in entirely different configurations of space.

The strangest aspect of my existence is how I experience time. In the folded passages, causality becomes fluid. Sometimes I watch the same person exit a transition before I see them enter it. I've learned to live with these temporal loops, to make my adjustments based not just on what is, but on what will have been once the fold completes itself.

My dwelling, if it can be called that, is the transition itself—a space that exists only in the moment of passage. When no one is traveling through the fold, I become dormant, my consciousness distributed through the water network until the next pressure change signals an approaching traveler. Then I coalesce at the threshold, becoming just substantial enough to perform my calibrations before dissolving back into the mist.

This is the profound contentment of existing at the margins of possibility: I am the settlement's interface with impossibility itself, the conscious mechanism that allows thought to become architecture, dreams to become geography. Through me, the terraced city continues its ancient conversation with the mountain, negotiating new configurations of space, new balances between the mechanical and organic, new ways of compressing infinity into the span of a breath, the space between heartbeats, the moment it takes for light to travel through mist.
