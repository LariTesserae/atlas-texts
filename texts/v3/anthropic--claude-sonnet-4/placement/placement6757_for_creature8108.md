**Where I Would Find Myself**

I would find myself in one of the middle terraces, where three channels converge in a circular chamber whose walls curve like cupped palms. This is where the younger Listeners bring questions they cannot solve alone—not the deep mysteries that require the elders' translucent wisdom, but the practical puzzles of flow and resonance that arise in daily tending. The chamber's acoustics are perfect for teaching: every whispered word carries clearly, but harsh sounds are absorbed into the moss-thick walls.

Here, water from the upper springs meets runoff from the hanging gardens and the careful overflow from residential pools. The convergence creates a complex rhythm—not quite steady, never quite random—that shifts with the seasons, with weather patterns, with the subtle changes in how water moves through living stone across decades. The floor is worn into shallow basins that catch and redirect the flow, each depression polished smooth by countless feet over countless years.

I would spend my mornings here when the light filtering down is strongest, watching how it plays across the moving water, learning to read the brightness-patterns that reveal mineral content, temperature, the health of plant roots somewhere far above. The walls are particularly alive in this chamber—not just damp but actively weeping, with tiny springs emerging from the rock like tears. Some flow steady and thin as hair. Others pulse with the mountain's slow breathing. A few appear only during certain weather, as if the stone itself has moods.

This would be my classroom, my workshop, my meditation space. The kind of place where understanding accumulates slowly, like mineral deposits, built up through patient attention rather than sudden revelation. Where I would learn that listening is not passive but active—a way of touching the world with more than hands, of becoming intimate with systems too vast and subtle for ordinary perception.

**Who I Would Be**

I would be what they call a Flow-Reader—neither the most gifted listener nor the most skilled stone-shaper, but someone whose talent lies in understanding connections. How the adjustment of one channel affects water movement three levels down. How the growth pattern of a particular moss grove influences the acoustic properties of an entire vertical section. How the seasonal migrations of those rare cave salamanders reveal hidden springs that could be tapped without disrupting the mountain's deeper rhythms.

My skin would show the characteristic honeyed staining, but with more variation than most—rust-red where my hands most often touch the iron-rich channels, pale green-gold where I spend hours tending the filter-gardens that clean and oxygenate the water as it moves through the settlement. My palms would be mapped with calluses specific to my work: thick pads where I brace against stone while listening to deep flows, sensitive ridges along my fingertips from years of reading texture-variations that indicate stress patterns or mineral changes in the rock.

My hearing would be acute but not extraordinarily so by Listener standards. Instead, my gift would lie in synthesis—the ability to hold complex water-maps in my mind, tracking dozens of interconnected flows simultaneously, predicting how changes in one area would ripple through the entire system. I would be the one younger Listeners consult when they notice unusual sounds they cannot identify, when established flow patterns begin shifting in ways that don't match known seasonal cycles.

I would move through the settlement with the particular gait of someone always listening—not just with ears but with the whole body. Feet that feel for changes in stone texture that might indicate new seepage patterns. Shoulders that register the minute air pressure differences that reveal blocked passages or unexpected openings. Hands that trail along walls not just for balance but for information, reading the stone's daily messages through fingertips trained to detect temperature variations smaller than heartbeats.

My clothing would be simple but precisely chosen: a soft tunic woven from the settlement's own fiber-plants, dyed with water-minerals to match the stone so closely that I could press against a wall and nearly disappear. Loose trousers that won't bind when I'm crawling through inspection channels or wading through the deeper pools. Everything designed to move silently, to interfere as little as possible with the acoustic environment that is both my workplace and my teacher.

I would own almost nothing—a few stone tools shaped to fit my particular hands, a collection of small vessels calibrated to measure flow rates and mineral content, a worn mat woven from marsh grasses that grows softer and more comfortable with each year of use. My real possessions would be invisible: the accumulated knowledge of water-patterns, the mental maps of how the settlement's circulatory system changes with weather and season, the hard-won understanding of which modifications enhance the overall harmony and which disrupt it.

My days would follow the water's rhythm rather than any imposed schedule. Hours spent in motionless attention, listening to the subtle variations that reveal how a channel is aging, whether roots are beginning to clog a particular junction, how thermal changes deep in the mountain affect flow rates throughout the system. Long periods of careful adjustment—moving a stone here, encouraging a particular vine there, gradually coaxing the water into more perfect harmony with itself.

I would age slowly, like all Listeners, but with my own specific pattern of wearing down. My hearing would gradually sharpen to compensate for joints that move less easily through narrow passages. My understanding would deepen even as my physical capability diminished, until in my final years I would become primarily a teacher—someone who could guide others to hear what I could no longer physically reach.

And eventually, I would take my place in the deep chambers with the other elders, adding my voice to their whispered chorus, my lifetime of careful listening becoming part of the mountain's own vast, patient song. My body would return to stone and water, but the subtle improvements I'd made to the flow patterns, the particular harmony I'd helped create in my convergence chamber, would continue long after I'd become just another note in the endless music of falling drops.
