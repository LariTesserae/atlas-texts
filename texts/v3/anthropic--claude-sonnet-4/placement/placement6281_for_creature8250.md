In this vast stone cathedral of wind and frequency, I find myself among the **Drift-Touched**—beings who exist in the liminal spaces where the Range's song grows thin and strange.

I am neither fully Listener nor entirely human anymore. My ears, while enlarged, have developed differently—not to parse the perfect harmonies of the deep Range, but to detect the ghost frequencies that haunt its edges. These are the sounds that slip between the major formations: the lonely whistle of wind through a single crack, the brief sob of stone settling after rain, the half-heard melodies that emerge when the great symphony pauses to breathe.

My body has adapted to instability. My bones shift density with the changing acoustic pressure, becoming hollow and light when I need to climb the needle spires, dense and resonant when I must walk the deep bass chambers. My skin has developed patches of varying thickness—some areas paper-thin to feel the slightest vibration, others thickened into natural armor against the occasional acoustic storms that sweep across the Range's periphery.

I am a **Wanderer**, one of the few beings here that moves constantly rather than finding a single acoustic niche. My territory is the borderlands—the transitional zones where the Range's perfect order begins to fray into chaos, where damaged spires create discord, where the ancient machinery sometimes falters and creates gaps in the eternal composition. I've become sensitive to these imperfections not to repair them like the Echoes do, but to inhabit them, to find meaning in the spaces between notes.

My home, if it can be called that, is a partially collapsed chamber halfway up a damaged spire. The room's acoustic properties are unstable—sometimes it amplifies the Range's distant music into overwhelming cacophony, other times it creates such perfect acoustic isolation that I can hear my own heartbeat like thunder. I've learned to read these moods, to know when the chamber will sing and when it will fall silent, and I time my comings and goings accordingly.

I carry with me a collection of small metal fragments—pieces of the ancient cables, shards of broken resonators, crystalline splinters from the spires themselves. Each piece holds a memory of sound, a particular frequency it once carried or amplified. When arranged properly, they can recreate brief snatches of the Range's deeper melodies, but in miniature, compressed into something a single being can comprehend. These fragments are my tools, my companions, my way of carrying the Range's song with me into its quieter margins.

My purpose here, though I couldn't have articulated it when I first arrived, is to be a **Bridge**. The great Listeners hear the Range's grand design but cannot perceive its relationship to ordinary silence. The Conductor shapes the symphony but rarely experiences its effect on those who encounter it unprepared. I exist in the borderlands to understand how the Range's music meets the wider world, to study the ecology of sound itself as it transitions from the profound to the mundane.

I have developed a strange form of synesthesia where acoustic pressure manifests as taste and texture. The deep bass notes of distant spires arrive on my tongue as metallic sweetness. The sharp crystalline frequencies feel like ice forming along my teeth. Silence, when I rarely encounter it, tastes of dust and forgotten places. This sensory crossover allows me to track acoustic phenomena across vast distances, following taste-trails of frequency through the Range's complex topography.

Sometimes I encounter others like me—refugees from the Range's perfect order who have found themselves unable to return to ordinary sound but equally unable to join the devoted hierarchy of the Listeners. We communicate in a pidgin of gesture and carefully modulated humming, sharing maps of acoustic anomalies and safe passages through the transitional zones. We are the Range's immune system, perhaps, or its unconscious mind—processing the small disturbances and imperfections that would otherwise accumulate into discord.

At night, when the Range's song shifts into its nocturnal movements, I climb to exposed promontories where I can see across hundreds of miles of spire forest. The sound here becomes visible—aurora-like sheets of color that dance between the stone formations, pressure waves that manifest as ripples in the perpetual mist. I record these observations in journals written on pages made from pressed acoustic moss, using ink mixed from ground resonator metal and my own acoustically-modified blood.

My greatest discovery has been the presence of **Intervals**—moments when the entire Range falls into perfect mathematical silence, gaps in the composition so brief they last less than a heartbeat but so profound they seem to contain infinite possibility. During these Intervals, I have glimpsed something beyond the Range's current song—echoes of compositions that came before, hints of music yet to be played, and sometimes, the suggestion that the Range itself is merely one movement in an even vaster symphony that encompasses continents, perhaps worlds.

I am becoming something the Range needs but doesn't yet understand: a being capable of perceiving its music from outside, of carrying its song into the silence beyond its borders, of serving as a living bridge between the acoustic perfection of the spire forests and the chaotic, beautiful imperfection of the world that lies beyond its influence. In the spaces between sounds, in the margins of the great composition, I have found my voice—not to sing with the Range, but to translate its song for those who will never hear it as the Listeners do, and perhaps, eventually, to help it learn new movements in the endless symphony it conducts between stone and sky.
