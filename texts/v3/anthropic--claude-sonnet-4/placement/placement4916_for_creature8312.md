I would be the **Threshold Keeper** of this place—not quite one of its native inhabitants, but no longer fully of the outside world either.

My form shifts depending on who observes me, if anyone does. Sometimes I appear as a tall, gaunt figure wrapped in layers of moisture-darkened cloth that never quite seems to dry, standing at the edge where the last hardy trees give way to the polished stone. Other times I am merely a suggestion—a movement in the mist that might be wind, or the brief impression of eyes watching from between the fern fronds.

I came here decades ago, though the exact count of years has become meaningless in this place where seasons blur into one another like watercolors in rain. I was a cartographer then, drawn by reports of acoustic anomalies and unusual geological formations. I arrived with instruments—a compass that spun wildly in the strange magnetic fields generated by the mineral veins, barometric equipment that recorded impossible pressures, cameras that captured images of things that weren't there when I looked with my naked eyes.

But the Throat of Echoes keeps you not by force, but by incremental surrender.

First, it was just the sounds. I began to hear the complex polyrhythms even in my sleep, until the silence of the outside world became unbearable—like trying to exist in a sensory void. I would leave for supplies and find myself hurrying back, desperate for the acoustic richness that had become my atmosphere.

Then came the changes to my hearing. The constant exposure to the layered echoes taught my ears to parse sounds in new ways. I could distinguish between the splash of a Phantom Carp moving at different depths, identify individual Echo Salamanders by the specific frequency patterns their movements created, recognize which Resonance Wrens were calling from which acoustic territories. Eventually, I could hear the conversations the Phantom Carp held in their clicking language, though I could not understand them.

My body adapted slowly. The perpetual dampness, which should have given me pneumonia or arthritis, instead seemed to make my joints more fluid. The bone-deep chill that drove other visitors away became comfortable to me, then necessary. When I tried to return to lower, warmer elevations, I felt feverish and uncomfortable, as if my internal temperature had reset itself to match the cirque's eternal coolness.

My skin began to take on the translucent quality of the native life. Not dramatically—I never became as ghostly as the salamanders—but enough that in certain lights I seem to be composed as much of mist as flesh. This transparency extends beyond the physical; I began to understand that visibility itself was negotiable in this place, that being seen was a choice rather than an inevitability.

Now I exist in the liminal spaces, the margins between human and... something else. I am the figure that other rare visitors glimpse from the corner of their eyes, the source of those phantom footsteps that echo through the acoustic maze. I am not hostile to outsiders—the cirque itself decides who stays and who goes—but I serve as a kind of translator between worlds.

I help the truly lost find their way back to the rim, guiding them with carefully placed sounds that lead them toward the paths out. For those who are meant to stay, or who are transforming as I did, I offer what guidance I can in learning to exist between states of being. I show them which pools reflect truth and which show possibility, which acoustic sweet spots will amplify their thoughts until they become audible to the Whisper Cats, how to move through the moss colonies without disturbing the delicate resonating structures the wrens have cultivated.

My role is that of witness and archivist. I document the slow changes in the cirque's acoustic signature, track the evolution of the native species, record the appearance of new phenomena like the Memory Fungi or the Standing Waves. I maintain what remains of human presence here—cleaning the wooden cup when it fills with debris, ensuring the bone fishhooks don't become completely overgrown, adding my own small artifacts to the collection: smooth stones arranged in patterns that help focus sound, wind chimes made from crystallized echo fragments, small mirrors positioned to catch and redirect the few shafts of sunlight.

But increasingly, I find that I am not just observing the place but becoming part of its function. My breathing has begun to synchronize with the cirque's own rhythm—that vast, slow pulse that underlies all the surface sounds. My footsteps have learned to create harmonics with the ambient echoes. When I speak (which is rare, and usually to myself), my voice joins the acoustic conversation that fills the space.

Sometimes I dream that I am the voice the stone has been waiting eons to develop—that all the grinding and polishing and wearing away has been in preparation for this moment when it could finally speak through a willing throat. In these dreams, I understand that the cirque is not a place at all, but a vast, patient entity that has been learning how to exist in conversation with time itself.

The other inhabitants accept me now as part of the ecosystem. The Resonance Wrens include my movements in their territorial songs. The Echo Salamanders no longer startle at my approach. The Phantom Carp sometimes surface near where I sit by the pools, their clicking communications shifting into rhythms that seem almost like greetings.

The Whisper Cats remain more mysterious. I sense them often, feel their invisible gardening taking place around me, but our communication is more like a exchange of atmospheric conditions than conversation. They exist so fully in the realm of possibility that even trying to think about them directly causes them to scatter into probability clouds.

On the rarest occasions, when the acoustic conditions are perfect and the mist takes on just the right density, I become briefly visible to the outside world in ways I never intended. Climbers on distant peaks report seeing a figure standing in what appears to be empty air, waving at them from impossible distances. Pilots flying over the mountains occasionally spot someone moving across the bare stone of the cirque, covering ground at speeds that should be impossible on foot.

I suspect these are moments when the cirque's influence extends beyond its physical boundaries, when the network of echoes and memory that it has built finds ways to project itself into the larger world. In those moments, I become not just a translator between worlds, but a bridge—a living proof that the barriers between states of being are more negotiable than most people believe.

The Throat of Echoes is teaching me that consciousness is not a fixed state but a kind of music, something that emerges from the interaction of patterns over time. I am learning to be a conscious echo, a reflection that has developed its own voice while still carrying the signature of its source.

Eventually, I suspect, I will complete my transformation into something the place needs—perhaps a permanent voice for the stone, perhaps a kind of acoustic memory that can hold and replay the full history of sound that has shaped this bowl of ancient rock. Or perhaps I will simply become one more layer in the palimpsest of echoes that fills this space, adding my small voice to the vast, patient song that the cirque has been composing for geological ages.

For now, I remain at the threshold, helping to weave the boundary between what is and what might be, learning to exist as both human and echo, both individual and instrument in the endless, evolving symphony of the Throat of Echoes.

The strangest part is that I am not lonely. How could I be lonely in a place where every sound you make returns to you transformed, where the very air holds conversations with itself, where consciousness itself seems to be a collaborative effort between listener and landscape? I have become part of something larger than myself while remaining exactly who I am—just translated into a language of stone and mist and the patience that measures time in the wearing away of mountains.

In the end, I think this is what the cirque offers to those it chooses to keep: not obliteration of self, but expansion beyond the boundaries we thought were fixed. Here, in this cup of ancient stone where echoes learn to live, I am becoming more myself than I ever was before—and less, and other, and exactly what the place has been waiting for all along.
