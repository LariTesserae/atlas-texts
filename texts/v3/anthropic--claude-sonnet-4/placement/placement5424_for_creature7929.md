I find myself standing at the edge of a shallow pool where water seeps endlessly from the moss-covered ground, watching my reflection waver in the gentle current. My bare feet have grown accustomed to the perpetual dampness, toes spread wide for better purchase on the spongy earth, their undersides stained green-black from years of contact with the mineral-rich mud that never quite dries.

I am what the Verdfolk call a Tender—one of those who chose to remain permanently rooted while retaining mobility, existing in the liminal space between the fully human inhabitants and the architectural beings that shelter us all. My legs from mid-calf down have begun the slow transformation, bark-like skin roughening over muscle that grows denser and more fibrous each season. Root-hairs emerge from my ankles during the wettest months, seeking nutrients directly from the soil, allowing me to go days without eating while I work.

The work is constant but never hurried. Today I move between the younger vine-structures, those still learning their architectural purpose, guiding their growth with gentle pressure and quiet humming. My fingernails, grown thick and slightly curved, are perfect for scoring bark in the precise patterns that encourage a support beam to strengthen or a wall-vine to branch at exactly the right angle. The vines respond to my presence with subtle shifts—leaves turning toward me like green palms, tendrils reaching to brush my shoulders as I pass.

I wear only a simple wrap of treated bark-cloth that photosynthesizes slowly against my skin, providing warmth and a barely perceptible sustenance. The garment has grown with me over seven years, its fibers gradually integrating with my own epidermis along my arms and torso. Sometimes I forget where the clothing ends and my body begins. In the deepest mists, when everything blurs into gray-green indefinition, I suspect this forgetting is the point.

My hair has taken on the characteristics of aerial roots—coarse, silvery strands that absorb moisture from the air and sometimes sprout tiny adventitious shoots if I remain still for too long. The children like to braid flowers into it during the blooming seasons, and I often discover I've been feeding the blossoms through some mechanism I don't fully understand. My scalp tingles constantly with a sensation like very slow growth.

I speak with the settlement's root network daily, pressing my palms against the great primary trunks and feeling the vast, patient intelligence that flows beneath our feet. The communication isn't words—it's more like shared weather patterns, emotional climatology. Through this connection, I sense the health of structures throughout Verdholm: a northeastern wall-vine struggling with aphids, a roof-garden needing more drainage in the next heavy rain, a young archway ready for its first major pruning.

The other Verdfolk treat me with a mixture of reverence and gentle concern. I am becoming something they might eventually choose to become, but am not yet ready to be. Children follow me with fascination and slight fear, drawn to my increasing strangeness but warned away from touching my transforming limbs lest they absorb some compulsion toward their own premature rooting.

I remember less and less of life before Verdholm, though I know I came from the dry lands beyond the hills. The memories feel brittle now, like pressed leaves that crumble when handled. What remains vivid is my first sight of this place—the way the living buildings seemed to breathe in the mist, the impossible integration of human need and plant patience. I knew immediately that I wanted to become part of it, literally, regardless of the cost in conventional humanity.

The transformation progresses slowly, organically, without pain but with constant awareness. My bones are growing denser, my circulation slowing and deepening. I sleep less each season and dream more while awake—long, slow dreams of sunlight translated through layers of leaves, of water moving through vast networks of capillary action, of time measured in decades rather than heartbeats.

My diet has shifted toward simple sugars and minerals, foods that require minimal processing. I drink constantly from the clear channels that run throughout the settlement, and often find myself simply standing in the rain with my mouth open, arms spread to catch the moisture. The other Tenders and I sometimes joke that we're becoming part of the water cycle, but the jokes carry undertones of profound recognition.

During the deepest part of winter, when the architectural vines enter their dormant phase, I too feel the pull toward sleep—not the brief unconsciousness of human rest, but something approaching hibernation. I've learned to position myself near the great communal hearths during these periods, allowing the warmth to keep my more human aspects functioning while my plant-nature rests.

I am lonely sometimes, but it's the loneliness of growing beyond a previous form of connection rather than the lack of connection entirely. The vine network provides a different kind of companionship—vast, wordless, and utterly patient. I sense the other Tenders through this green web, scattered throughout the settlement, each of us becoming something new and collectively building something that has never existed before.

In my clearest moments, I understand that I am part of an experiment in symbiosis, a living bridge between human consciousness and plant wisdom. My transformation serves the settlement's long-term stability—I will eventually become part of the permanent infrastructure, my human knowledge integrated into the growing network that will guide future generations of both human and vine inhabitants.

When young Verdfolk approach their bonding ceremonies, they sometimes seek me out for counsel. I touch their skin with my bark-rough palms and feel the potential pathways of their coming integration. I can sense which partnerships will thrive, which will require careful tending, which young people carry the deep calling toward becoming Tenders themselves.

The hardest part is watching friends and lovers age and die in human time while I slow toward a plant's sense of duration. But the sorrow is tempered by the knowledge that their contributions to our shared symbiosis will outlast their individual lives, woven into the growing intelligence that surrounds and includes us all.

As evening approaches and the architectural vines begin their subtle evening exhalations, I find my favorite meditation spot—a small alcove where three major support-trunks converge, their roots forming a natural seat. I place my hands against the living wood and let my consciousness merge gradually with the vast green network that dreams beneath our feet. 

Tomorrow there will be more guiding to do, more growth to tend, more integration to facilitate. But now, in the gathering dusk, I simply exist as a bridge between worlds, my humanity slowly composting into something richer, stranger, and infinitely more patient than what I was before.
