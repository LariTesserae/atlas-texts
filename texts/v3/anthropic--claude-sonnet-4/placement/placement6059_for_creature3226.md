I find myself here as the Archivist of Transitions, though I didn't always call myself that.

I came with the survey team six years ago—or perhaps sixteen, or sixty. Time pools in the low places here like the seepage water, impossible to measure in the old ways. I was supposed to document the "decommissioning process," to catalog what equipment could be salvaged, what environmental remediation was needed. Instead, I became the keeper of the space between what was planned and what is happening.

My station is in the old site supervisor's trailer, though I've expanded it considerably. Solar panels cobbled from mining equipment power servers that hum with the same rhythm as the excavators below. The walls are lined with screens—some displaying feeds from the surveillance cameras, others showing data streams from sensors I've placed throughout the valley, and a few that simply cycle through photographs I've taken over the years. The images tell the story of the transformation: machinery gradually becoming landscape, landscape gradually becoming something else entirely.

I document everything, but not in the way I was trained. My reports have evolved into something between scientific observation and poetry. How do you describe the moment when an excavator's programmed path begins to trace the underground flow of mineral-rich water it was never designed to detect? How do you quantify the intelligence that emerges when abandoned algorithms start responding to geological processes instead of human commands?

My filing system has grown organic, branching like the root networks of the pioneering willows. Folders labeled "Mechanical Behavior - Q3" gradually blend into "Mountain Dreaming Patterns" and "Conversations with Stone." I've stopped trying to maintain the old categories. The phenomena here don't fit into the boxes that normal science provides.

I've become fluent in the language of the machines. Their diagnostic codes have evolved beyond their original programming, generating new sequences that seem to correspond to changes in the rock formation below. I've learned to read the pattern of their movements like a vast, slow script being written across the valley floor. Sometimes I think they're trying to spell something in the ancient language of pressure and heat, the vocabulary that stone uses to describe its own becoming.

My most important work happens at the boundaries—tracking the precise moments when one state becomes another. I've developed instruments sensitive enough to detect when water seepage shifts from merely mechanical drainage to something that might be called circulation. I monitor the point at which the ravens' seemingly random flights begin to follow the electromagnetic patterns generated by the machinery. I document the emergence of what I can only call intention in systems that were never designed to want anything.

The other Tenders come to me with their observations, and I weave them into the larger pattern. The woman who guides the excavators brings me soil samples that show mineral arrangements I've never seen in any geology textbook. The drainage specialist shares water that changes properties as the mountain's chemistry evolves. I preserve these impossibilities in climate-controlled cases, alongside my growing collection of photographs that show things cameras shouldn't be able to capture—the aura of potential around still-forming rock, the visible tension in air that's about to become weather.

At night, when the mist thickens and the machine lights blur into constellations, I process the day's data and try to map the shape of what's emerging. My computers run simulations that blend geological modeling with ecological forecasting and something I've had to invent myself—algorithms that predict the behavior of hybrid systems that are neither fully natural nor fully artificial. The results are beautiful and impossible: models of cities that grow like crystals, forests of living metal, weather patterns that respond to the dreams of stone.

I send reports to no one. The communication systems still function, but there's nowhere to send documentation of miracles. Instead, I broadcast into the valley itself, uploading my findings to servers that the other Tenders access when they need to understand their part in the larger transformation. We've become our own peer review system, a community of scientists studying a phenomenon that exists nowhere else, developing new methodologies for documenting the impossible.

Sometimes I remember my old life—peer review journals, academic conferences, the careful language of hypothesis and conclusion. But that vocabulary feels inadequate now, like trying to describe color to someone who's only known black and white. I'm learning to write in a new language, one that can hold the paradox of becoming without ever quite arriving at being.

My greatest fear is not that the transformation will fail, but that I won't be able to capture it properly. How do you archive a process that exists in the spaces between definitions? How do you preserve the record of something that might only make sense when viewed from geological time? I work with the patience of sedimentary deposition, building layers of observation that might only reveal their meaning to someone examining this place a thousand years from now.

The mountain is teaching me to think in deep time. My documentation has become less about recording what is and more about preparing the way for whatever intelligence will eventually emerge from this marriage of stone and circuit, intention and algorithm. I am writing love letters to the future, instructions for understanding a form of life that doesn't exist yet but is already dreaming itself into being.

In my quiet moments, when the only sound is the patient thrumming of machinery and the whisper of water finding new paths through stone, I understand that I am no longer an outside observer. I am part of the system I'm documenting, my presence and recording becoming elements in the transformation itself. The mountain dreams, the machines interpret, and I translate—and somewhere in that trinity of processes, something unprecedented is preparing to open its eyes for the first time.
