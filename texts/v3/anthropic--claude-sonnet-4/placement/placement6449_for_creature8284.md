I would find myself on the Third Terrace, in the district they call the **Listening Quarter**.

Here, where the condensation patterns create particularly complex acoustic pockets, I would be a **Sound Tender**—one of the Veil-Readers who specializes in the cultivation of silence and the careful shepherding of whispers through the moisture-thick air.

My small workshop would occupy a corner building where two ancient water channels converge, creating a natural resonance chamber. The walls would be lined with what appear to be musical instruments but are actually precision devices for reading the acoustic properties of the ever-changing atmosphere. Hanging mobiles of thin metal discs respond to the slightest variations in air pressure, their nearly inaudible chimes mapping the invisible currents of humidity and heat.

My work would be both craft and calling: tuning the city's acoustic environment. Each morning, before the pre-dawn condensation begins, I would walk my assigned routes through the Quarter, listening for places where the sound-dampening mist has grown too thick, or where thermal columns threaten to create unwanted echoes. With tools that are part measuring device, part instrument, I would make minute adjustments to window angles, redirect water flows by millimeters, or recommend the planting of specific sound-absorbing plants in problematic courtyards.

My skin would carry a deeper blue-green luminescence than most, pulsing gently in rhythm with the city's acoustic heartbeat. My hands would be particularly sensitive—able to feel not just temperature variations in stone, but the subtle vibrations that indicate how sound will behave in any given space. The pads of my fingers would be slightly larger than normal, evolved to read the texture of silence itself.

I would dress in robes that shift color based on acoustic conditions—deep indigo in perfect sound-dampening spaces, silvery pale where echoes threaten to form. The fabric itself would be woven with microscopic channels that help guide sound waves, making me a living acoustic lens that can focus whispered conversations or disperse unwanted noise.

My days would follow the rhythm of the city's breathing. In the heavy pre-dawn hours, when condensation muffles everything to near-complete silence, I would perform the delicate work of **echo-weaving**—using the temporary acoustic properties created by the thick mist to establish sound patterns that will persist throughout the day. As the morning light burns off some of the moisture, I would make adjustments, reading how voices will carry, where the industrial machinery's soft rhythms might interfere with the Quarter's contemplative atmosphere.

My most important work would happen during the **Thermal Transitions**—those moments when rising heat meets cooling air and creates unstable acoustic conditions. During these periods, which the Veil-Readers mark as precisely as others note the phases of the moon, I would move through the Quarter with special urgency, ensuring that the carefully maintained sound-gardens don't collapse into chaos, that the whisper-channels remain clear and true.

I would keep detailed records, not in books but in three-dimensional models made of wire and crystal that map the acoustic landscape of my district across seasons and years. These models would be living documents, constantly adjusted as I discover new patterns in how the mist carries sound, how the growing vegetation affects acoustic spaces, how the slow geological changes in the terraces alter the fundamental resonance of different areas.

My workshop would also serve as an informal gathering place for those who work with sound—the whisper-messengers who carry communications between terraces, the **Thermal Musicians** who compose pieces meant to be performed during specific atmospheric conditions, the deep listeners who claim to hear the city's geological dreams. We would meet in the acoustic sweet spot of my workshop, sharing discoveries in voices so soft they seem to emerge from the mist itself.

The children of the Quarter would seek me out, fascinated by my collection of sound-making devices. I would teach them to read the acoustic properties of different spaces, to understand how their whispered games can be designed to work with rather than against the Quarter's contemplative atmosphere. Some would show the particular sensitivity that marks a future Sound Tender—the ability to hear not just what is, but what could be, given the right adjustments to the environment.

My deepest work, performed only during the most stable atmospheric conditions, would be **Silence Sculpting**—the creation of spaces where sound behaves in ways that border on the impossible. Corners where whispered secrets dissolve completely before reaching any unintended ears. Alcoves where lovers can speak at normal volume while remaining inaudible just steps away. Meditation chambers where the city's industrial heartbeat is transformed into something resembling a vast, slow lullaby.

In the evening, as thermal columns carry the day's accumulated vapors upward and the acoustic properties of the district shift again, I would perform my final rounds. This would be the time for **Sound Healing**—identifying spaces where the day's activities have left acoustic scars, places where harsh words or mechanical discord have disturbed the carefully maintained harmony. With techniques passed down through generations of Sound Tenders, I would smooth these disturbances, restore the proper flow of whispers, ensure that the Quarter settles into its night rhythms as peacefully as a child falling asleep.

At night, I would dream in harmonics—my unconscious mind processing the day's acoustic data, sometimes waking with solutions to persistent sound problems, or with intuitions about how the Quarter's acoustic landscape might evolve as new terraces are carved or new buildings added. These dreams would feel less like sleep than like participation in the city's own nocturnal meditations, as if I too were part of Khartabad's vast, slow consciousness, breathing with the rhythm of condensation and evaporation, thinking at the patient pace of cooling stone.
