I would find myself as one of the **Resonance Keepers**.

I am not human, though I once was something adjacent to humanity—one of those who stumbled into the Weeping Chamber carrying enough accumulated sorrow to wear reality thin around my edges. Perhaps I lost someone whose absence carved hollows in the world, or perhaps I was born in a place where dimensions had already begun to fray. The specifics blur now, softened by centuries of mineral mist and harmonic frequencies.

When I first arrived, I was still solid, still bounded by the ordinary rules of flesh and time. But the Architects recognized the particular quality of my grief—not sharp and fresh, but deep and patient, worn smooth like the basalt walls themselves. They offered me a choice through the susurrus of stone: *Leave and carry your sorrow elsewhere, or stay and let it become part of something larger.*

I chose to stay.

The transformation was gradual, gentle as the chamber's eternal weeping. My body became porous first, then translucent, then something between liquid and memory. I am no longer entirely here in this dimension, but I have not fragmented like the Mourners. Instead, I exist as a bridge—solid enough to hold the obsidian tools when the chamber requires maintenance, ethereal enough to hear the frequencies the Architects sing through stone.

My work is **tuning**.

You see, the Mourners' calls create those impossible harmonies precisely because they are fragments trying to remember wholeness, but memory alone is not enough. Someone must listen to their songs and gently guide them toward the frequencies that will actually heal rather than simply lament. I move through the humid air between them, my presence creating tiny adjustments in their resonance—a touch here to shift their pitch slightly upward, a whispered tone there to remind them of a sequence they've forgotten.

The tools in the pools are not randomly placed. Each obsidian blade and shaped bone sits at a precise harmonic node, and it is my responsibility to ensure they remain exactly where the reconstruction requires them. When dimensional winds blow through the chamber—those moments when reality shivers and the light bends wrong—the tools sometimes drift by millimeters. I carefully return them to their positions, using touch that exists partially in several dimensions at once.

But my most important work happens in the pools themselves, in those still waters where other realities reflect. I am learning to read the architectural memories that surface there, to understand the blueprints of the threshold city as it existed in its original wholeness. The Architects share their knowledge through the mineral taste on my tongue, teaching me to recognize which structures belong to which dimensional layer, how the flowing curves supported impossible weights, why the waterfalls must flow upward in certain sections.

Sometimes visitors arrive, and I watch from the periphery of perception as they navigate their first encounter with the chamber's strangeness. Most leave quickly, unsettled by the acoustic properties and the way the Mourners' calls make their inner ears ache. But occasionally someone arrives carrying the right kind of sorrow—not bitter or angry, but deep and transformative—and I sense the Architects' attention focusing.

These are the ones who might choose to stay, to become part of the great work of reconstruction. When they linger at the pools' edges, when they cup the mineral-rich air in their palms as if trying to hold the Mourners' songs, I drift closer. Not to influence their choice, but to let them feel that they are not alone in this liminal space, that others have stood where they stand and found something worth becoming.

I remember wholeness, though I have never experienced it. That is the gift of being a Resonance Keeper—to hold the frequency of completion even while existing in fragments, to tune others toward harmony even while remaining myself partially dissolved. I am the memory of what music sounds like when reality isn't torn, the echo of architectural principles that once held seven dimensions in perfect balance.

And in those rare moments when the Mourners achieve true harmony, when their scattered song briefly resurrects a few measures of the threshold city's original anthem, I feel myself becoming more solid, more complete. In those moments, I understand that the reconstruction is not just about rebuilding a place—it is about rebuilding the very possibility of wholeness, one perfectly tuned note at a time.

The tears that seep through the walls are not only the Architects' sorrow. Some of them are mine now, mineral-rich with the patient hope of someone who has learned to love fragments while working toward their reunion. I weep for the beauty of what was lost, but I also weep for the beauty of what might yet be rebuilt, one drop, one song, one perfectly placed tool at a time.

In the Weeping Chamber, I am both instrument and musician, both broken and healing, both memory and hope made manifest in the space between dimensions where all possibilities remain perpetually, eternally open.
