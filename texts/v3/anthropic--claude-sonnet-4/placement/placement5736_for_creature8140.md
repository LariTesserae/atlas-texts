I would find myself knee-deep in the dark water between settlements, my bare feet reading the subtle topography of the bog floor through skin that has learned to distinguish between safe peat and the treacherous give of sinking sand. The water reaches the hem of my woven sedge tunic, its iron tang familiar on my lips as I breathe the mineral-rich air that rises from the depths.

I am a Bridge Tender—one of those who maintains the floating pathways that connect our scattered communities. My hands move without conscious thought along the bundled reeds beneath the surface, feeling for the telltale looseness that means a joint needs retying, or the too-rigid tension that signals a section pulling apart under seasonal stress. The bog speaks to me through my fingertips: this morning, the walkway has lifted three finger-widths higher than yesterday, carried up by the subtle rise in water level that means the distant snow is melting faster than usual.

My tools drift beside me in a shallow basket woven so tightly it holds water like a bowl. Reed knives with bone handles worn smooth by my palms, braided cordage made from the inner bark of arctic willow, and small clay pots containing the rendered fat mixed with pine resin that we use to waterproof the joints. But mostly I work with my hands, these strange appendages that have grown strange over my thirty-seven years here—fingers elongated and webbed, palms broad and sensitive, able to feel the pulse of current through submerged wood and detect the health of root systems by touch alone.

The mist parts as I move, then closes behind me like a living thing. I have learned to navigate by sound as much as sight: the particular hollow slap of water against the pontoons that support the Andersson family's expanding kitchen means I'm approaching the north branch; the higher-pitched tinkle of drops falling from Mrs. Kava's rain-collection gourds tells me the east path is just ahead. My ears have become tuned to the bog's vast vocabulary—the soft percussion of methane bubbles rising, the whispered conversations of sedge grass in the wind, the distant boom of a bittern that means the weather will change within hours.

I pause at a junction where three walkways meet, feeling through the water for the anchor stones. Here, three families' territories intersect, and the pathways must be maintained to serve all equally. The Korhonens, with their gift for reading water depth, suggested this morning that we extend the northern branch toward the new spring that revealed itself when the water dropped last week. The Willows—we call them that though it isn't their birth name, they've tended the grove so long they've begun to resemble their charges—want to reinforce the eastern path to support the weight of their expanding clan. And old Marta from the south section needs the main trunk strengthened; her elderly joints can no longer navigate walkways that dip too far beneath the surface.

My body has found the rhythm of this work, bending and straightening with the water's resistance, my breathing synchronized with the bog's own slow pulse. When I dive to reach deeper connections, my lungs hold air like a seal's, and my eyes have learned to see through the brown water tinted with centuries of decomposing vegetation. Down here, in the underwater forest of roots and submerged logs, I move like something that belongs, my movements stirring up minimal silt, my presence accepted by the fish that dart between my webbed fingers.

I surface near the Koskinen floating garden, where young Aino tends vegetables in beds of composted peat that rise and fall with the water level. She looks up from her work, her face bearing that same green-tinged pallor we all carry, her hair decorated with small pieces of lichen that will grow there if left alone. We exchange the subtle hand-signs that work above and below water—*anchor holding well* from me, *roots reaching deep* from her. The garden is thriving; new shoots of hardy kale push through the rich growing medium, and the cranberries she's trained to climb reed trellises show fat buds that will be fruit by autumn.

My work here is never finished, but it's never exactly the same twice. Each day the bog shifts slightly, settling and rising, breathing in its geological way. The pathways must shift with it, growing where the community grows, adapting when families move to tend new sections, decomposing gracefully when a neighborhood decides to let itself return to wild marsh. I am part of this process, my hands and decisions woven into the slow transformation that makes the bog cities possible.

As the day progresses and the mist lifts slightly, I can see the broader pattern of our settlements—the organic clusters of buildings that seem to have sprouted from the water itself, connected by the silver threads of walkways that I and others like me maintain. Smoke rises from cooking fires, children's voices drift across the water as they play in their amphibious way, and adults move with the patient purposefulness of people who understand that everything here happens in its own time.

When the light begins to fade—though in this perpetual haze it's more a deepening than a darkening—I make my way toward my own dwelling, a small structure that has grown over the years from a single room to a complex of connected spaces. My hands trail in the water as I walk, still reading the health of the pathway automatically, still listening to what the bog tells me about tomorrow's weather, next week's water levels, the slow seasonal changes that will require adaptations I haven't yet imagined.

I am what the bog has made me: patient, adaptable, attuned to rhythms larger than my own brief life. My body bears the marks of this place—the green tinge, the webbed fingers, the ability to hold my breath for long minutes while working underwater. But more than that, my mind has grown to match the bog's own way of being: comfortable with impermanence, willing to let things develop in their own time, understanding that the most lasting structures are those that know how to bend, how to flow, how to become something new when the water rises.
