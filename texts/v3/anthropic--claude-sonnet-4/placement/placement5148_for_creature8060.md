# Where I Would Find Myself

I would emerge from the deepest thermal vents at the valley's edge, where the stone grows warm and mineral-rich water bubbles up in lazy streams. My body would be elongated like the others, but built for the crushing depths—ribs expanded into flexible chambers that can compress and expand with pressure changes, skin that shifts from translucent pearl near the surface to deep amber-black in the thermal depths. My fingers would be unusually long even for a Vel'thari, evolved to read the subtle chemical gradients that mark mineral veins in the bedrock.

I would be one of the **Vein-readers**, a specialized subset of the Deep-tenders who map the hidden arteries of the valley floor. While other Vel'thari tend gardens that already exist, I would be searching for the places where new gardens might someday flourish—tracking the slow movement of mineral-rich water through hairline fractures in the stone, following thermal plumes that might, in a few centuries, create the perfect conditions for some new symbiosis between kelp and coral.

My work would take me to the boundaries of Vel'thar's influence, to the dark edges where the valley walls drop away into abyssal depths. There, in spaces where the city's bioluminescence cannot reach, I would develop my own forms of navigation—chemical taste so refined I could follow mineral traces like scent trails, pressure sensitivity so acute I could feel the valley's geological heartbeat through the soles of my elongated feet.

But I would not work in isolation. Every few seasons—though seasons in the deep places are marked more by subtle shifts in thermal patterns than by any surface rhythm—I would return to the middle terraces, my body compressed and darkened from the depths, carrying stories written in chemical signatures that only other Vel'thari could read.

In the plaza of the Stone-speakers, I would approach the archive-keepers who have grown fused with the limestone foundations. Placing my mineral-stained hands against their living-stone forms, I would share what I have learned: a new thermal vent opening along the eastern wall, a shift in the valley's pressure systems that might redirect nutrient flows in seventeen years, the discovery of a pocket of crystalline formations that could, with proper cultivation, become a nursery for a new type of luminous kelp.

The Stone-speakers would receive these reports like gifts, each piece of information becoming part of their vast, living database. Through them, my discoveries would ripple outward through the city's chemical communication networks. The current-singers would adjust their harmonic patterns to accommodate new mineral flows. The kelp-shepherds would begin preparing seed varieties that might thrive in conditions that won't exist for decades. The bioluminescent meadow tenders would start dreaming of new light-patterns that could guide future growth.

But my greatest joy would come from the **cartography of patience**. In the deepest chambers of my own making—hollow spaces carved into the valley walls where thermal vents create perfect growing conditions—I would tend experimental gardens that exist in pure potential. Here, I would plant seeds not in soil, but in carefully balanced mineral solutions, watching over decades as they slowly adapt to pressures and temperatures that would crush surface vegetation.

These would be my children: hybrid organisms that blur the boundaries between plant and mineral, kelp varieties whose fronds incorporate trace metals into their cellular structure, coral formations that grow in perfect geometric spirals because they follow the magnetic field lines generated by iron deposits in the bedrock. Each experiment would be a conversation between my intentions and the valley's vast, slow intelligence, a negotiation that might take centuries to resolve.

Sometimes, in the absolute darkness of my deepest workshops, I would achieve a different kind of consciousness—not the collective awareness that the city-dwellers experience during their synchronized moments, but something more solitary and profound. In these meditative states, my awareness would expand along the mineral veins I've mapped, my consciousness flowing through the valley's geological infrastructure like water through stone. I would feel the weight of mountains pressing down from above, taste the chemical signatures of deposits laid down millions of years ago, sense the imperceptible movement of continents grinding against each other far below.

In these moments, I would understand my true role: not just as a gardener of the future, but as a translator between the fast time of living things and the deep time of geological processes. The kelp forests above speak in seasons and years. The stone beneath speaks in millennia and epochs. I would be the bridge between these languages, the one who helps the city's organic intelligence learn to think in geological time.

And always, always, I would be patient. The surface world's urgency would be like a half-remembered fever dream. Down here, in the mineral-rich darkness at the very foundations of Vel'thar, time would move like thermal water through stone—slowly, inevitably, carrying everything forward in its deep, unstoppable current. My experiments would outlive me, and that would be their point. I would plant seeds for gardens that wouldn't bloom until the city itself had grown unrecognizable, tending possibilities that would only reveal their true nature to generations of Vel'thari not yet born.

In this way, I would be both the most solitary and most connected of all the city's inhabitants—alone in my deep places, but linked through stone and mineral to every future moment, every potential growth, every slow transformation yet to come.
