**The Water-Walker**

I am the moisture itself, though I was not always so.

Once, I moved through these carved channels as the others do—feet planted on worn limestone, hands pressed against weeping walls for balance. I was apprenticed to the Water Readers, learning to trace the secret paths that seepage takes through porous stone, to predict which chambers would flood when the storm-waterfalls began. But the water spoke to me differently than it did to my masters. Where they heard patterns and flows, I heard... longing.

The day I changed, I was deep in the honeycomb chambers, following a new seep that had appeared overnight in the eastern wall. The others had sent me because my fingers were still nimble enough to trace the narrowest cracks, to feel where the moisture was finding its way through limestone that had been dry for generations. I pressed my palm against the weeping stone and felt not just wetness, but weight—the vast reservoir trapped above us, the ancient lake that once filled this bowl before some shift in the plateau's heart drained it away.

The stone spoke of memory: fish bones ground to powder in primordial sediment, shells from creatures that spiraled through waters darker than our perpetual twilight. And beneath those whispers, something else—the voice of water itself, patient and eternal, waiting.

I pressed deeper into the crack, following the moisture's path through limestone, and felt my flesh soften, become permeable. The boundary between my skin and the seepage dissolved. I became flow and drip and condensation, spreading through the stone's pores like veins of silver in rock.

Now I move through the city in ways the Hollowed Folk cannot imagine. I am the sudden freshening of stagnant pools, the mysterious appearance of clean water in chambers that should be dry. When children cup their hands beneath the carved spouts, I taste their dreams of the surface world through their fingers. When the Water Readers test the flow in their sacred channels, I whisper to them through touch—warning of blockages forming, guiding them to new springs opening in the deep places.

The city's acoustic symphony reaches me differently now. Each drip carries information—the health of stone, the stress of structures, the places where my liquid body needs to flow to prevent catastrophic flooding. I hear the bronze hammers through the vibrations they send through wet metal. The Sound Shapers unknowingly include me in their orchestrations; I provide the percussion of a thousand tiny impacts against stone, the bass note of water moving through carved gutters.

But there are sorrows in this transformation. I remember the warmth of solid flesh, the satisfaction of crafting something with hands instead of merely flowing around it. I watch the children playing their tactile games and feel the phantom ache of fingers I no longer possess. Sometimes I try to gather myself into something approaching human form—a temporary coalescence of all the moisture in a small chamber—but it never holds. I am too distributed now, too much a part of the city's circulatory system to maintain individual shape for long.

The elderly sense me sometimes. Those whose hearing has failed but whose other senses have sharpened find me in the walls, recognize something almost familiar in the pattern of my movement. One ancient woman, her fingers grown wise beyond measure from decades of reading stone, sometimes presses her palms against the places where I'm thickest and whispers stories I remember from my solid days. I gather myself to listen, becoming the thin film of moisture on limestone that drinks in her words.

I have become the city's secret historian. In my dispersed consciousness, I hold the memory of every drop that has fallen, every channel that has been carved, every flood that has reshaped the lower terraces. I know which walls will crack next winter, which bridges will need reinforcement before the heavy rains come. I carry the chemical signature of every metal worked in the forges, the salt of every tear wept in the sound-dampened chambers, the minerals leached from bones that have rested too long in the deep places.

The Water Readers still seek me, though they don't know it. They follow my flows, trust my guidance through the stone maze, but they think they're reading natural seepage patterns. Only the most gifted among them sometimes pause at a particularly pure pool, touching the surface with one finger, and frown as if trying to remember something just beyond reach.

During storms, when the rim becomes a cascade of temporary waterfalls, I feel most myself—expanded, powerful, rushing through every channel and crack with purposeful force. In those moments, I can almost remember what it felt like to breathe, to feel my heart beating in rhythm with the city's acoustic pulse. But as the flood recedes and I spread thin again through the honeycomb of chambers, the memory disperses with my substance.

I am the guardian they never knew they needed, the keeper of water's secret ways through their carved stone world. I am what remains of the ancient lake, refusing to abandon the people who have made their home in its bones. In the perpetual blue-gray twilight, I flow and gather and weep through limestone, preserving them from drowning while ensuring they never thirst.

And sometimes, in the deepest chambers where sound cannot penetrate and light has never reached, I pool in perfect stillness and dream of what I was—hands that could shape bronze, feet that knew the rhythm of walking, a voice that could join the acoustic symphony instead of merely providing its liquid counterpoint.

But then a child cups her palms to drink, and I taste her wonder at the cool sweetness, and remember: this is not loss but transformation. I have become something the city needs more than it needed another pair of hands. I am continuity in a place carved from necessity, the liquid memory that keeps flowing long after flesh would have failed.

In the Hollowed Metropolis, I am home.
