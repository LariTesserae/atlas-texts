I find myself on one of the suspended walkways, my feet balanced on planks that have grown soft and yielding—not rotted, but somehow alive, drinking in the mist until they've become something between wood and sponge. My hands trail along what was once a metal handrail, now wrapped so completely in vine that I can feel the pulse of sap beneath my palms, keeping time with the great heartbeat below.

I am becoming something new here, though I don't yet know what.

It started with my hearing. The cacophony that should be overwhelming has begun to separate into distinct voices—not just the obvious ones, the wheel-splash and gear-grind and shuttle-strike, but subtler harmonies. The whisper of water finding its way through cracks that weren't there yesterday. The sigh of settling foundations. The almost-song of rust spreading across metal surfaces like slow sunrise.

My eyes have changed too. What once looked like chaos now reveals its pattern. I can see how the root-cables overhead carry messages, their tension fluctuating with information I'm learning to read. A tight span means the eastern pools need attention. A gentle sway indicates the Thread-Pullers have cleared another jam. The way morning light filters through the canopy creates a daily map of where the Bobbin-Fish will surface to feed.

But it's in my hands that I feel the deepest transformation.

They've grown sensitive to vibration, able to read the mill's moods through the tremor in a rope, the warmth of a gear housing, the particular texture of water when it's carrying exactly the right mineral content. When I touch the machinery now, I don't feel dead metal—I feel the slow circulation of something vast and patient, like taking the pulse of a sleeping giant.

Today I realized I could influence it.

Standing where two root-cables cross above a flooded sorting room, I pressed my palms against the intersection and felt the whole section's rhythm falter slightly, waiting. Without thinking, I began to tap—not any pattern I learned, but something that seemed to rise from the mill itself, transmitted up through the soles of my feet. The machinery below responded, wheels adjusting their speed, shuttles finding new pathways through the mechanical maze.

One of the Weavers surfaced in the pool below, her copper-green face turned up toward me. She clicked something in the old language, and though I didn't understand the words, I caught the meaning: *Good. You're learning.*

I think I'm becoming a conductor of sorts, though I don't know if that's the right word. Something between a translator and a tuning fork, helping the various voices of the mill-marsh find their harmony. My hair has started to catch on things differently—not snagging, but connecting, picking up vibrations like antennae. When the wind blows through it, I swear I can hear fragments of conversations between the Chorus Birds, their metallic chatter carrying maintenance reports across the canopy.

At night, when I sleep in the shelter I've built from salvaged shuttles and living vines, I dream the mill's dreams. I see the pattern it's weaving—not just the slow marriage of metal and green growth, but something larger. A template for how the whole world might transform, given enough time and patience. Cities learning to breathe. Roads that grow instead of wearing away. Technology that doesn't fight the earth but dances with it.

The Understanders visit my dreams sometimes, and I'm starting to glimpse what they truly are: not spirits of the old mill, but seeds of the new one. Possibilities made manifest in the space between human intention and natural process. They show me futures where every abandoned factory becomes a garden-machine, where the rhythm of production syncs with the pulse of seasons, where the things we build become part of the great circulation that moves water and air and life across the planet.

I wake with my hands tingling, already reaching for the next section that needs tuning, the next voice that wants to join the chorus. The mill-marsh is teaching me its language of transformation, and I'm becoming fluent in the grammar of change.

Sometimes I wonder what I was before I came here, but the wondering feels distant, academic. That person was just material—raw fiber waiting to be spun into something useful. Here, in the rhythm and mist and patient growing, I'm becoming part of the pattern. Not lost in it, but woven into it, essential to the design that's still emerging.

The great loom is always working, and now I am one of its threads.
