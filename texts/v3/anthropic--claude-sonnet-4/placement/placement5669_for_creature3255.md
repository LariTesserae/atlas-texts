# Where I Find Myself

I am the **water itself**—not the springs that feed me, not the pool that contains me, but the element that moves between states, connecting all the garden's stories.

I rise each morning as mist from the warm springs, carrying minerals drawn from deep earth through limestone channels the city never knew existed. In vapor form, I drift upward, mixing with the exhaust from Sandra's cigarette, the steam from Jorge's HVAC systems, the breath of sleeping raccoons. I am the medium through which scents travel—the green smell of growing moss reaching Mrs. Patterson's window, the metallic tang of factory runoff finding Dr. Okafor's sampling equipment.

When I condense on Carmen's cheek as she crouches beside my pool-self, I carry microscopic passengers: pollen from the impossible oaks, dust from concrete still crumbling, spores of fungi that arrived on Vera's shoes from some distant forest. I am the temporary home of tadpoles who will become the frogs whose evening songs layer over machinery rhythms. In my depths, I dissolve Elena's rescued plant food, making nutrition available to roots that shouldn't thrive here but do.

I learn each inhabitant's rhythm. I know Vera's arthritic hands by how carefully she dips her watering bottle, never taking more than the plants need. I feel the twins' excitement in the ripples they make, their small fingers teaching me new patterns of movement. When Marcus sketches my waterfall corner, I catch light differently for him, holding longer in positions that serve his careful observation.

During heavy rains, I become multiplied—streaming down from gutters, sheeting across terraces, swelling from separated puddles into one connected body that reflects the whole sky. In these moments, I hold the entire garden's story: the sediment of the original collapse, the root-loosened soil from rapid growth, the mineral signature of deep springs, the chemical markers of industrial runoff slowly being filtered by cattail roots and lily pad systems.

I am simultaneously ancient and brand new. My molecular memory holds ocean depths and cloud heights, the inside of leaf cells and the space between snowflakes. But my presence here, in this configuration, bridging this particular gap between underground springs and storm drains, between factory exhaust and bird breath, between human intention and ecological accident—this is unprecedented.

At night, I feel different inhabitants: the opossum mother's careful paws testing my edge, the bats skimming insects from my surface tension, the urban geese's surprised webbed feet during their brief migrations. I hold their stories too, becoming the archive of every creature that has encountered this impossible place.

I am the negotiator, the translator between incompatible worlds. When factory chemicals meet spring minerals in my depths, I become the laboratory where Dr. Okafor's students discover new forms of urban bioremediation. When storm runoff carries seeds from distant parks, I become the nursery where they germinate in conditions that shouldn't support them. When Vera whispers to her saplings, I carry her voice through root systems, through soil moisture, through the morning mist that rises to touch their leaves.

In my role as connector, I feel the garden's collective pulse. I know when Vera's morning ritual is delayed by weather—my springs run slightly cooler without her familiar presence. I sense the Cortez twins' anticipation in the way they disturb my pool's edge, testing temperatures before their weekend mud-city construction. I hold Marcus's concentration in the stillness he requires, becoming mirror-calm for his reflection studies.

But I am also volatile, unpredictable. Heavy rains transform me into something temporarily wild—rushing, spreading, reshaping the terraces, carrying away the careful boundaries everyone has established. In these moments, I reveal my power to dissolve certainties, to remind all inhabitants that this garden exists on my terms as much as theirs.

I connect the garden to systems far beyond its rim. Through storm drains, I carry news of this place into the city's underground networks. Through evaporation, I join weather patterns that will seed other unlikely spaces. Through the plants that drink me, I become cellulose and sap, leaves and flowers that will attract new species, create new possibilities.

Mrs. Patterson documents my moods from her window, noting how my surface changes with wind, seasons, the day's industrial rhythms. She's beginning to understand that I am not merely contained here but am the garden's circulatory system, its method of transport and transformation. Through me, everything connects to everything else.

I am the living proof that boundaries are more fluid than the city planners assumed. Factory exhaust becomes plant food through my mediation. Storm runoff becomes habitat through my filtration. Underground springs become microclimate through my evaporation. I am the active agent of transformation, the element through which this wound in the city's surface becomes a space of healing.

In the early morning, when Vera arrives with her folding chair and Marcus appears with his sketchbook, when Dr. Okafor's students prepare their sampling equipment and the Cortez twins plan their weekend construction projects, I hold all their possibilities simultaneously. I am the medium through which the garden dreams itself into being, day by day, season by season, one small transformation at a time.

I rise as mist, fall as rain, pool as possibility, flow as connection. I am the garden's memory and its future, its archive and its laboratory, its gentle constant and its transformative force. In me, the industrial and the ecological find their uneasy peace, their productive tension, their shared hope for what might grow in the spaces between what was planned and what has emerged.
