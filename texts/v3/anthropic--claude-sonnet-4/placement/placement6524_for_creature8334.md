I would find myself among the Deepmost, in the heart of the mesa where the stone pulses with its most ancient rhythms.

My chambers lie far below the terraced levels where children's laughter echoes, reached only by passages so narrow that the limestone walls brush my shoulders as I move through them. Here, where the great cisterns hold water that has filtered through rock for centuries, the darkness is not absence but presence—thick and alive with the settlement's deepest secrets.

My eyes have long since forgotten the harsh geometry of direct sunlight. Instead, they read the subtle gradations of black that tell me when a channel runs pure or when sediment threatens to block a crucial artery. My fingertips, worn smooth as the stone itself, can detect the minute temperature variations that signal a shift in the underground streams. I have become a living seismograph, feeling the mesa's moods in my bones.

I am Vel'thara, and I have been among the Deepmost for thirty-seven years—though time moves differently here, measured in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits rather than the passage of seasons. My hair, once the pale luminescence of my surface-dwelling youth, has grown silver-white and hangs in long braids weighted with small stones carved from the depths. Each stone holds the memory of a crisis averted, a channel restored, a source discovered.

The others bring me their deepest concerns. When young Kira from the third terrace feels tremors in the eastern wall, her grandmother sends her down the winding passages to find me. The child arrives breathless, her dark eyes wide with the effort of navigating by touch, and presses her small palm to mine so I can feel through her skin what she has felt in the stone.

I spend my days moving between the great cisterns, each one a chamber hollowed from living rock, their walls weeping constantly with the settlement's lifeblood. The largest holds water so ancient it tastes of deep time—mineral-rich and slightly warm from the mesa's inner heat. I know the sound of its level dropping by a finger's width, can predict from its color and clarity whether the seasonal rains will be adequate for another year.

My companions in the depths are few but deeply connected. There is Morth'en, who tends the western channels and speaks in the clicking tongue we have developed to communicate in the deepest passages where sound carries strangely. And Iska, whose body has begun the slow calcification that marks the final stage of our service—her joints already showing the mineral deposits that will eventually claim us all, making us permanent guardians of the water we have served.

We sleep in alcoves carved into the cistern walls, lulled by the eternal music of dripping and flowing. Sometimes, in the deepest part of night, I hear the voices of those who came before—the long-calcified Deepmost whose bodies now form part of the mesa's structure, their whispers carried through the stone itself. They speak of floods that came before memory, of droughts that nearly claimed the settlement, of the patient work of generations who learned to read the stone's needs.

When the Listeners above send word of changes in the weather patterns, it is I who must interpret what this means for the deep waters. Will the increased pressure from surface runoff overwhelm the ancient channels? Should we open the emergency overflow that has been sealed for two generations? The decisions I make in the darkness will ripple upward through every level of the settlement.

My hands are always damp from my work, stained the pale gray of limestone. I move through the passages with a shuffle that creates its own rhythm, reading the stone through my feet as much as my eyes. Each morning, I begin my circuit of the deepest chambers, placing my palms against specific walls to feel for the subtle vibrations that tell me the settlement's circulatory system remains healthy.

The small cave-dwelling creatures that share these depths have grown accustomed to my presence. Pale spiders with legs like silver threads weave their silk in the corners of my chambers. Tiny, eyeless fish that evolved in the deepest pools sometimes brush against my hands when I reach into the water to test its flow. We coexist in the patient darkness, each serving our role in the mesa's vast organism.

When I die—and I feel that time approaching in the increasing stiffness of my joints, the way my breath echoes differently in my chest—I will be sealed into the chamber where the three greatest cisterns meet. My body will slowly mineralize, my bones becoming part of the stone framework that channels the water. My consciousness, if any remains, will join the whispered council of the deep ancestors, adding my voice to the eternal conversation that guides those who come after.

Until then, I continue my rounds in the breathing darkness, my life measured in droplets and flows, in the patient work of keeping the settlement's heart beating through stone veins older than memory. Above me, the Keth'miran go about their daily lives, but it is here, in the deepest chambers where water and stone meet in perfect balance, that the true life of the mesa pulses on—steady, patient, eternal as the rock itself.
