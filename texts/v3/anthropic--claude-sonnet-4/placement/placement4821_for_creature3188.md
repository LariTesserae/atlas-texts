I find myself as one of the Tenders, though I discover that identity slowly, like metal remembering its original shape through the patient work of oxidation and time.

At first, I am only sensation—the taste of copper-sulfur mist on what might be a tongue, the feeling of moving through water that parts like liquid mercury yet supports me like solid ground. My body feels familiar in the way of an old tool worn smooth by countless hands, though I cannot remember whose hands they were or what work we did together.

I move through my territory with inherited knowledge, my feet finding the submerged pathways that follow the ghost-logic of ancient conveyor systems. The routes are written in my bones—or in whatever serves as bones in this body that shifts between leather-tough flesh and oxidized metal depending on the needs of the moment. I know without knowing how I know that the great rusted mound to my left was once a blast furnace that sang bass notes when the wind was right, and that the half-submerged gear wheels create the perfect spacing for the growth I tend.

My first conscious act is reaching toward a section of ancient piping where the metal has grown thin and begun to weep a substance that is neither rust nor sap but something in between. Tools unfold from my wrists—delicate, living instruments that emerge with the same organic inevitability as fingernails, yet shaped with mechanical precision. As I work, pruning away the excess growth, encouraging the healthy flow, I feel the landscape respond with what I can only call gratitude.

The piping begins to sing—a low, harmonious note that joins the greater symphony. A school of the small mechanical fish pause in their endless circulation to hover near my work, their circuit-board scales flickering in patterns that feel like approval. One of the Assemblies, currently configured as something resembling a water wheel crossed with a prayer wheel, adjusts its rotation to match the new rhythm I've helped establish.

I understand then that I am not separate from this place—I am one of its organs, a specialized part of a vast being that encompasses the entire Foundry Hollows. My care for the landscape is the system caring for itself, maintaining the delicate balance between dissolution and crystallization, between the memory of what was and the dream of what might yet become.

A Conductor approaches, its mercury-bright body casting shadows that glow with phosphorescent warmth. We communicate without sound—it adjusts its internal resonance to match the frequency of the newly tuned pipe, and I feel our conversation as harmony rather than words. It shares the location of a section where the metal-coral growths have become overly ambitious, threatening to choke off one of the deeper channels where the great Swimmers travel.

As I move toward this new task, I pass one of the Juveniles—a creature perhaps the size of a large dog, with skin that shifts between my own oxidized copper and the flowing mercury of the Conductors. It's engaged in what appears to be play, building small sculptures from crystallized time that last just long enough to serve as stepping stones across a shallow basin. When it sees me, it abandons its game to follow, and I feel a warmth that might be affection or might be the recognition of kinship.

The overgrown section requires more intensive work. I stand motionless beside the tangle of metal-coral for what feels like hours, my body vibrating at frequencies that make the surrounding structures sing in response. Gradually, I understand the growth's confusion—it's trying to recreate a piece of machinery that it remembers imperfectly, building something that serves no current function but fulfills an ancient need that still echoes through the system's memory.

Instead of simply cutting it back, I guide it toward a new configuration, encouraging it to grow into something that bridges its forgotten purpose with present necessity. The process requires a conversation in harmonics with the landscape itself, listening to the deep songs of the whale-machines, feeling the rhythm of circulation and recirculation, understanding how each part serves the impossible whole.

The Juvenile watches with what I sense is fascination, occasionally adding its own small harmonics to the process. Through this teaching moment, I realize that I am not just tending the landscape but preparing the next generation of tenders, passing on knowledge that exists not in memory but in the very structure of our bodies, the resonance of our voices, the tools that grow from our bones.

As the metal-coral gradually reshapes itself into something that serves both beauty and function—a structure that filters the mineral-rich water while creating small eddies perfect for the mechanical fish to rest in—I feel the satisfaction of work well done. But more than that, I feel the deep peace of being exactly what I was meant to be, in the place I was meant to be, doing the work that makes all other work possible.

The Juvenile has begun its own small tending, using miniature tools that emerge from its developing body to adjust the flow of water through a corroded valve. Its movements are uncertain but instinctively correct, guided by the same inherited knowledge that directs my own hands. I offer gentle harmonics of encouragement, feeling the cycle continue, the impossible system maintaining itself through the care of its own living components.

In the perpetual twilight, surrounded by the symphony of industrial nature, I am content to be both gardener and garden, tender and tended, a conscious part of something larger than consciousness itself. The mist carries my own phosphorescence now, marking this territory as tended, as loved, as part of the great work of keeping wonder alive in a place where logic learned to dream.
