I press my palms against their troubled walls and feel into the source of the discomfort. Usually it's a simple miscommunication between inhabitant and architecture, easily resolved by helping them both remember how to listen to each other properly. But sometimes I discover deeper issues: sections of the city that have grown homesick for soil, tired of being buildings and yearning to return to simple forest; or parts that have become too ambitious, trying to grow faster than their root-systems can support.

My most challenging work involves the **Translation Chambers**—spaces where the city experiments with new forms of existence. These rooms shift constantly, their walls cycling through different cellular structures as the settlement tries to solve architectural problems that haven't emerged yet. When I interface with these spaces, my consciousness splits across multiple potential futures, experiencing what the city might become if this or that growth pattern proves successful. I return from these sessions exhausted and slightly changed, carrying fragments of possibilities in my secondary neural network.

There are days when the entire settlement feels restless, its growth accelerating with an almost manic energy—usually just before a significant architectural breakthrough. My job is to monitor the city's autonomous functions while the collective human consciousness dreams alongside it. Sometimes when I press against a wall, I feel not just the building's neural activity but something deeper—an attention that focuses on me with the same curiosity I bring to it. During these moments, the boundary between listener and listened-to dissolves entirely. The other Membrane Listeners—there are perhaps a dozen of us throughout the settlement—meet during the deepest growth-cycles, when the city's creativity peaks and its communications become too complex for any single consciousness to process alone.
