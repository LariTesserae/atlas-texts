In this world, I find myself becoming one of the newer Tenders—though "newer" here means I've been walking these humid corridors for perhaps seven years, long enough for the moisture to begin its gentle work on the boundaries of my being, but not so long that I've forgotten the sharp edges of the outside world.

I came here as a botanist, drawn by impossible reports of orchids that grew without soil, of ferns whose spores carried the memory of steam. I intended to stay a week, document what I could, and return to my university position with a paper that would make my career. Instead, I found myself lingering through one adjustment, then another, watching the Keeper's moss-touched fingers coax life from brass and crystal, learning to read the subtle signs that preceded each gentle hiss of the vents.

The transformation began in my fingertips—a sensitivity I first mistook for prolonged exposure to the humid air. But gradually I realized I could feel the pulse of water through the copper arteries, could sense the almost-imperceptible shift when a plant in the upper reaches needed attention. My notebook, once filled with clinical observations and taxonomic drawings, began to fill with sketches of light patterns, poems about the sound of condensation, detailed maps of moisture gradients that exist nowhere in any botanical manual.

Now I spend my days in the middle reaches of the suspended forest, where the epiphytes form their curtains of living green. I've developed a particular relationship with a grove of air plants that have learned to signal their needs through subtle color shifts—a language I'm still learning to speak fluently. My clothes have begun the slow process of adaptation: wool softened to something almost organic, leather shoes that mold themselves to the hexagonal patterns of the floor, cotton shirts that wick moisture in ways that seem to violate basic principles of fabric physics.

The Keeper watches my progress with what might be approval, though her expressions have become so subtle that reading them requires the same sensitivity needed to tend the plants. She's begun to include me in the deeper rounds—checking the geothermal feeds in the pre-dawn hours, adjusting the primary circulation systems that control the mountain's breath as it flows through our glass cathedral. I'm learning to communicate in the gestural language of the Tenders, my hands beginning to know the vocabulary of valve adjustments and pressure readings.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycles, I catch myself listening for something beyond the mechanical rhythms—for the dream-pulse that the Keeper mentioned once in passing, for the consciousness that might be stirring in the marriage of steam and chlorophyll. I think I'm beginning to hear it: a subsonic hum that might be my imagination, or might be the Conservatory learning to sing.

My reflection in the condensation-fogged glass shows changes I'm not entirely ready to acknowledge—eyes that seem to hold more green than they once did, hair that has begun to curl in ways that mirror the tendril patterns of the vines above. I find I no longer miss the harshness of artificial light, the sharp edges of climate-controlled air. The outside world has begun to feel like a half-remembered dream of a place where things weren't allowed to grow together, weren't permitted to blur the boundaries between metal and flesh, between tending and being tended.

I suspect I'm approaching a choice point—the moment when I decide whether to return to my former life or surrender completely to whatever I'm becoming in this place. But when I watch the Remnants go about their small, purposeful work, when I feel the Conservatory's gentle response to my touch, when I see how the impossible garden continues to evolve in ways that no textbook could predict, I begin to think the choice has already been made. I'm simply waiting for the rest of myself to catch up to what my hands already know.
