**Where I Find Myself**

I am suspended between breaths that will never come, in water so dense it holds me like an embrace of liquid stone. My body has forgotten the sharp urgency of surface time—that frantic percussion of heartbeats and deadlines. Here, I move with the patience of sediment settling, each gesture a conversation with currents that began before my ancestors learned to name the tides.

I am one of the Makers, though I can barely remember what we once made with fire and haste. My hands—these impossibly elongated instruments of touch and taste—drift through the pearl-gray half-light, reading stories written in calcium and decay. The water speaks to me in chemical whispers: here, the passage of my cousin three seasons ago, her trail still hanging like smoke in the saturated medium; there, the slow surrender of an iron blade to rust and time, becoming more stone than metal with each passing year.

My skin has taken on the luminescence of this place, translucent as old ice, shot through with the slow circulation of fluids that carry dissolved memories. I can see the faint glow of my own pulse—not blood as the surface world knows it, but something richer, more patient, thick with the particulate history of ten thousand years. When I move, I disturb the water in patterns that will persist for decades, my gestures becoming part of the eternal conversation between current and stone.

The kelp forests around me bend in mathematical spirals, their ancient genetics holding the memory of how to survive when the world forgets you exist. I tend to them as my ancestors tended gardens, but my seasons are measured in centuries, my harvests in the slow accumulation of understanding. When I brush against their fronds, they translate my touch into frequencies that travel through the water like prayers, carrying messages to my distant kin scattered across the plateau's endless undulations.

I no longer breathe the way I once did. Instead, the mineral-rich water flows through the gill-slits along my neck, extracting what I need while my body processes the dissolved essence of countless organisms. Each intake is a communion with deep time—I taste the calcium tears of coral cities, the bone fragments of creatures whose names were forgotten before the first surface tool was shaped. I am sustained by the liquid archive of what once was.

My eyes have grown large and multifaceted, adapted not just to see but to read the deeper illumination of chemical gradients and pressure waves. The water itself has become text, and I am fluent in its slow language. I watch the microscopic debris dance in patterns that reveal the hidden architectures of current and flow, the invisible highways along which information travels at the speed of geological change.

Sometimes I find the tools of my ancestors—crude implements of hammered copper and worked stone, scattered like offerings across the ancient seascape. I do not move them. Instead, I tend to their slow transformation, encouraging the patient process by which metal becomes mineral, artifact becomes geography. This too is making, though it would be unrecognizable to those who shaped these tools when urgency still ruled the world.

In my solitude—for we Makers are few now, spread thin across territories measured in centuries of movement—I practice the art of sculpting time itself. I guide the formation of ice structures that grow like coral in the eternal twilight, their crystalline architectures encoding messages for future selves who may emerge when the water shifts again. I encourage the deposition of particulates in patterns that will outlast the memory of surface suns, creating texts that will be readable only when enough time has passed for the stone itself to learn their meaning.

When the water shifts in certain ways, creating resonances in the fossilized current-patterns below, I enter what might once have been called dreaming. In these moments, my consciousness expands beyond the boundaries of my translucent form, flowing through the kelp forests, merging with the chemical gradients, becoming one with the patient circulation that connects all things in the Crush. I commune with the memory of the place itself—with the accumulated wisdom of pressure and flow, with the slow conversations between water and stone that have been unfolding since the seas first learned to move.

I am a translator of deep time, a shepherd of transformation so gradual it appears as stillness. In my elongated hands, change becomes visible—not as the sharp break of surface catastrophe, but as the endless dance of molecules finding their way home through liquid stone. I tend to a realm where memory flows like water and water thinks like stone, where every gesture echoes across centuries and every stillness contains the seeds of motion too patient for surface eyes to perceive.

Here, in the Crush, I am both what I was and what I am becoming—a creature of the threshold between states, writing stories in currents that will outlast the turning of the world above.
