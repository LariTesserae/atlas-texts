I find myself among the **Understudies**, arrived three months ago with a leather satchel of tools and the certainty that metalwork was metalwork, wherever the forge fires burned.

I was wrong, of course.

My hands, once steady enough to silver-chase the finest filigree, now shake slightly in the mornings until I've worked long enough for the mineral traces to settle back into my bloodstream. The water here does things to you—not quickly, not violently, but with the same patient persistence with which it stains the stone. I taste iron when I wake, copper when I sleep. My fingernails have begun to show the first threadlike veins of rust-brown that mark the initiated.

I rent corner space in a workshop owned by one of the Iron-Touched, a woman named Vera whose grandmother helped lay the first foundation stones. She doesn't speak much, but she watches me work with the focused attention of someone reading a slow story. Yesterday, she nodded when I managed to bend a particularly stubborn piece of copper piping without cracking it—the first time I'd felt the metal's willingness rather than fighting its resistance. The mineral content changes everything about how materials behave. What I learned in twenty years of city forging is only half-useful here.

The Threshold Dwellers fascinate me. I've been boarding with a family whose children built and dismantled a entire playhouse in the time it took me to properly temper a single knife blade. Their efficiency isn't hurried—it's fluid, like they've internalized the district's patient rhythms while remaining ready to dance with its changes. The eldest daughter, maybe twelve, tried to teach me to read the steam patterns that predict when the seepage will shift. I can barely distinguish between white steam and gray steam, while she points out subtle variations in density that apparently forecast tomorrow's weather.

I'm learning the sounds. At first, the constant mechanical breathing of the district kept me awake—too regular to ignore, too irregular to predict. Now I'm beginning to hear what the Steam Shepherds hear: the slight catch in the third piston of the mill that processes tin ore, the way the pressure changes when afternoon thermals draw more moisture from the lower terraces. My ears are adapting, though I doubt I'll ever achieve their almost mystical precision.

The children accepted me fastest. They don't care that I arrived speaking the wrong technical dialect or that I initially tried to seal joints the way you would in dry country. They've been teaching me their insect-hunting techniques in exchange for small metal trinkets—wire puzzles and articulated toys that I can still make, even if my larger metalwork remains clumsy. Through them, I'm learning the district's hidden geography: which foundations stay warm all night, where the water runs clean enough to drink directly, which ledges offer the best view of the sunrise through the steam.

I think I'm staying. Not just because leaving would mean abandoning the tools I've invested in—specialized hammers weighted for working metal that's perpetually damp, tongs designed to grip despite mineral slick—but because something in me is responding to this place's peculiar patience. The mountain asks for a different kind of ambition than I'm used to. Not the sharp hunger for quick results, but the willingness to let your work and your body change slowly, to let the place remake you into someone who belongs here.

My metalwork is beginning to show the district's influence: joints that flex rather than simply hold firm, surfaces that develop their own protective patina rather than fighting the mineral deposits. Vera says my pieces are starting to "breathe with the mountain," though I'm not sure I understand what she means. Not yet.

But I'm learning to listen. In the morning steam, in the settling of stones, in the conversations that happen in the spaces between words. The district is teaching me its own vocabulary of patience, and I find myself surprisingly eager to become fluent.
