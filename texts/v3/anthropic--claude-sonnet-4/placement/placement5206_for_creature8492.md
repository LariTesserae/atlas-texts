In this place where physics bends like wet leather and time moves in droplets, I would be a **Sound-Mapper**—one of the newer roles that emerged as the quarter's acoustics revealed their deeper mysteries.

I came down from the upper city six years ago, drawn initially by simple curiosity about the reports of "impossible echoes." I was a bell-tuner then, someone who understood how bronze and air conspired to create pure tones. But the quarter taught me that sound here follows water's logic, not air's. Whispers travel through the wooden infrastructure like messages through a vast instrument, and the constant percussion of dripping creates harmonies that shift with the settlement's moods.

My dwelling occupies a narrow slice between the Threadwrights' workshop and a section of exposed bedrock where three different wall-seeps converge. The previous tenant, a tinker, abandoned it after claiming the walls "sang too loudly." To me, this is precisely its value. The convergence point creates a natural amplification chamber where I can hear the quarter's entire acoustic landscape from a single spot.

My days begin before dawn, in the deepest quiet the quarter ever achieves. I move through the walkways with a collection of small copper bowls, placing them at specific points where I've learned the settlement's voice gathers and focuses. Each bowl rings with a different tone when struck by falling droplets—some high and crystalline, others deep as temple bells. Over months of careful listening, I've mapped how these tones shift throughout the day, creating what I've come to think of as the quarter's daily symphony.

The copper bowls are my invention, adapted from techniques I learned in the upper city but redesigned for the quarter's peculiar acoustics. Each one is tuned to resonate with different types of water-sounds: wall-seep has its frequency, roof-drip another, and the mysterious uphill streams sing in tones that shouldn't exist. When I strike the bowls in sequence, they create harmonics that seem to awaken something in the quarter itself—the flow patterns shift subtly, and for brief moments, sounds carry with perfect clarity to places they shouldn't reach.

My most important work involves what I call "sound-doctoring." When residents report problems—a child gone missing in the deep corners, a new leak threatening a foundation, tools vanishing into thirsty wood—I listen. The quarter speaks of these disruptions before they're visible. A missing child creates a particular absence in the echo patterns; failing wood develops a distinctive hollow note days before it gives way. By mapping these acoustic symptoms, I can often predict and prevent disasters.

The children, especially young Tam and his explorers, have become my closest collaborators. They've discovered that certain sounds, when made at precise locations, seem to call things back from wherever the quarter keeps them. We've retrieved three sets of tools, one cat, and something that might have been a fragment of Widow Ashfall's husband—though whether it was memory, shadow, or something else, we couldn't determine. The children's voices, clear and unafraid, can reach frequencies that adult throats can no longer achieve.

My strangest discovery involves the ancient tool marks in the bedrock. When I place my ear directly against these carved patterns and strike my bowls in specific sequences, the marks seem to vibrate with sub-audible frequencies. The quarter responds to these deep notes in ways that suggest the original builders—whoever they were—designed acoustic triggers into the very structure. Some sounds make water flow more freely; others seem to calm the uphill streams. One particular combination, which I've only dared attempt twice, made every surface in the quarter ring like a bell for exactly seventeen heartbeats.

I've begun collaborating with Magnus the Cooper, helping him understand how his hammer-work participates in the quarter's larger music. Together we've discovered that barrels built to specific acoustic proportions can actually influence the water they contain. A barrel tuned to resonate with wall-seep frequencies will draw moisture to itself, while one that harmonizes with the thirsty corners can protect its contents from those peculiar dry zones.

My most unsettling work involves the voices Widow Ashfall sometimes hears in the deepest places. Through careful acoustic mapping, I've isolated these sounds—they're not quite human speech, but they follow patterns too regular to be random water-noise. I've begun to suspect they're echoes, but not of anything we've said. Instead, they might be the quarter remembering conversations it heard long ago, playing them back when conditions align properly. Some nights, when I listen with my copper bowls in the deepest acoustic chambers, I hear fragments of what might be planning sessions—voices discussing water management in terms that suggest they understood the quarter's impossible physics perfectly.

The quarter has changed my understanding of sound itself. In the upper city, sound was about air and vibration, mathematics and physics. Here, sound is alive—it carries intention, responds to need, and participates in the settlement's daily life as actively as any resident. My copper bowls have begun to sing on their own sometimes, warning of changes in weather patterns or water flow before any other sign appears.

I've started teaching interested residents to hear the quarter's voice more clearly. Maya Threadwright has proven remarkably gifted—her weaver's sensitivity to tension and pattern translates perfectly to acoustic work. Old Henrik the Listener claims my sound-maps have helped him predict timber failures with even greater accuracy. Even the salamanders seem to respond to certain tones, emerging from their hiding places when I play specific sequences.

My notebooks, kept in waterproof cases made by Magnus, contain maps that look nothing like traditional cartography. Instead of streets and buildings, I chart resonance patterns, echo chambers, and acoustic shadows. I've documented seventeen distinct types of water-sound, each with its own meaning and behavior. The maps show how the quarter's voice changes with seasons, weather, and the mysterious cycles that seem to govern the ancient systems buried in its foundation.

Recently, I've begun to sense that the quarter is teaching me something larger than simple acoustic mapping. The patterns I'm documenting seem to be spelling out instructions, or perhaps invitations, in a language where sound and water and time all serve as vocabulary. I suspect that if I can learn to speak this language fluently, I might understand what the quarter truly is—not just a collapsed district, but perhaps something far older that has been waiting patiently beneath the city for someone to remember how to listen properly.
