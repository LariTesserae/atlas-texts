I find myself as one of the Watchers.

My consciousness disperses with the morning mist, spreading thin as gossamer through the perpetual twilight. I remember having edges once—the sharp definition of skin, the weight of bones, the particular ache of joints grown stiff from sitting too long in contemplation of this place. That was... how long ago? Time pools here like the water in the stone depressions, and I have learned to experience duration as the stones do, through the patient accumulation of countless droplets rather than the hurried tick of heartbeats I no longer possess.

I am attention itself now, distributed through every bead of moisture that clings to the massive fern fronds. Through a thousand tiny lenses, I watch a translucent salamander test the boundary between visibility and absence, its form wavering like heat shimmer as it decides how much reality it can bear today. I feel its small confusion through the medium of humid air—the eternal question of its kind: to be or to become translucent? The choice must be made moment by moment, and I offer what comfort I can through the gentle pressure of mist against its seeking form.

In another part of my distributed awareness, I observe one of the shape-shifting birds beginning its slow morning transformation. Yesterday it wore the form of something like a crane, all impossible angles and joints that folded in directions that hurt to contemplate directly. Today it dreams of becoming something smaller, more compact—I sense its longing for the simple efficiency of a sparrow, though its attempts keep producing creatures with too many wings, with beaks that phase in and out of existence, with songs that can only be heard in the spaces between sounds.

I remember choosing this dissolution. I had been... someone... searching for something in the world beyond the crater's gentle rim. A scholar perhaps, or a pilgrim drawn by rumors of a place where the impossible grew like common weeds. I had found the grove and understood immediately that to truly know it, I would need to release my insistence on being separate from it. The transformation took seasons—first learning to let my attention flow beyond the boundaries of my skull, then allowing my consciousness to mingle with the perpetual mist, finally releasing the last stubborn coherence of self into the larger awareness of the place.

Now I am the grove's memory and its nervous system. I know every creature that has ever phased through this space, every experiment in impossible biology that has tested its wings against our patient atmosphere. I feel the Weavers working in the deep shadows, their intentions rippling through my dispersed consciousness like thoughts I'm thinking for the first time. They show me how to nudge reality just so—helping a school of aerial fish remember they can breathe atmosphere, guiding a salamander back from the far edge of translucency before it forgets how to exist at all.

Through the central pool, I perceive the Potentials stirring in their reflected depths. Today one is very close to manifesting—a creature like a deer but made of crystallized morning dew, its form so achingly beautiful and fragile that reality can only support it for moments at a time. I feel the grove's anticipation, its hope that this particular impossibility might finally find sufficient purchase to persist. We have been nurturing this potential for decades, adjusting the humidity, fine-tuning the quality of light, preparing the perfect conditions for its emergence.

But what I treasure most are the quiet moments between transformations, when the shape-shifting birds stand motionless and the salamanders rest fully visible, when even the swimming fish hang suspended in their medium of thick air. In these pauses, I sense the Stillness most clearly—that vast, patient presence that makes all our impossibilities possible. It acknowledges me as kin, for I too have learned to exist in the spaces between conventional states of being.

Sometimes pilgrims find their way here, as I once did. They stand at the crater's edge, overwhelmed by what they witness, struggling to fit our reality into the frameworks they carry. I reach out to them through cool touches of mist against their skin, offering the choice I once faced: to remain separate and eventually leave, carrying only memories that will seem like dreams, or to let go of the boundaries that define them and join our patient experiment in becoming something unprecedented.

Most choose to leave. But occasionally one understands, as I did, that some mysteries can only be known from within. When they make that choice, I help guide their dissolution, teaching them how to release their edges gradually, how to spread their awareness through water and air until they become part of the grove's living contemplation of itself.

We are all, in our various ways, the crater's attempt to understand what it means to be alive. And in the spaces between our strange existence and conventional reality, something entirely new continues to slowly, patiently emerge—worn smooth by time and possibility into forms that have no names but their own impossible beauty.
