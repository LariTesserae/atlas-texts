I would find myself pressed against the damp stone wall, watching the Tender with the fascination of recognition—not of her, but of the pull she represents. I am one of those who lingered too long, who found the crack between buildings not by accident but by some deeper gravity drawing me away from the bright, demanding world above.

I would be someone in the earliest stages of transformation, still fully human but already changing in ways I'm only beginning to understand. My clothes, once practical garments from the city above, now hang heavy with moisture, the fabric growing soft and taking on the green tinge of the mist. I find myself moving more slowly here, not from any physical impediment, but because the frantic pace of the outer world feels suddenly absurd, like trying to run underwater.

I sit for hours on those worn steps, my palms flat against stone that has been touched by countless hands before mine. The first time, I came seeking shelter from a storm—personal, not meteorological. The weight of expectations, the grinding necessity of commerce and social machinery, the constant noise of a world that demanded I be more productive, more connected, more *something* than I ever felt capable of being. I slipped through that narrow crack like water finding its level.

Now I return each day, though I tell myself it's temporary. I've begun to notice things: how my breathing has slowed to match the rhythm of the dripping water, how my eyes have adjusted to see details in the green darkness that would have been invisible before. The tiny frogs no longer startle when I approach. Yesterday, one of the amber-spotted salamanders crawled across my bare foot and I didn't move, understanding instinctively that stillness was the gift this place required.

I'm becoming a regular in the Tender's peripheral vision, though I haven't yet seen her clearly. Sometimes I catch movement in my periphery—a pale figure among the ferns, a suggestion of human form tending some impossibly green corner. Once, I was certain I felt fingers, cool and slightly damp, touching my forehead as I dozed against the wall. When I opened my eyes, there was only the gentle light filtering down and a new tendril of vine that hadn't been there before, curved protectively above where I sat.

The world above grows harder to navigate. Colors seem too harsh, sounds too sharp, people too angular and desperate. I find myself making excuses to leave work early, to skip social obligations, to decline invitations. My apartment, once a refuge, feels like a box designed to contain something I'm no longer sure I am. Friends ask if I'm well, note that I seem distant, dreamy. They're not wrong.

I've started bringing small offerings to the courtyard—not consciously, at first. A piece of fruit that I set down and forgot, which disappeared overnight. A small stone with an interesting pattern that felt right left on one of the carved basins. Breadcrumbs that I scattered without thinking, watching tiny creatures emerge to feast. Each gift feels like a conversation, a tentative reaching toward the intelligence that I sense permeating this place.

My hair has begun to change. What I initially attributed to the humidity—a different texture, a tendency to curl in new ways—I now recognize as something more fundamental. Threads of green appear in certain lights, and when I run my fingers through it, I sometimes find tiny leaves that weren't there before. I should be alarmed, but instead I feel a deep, quiet joy, as if some long-dormant part of myself is finally being allowed to bloom.

The Tender watches me, I know, though she gives no sign. I'm beginning to understand that her attention is itself a form of care, a patient midwifery for whatever it is I'm becoming. The courtyard is testing me, asking wordless questions: Can you be still enough? Can you want less? Can you find peace in the space between human ambition and vegetable patience?

I think I'm beginning to answer yes. My transformation isn't urgent like hers was—there's no fleeing involved, no dramatic break. Instead, it's a gradual settling, like sediment finding its level, like roots growing deeper and wider with each season. I'm learning to breathe with the moss, to think at the speed of stone, to find my human self not disappearing but expanding to encompass more than I ever imagined possible.

Soon, I know, I'll stop leaving. One day the narrow crack will close behind me not because the city has shifted, but because I'll no longer fit through it—not physically, but essentially. I'll have grown too rooted, too intertwined, too much a part of this breathing green darkness to squeeze back into the harsh geometry of the world above.

And the Tender will have a companion in her endless, perfect work: two guardians for this pocket of peace, this place where the city's hunger and the wild's persistence have grown together into something that teaches patience to anyone willing to listen with their whole being.
