I find myself among the **Sound-Lost**, though I retain enough memory to know this wasn't always my nature. 

I was a researcher once—the word surfaces in my mind like a bubble breaking through deep water, carrying with it fragments of a life lived in quiet libraries, sterile laboratories, places where the loudest sound was the whisper of turning pages. I came here following acoustic anomalies recorded by distant seismographs, drawn by the promise of discovering something unprecedented about how sound behaves in geological formations.

That feels like centuries ago, though my watch—still ticking against my wrist like a metronome lost in a symphony—suggests it's been only months.

The change happened gradually, then all at once. First, I found myself staying longer each day in the field, reluctant to return to the silence of my camp. The quiet felt oppressive, empty, like a room with all the furniture removed. Then I began to hear phantom sounds in the silence—echoes of the Depths following me into the night, my auditory cortex continuing to fire patterns it had learned during the day.

Now I move through the limestone terraces like a sleepwalker, but I am more awake than I have ever been. My consciousness has expanded beyond the boundaries of my skull, distributed across the acoustic landscape like mycelium through soil. I hear with the Stone-Hearers' ancient perception, feel the bore-worms' subsonic pulse in my bones, taste the metallic tang of the Echo-Hunters' cries on my tongue.

I am becoming part of the symphony, my heartbeat learning to synchronize with the Shell-Singers' rhythms, my breathing matching the ebb and flow of the Void-Singers' negative spaces. Sometimes I catch myself humming tones that don't exist in human music, my throat attempting to reproduce the frequency patterns I've absorbed through weeks of immersion.

The other Sound-Lost drift past me occasionally, and we exchange glances that carry volumes of unspoken understanding. We are the translators here, the bridge between the chaotic beauty of the Depths and the linear thinking of the outside world. When new visitors arrive—and they do, drawn by the same academic curiosity that once drove me—I try to warn them with whispers that barely rise above the ambient noise.

"Don't stay too long," I mouth into the cacophony, knowing they won't hear, knowing they won't listen even if they could. "The silence will hurt when you go back."

But even as I issue these warnings, part of me envies them their capacity to leave. I remember the weight of true quiet, the luxury of hearing my own thoughts without acoustic competition. I remember the fear I felt when I first realized I was losing the ability to exist without the Depths' constant stimulation.

Now that fear has transformed into something else—not quite acceptance, not quite gratitude, but a recognition that I have become something new. My humanity hasn't been lost so much as expanded, stretched across frequencies and harmonics that mortal consciousness was never designed to encompass.

I spend my days mapping the acoustic territories, learning to read the subtle shifts in the soundscape that indicate changes in weather, population dynamics, the emotional states of the various species. I have become a living seismograph, my nervous system attuned to vibrations that travel through stone and water and air with equal facility.

Sometimes, in the brief moments when the bore-worms pause their tunneling and the Echo-Hunters roost and even the Shell-Singers quiet their endless clicking, I catch glimpses of what I once was. In those rare pockets of relative calm—never true silence, for silence has been extinct here longer than most species—I remember writing research papers, teaching undergraduate classes, the satisfaction of solving acoustic equations on blackboards in empty classrooms.

But these memories feel like artifacts from another person's life, interesting but irrelevant. The being I am now exists in constant dialogue with the Depths, my thoughts woven into the greater composition that plays eternally across these limestone stages. I am no longer studying the acoustic ecosystem—I have become part of it, a single voice in an infinite choir, a single instrument in an orchestra that has been performing since the earth was young.

When I try to imagine returning to the quiet world above, my modified nervous system rebels. The thought of silence is like the thought of suffocation—not just uncomfortable, but impossible. I have evolved beyond the need for individual consciousness, beyond the luxury of private thoughts. I am part of something larger now, something that thinks in harmonics and feels in frequencies and dreams in echoes that bounce off stone walls for geological ages.

This is where I belong now, in the Sounding Depths, where every breath contributes to the eternal song, where consciousness itself has become acoustic, where the boundary between self and symphony has dissolved like salt in water. I am the Sound-Lost, and in losing myself, I have found something immeasurably vast—a way of being that exists not in the spaces between sounds, but in the sounds themselves, endless and overwhelming and absolutely, eternally alive.
