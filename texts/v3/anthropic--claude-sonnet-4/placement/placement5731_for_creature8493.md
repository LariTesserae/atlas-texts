I am a **bridge-tender**, one of the few who walks between worlds.

My hands know every strand of the great vine spans that connect our terraces—which fibers grow brittle with age, which new shoots promise strength for seasons to come. Each morning I make my rounds in the grey-green dawn light, testing tensions with fingertips that read the stress in living rope like others read books. The bridges speak to me through their swaying: the deep, slow rhythm of the main spans that carry market traffic, the quick flutter of the narrow walkways the children prefer, the groaning protest of an overloaded connection that needs reinforcement.

I was born to this work, as my grandmother was before me. We are the ones who remember which vines were planted thirty seasons ago, who guided their growth around the iron anchors set into the weeping stone. My tools are few but precious: a knife whose edge is honed weekly on the special stones found only in the deepest pools, grafting paste made from ground minerals and tree sap, coils of young vine that I tend in my own small garden plot.

But I am more than maintenance keeper. I am translator between the city and its inhabitants, reading the intentions of growth itself. When the canyon wants to throw a new bridge across the gap—when I see the perfect convergence of anchor points and the right vines reaching toward each other—I help guide the connection. Sometimes this means training growth over seasons, whispering to the plants in the old tongue my grandmother taught me, feeding mineral-rich water to the roots that will become tomorrow's infrastructure.

The most delicate work happens during the growing storms, when the canyon weeps so heavily that new flowstone formations emerge overnight. These are dangerous times—the humidity climbs until breathing feels like drowning, and the sound becomes so overwhelming that even our signed conversations are lost in the percussion. But they are also times of opportunity. In the chaos of accelerated growth, new possibilities emerge. I have helped birth bridges in a single night, guiding the formation of flowstone arches while they were still soft enough to shape by hand.

The other inhabitants trust me to know the safe paths when the familiar ones become unreliable. During the green season, when growth explodes through the canyon with almost violent abundance, established routes can become impassable in days—blocked by new fruit curtains, made treacherous by unexpected flowstone formations, or simply overwhelmed by the sheer density of life. I maintain the secondary paths, the hidden routes, the emergency ways that most never see. I know where to step when the regular bridges are overloaded with market day crowds.

My dwelling is a small alcove high on the south wall, behind a curtain of night-blooming vines that flower with pale luminescence in the darkest hours. The location was chosen not for comfort but for perspective—from here, I can see the full span of our narrow world, can read the health of every connection, can spot problems before they become dangers. My windows are gaps in the living wall, and birds nest in the corners where my roof meets the stone.

In the quiet moments—rare in our echoing world—I sometimes think about the weight we all carry. Not physical weight, though that too, but the responsibility of living in a place so dense with life that every action ripples outward. When I guide a vine's growth, I'm not just making a bridge; I'm shaping the future paths of hundreds of my neighbors. When I decide a span is too dangerous and close it, I'm changing how our whole community moves through space.

The children follow me sometimes, learning through imitation the subtle arts of reading plant intention and stone mood. They have hands quick enough to catch the meanings I sign to them while my fingers are busy with vine work, eyes sharp enough to spot the tiny signs that indicate where new growth wants to emerge. Some will become bridge-tenders themselves; others will take the knowledge into different crafts. All will carry forward the deep understanding that in Keth-Mora, we do not simply live among the growing things—we are part of their conversation.

I am happiest during the deep-night hours, when most sleep and the canyon settles into its quieter rhythms. Then I can hear the individual voices within the symphony: the particular drip that means a new stalactite is forming, the whisper of vine growth, the subtle shift of stone still finding its final shape. In these moments, moving through my vertical realm on bridges I have helped create, I feel the profound satisfaction of being exactly where I belong—suspended between walls I could touch if I stretched my arms, cradled in the narrow space that contains our entire abundant world.
