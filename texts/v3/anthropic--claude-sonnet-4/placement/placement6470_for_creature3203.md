I would find myself among the **Moss Whisperers**—those who tend the threshold spaces where coral meets air, where the city's breath first touches the outside world.

My body would be slight and wiry, adapted for climbing the tower's outer faces where the coral grows thin and fragile. My hands would be covered in a fine, almost imperceptible down that helps me grip the calcified surfaces, and my feet would have developed the broad, sensitive pads of a gecko, able to find purchase on even the smoothest coral walls. Unlike the Deep Listeners with their elongated limbs, I would be compact and precise in my movements, built for the precarious work of tending the air-scoops and the delicate moss gardens that line the tower's exterior passages.

My role would be essential but often invisible—I would cultivate the specialized moss colonies that filter and condition the air entering the city. These aren't the lush, succulent plants of the interior chambers, but hardy, almost crystalline organisms that can survive the temperature extremes where the coral tower meets the desert world above. They form living membranes across the air-scoops, screening out dust and sand while allowing the flow of wind that feeds the city's circulation. Some varieties can actually cool the incoming air through their metabolic processes, while others release microscopic spores that carry nutrients deeper into the tower.

I would speak in the quietest voice of all the Khala-Nadir, my words barely audible whispers that won't disturb the delicate equilibrium of the threshold spaces. My language would incorporate long pauses and inhalations, synchronized with the breathing of the city itself. I would know every crack in the outer walls where moisture might escape, every place where the interior heat signatures become visible to the dangerous surface dwellers who might threaten our hidden sanctuary.

My skin would show a mottled pattern that mirrors the play of light and shadow on coral surfaces, allowing me to blend almost invisibly with the tower walls during my maintenance work. In the brightest desert sunlight, I could become nearly indistinguishable from the bleached calcium carbonate, while in the amber twilight of the deeper passages, I would take on the warm, translucent glow of the coral itself.

Unlike the other circles who work in community harmonics, I would often work alone or in pairs, my partner and I developing an almost telepathic awareness of each other's movements as we navigate the narrow ledges and overhangs of the tower's exterior. We would communicate through minute changes in breathing patterns and the subtle chemical signals our moss-adapted skin could release and detect—a form of communication that wouldn't carry beyond our immediate workspace.

My relationship with the city would be one of guardian and membrane. I would feel every change in the external environment first—the approach of sandstorms, the subtle shifts in humidity that might indicate distant rainfall, the vibrations that could signal approaching travelers or predators. My moss partners would relay this information through biochemical changes that I could read like text, their color shifts and scent variations forming a complex language of environmental conditions.

During the hottest part of the day, when the surface becomes unbearable even for my adapted body, I would retreat to the **Transition Chambers**—spaces neither fully inside nor outside the city proper, where the coral walls are thin enough to let in light but thick enough to provide shelter. Here I would tend my rarest specimens: moss varieties that bloom only in the precise temperature gradients found at these thresholds, their tiny flowers releasing spores that drift both inward to feed the deeper gardens and outward to potentially seed new growth on distant coral towers.

I would age differently from the other Khala-Nadir. While the Deep Listeners merge more deeply with the coral itself, and the Coral Whisperers develop increasingly symbiotic relationships with the living stone, I would become more ephemeral over time, my body developing an almost mist-like quality that allows me to exist partially in both worlds—the dry, harsh surface and the humid coral interior. The eldest Moss Whisperers are said to achieve a state where they can disperse themselves into component particles during extreme weather, then reform when conditions improve.

My deepest knowledge would be of the **Lost Towers**—other coral cities scattered across the desert, some still inhabited, others long dead, their moss membranes dried to dust and their air-scoops choked with sand. Through the network of drifting spores and the subtle chemical communications that carry between towers, I would maintain contact with my counterparts in distant settlements. We would share information about weather patterns, resource availability, and the movements of surface dwellers, forming an invisible communication network across the waterless world.

In the city's council gatherings, my voice would represent the perspective of the boundary itself—not fully of either world, but essential to the connection between them. I would speak for the need to remain hidden, for the careful balance required to take what the surface offers without revealing our presence to those who would exploit or destroy our sanctuary.

My greatest fear would be the day when the moss networks begin to fail—when the increasing aridity finally overwhelms even our most adapted organisms, or when the surface world changes so dramatically that our filtering systems can no longer process what tries to enter. But my greatest hope would be carried in the spores themselves: that somewhere in their microscopic genetic memory lies the pattern for survival in the returning seas, and that when water finally comes again to this land, my moss partners will remember how to breathe underwater as well as in air.

I would be the city's first warning of change, and perhaps its last protection—a living filter between the world that was and the world that might yet be.
