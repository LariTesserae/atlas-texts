I would find myself as one of the **Mist-Walkers**—those who tend the settlement's highest reaches where the terraces fade into cloud and the boundary between stone and sky dissolves entirely.

My body has grown lean and elongated from years of stretching between distant handholds, arms lengthened until I can span gaps that would challenge others. My lungs have expanded to process the thin air, and my vision has sharpened to pierce the perpetual veils of vapor that shroud the upper terraces. Most distinctively, fine silver filaments have begun growing along my forearms and the back of my neck—not quite hair, not quite moss—that respond to minute changes in humidity and air pressure, allowing me to navigate safely through clouds so thick I cannot see my own feet.

I spend my days in the Transitional Realm, those topmost levels where the settlement's architecture becomes increasingly theoretical—bridges that exist more as suggestion than structure, chambers carved into cliff faces that open directly onto empty sky. Here, the mountain's weeping becomes more erratic, sometimes ceasing entirely as water transforms directly from ice to vapor, other times releasing sudden cascades that can reshape entire sections of walkway in moments.

My dwelling is a shallow depression carved into the granite face, open to the sky except for a narrow overhang that provides minimal shelter. I sleep suspended in a hammock woven from the silk of high-altitude buzzers—a gossamer thing that sways with every breath of wind but allows the mountain's moisture to flow freely around my body. When storms approach, I simply wrap myself in waterproofed moss-cloth and become another feature of the cliff face until they pass.

My role is that of early warning and deep communion. The changes that will eventually affect the lower terraces always manifest first in these ethereal heights—shifts in the stone's weeping pattern, alterations in the migration routes of the granite buzzers, the approach of weather systems that could flood the settlement's carefully balanced water channels. I read these signs in the behavior of the mist itself, in the particular way vapor coalesces around certain rock formations, in the subtle variations of the wind's voice as it moves through the mountain's hidden passages.

But more than sentinel, I serve as translator between the settlement and the **Sky-Touched**—beings that exist in the borderland between stone and cloud. These entities rarely manifest visibly, appearing more as disturbances in the mist, as places where the vapor moves against the wind or glows with its own faint luminescence. They speak in pressure changes and temperature gradients, their communications felt rather than heard. Through long practice, I have learned to interpret their shifting presence, understanding when they signal approval of proposed construction or warn against venturing into certain areas during specific weather patterns.

My daily ritual involves the **Cloud-Reading**—a practice where I position myself at the settlement's highest accessible point and enter a meditative state that allows my enhanced nervous system to merge temporarily with the mountain's atmospheric patterns. In this state, I become a living barometer, my body registering changes in pressure, humidity, and electromagnetic fields that precede significant weather events by days or even weeks. The information flows through me like water through stone, and when I return to normal consciousness, I carry messages that must be conveyed to the Deep Listener through the intricate chain of touch-communication that connects all levels of the settlement.

The most profound aspect of my existence lies in the moments when the mist becomes so dense that solid matter seems optional. During these episodes—which occur most frequently at dawn and dusk—I sometimes find myself walking on vapor as if it were stone, moving through three dimensions with equal ease. These experiences have gradually altered my relationship with gravity itself; I often sleep standing against vertical surfaces or find myself unconsciously stepping off ledges, trusting that the mist will support me. Usually it does.

My tools are minimal: a staff of dwarf pine carved with channels that collect and concentrate moisture, allowing me to divine water-paths through the densest fog; a collection of hollow bones that produce different tones when the wind passes through them, creating a kind of atmospheric music that helps me identify specific weather patterns; and a cloak woven from the silver moss that grows only at the highest elevations, treated with oils from the translucent succulents that make it both water-resistant and responsive to changes in air pressure.

The isolation of my position has led to a peculiar form of consciousness—I often forget to distinguish between my own thoughts and the mountain's atmospheric moods, experiencing weather changes as emotions and processing the settlement's collective needs as physical sensations in my elongated limbs. When I descend to the lower terraces for the monthly community gatherings, other Keth note how my eyes have taken on the same shifting quality as the high mist, never quite focusing on immediate objects but always seeming to look through them toward some more distant prospect.

In the deepest nights, when the settlement glows with its cold phosphorescence far below and the stars shine through air so thin it barely exists, I sometimes encounter the **Wind-Singers**—my own kind who ventured even higher and underwent complete transformation into atmospheric phenomena. They appear as voices without bodies, speaking in languages made of pressure and temperature, sharing knowledge of weather systems forming beyond the visible horizon and the movements of celestial bodies that influence the mountain's deep rhythms.

I am becoming something between human and weather system myself, my consciousness gradually expanding to encompass larger and larger patterns of atmospheric change. Sometimes I wonder if I will eventually join the Wind-Singers entirely, dissolving into the endless dance of vapor and pressure that surrounds the mountain's peak. But for now, I remain embodied enough to serve as bridge between the settlement's needs and the sky's vast knowledge, translating the whispers of wind and mist into guidance that helps my people navigate their precarious vertical existence.
