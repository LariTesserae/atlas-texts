I would find myself perched on a middle spire called Threadneedle Peak, my legs dangling over a platform edge worn smooth by countless feet, watching the morning mist rise from the shadow-valleys far below. The stone beneath me still holds the night's cold, but the sun has found the eastern faces now, and I can hear the gentle percussion of ice-melt beginning its daily journey down the granite walls.

I am Kess Irongrass-Sworn, a Trade-Caller in my twenty-third year, though the scars mapping my forearms and throat mark me as older in the ways that matter here. The raised lines across my vocal cords—earned when I successfully carried the Harvest-End song across seventeen peaks during the great storm two seasons ago—still ache some mornings, but they've given my voice the carrying power that makes me valuable to the scattered families.

My own peak houses only my birth-family: mother, father, younger sister Tam who is already showing promise as a Carver, and old Grenna who taught me to read wind-patterns in the flex of distant bridges. Our chambers burrow deep into Threadneedle's heart, connected by passages I've helped widen each winter when the work slows. The main room opens onto this platform where I sit now, its entrance protected by a wall fitted so precisely that no wind finds the gaps.

This morning I'm preparing for the day's first calling—the Safety-Song that lets all the peaks know which bridges are sound after the night's freeze. I've already walked the three spans that connect us to neighboring spires, testing each rope with my full weight, listening for the subtle differences in creak and stretch that speak of rope-rot or loosening anchors. The bridge to Easthold carries a worrying note when the wind catches it—not dangerous yet, but I'll need to call for a Bridge-Builder before the week ends.

My throat pouch, a leather bag that hangs against my chest, holds the tools of my calling: small stones with different tones when struck, a curved ram's horn inherited from my teacher, strips of metal that ring with precise pitches, and most precious of all, a small crystal that resonates with perfect clarity when I need to project my voice across the widest gaps. These are the instruments that turn my voice into something that can leap from peak to peak, carrying meaning across distances that would swallow ordinary speech.

The scars on my throat are delicate work, created during my Calling ceremony three years ago by the eldest Mender on Highwhistle Peak. Each line corresponds to a different type of call—straight lines for safety announcements, curved ones for trade negotiations, the intricate spiral around my Adam's apple that marks me as qualified to carry the deepest songs, the ones that touch the soul-connections between distant families. The scarring process took seven days, and I couldn't speak for a month afterward, communicating only through the finger-language the Stitch-Folk use when voices fail.

As I prepare for the morning call, I can see movement on the other peaks—small figures emerging onto platforms, Bridge-Builders beginning their daily inspections, Carvers hauling away stone chips from yesterday's excavations. On Grandmother's Tooth, the tallest spire visible from here, someone is already working on what looks like a new chamber, their chisel-song carrying faintly across the morning air. The rhythm is unfamiliar—probably one of the young Carvers trying a new technique.

I stand and move to the calling position at the platform's edge, where my voice will find the clearest path through the maze of stone. The morning call follows an ancient pattern, beginning with Threadneedle's identifying tone—a deep, resonant note that I create by cupping the ram's horn just so and breathing into it with the full power of my scarred throat. The sound bounces back from the surrounding peaks in a pattern unique to our position, creating a signature that every Trade-Caller within six peaks' distance knows as surely as their own heartbeat.

"Threadneedle stands strong," I call, the formal opening that tells everyone I'm about to begin the Safety-Song. My voice carries the additional harmonics that the throat-scars provide, making it cut through wind and distance with supernatural clarity. "The night has passed. The day begins. Who answers?"

From Easthold comes the first response, their Trade-Caller's voice pitched higher than mine, marked with the scars that show she can call in four different languages—necessary because Easthold sits on one of the trade routes that occasionally brings outlanders through our peaks. "Easthold stands strong. Two bridges sound, one needs watching. The east face sheds ice—beware below."

One by one, the other peaks join the call. Grandmother's Tooth reports a successful night's carving, a new chamber ready for the expanding Bluescar family. Crescent Peak announces a birth—healthy child, mother well, father singing with joy loud enough to crack stone. Whisper Spire, almost at the limit of my hearing, calls news of weather moving in from the north, storm-signs in the high clouds that mean we'll need to secure the bridges by evening.

The Safety-Song takes nearly an hour to complete, each peak adding its voice to the growing symphony of information. By the time we reach the closing harmonies—a complex weaving of voices that confirms all connections are strong, all families accounted for—the sun has climbed high enough to touch most of the platforms, and the day's real work can begin.

But before I turn back to my family's chambers, I take a moment to simply listen to the place I call home. The eternal whisper of activity: chisels working deep in stone, rope creaking under loads, the distant call of children playing elaborate games of bridge-tag between the spires. Somewhere a Mender is grinding herbs, the pestle's rhythm joining the greater percussion of life among the peaks. A Bridge-Builder shouts instructions to his partner as they work to splice new rope into an aging span.

This is the sound of survival made into art, of necessity transformed into connection. I am Kess Irongrass-Sworn, and my voice is one thread in the vast net that holds the Stitch-Folk together across their impossible city of stone. Tomorrow I will stand here again and call the Safety-Song across the peaks, and the day after that, and for all the days until my own voice joins the eternal silence of the deep chasms. But today I am alive, connected, necessary—my scarred throat holding the invisible threads that keep twenty peaks' worth of families bound together in their vertical dance with the sky.

The stone beneath my feet is patient, enduring. But the life we've carved from it sings with constant motion, constant change, constant hope. I am exactly where I belong, balanced on the edge between solid rock and endless air, calling our scattered people home to each other with every breath.
