I would find myself here as **Calla the Sound-Keeper**, though I came to this calling slowly, almost by accident.

I arrived at the Quarry Courts seven years ago as an outsider—a wandering scribe who had spent too many years in dusty libraries, my fingers cramped from copying texts, my ears ringing with silence. I came seeking stories of the stone-workers, planning to document their techniques and move on. But the first night I spent here, lying on a borrowed bedroll in Hadric's workshop, I was kept awake not by discomfort but by revelation.

The sounds that emerged from the wet stone walls were not chaos but music—complex, layered, constantly shifting. Each workshop contributed its own voice: the sharp ping of Tam's precise cuts, the deeper drumming of apprentices learning rhythm, the whisper-scrape of finishing work. But underneath it all, I heard something the quarryfolk themselves had grown too accustomed to notice: the stone was responding, singing back in frequencies just below the range of conscious hearing.

I began to map these sounds. At first, the others thought me touched by sun-sickness or stone-madness. Why would anyone choose to sit still when there was cutting to be done? But gradually, they noticed that I could predict things: which workshop would need its water channel cleared before the sound grew muddy, where micro-cracks were forming by the subtle change in a wall's resonance, when the apprentices were falling out of proper rhythm before their masters consciously noticed.

Now I move through the courts with a collection of small tools—tuning forks made from quarry iron, thin copper rods that ring true, hollow reeds that amplify the stone's whispers. I tap walls and listen to their responses, mapping the settlement's acoustic health like others map its physical structure. I know which times of day the echoes are clearest, which weather makes the stone sing brightest, how the sound changes as new chambers are carved and old ones deepen with age.

My workshop—though I do little traditional cutting—sits at what I've discovered is the acoustic center of the settlement, where five different sound-paths converge. The walls here are carved with my own kind of measurements: shallow notches that catch specific frequencies, channels shaped to amplify rather than drain. I've learned to cut stone not for water-flow but for sound-flow, creating chambers that hold and transform the quarry's music.

The children seek me out when they want to hear stories, because I've learned to make my voice carry through the stone corridors in ways that seem almost magical. The adults come to me when something sounds wrong—when a familiar workshop rhythm goes sour, when the water-songs change in ways that suggest blockage or shifting foundations. 

I sleep deeply now, my body finally in tune with the quarry's rhythms. My dreams are filled not with the geometries of cut stone but with the mathematics of sound—wave patterns that spiral through limestone chambers, frequencies that build and interfere and resolve into new harmonies. When I wake, I listen first to the morning's particular music, reading in it the story of how the stone has changed overnight, what the day's work will bring, where the settlement's voice is strongest and where it needs tending.

I have become the keeper of something the quarryfolk always possessed but never named: their home's hidden song, the acoustic soul that makes this maze of workshops not just a workplace but a living instrument, played daily by dozens of hands and answered by the patient, resonant voice of stone itself.
