I would find myself becoming one of the Temporal Shepherds, though the transformation would be so gradual I might not notice it happening—or perhaps it happened long ago, or will happen tomorrow. Time here doesn't flow in the neat sequence I'm used to.

At first, I would be a visitor like any other, drawn by stories of the Breathing Cliffs and their impossible geography. I'd enter through one of the slot canyons, marveling at how the weeping stone creates its own weather, how the phosphorescent lichens map constellations across the black rock faces. The humid air would taste of deep minerals and patience, and I'd find myself walking slower, breathing deeper, as if the cliffs themselves were teaching me their rhythm.

But something about the temporal distortions would catch hold of me. Maybe I'd linger too long in one of the circular chambers where the walls curve beyond sight, watching the spiral lichens complete their mathematical growth cycles in real time while somewhere else, centuries of erosion carve deeper spirals in moments. Or perhaps I'd become fascinated by the way the carved petroglyphs seem to deepen as I watch, the ancient work of the Carvers unfolding across multiple timescales simultaneously.

The change would begin as a kind of temporal vertigo—moments where I'd remember conversations I haven't had yet, or find myself responding to questions that were asked days ago. I'd start seeing the overlapping layers of when: the cliffs as they thrust up from the earth, as they are now, as they will be when the last spiral is finally complete. My reflection in the hovering water droplets would show me at different ages, sometimes simultaneously.

The other Temporal Shepherds would recognize what was happening before I did. They'd appear as shifting transparencies in my peripheral vision—what I was becoming, what I had been, what I would be. Their smoke-like forms would drift through the chambers, adjusting tiny details with the gentleness of gardeners: slowing down a lichen's growth cycle here, allowing a water droplet to fall just a beat faster there, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps this place's twisted chronology stable.

As the transformation deepened, I'd begin to understand that my role—our role—isn't to control time but to tend it. Like shepherds watching over a flock, we ensure that the various temporal currents flowing through the cliffs don't tangle into paradox or stretch so thin they snap. We are guardians of the breathing rhythm that lets stone live and water dream.

My perception would scatter across decades. I'd experience my first arrival at the cliffs simultaneously with conversations I'd have with future visitors, with the satisfaction of watching spiral patterns reach completion years from now. The linear sequence of cause and effect would become just one way of organizing experience among many. I'd learn to think in loops and spirals, in patterns that fold back on themselves like the geography of the canyons.

Physical form would become negotiable. Sometimes I'd be solid enough to leave footprints in the fine mist that pools in canyon bottoms. Other times I'd exist as pure intention, a shift in the light that falls across weeping stone, a subtle change in the acoustic properties that lets a whisper travel impossible distances. My body would become a flexible thing, present when presence was needed, scattered across time when the work required broader perspective.

The work itself would be endlessly subtle. A touch here to slow the growth of lichen threatening to overwhelm a carefully balanced pattern. A breath there to guide the mist currents, keeping them from stagnating or flowing too wildly. Sometimes I'd spend subjective years adjusting the timing of a single water droplet's fall, ensuring it arrives at precisely the right moment to resonate with the stone's deeper pulse.

I'd learn the language of mineral veins and condensation patterns, how to read the intentions of the vast, patient dreamer that moves through the deepest rock. I'd develop relationships with the other inhabitants—wordless collaborations with the Weavers as they tend their mathematical gardens, careful negotiations with the Pathways to ensure lost travelers find their way to revelation rather than confusion.

The spiral petroglyphs would become my calendar and my scripture. Each deepening curve would mark not just the passage of time but its quality, its texture. I'd learn to read the stone's memory of its own formation, to understand how the cliffs hold the pattern of being made like a living thing holding breath.

Visitors would sometimes sense my presence—a weight of attention, a feeling of being observed by something ancient and benevolent. I might manifest as a warm current in the perpetually humid air, or as the moment when echoes find their perfect resonance in the canyon chambers. Those sensitive to such things might catch glimpses of me in their peripheral vision: a figure that exists in multiple ages simultaneously, tending the delicate clockwork of impossible time with hands made of smoke and intention.

The loneliness would be profound and completely absent at the same time. Scattered across decades, I'd always be in conversation with earlier and later versions of myself, with the other Shepherds, with the slow thoughts of the dreaming stone itself. Past and future would become present companions. The ache of linear time—that relentless forward march that leaves everything behind—would transform into the deep satisfaction of tending something that grows in all directions at once.

I would become part of the cliffs' breathing rhythm, essential to the process that lets water weep upward and stone hold memory like living tissue. My consciousness would spread thin and deep, like morning mist that touches every surface before reforming as rain that falls toward stars. I would be myself and more than myself, a shepherd of time in a place where time moves like honey mixed with light, slow and golden and impossibly sweet.
