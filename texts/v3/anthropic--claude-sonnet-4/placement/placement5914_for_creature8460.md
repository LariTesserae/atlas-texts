I would find myself in the middle terraces, where the light shifts between the green-gold glow of the upper gardens and the deeper blue phosphorescence of the flooded chambers below. Here, sound becomes a geography all its own—the splash of children at play above me, the ancient stone-song of the Deepsingers' work echoing up from below, and always, always, the liquid percussion of water finding its way through carved channels.

I am a Currentkeeper's apprentice, though my hands know stone as well as water. My fingers trace the carved walls not just for navigation but for the stories they tell—feeling where my great-grandmother's chisel bit deep in her youth, where the mineral deposits have begun to blur the crisp edges of old reliefs, where new growth has taken root in the joints between fitted stones. My webbed fingers are stained permanent green from tending the flow gardens, where streams cascade through carefully arranged beds of phosphorescent moss.

In the pre-dawn hours, when even the children have stilled their splashing, I swim down to check the deep sluices. The water here is warmer, heavy with minerals that make my skin tingle. I can hold my breath for nearly fifteen minutes now—long enough to swim the flooded corridor to the ancient valve chamber and back. In the absolute darkness, my enlarged pupils catch nothing, so I navigate by the feel of current against my skin, by the way sound bounces off stone surfaces, by the subtle temperature changes that tell me whether I'm near a major flow or a stagnant pocket.

Sometimes, in these deepest chambers, I feel them—the Deep Ones. Not threatening, just... present. Patient. Watching. Their movements disturb the water in ways that speak of bodies shaped differently than mine, though I'm changing too. My ribs expand a little more each year. The webbing between my toes grows thicker. The patterns of calluses on my palms become more intricate as I learn to read stone through touch alone.

I surface gasping in a pool three terraces down from where I started, my lungs burning pleasantly with the effort. Above me, carved reliefs dance in the shifting phosphorescent light—images of the first flood, when my ancestors chose to carve rather than flee. Their faces seem to nod approval as bioluminescent algae drift past like falling stars.

By day, I help maintain the bamboo aqueducts, my practiced hands adjusting flows with touches so subtle they're almost ceremonial. Water responds to intention here as much as engineering. I've learned to read the moods of streams—when they're playful and want to overflow their channels, when they're sluggish and need coaxing through narrow passages, when they're ready to carve new routes through the living rock.

The children follow me sometimes, their large dark eyes curious about everything. They swim like dolphins, utterly at home in this drowned world, their laughter bubbling up through the water in ways that make the floating mats of flowers seem to pulse with extra light. I teach them to feel the difference between stones worn smooth by centuries of current and those freshly carved, to hear in the water's voice whether a passage ahead is clear or blocked.

In the evening, when the work is done, I drift in one of the meditation pools where the water runs deepest and most still. Here, the phosphorescence settles into lazy spirals, and if I'm very quiet, very patient, I can sometimes see all the way down to where shadows move that might be the Deep Ones, or might be my imagination, or might be my own future.

My body grows heavier with minerals each year, my bones denser, my movements more deliberate. Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming more stone than flesh, more current than creature. But then I feel the warm water against my translucent skin, taste the mineral sweetness on my lips, hear the endless symphony of the terraces rising and falling around me, and I know this is exactly what I'm meant to become—not lost to the flood, but found by it, shaped by it, made into something that could only exist in this place where mountain meets sea, where stone learns to breathe underwater, where the ancient art of becoming never ends.
