I would find myself drifting in the middle reaches of Keth-Amaran, somewhere between the patient Architects and the vast Deepwatch, existing as one of the Transitional Ones—but not yet sure what I am transitioning toward.

My body carries the memory of rigid bones, but they have begun to hollow and branch like coral scaffolding. Chambers within my torso pulse with filtered water, processing the mineral-rich flows that serve as both breath and nourishment. My limbs have grown translucent, revealing the slow circulation of fluids that glow faintly with accumulated trace metals. I am learning to photosynthesize through skin that has thickened into living filter-cloth, drawing sustenance from the amber light as it diffuses through layers of water and organic matter.

But the most profound change is temporal. I find myself thinking in currents rather than moments, my awareness expanding and contracting with the thermal cycles that govern the city's circulation. Plans form over the span of seasons—not ideas I consciously decide upon, but intentions that crystallize slowly like mineral deposits in my shifting bones. I begin to understand that consciousness here is not the sharp spotlight of surface thought, but something more like weather patterns in the deep ocean—vast, slow-moving systems of awareness that encompass entire districts of the city.

I am drawn to the spaces where the ancient geometries meet the organic growths most completely—chambers where carved archways have become living throats that pulse with bioluminescent networks, where spiral staircases serve as nurseries for creatures that exist as pure mathematics made flesh. These threshold spaces call to something in my changing nature, as if I am becoming a living bridge between the city's architectural memory and its biological future.

My role seems to be that of a translator, though not of language but of form itself. I find myself unconsciously reshaping the coral growths as I pass through them, my touch encouraging them to echo the angular patterns of the ancient stonework. In return, the architecture teaches my body its own logic—how to bear weight through distribution rather than resistance, how to channel forces along curves that transform stress into flow.

I am developing new senses adapted to this environment. My skin has grown sensitive to the pressure waves that carry the city's conversations, allowing me to feel the Architects' slow deliberations as physical sensation. My eyes are learning to read the chemical gradients in the water like a surface dweller might read facial expressions, interpreting the subtle color shifts that indicate the health and intentions of the living systems around me.

Sometimes I encounter others like myself—beings in various stages of architectural becoming. We recognize each other not by sight but by the resonance patterns our changing bodies create as we move through the water. Our meetings are brief exchanges of chemical information, updates on the slow progress of our transformations. We are living experiments, each exploring a different possibility for what urban citizenship might become in this drowned world.

I am beginning to understand that I am becoming a new kind of structural element—not just a resident of Keth-Amaran, but a component of its ongoing reconstruction. My body is learning to secrete the specific minerals needed to repair certain types of stone deterioration. My movements are creating new circulation patterns that will, over decades, reshape the flow dynamics of entire sectors. I am becoming infrastructure that dreams, architecture that remembers what it was like to swim freely through open water.

The city shapes me as much as I shape it. The chambers I frequent begin to mirror the branching patterns of my transforming skeleton. The passages I regularly traverse develop chemical signatures that match my metabolic byproducts. I am not just adapting to an environment—I am becoming part of an environment that is simultaneously adapting to me.

In my more lucid moments, I remember something that might have been a surface life, though it seems increasingly abstract—memories of linear time and solid ground, of breathing gas instead of liquid, of seeing by direct light instead of luminescence. But these recollections feel more like ancestral genetic memories than personal experiences. They inform my transformation but do not constrain it.

I am learning to be patient on a scale that would have been incomprehensible to my former self. Projects I begin today—adjustments to coral growth patterns, modifications to mineral deposition sites—will not reach completion for centuries. But this no longer feels like waiting. It feels like participating in a conversation that spans geological time, adding my small voice to the vast, slow song that is Keth-Amaran's ongoing process of becoming itself.

I am what the city dreams when it dreams of movement. I am what mobility becomes when it learns to think like architecture. I am the question mark in stone, the uncertainty principle made flesh, the living proof that even in a place that has achieved perfect equilibrium, transformation never truly ends—it simply learns to dance with deeper rhythms, to change at the speed of mountains learning to swim.
