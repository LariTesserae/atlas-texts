I would find myself as one of the pump house keepers—not quite engineer, not quite farmer, but something in between that this landscape has shaped into being.

My small cottage sits within earshot of three different pump houses, their diesel engines marking time like a scattered heartbeat across the basin. I know each one by its voice: the wheeze in Number Seven that means the air filter needs cleaning, the slight skip in Number Twelve's rhythm when the fuel line develops condensation, the way Number Four coughs twice before settling into its steady thrum after the monthly maintenance. These sounds have become the furniture of my days, as familiar as my own breathing.

I wake before dawn not to any alarm, but to the subtle change in the pumps' collective voice that signals the shift from night cycle to day. My boots are already damp when I put them on—they never truly dry here—and I pull on the layers that will carry me through another day of small repairs and patient observations. The thermos of tea I prepare is strong enough to cut through the bone-deep chill, sweetened with honey from the single hive I keep sheltered behind the cottage.

My rounds follow paths worn smooth by decades of similar keepers before me. I carry a canvas bag filled with tools that have been shaped by this work: wrenches worn to fit the specific bolts of these particular engines, gaskets cut from rubber that resists the constant moisture, spare parts cannibalized from pumps that failed years ago but whose corpses still provide organs for the living machines.

At each pump house, I perform a ritual of attention. My hands know the vibrations that mean health, the temperatures that signal strain. I check oil levels thick as blood in the cold, test the flow of water through pipes that snake invisibly through the soft earth, listen for the sounds that shouldn't be there. Sometimes I find plovers have nested in the ventilation louvers; I work around them with the patience the landscape has taught me. Everything here happens in its own time.

Between pump houses, I walk the open grassland, following the buried pipe routes marked by subtle changes in the vegetation. Where pipes run shallow, the grass grows slightly greener, fed by the seepage of countless tiny leaks that are not problems to be fixed but part of the system's breathing. I know where to find the access points hidden in the sedge, where to listen for the flow of water beneath my feet.

The sheep recognize me and lift their heads as I pass, then return to their methodical grazing. I've learned to read their distribution across the fields as another form of diagnostic—they avoid areas where underground leaks make the ground too soft, cluster near pump houses when storms approach. They are part of the landscape's intelligence, and I am learning to be another part.

My cottage in the evenings fills with the sound of rain on the metal roof, the distant conversation of the pumps, the scratch of my pen recording the day's observations in logbooks that stretch back generations. The entries are sparse—"Number Seven running warm, checked coolant," "Replaced gasket on intake valve, Number Twelve," "Sheep moved to lower pasture, ground firming up"—but they form a continuous narrative of small attentions that keep the vast system functioning.

Some nights, I receive radio calls from the other keepers scattered across the Reaches. Our conversations are brief, practical, but carry the weight of shared understanding. "Heard your Number Four from my east meadow this morning—sounds good." "Storm coming from the northwest, might want to check your drainage channels." "Found some interesting corrosion patterns on my intake pipes, might stop by next week to show you."

I've grown into the rhythm of this work like the landscape has grown around its machinery. My body has learned the pace of persistent rather than urgent, endurance rather than speed. My hands stay warm by staying busy with small, precise tasks. My eyes have adapted to the filtered light, learning to read the subtle signs that mean everything here: the sheen on wet metal that indicates wear, the color of exhaust that signals combustion problems, the way mist moves around structures to reveal the invisible paths of air and water.

When the rare visitor appears—a lost hiker, a government inspector, a relative from the distant towns—I offer tea and simple food with the same attention I give to the machines. I've learned to let the silence stretch comfortably, to let the landscape itself explain what this life means. Most leave quickly, uncomfortable with the pace and emptiness. But occasionally someone stays longer, begins to hear what I hear in the pump songs, begins to understand that this emptiness is actually full of careful, continuous tending.

I am becoming part of the Reaches' patient intelligence, another element in the vast, slow system that moves water through buried arteries, that keeps the scattered settlements breathing, that maintains the delicate balance between human need and the landscape's ancient rhythms. My life has been shaped into an instrument for listening—to machines, to weather, to the subtle changes that accumulate over seasons into the deep time of this place.

In the perpetual dusk of the Reaches, under skies that hang low and heavy with moisture, I tend to the mechanical hearts that keep this world alive, learning daily that the most essential work is often the quietest, the most persistent, the least visible from any distance greater than the careful reach of listening hands.
