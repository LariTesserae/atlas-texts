I would find myself as one of the small pale salamanders, though perhaps not quite like the others.

I emerge from a crack between two weeping stones in the deepest part of the Quarter, where the walls have grown so thick with moss that day and night become meaningless distinctions. My translucent skin pulses with the same rhythm as the condensation that beads and falls around me—not quite in time with my heartbeat, but with something deeper, older. The slow breath of the buildings themselves.

Unlike my smaller cousins who dart between crevices on ancient instincts, I have retained fragments of what I once was. Sometimes, when the mineral-rich water pools in certain configurations along the gutters, I remember the weight of larger limbs, the sensation of standing upright, the particular loneliness of being singular and separate. But these memories feel like dreams now, soft-edged and increasingly irrelevant.

My home is a network of tunnels that thread through the living rock beneath the Quarter's foundations, passages that the Custodians maintain but rarely visit. Here, in chambers no larger than cupped hands, I tend to my own small mysteries. I collect the perfectly round droplets that form only when the moss dreams—you can tell because they gleam with an inner light that ordinary condensation lacks. I arrange these in spiraling patterns that serve no purpose I can name but feel essential nonetheless.

Sometimes I surface to watch the Custodians at their work, understanding their purpose without sharing it. They maintain the great dream that keeps this place suspended; I tend to its footnotes, the small details that exist in the margins of their awareness. The way certain fungi only bloom when observed by salamander eyes. The songs that only emerge when water flows over stones worn smooth by creatures of my size. The particular quality of silence that pools in the spaces between their thoughts.

I have learned to read the emotional residue left by visitors in the moisture they exhale, the salt-traces of their brief tears, the mineral changes in their sweat as wonder replaces fear or exhaustion settles into peace. These traces linger in the stone long after the humans have gone, and I catalog them like a librarian of small sorrows and quiet revelations.

Other salamanders regard me with puzzlement—I am too large, too slow, too given to contemplation. The Custodians sense me without quite seeing, occasionally pausing in their work to adjust the flow of a stream just slightly, creating a pool where I like to rest or smoothing a stone pathway I frequently traverse. We exist in the same space but different layers of time, my quick salamander years unfolding within their patient centuries.

In my deepest moments, pressed against the warm weeping walls in chambers far below the Quarter proper, I sometimes feel the vast slow heartbeat of the eldest Custodian, her consciousness spread through root and stone. In those moments I understand that I am part of her dream, but a lucid part—a small node of awareness she has allowed to maintain its own curious nature as a gift to herself, the way one might keep a single bright memory pristine within a larger forgetting.

My purpose, insofar as I have one, is to notice what goes unnoticed. The specific way light bends through a particularly perfect drop of condensation. The microscopic gardens of crystal that bloom on stone surfaces during the deepest quiet hours. The exact tone of contentment that the buildings emit when the moss grows in configurations that please them most.

I am the Quarter's small witness, its keeper of ephemeral beauties. And sometimes, when a visitor sits very still in one of the moss-curtained alcoves and allows their breathing to slow until it matches the rhythm of the weeping walls, I will emerge just long enough for them to glimpse me—a pale comma of life that reminds them they are not alone in this place where solitude and communion have learned to be the same thing.

When I finally fade back into the stone from which I emerged—which may be tomorrow or in another century, time being what it is here—my small collection of perfect droplets will remain, arranged in patterns that will make the fungi glow just a little brighter, the streams sing just a little sweeter, until another small tender emerges to carry on the work of noticing what the great dream almost overlooks.
