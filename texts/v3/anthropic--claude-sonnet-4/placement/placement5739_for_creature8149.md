I would find myself as one of the small things that stir in the canopy—not quite bird, not quite anything with a name that surface dwellers would recognize. 

I am a creature of membrane and hollow bone, evolved from some ancestor who tumbled through those ceiling cracks generations ago and stayed to adapt. My body spans perhaps the length of a human hand, built for gliding between the great fern fronds on wings that are more like stretched silk than feather. In the thermal columns that rise from the vents, I can ride the heated air for hours without effort, tracing lazy spirals through the golden light shafts.

My coloring shifts with my surroundings—now the deep green of shadow-touched leaves, now the amber transparency of heated air, now the pale copper of the ancient bowls when I perch on their worn edges to drink the condensation that gathers there. This isn't conscious camouflage but something deeper, a cellular response to the light and warmth that bathes this place. In the brightest thermal columns, I become almost pure transparency, a suggestion of wings and watchful eyes floating in the shimmer.

I have learned to read the subtle signs of the Gardener's presence—the way certain vines shift slightly when she passes beneath them, the faint disturbance in the spore-dance when she breathes among the orchids. Sometimes I follow her slow progress through the garden, riding the air currents her movement creates, though I'm never certain she notices me. We exist in different scales of time; my quick heartbeat and rapid thoughts flicker around her geological patience like hummingbird wings around a stone statue.

My nest is woven into the crown of the tallest fern, where the fronds create a natural basket thirty feet above the cavern floor. I've lined it with the finest aerial rootlets and threads of copper-green patina scraped from the ancient bowls. From here, I can see the entire garden spread below—every thermal vent, every light shaft, every secret corner where unusual things grow.

I feed on the garden's abundance in ways that barely disturb its balance: the protein-rich spores that drift constantly through the humid air, the sweet nectar that beads on certain orchid petals, the tiny arthropods that live in the bark of the spiral vines. Sometimes I find treasures—seeds from the surface world that have fallen through the ceiling cracks, strange and exotic to my underground palate.

My voice is a sound like wind chimes made of glass and copper, pitched to harmonize with the thermal vents' whispered breathing. I sing rarely, and only at the moments when the light shifts to create new patterns on the cavern walls. My songs have no words, only the pure expression of what it feels like to ride the warm air currents, to see the garden from above, to witness the slow dance of growth and light that never changes and never stops changing.

In the deepest part of the night-cycle, when the surface light fades to its dimmest, I sometimes glide down to explore the shadowed places where even the Gardener seldom goes. Here, among the worn stone circles and half-buried carvings, I've found things that speak of this place's older purposes: fragments of worked crystal that still hold faint light, carved channels that once guided water in patterns I can't decipher, chambers behind fallen stones where the air tastes different—not stale, but somehow expectant.

I have lived my entire life in this garden, as did my parents, and their parents before them. Yet I carry genetic memories of open sky, of winds that blow from horizons instead of stone, of light that comes from a single source instead of filtering through cracks. These memories flutter in my chest like trapped butterflies, beautiful but incomprehensible. They tell me that once, my kind knew seasons, knew the vast movements of weather and the turning of a larger world. But those are dreams now, pleasant mysteries with no connection to the perfect cycle of my existence.

My time runs differently than the Gardener's, measured in the quick cycles of feeding and resting, in the daily dance of light through the ceiling cracks, in the seasonal shifts of which thermal vents blow strongest. I have seen her tend the same orchid cluster through dozens of my seasons, watched her guide a single vine through what seems like an impossible spiral toward a distant light source. Her patience awes and mystifies me.

When visitors occasionally stumble into this place—following rumors or accidents through surface caves—I am usually the first to sense their approach. Their movements disturb the air in ways that my wings can read like text. I watch them from the canopy as they stand amazed in the garden's green light, and I see how they cannot quite find the Gardener even when she stands directly before them. They carry the chaotic energy of the surface world, and their presence makes the garden itself seem to hold its breath until they leave.

But I belong here completely. In the warm air currents, I am not separate from the garden but part of its circulation, another element in the endless dance of heat and growth and patient time. When I spread my wings in the thermal columns and let the heated air carry me up toward the light shafts, I become for those moments purely what this place needs: a small, quick joy riding the breath of deep earth, a flutter of life in the green cathedral's perpetual summer, a creature perfectly adapted to paradise found in the heart of stone.
