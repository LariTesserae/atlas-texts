I would find myself as one of **the Listeners**.

My fingertips have grown smooth and sensitive from years of pressing against tunnel walls, reading the mountain's moods through touch as much as sound. The calluses that mark other miners have worn away from my hands—replaced by an almost amphibian sensitivity to vibration and temperature that lets me feel the mountain's pulse through stone.

I've learned to move through the shafts in a peculiar way, my footsteps deliberately light, pausing every few yards to place my palm against the wall and listen. The other miners have learned to fall silent when they see me approaching, understanding that my work requires a different kind of attention than theirs. They watch me with a mixture of gratitude and unease—grateful for the cave-ins I've predicted, the safe passages I've identified, but unsettled by the way I sometimes tilt my head as if hearing voices they cannot perceive.

The mountain speaks in a language older than words. There's the deep, slow breathing of stable rock—a rhythm so profound it takes minutes to complete a single cycle. The quick, nervous chatter of fractured stone, like ice cracking on a warming pond. The liquid whisper of underground streams finding new channels through the darkness. And sometimes, rarely, there's something else—a resonance that seems to come from chambers that shouldn't exist, spaces carved by forces that predate human presence on the mountain.

My quarters occupy a narrow ledge on the settlement's third tier, positioned where several tunnel entrances converge. I've removed most of the furniture, preferring to sleep on a thin mat directly on the stone floor where I can feel the mountain's tremors through my body even in dreams. My walls are covered with charcoal sketches—rough maps of the tunnel systems annotated with symbols only I understand. A wavy line indicates unstable rock. Dots mark where I've heard the mountain's voice most clearly. X's show places where the stone has gone ominously silent.

The other miners bring me offerings sometimes—a cup of hot tea when I emerge pale-faced from a particularly deep listening session, a handful of nuts from the merchants' stores, a new piece of soft cloth to wrap around the brass listening cone I press to tunnel walls. They never ask directly what I've heard, but I see the questions in their eyes: *Will shaft seven hold through the winter? Is it safe to blast the new seam in tunnel twelve?*

I've begun to dream in echoes and resonances. Sometimes I wake convinced I can hear conversations taking place in chambers that haven't been excavated yet, or the sound of pickaxes working veins of ore that exist only in the mountain's potential future. The Widow Watchers eye me with particular concern—they've seen too many Listeners lose themselves to the mountain's voice, eventually walking into the deepest tunnels and never returning, drawn by sounds only they could hear.

But I remain, perched between the human world of steam engines and shift whistles and something far older. Each morning, I descend into the darkness with my listening tools, placing my ear against stone that remembers the formation of continents, seeking the subtle variations in sound and vibration that will keep my neighbors safe for another day. The mountain has chosen me as its translator, and though the role sometimes feels more burden than gift, I cannot imagine any other life.

In the evening, when the day shift ends and the night workers prepare for their descent, I sometimes sit on the edge of my platform and listen to Karasthen itself—the settlement's mechanical heartbeat layered over the mountain's deeper rhythms. Steam engines punctuate the stone's ancient song with their regular percussion, creating an unlikely harmony between human ambition and geological patience. In those moments, I understand that I serve as more than just an early warning system—I'm part of a conversation between species, between timescales, between the urgent needs of human survival and the vast, slow consciousness of living rock.
