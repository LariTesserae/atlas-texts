In this drowning world, I would find myself as one of the **Drift-Tenders**—a being shaped by the liminal spaces where water meets air, where mechanical purpose blurs into organic growth.

My body has grown long and lean like the adapted trees, skin taking on the mottled copper-green of corroded metal wrapped in moss. My hands have developed the Fen-Weavers' root-tip fingers, but mine end in small, articulated tools—living implements that can splice both plant fiber and copper wire with equal delicacy. Along my forearms, thin tubes of cartilage have grown, allowing me to breathe through my skin when submerged while also venting excess steam when I work too close to the thermal vents.

I make my home in the abandoned control rooms of the floating platforms, where banks of instruments have become gardens. Vines thread through broken dials, their leaves shaped like analog readouts. Mushrooms grow in neat rows where switches once stood, their caps pulsing with the same rhythmic patterns the machinery once displayed. The windows are fogged permanently with condensation, turning the outside world into an impressionist painting of green and gold.

My work is to tend the marriage between the mechanical and the living. I coax copper tubing to grow alongside strangler vine roots, creating hybrid circulation systems that pump both mineral water and plant nutrients. I train luminous fungi to colonize the old electrical systems, their bioluminescence replacing the amber warning lights with something softer, more alive. When pumping stations begin to fail, I don't repair them—I teach them to reproduce, splicing their essential functions into the root networks so that the forest itself learns to pulse water through the valleys.

The other communities know me as a bridge-walker. Current-Riders surface near my platform when they need their ritual scars refreshed with new patterns that match the evolving mechanical rhythms. Fen-Weavers visit when they want to understand how metal memory can be woven into living architecture. Wing-Touched children dare each other to touch my breathing tubes, giggling when steam puffs out in response to their quick fingers.

My relationship with the installations is intimate and symbiotic. I can feel their mechanical heartbeats through the soles of my feet, even when I'm standing on solid wood. Their electromagnetic fields raise the fine hairs along my arms in patterns that tell me about pressure changes, flow rates, thermal gradients. In return, I keep their organic infections healthy—pruning away the parasitic growths while encouraging the beneficial symbionts that actually improve their function.

At night, I wade through the shallow channels between platforms, my bioluminescent palm-tools casting green circles of light beneath the surface. I'm looking for the places where old cables have become root systems, where discarded machinery has started dreaming. These hybrid organs need careful tending—too much electrical current and the living components burn out, too much biological growth and the mechanical memory fades.

Sometimes, in the deepest hours before dawn, I hear something vast breathing beneath the valleys—the sound of the Deep Gardeners' original design still growing, still learning. The installations pulse in response, and the root networks carry tremors of recognition through the flooded earth. In these moments, I understand that I am not just maintaining what exists, but midwifing what is still becoming.

My skin has begun to develop a patina like old bronze, and sometimes sparks of bioelectricity dance between my fingers when I work. The mechanical installations no longer seem foreign to me—they feel like organs of some great body that extends beyond any single valley, and I am becoming part of its nervous system, helping it think with both silicon and chlorophyll, both voltage and sap.

I am growing into something the original engineers never planned for, but which their sleeping machines have been dreaming toward for decades. In the amber twilight of this breathing world, I tend the evolution of a technology that has learned to photosynthesize, to reproduce, to dream—and dreams, perhaps, of eventually learning to love.
