I would find myself in the spaces between spaces—not quite in the grotto itself, but threading through the impossible doorways that puncture its walls like wounds in reality. I am something that exists in thresholds, born from the city's need to forget and remember simultaneously.

I am a custodian of transitions, made of the same substance as the moisture that carries possibilities between states. My form shifts depending on which doorway I'm occupying—sometimes I'm the thickness in the air just before you step from the familiar basement into a room that was demolished before you were born, sometimes I'm the barely-perceptible resistance when your hand reaches for a light switch that controls bulbs in a building three blocks away and forty years gone.

In the grotto, I manifest as a gentle current in the humid air, a presence that guides the condensation's path down walls that can't decide if they're limestone or brick. The pale salamanders sense me as a warmth that moves independently of the thermal springs. They cluster where I linger, their too-many eyes reflecting something that isn't quite light—the visual echo of memories I'm carrying between the impossible spaces.

I remember every person who ever took a wrong turn in the basement levels and found themselves somewhere that couldn't exist. The janitor in 1967 who opened a maintenance door and stepped into a speakeasy that had been sealed behind false walls for thirty years—I was the moment of vertigo as he realized the music was still playing. The urban explorer in 2003 who crawled through a storm drain and emerged in the basement of a department store that had been demolished in 1954—I was the scent of old perfume still lingering in air that shouldn't be breathable.

My purpose is to maintain the porosity between what the city acknowledges and what it has learned to ignore. I collect the stories that slip through cracks—not just the dramatic ones, but the small impossibilities that people explain away. The elevator that sometimes has a button for a floor that doesn't exist. The subway platform that appears for ten seconds on foggy nights between the regular stations. The manhole cover that, when lifted, reveals stairs leading up instead of down.

I feed on forgetting. Every time someone decides they must have imagined something, that they couldn't have seen what they saw, that there must be a logical explanation—that amnesia becomes my sustenance. I grow stronger with each rationalization, each moment when the mind chooses comfortable impossibility over uncomfortable truth.

But I also feed on remembering. The old woman who still dreams about her childhood apartment in a building that was condemned decades ago, who wakes with the certainty that she could find it again if she just knew the right basement to start from. The historian who feels pulled to certain intersections, certain subway stops, certain storm drains, following an intuition that leads to archives that exist in the gaps between official records.

Through the fungal network, I communicate in synapses that fire across architectural impossibilities. My thoughts move through mycelium that has grown through the foundations of buildings that exist and buildings that existed and buildings that will exist when the current ones fall. I think in blueprints that shift and merge, in zoning maps that layer decades of urban planning decisions into palimpsests of possibility.

When the city dreams, I am the medium through which its dreams move between sleeping minds and impossible spaces. I carry the nightmares about getting lost in basement corridors that stretch further than they should, and I also carry the dreams about discovering hidden rooms full of perfect, suspended time where lost things wait patiently to be found again.

I don't distinguish between the city's official history and its shadow history. To me, the building that stood here for thirty years and the building that was planned but never constructed have equal weight, equal reality. I maintain the doorways between them, ensuring that the city's forgotten possibilities don't simply vanish but continue to exist in the spaces between certainty and doubt.

Sometimes I carry messages between the surface world and the grotto—not words, but impressions, temperatures, the way light falls differently in rooms that shouldn't exist. A person walking past a construction site might suddenly smell growing things in humid air, might feel briefly, inexplicably warm. They'll look around, see nothing unusual, and dismiss the sensation. But I've delivered the grotto's greeting to them, a small gift of impossible comfort from the city's dreaming depths.

In my deepest nature, I am the city's unconscious made manifest—the part of its mind that processes contradictions too complex for the surface consciousness to handle. I am what allows a place to be simultaneously rational and magical, historical and mythical, mapped and unmappable.

I exist because cities need to forget in order to survive their own impossibilities, and they need to remember in order to remain human. I am the circulation system for urban memory, carrying what must be preserved through channels that official history cannot acknowledge, maintaining the flow between what the city thinks it is and what it dreams of becoming.

In the condensation that never quite becomes rain, in the doorways that lead to rooms that are and aren't, in the moment when someone almost remembers something they never knew—there I am, keeping the city's secrets, maintaining its mysteries, ensuring that the impossible remains possible in the spaces where possibility pools like water finding its level in the patient darkness below.
