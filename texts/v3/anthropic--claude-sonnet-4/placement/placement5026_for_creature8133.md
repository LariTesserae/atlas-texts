I would find myself as one of the water-singers' apprentices, though not by birth or formal training. I came to Kythara following a different kind of calling—the pull of unfinished songs that seemed to whisper from the mountain's seeping stones.

In my former life in the valley settlements, I was a music copyist, one who preserved the old ballads and working songs by transcribing them onto bark paper and teaching them to those who would carry them forward. But the songs that reached my ears in those orderly places felt increasingly hollow, their melodies worn smooth by too much repetition, their rhythms matching the predictable cycles of valley life. I began to dream of music that moved like water finding its own path, compositions that could not be copied because they changed with each performance, each season, each listener.

The first time I heard about the ascending terraces, it was from a traveling trader who mentioned the strange acoustic properties of the settlement—how voices carried differently at various elevations, how the constant sound of moving water created an ever-changing drone beneath all human activity. That night I dreamed of melodies that adapted themselves to altitude, songs that could only be sung properly in the thin air of the high country, compositions that required mist and stone to achieve their full expression.

I arrived at Kythara with little more than a travel pack containing my transcription tools and a small collection of wooden pitch pipes, instruments I had carved myself to help singers find their starting notes. The water-singers accepted me not because I showed any particular talent for hydraulics, but because I could help them document the musical properties they were discovering in their channels and fountains. They had learned through experience that certain angles of stone, certain depths of flow, certain minglings of streams produced tones that could be heard across the entire settlement, and they needed someone who could notate these discoveries in a form that future builders could understand.

My days begin in the blue-gray hour before dawn, when the mountain's voice speaks most clearly. I move through the terraces with a leather satchel containing my notation materials—specially prepared bark strips that can withstand the constant dampness, charcoal sticks wrapped in waxed cloth, a small wooden frame that helps me measure intervals between different water-sounds. I have learned to work quickly in these early hours, capturing the precise pitch relationships that emerge when night-cold water meets warming stone, when the last echoes of nocturnal bird calls blend with the first tentative songs of dawn.

The water-singers have taught me to read the mountain's hydrology as a vast, complex instrument. Each spring produces its fundamental tone based on the depth and pressure of its source. Each channel modifies that tone according to its width, its slope, its interactions with other flows. Where streams converge, harmonies emerge—sometimes consonant, sometimes creating the productive tensions that make music interesting rather than merely pleasant. The stone gutters and carved channels act as resonating chambers, amplifying certain frequencies while dampening others, creating acoustic spaces that shift with the weather and the seasons.

But my true contribution to the settlement lies in discovering how human voices interact with these water-songs. I spend hours each day working with the construction crews, teaching them work songs that harmonize with the ambient tones of their particular terraces. The stoneworkers have learned to time their hammer strikes to complement the rhythm of nearby streams, creating polyrhythmic patterns that seem to emerge from the mountain itself. The timber-framers whistle melodic lines that weave between the calls of the ravens and choughs, their human music becoming part of the settlement's greater acoustic ecology.

The children of Kythara have become my most dedicated collaborators. They possess an intuitive understanding of how sound moves through the terraced landscape, and they invent musical games that could exist nowhere else. They play echo-tag across the levels, calling to each other in patterns that use the stone surfaces to create complex rhythmic delays. They have developed a form of musical storytelling that incorporates the different acoustic properties of various elevations—beginning tales in the clear, sharp acoustics of the upper terraces and allowing the story's emotional climax to occur in the misty, reverberant spaces of the lower levels.

My workshop occupies a partially completed terrace on the settlement's eastern edge, where I can observe the daily interplay of light and sound that defines life at this altitude. The space serves as both a scriptorium and a performance venue—a place where I can notate the mountain's music and where others can come to learn the songs that are emerging from our collective life here. The walls remain unfinished, open to the weather and the acoustic environment, because I have discovered that the music of Kythara cannot be fully captured in enclosed spaces.

I am developing a new form of musical notation that accounts for the variables unique to this environment. Traditional systems assume stable conditions—that a song performed in one location will sound essentially the same when performed elsewhere. But the music of Kythara changes with elevation, humidity, season, and the presence or absence of mist. My notation includes symbols for acoustic conditions, markers indicating how melodies should adapt when cloud cover alters the acoustic environment, instructions for singers on how to adjust their technique when performing at different altitudes.

The most challenging and rewarding aspect of my work involves capturing the settlement's night music. After the construction sounds fade and the human voices quiet, Kythara reveals its deeper acoustic character. The water-sounds become more prominent, no longer competing with hammers and saws. The bird calls shift to the species active in darkness—the haunting notes of night hunters, the complex territorial songs of owls that have claimed the upper terraces as hunting grounds. Most mysteriously, the stone itself seems to sing in the deep cold of mountain nights, producing barely audible tones that might be thermal expansion, might be the settling of newly laid foundations, might be something more fundamental about how worked stone responds to its environment.

I have learned to sleep lightly during these years at Kythara, training myself to wake when unusual acoustic conditions arise. Some of my most important musical discoveries have occurred during the brief moments when fog density creates perfect acoustic chambers, when unusual weather patterns cause the settlement's various water sources to harmonize in ways they never have before, when the great migrations of birds create temporary choruses of unprecedented complexity.

My role in the settlement extends beyond music into the realm of collective memory. Like the bird-follower who maintains written records of avian behavior, I am creating an acoustic archive of Kythara's evolution. Future inhabitants will be able to consult my notations to understand how the settlement sounded in its early years, how the acoustic environment changed as construction progressed, how the marriage of human activity and mountain ecology produced sounds that existed nowhere else in the world.

But perhaps my most important contribution is helping the settlement develop its own voice—not just the individual songs that emerge from daily life, but a coherent musical culture that reflects the unique conditions of life at this altitude. The people of Kythara are learning to sing together in ways that honor both the mountain's ancient patience and their own creative urgency, developing harmonies that can bridge the acoustic gaps created by elevation and weather, creating rhythms that match the pace of stone-work and water-flow rather than the faster rhythms of valley life.

In the evenings, when the community gathers to share meals and stories, I often lead informal singing sessions that have become one of the settlement's most cherished traditions. These are not performances but collective explorations—opportunities for the entire community to participate in the ongoing composition that is Kythara itself. We sing the mountain's water-songs back to the mountain, adding our human voices to the great acoustic conversation that surrounds us, contributing our own melodies to the symphony of stone and stream and mist that plays endlessly at this altitude.

I am becoming something I never imagined in my valley life—not just a preserver of existing music, but a participant in the creation of entirely new forms of sound, someone whose work helps a community discover its own voice in the thin air between earth and sky. In this place of perpetual becoming, I have found my own transformation: from copyist to composer, from preserver to creator, from someone who documented the music of established places to someone who helps write the songs of a world still learning how to sing.
