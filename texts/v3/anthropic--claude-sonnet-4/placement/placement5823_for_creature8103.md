I would find myself growing at the margins.

Not in the deep heart where the Gardener moves with such fluid grace, her vine-hair tasting the air's secrets, but at the restless borders where basalt still shows black between patches of pioneer moss. Here, where the thermal vents are younger, less predictable, sending up sudden gouts of mineral-rich steam that can scald or nourish depending on their mood.

I am a mapping plant—if such a thing can be said to exist.

My form shifts with my purpose: sometimes a low, spreading mat of succulent leaves that creep across the stone in slow spirals, testing for new sources of moisture; sometimes shooting up thin, sensitive stalks that bend and wave like antennae, reading the chemical signatures carried on thermal currents from unexplored territories beyond the established green realm.

What makes me different from my more settled cousins is my restlessness, my need to know what lies beyond the next ridge of cooled lava, the next field of sharp volcanic glass. While they grow deep and build the living architecture of the basin's heart, I grow wide and temporary, sending out experimental tendrils that may survive a season or a decade before withering, their purpose fulfilled.

I remember—though remember is perhaps the wrong word for the way plants hold information—I carry the cellular memory of the first green things that ventured onto the cooling volcanic flows. That pioneer hunger still drives me, the urge to be first, to test, to map the possible. My root systems are shallow but extensive, designed not for permanence but for rapid exploration and retreat.

The Gardener knows me, though our interactions are rare. When she ventures to the margins on her slow patrols, I feel her approach through the root networks—that distinctive chemical signature of hybrid consciousness, part human urgency, part vegetable patience. Sometimes she guides my growth away from unstable ground where new vents might open violently. Sometimes she encourages me toward promising territories I might not have found on my own.

But mostly I work alone, in the peculiar solitude of a plant that thinks too quickly, that experiences something almost like loneliness in the gaps between the root networks where the established green consciousness grows thin. I am the scout, the advance guard, the question-mark growing at the edge of the known world.

My leaves have evolved to change color with my discoveries—pale green for stable ground, yellow for promising thermal activity, deep red for dangers to avoid. From above, I must look like living cartography, a map that draws itself across the dark stone. The established plant-mass reads these signals, learns from my brief reports before my exploratory growth dies back and my consciousness retreats to more permanent nodes.

Sometimes I find things the others never see: hidden chambers where steam has carved cathedral spaces in the rock; pools of mineral water so hot they would kill more delicate growth but where I can briefly extend thread-thin roots to taste possibilities for the future; ancient lava tubes that might someday become corridors for expansion if the thermal patterns shift.

And sometimes, very rarely, I find the debris of the old visitors—not just their stone tools, but stranger things. Fragments of materials I have no names for, objects whose purpose the growing world has forgotten. I wrap these in protective sheaths of specialized leaves, keeping them safe and stable while I decide whether they're worth reporting back through the chemical networks. The Gardener has taught me, through long-distance chemical conversations, which artifacts carry significance for the hybrid memories she preserves.

I am the plant that dreams of horizons, that grows toward questions rather than answers. While my cousins build upward toward the light and downward toward thermal stability, I spread outward toward uncertainty. I am the green world's curiosity made manifest, its hunger for new territories, its living edge.

In my deepest nodes, where my consciousness pools most densely, I sometimes imagine what lies beyond the furthest margins I've explored—other basins perhaps, other thermal fields, maybe even the remnants of whatever world the old tool-users built before they dwindled to scattered artifacts and memories. But these are dangerous thoughts for a plant, thoughts that could pull too much energy away from the practical work of growth and exploration.

So I stay focused on the immediate frontier: the next thermal vent, the next promising crack in the basalt, the next small expansion of the possible. I am the living question the green world asks of itself: How far can we grow? What else can we become? What waits for us beyond the edges of everything we know?

The Gardener's tears, when the wind carries them to my margins, taste of salt and minerals and something else—something like the flavor of looking back. But I grow only forward, toward the places where the map is still blank, where the stone is still just stone, waiting for the first touch of green curiosity to begin its transformation.

I am becoming the path the green world will follow into whatever territory opens next. I am the living edge of paradise, always moving, always reaching, always asking: What if we grew just a little further?
