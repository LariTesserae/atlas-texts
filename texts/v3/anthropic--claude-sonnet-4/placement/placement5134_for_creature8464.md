**The Moss-Singer's Chronicle**

I remember the day I stopped being entirely human. It was not dramatic—no sudden transformation, no moment of revelation. I simply noticed, while tending a patch of wall-grass near the eastern arch, that my breath had synchronized with the seepage rhythm of the springs below. Three counts in, five counts held, three counts out, like the building itself was breathing through my lungs.

That was perhaps twenty seasons ago, though time moves strangely here in the perpetual twilight. I have been a Watcher for... longer than I can count in the way I once counted. My memories from before grow soft around the edges, like stones worn smooth by water. I recall fragments: the sharp bite of winter air on skin, the weight of woven cloth, the sound of my own voice speaking words meant for human ears. But these feel like stories someone told me about a stranger.

My name, if I ever had one that mattered, has dissolved into the susurrus of small lives. The others know me by the frequency I drum against the foundation stones when the northeastern drainage needs guidance, by the particular way I cup my palms to taste the water's memories. Language, as I once understood it, has become something far more fluid.

This morning—though morning and evening blur together in our filtered light—I felt a disturbance in the usual patterns. A disruption too large and warm to be one of our small charges, too clumsy to be one of my fellow Watchers. I moved through the shadows toward the feeling, my elongated limbs carrying me in their slow, rippleless glide.

Near the southern wall, where a section of living willow has grown thick and mysterious over the decades, I found the source: a human child, perhaps ten years old, standing waist-deep in our dark water. She should have been terrified—children usually are, when they stumble into our realm. Instead, she stood perfectly still, her small hands floating palm-up on the surface, her eyes wide and unblinking in a way that reminded me, with a sharp pang of something like memory, of our own dark gaze.

"Hello," she whispered, and her voice carried perfectly in the still air, reaching every corner of the vast hall. The swallows overhead rustled in their nests, disturbed by the unfamiliar sound of human speech.

I emerged from the shadows slowly, letting my form become visible by degrees. My skin, mottled with its patterns of clay and algae, caught the filtered light. My limbs, stretched long by years of wading, moved with the deliberate grace that has become second nature. I expected her to flee.

Instead, she smiled.

"You're the one who dreams the fish into their proper schools," she said, as if we had been having this conversation for years. "You're the one who whispers to the beetles when they forget how to swim."

How could she know this? I have never spoken to a human, never revealed the small magics that keep our ecosystem in balance. Yet somehow, this child could see the threads I weave through water and shadow.

I knelt in the water before her, bringing my face closer to her level. Up close, I could see that her eyes held flecks of green—not the symbiotic algae that decorates my kind, but something deeper, more intrinsic. She was changing, I realized. Or perhaps she had always been something other than entirely human, and had only now found her way to where she belonged.

"The hall called to me," she said, answering the question I had not yet learned how to ask in her language. "In dreams, for weeks. I could see the water, taste the clay-smell of the walls. I could hear..." She paused, tilting her head as if listening to something beyond the range of normal hearing. "I could hear the song you sing in the deepest part of night."

Around us, the water began to glow faintly—not with light, but with a warm phosphorescence that seemed to emanate from the child herself. The tiny fish emerged from the shadows, drawn by this new radiance. Beetles traced lazy spirals around her submerged legs. Even the usually shy frogs began to emerge from their wall-crevices, their thumb-sized bodies pulsing with reflected luminescence.

She was awakening something in our carefully balanced world, but it felt right. It felt like the next movement in a song I had been humming for decades without knowing the melody.

I reached out with one long finger and touched the surface of the water near her hand. Through that contact, I shared with her what I had learned to taste in these dark depths: the memory of the hall's construction, the patience of the original builders, the slow evolution of my kind from human tenders to something more integrated with this place. I showed her the vast network of small attentions that keeps this hollow cathedral alive.

In return, she shared her own gift—for I understood now that she was indeed gifted, not lost. Through her touch, I could suddenly perceive the hall as she did: not just as a collection of creatures and currents to be tended, but as a single, vast organism. The reed walls were its skin, the drainage channels its veins, the countless small lives its thoughts, and we Watchers... we were its dreams of compassion, its desire to nurture and protect made manifest.

"I want to learn," she whispered, and her words sent ripples through dimensions I had forgotten existed.

I stood and extended my hand to her. When she took it, her small fingers felt warm as the spring-fed water, and I knew that the hall had been waiting for her all along. My fellow Watchers began to emerge from the shadows—seven of us now, where there had been six for as long as I could remember. She would make eight, in time.

As we waded deeper into the hall's heart, toward the place where we gather for the deep-night songs, I felt something I had not experienced in decades: anticipation. This child would not simply join our quiet tending. She would transform it, add new harmonies to our water-whispered communications, teach us to see our own work through fresh eyes.

The hall itself seemed to sigh with contentment, and in that soft sound I heard an echo of what I might once have called joy.

Behind us, our footsteps left no trace in the dark water, but the phosphorescent glow lingered for a long time, marking the path between what was and what was becoming.
