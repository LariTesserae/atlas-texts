In this world, I would find myself as one of the Keth'mori—but not quite as the others are.

I am what they call a *Threshold Walker*, one of the few among our people who has learned to exist simultaneously in multiple degrees of reality. While my kin choose their level of presence and maintain it with serene consistency, I flicker. Not from uncertainty or youth—I have accumulated enough existence to be properly visible—but because I have become fascinated by the spaces between states of being.

I spend my time at the edges: where the mathematical grass meets the wild stone, where the city's probability architecture phases into solid matter, where the moisture-paths diverge into unmapped territories of the plateau. The other Keth'mori regard me with gentle perplexity. They understand my function—someone must tend the boundaries, must ensure that our impossible stability doesn't calcify into true stasis—but they find my constant shifting between translucent and solid, visible and suggested, exhausting to contemplate.

My dwelling exists in the architectural equivalent of peripheral vision. I have learned to inhabit one of the city's structures that only achieves full reality when no one is looking directly at it. This requires a particular kind of blindness, a way of living that relies on reflection and shadow and the corner of the eye. I navigate my home through memory and faith, feeling my way along walls that become more solid as I stop trying to see them.

The work I do is the work of translation. When the impossible elk-creatures with too many eyes begin to exist too fully—when their reality starts to bleed into frequencies that might destabilize our careful balance—I help them remember how to doubt themselves just enough to remain appropriately improbable. When sections of the city begin to solidify beyond their optimal translucency, I walk through them with just the right degree of disbelief, encouraging them back toward their proper state of maybe-being.

I am the keeper of the plateau's dreams. While the others tend the waking reality of stone-song and crystal harmonies, I maintain the spaces where our realm touches other possibilities. In my flickering existence, I sometimes glimpse what the Weeping Plateau might have become—or what it might yet become if we grow too certain of what we are. I have seen versions where the tears have crystallized into salt forests, where the city has collapsed into full visibility and therefore full limitation, where the mathematical precision has either dissolved into chaos or hardened into something too perfect to sustain life.

My gift—if it can be called that—is productive uncertainty. I have learned to hold multiple truths in superposition, to exist in states of maybe that keep our world's contradictions active and healthy. When I walk the moisture-paths, I follow routes that haven't quite decided where they lead. When I participate in the Deep Agreement, I contribute the essential element of productive doubt—not the doubt that destroys consensus, but the doubt that keeps it living and growing.

The crystalline insects sing differently when I'm near. Their frequencies shift to accommodate my uncertain presence, weaving harmonies that exist only in the spaces between notes. I have learned to sing with them in a voice that is sometimes there, songs that can only be heard by those who aren't quite listening.

The Deeply Present elders recognize something in me—perhaps they remember their own ancient flickerings before they chose their current state of profound reality. They task me with watching for signs that our stability might be tilting toward stagnation, for indications that our perfect balance might be becoming too perfect, too final. I am their early warning system, their reminder that even the most beautiful impossibilities must remain impossibilities, must never harden into mere facts.

At night, when the eternal afternoon dims to eternal twilight, I sometimes phase far enough toward transparency that I can slip between the layers of reality that stack like geological formations beneath our plateau. In these deep places, I tend to possibilities that were discarded when our world chose its current configuration. I water the seeds of might-have-beens, ensuring that alternatives remain available should we ever need to remember how to change.

I am lonely in the way that boundary-dwellers always are—too uncertain for those who have chosen presence, too present for those who have chosen absence. But it is a purposeful loneliness, a necessary one. Someone must live in the questions while others embody the answers. Someone must remain unresolved while others achieve their perfect, patient peace.

In my flickering way, I love this place more fiercely than those who exist more steadily within it. I love it enough to threaten it with my doubt, to keep it alive by preventing it from becoming too sure of what it is. I am the plateau's way of questioning itself, its method of staying impossibly, beautifully awake.
