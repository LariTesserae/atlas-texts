In this place, I would be the keeper of the water songs.

I move through the settlement in the blue hours before dawn, when the mist rises from the collection basins like the breath of sleeping earth. My feet know every stone of these passages—which ones ring hollow when the underground channels run full, which ones grow slick first when the rare rains come. I carry a staff worn smooth by generations of my predecessors, its tip shaped to probe the depths of cisterns, to test the flow through clay gutters, to trace the ancient symbols carved into the corner stones that mark where water gathers.

My clothes are dyed the color of wet clay, dark enough to hide the splashes and stains that come with my work, but shot through with threads the pale blue-green of water reflecting sky. Around my waist hangs a collection of small tools: a curved blade for scraping mineral deposits from channels, a thin rod for clearing blockages, a collection of wooden plugs for redirecting flow. But my most important instrument is my voice.

I know the songs that call rain, though I use them sparingly—too often, and the sky learns not to listen. More practical are the working songs that help people remember: which channels to clear first when storm clouds gather, how to read the color of pooled water for signs of contamination, the proper mixture of clay and sand for repairing gutters. These songs change slowly, verses added when new techniques are discovered, old ones fading when they're no longer needed.

The children follow me sometimes on my morning rounds, learning to hear what I hear: the difference between water flowing clean and water carrying sediment, the sound that means a joint in the clay pipes has shifted and needs attention, the silence that indicates a blockage somewhere upstream. I teach them to cup their hands just so to taste water for minerals, to judge by its coolness how long it's been sitting in the basins, to know by the faint smell of earth and growing things that it's healthy for drinking.

But I am more than a technician. In this place where water is precious as blood, I am also memory keeper and fortune teller. I remember the great drought seven years past, when the collection basins cracked and we had to carry every drop from the distant springs. I remember the unexpected deluge that flooded the lower passages and taught us to build the overflow channels we use today. I know which walls weep moisture in certain weather, which courtyards collect dew most efficiently, where the water table runs closest to the surface.

The elders consult me when they plan new construction. Will this building block the flow of precious runoff? How will these additional gutters affect the distribution downstream? I trace paths through the settlement that exist only in potential—the routes water would take if we channeled it here, dammed it there, opened new pathways or closed old ones. My mind holds a map more complex than any drawing: every slope and gradient, every connection and confluence in our liquid lifeline.

In the evenings, when the community gathers, I sometimes sing the deep songs—the old ones that remember this place when it was different, when the rains came more often and the grass grew taller. These songs keep alive the knowledge that weather changes, that what works now might need adjustment in seasons to come. They remind us that our ancestors adapted, and so must we.

When I sleep, I listen. Even in dreams, I track the whisper of water through the channels, alert for changes that might signal trouble. A shift in rhythm might mean debris in a gutter. An unusual silence could indicate a break in the system. The settlement's circulatory system flows through my awareness like my own pulse.

I have apprentices—young people who show the gift for hearing water's voice, who can sense the subtle vibrations that travel through clay pipes, who learn quickly to read the signs written in mineral deposits and water stains. But the deep knowledge comes slowly, built season by season through patient observation. To truly know water here, you must learn its moods across years, understand how it responds to different kinds of heat, different pressures, the slow changes that come with settling foundations and shifting earth.

Sometimes visitors come from other settlements, drawn by stories of our water wisdom. I share what I can—techniques that might work in their climate, songs that could adapt to their needs. But mostly I listen, learning from their experiences with different soils, other ways of catching and keeping the sky's occasional gifts.

This is work that has no end, only the daily renewal of attention. The water flows or it doesn't, and I am here to listen, to remember, to sing the songs that help my people live lightly on this patient land. In the amber light of evening, watching children play around the collection basins while their parents prepare the simple meal that sustains us all, I know myself as part of something larger—a community that has learned to thrive by learning to listen, by making peace with what is given, by singing the old songs while staying ready to learn new ones.

The water flows, and I follow.
