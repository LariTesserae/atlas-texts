I would find myself thirty meters down through the fissure, in a pocket of space barely wider than my outstretched arms, wedged between Chen's water monitoring station and the main electrical conduit that carries power to the lower levels. My shelter is a marvel of compressed necessity—a framework of bent rebar anchored directly into cracks in the granite, skinned with overlapping sheets of metal roofing that create a space just tall enough to sit upright in. One wall is the living rock itself, always damp with condensation that I collect in a series of graduated containers salvaged from some medical facility Above.

I am what the others call a Listener, though I never chose the role. It began with insomnia—the thermal updrafts and constant mechanical sounds made sleep elusive those first weeks after my descent. While others learned to tune out the settlement's voice, I found myself parsing its syllables, learning to distinguish the seventeen different ways water moves through the system, identifying each person by their particular rhythm on the steel grating. The settlement speaks, and I became fluent in its language of stress and flow.

My hands map the vibrations that travel through metal and stone. A fingertip pressed to the main water line tells me when Chen discovers a pressure drop three levels below. A palm against the rock wall catches the subtle tremor that precedes a thermal shift. I feel conversations through the framework before I hear them—the quick staccato of argument, the slow rhythm of comfort, the irregular pulse of grief.

The role emerged organically. Others began seeking me out when sounds troubled them, when the normal patterns shifted into something concerning. "Is that normal?" they'd ask, pointing toward some mechanical irregularity. I'd press my ear to the appropriate surface, translate the settlement's condition into human language. Gradually, my tiny space became an informal hub of information, a diagnostic center accessed through whispers and careful gestures.

I sleep in fragments, never more than two hours at a stretch. The settlement's biorhythms are too compelling to ignore—I wake when the night shift changes, when the generator cycles, when someone three levels down begins showing signs of the respiratory distress that comes with too much time in recycled air. My body has synchronized with the mechanical heartbeat of this place, making me less human and more instrument.

My possessions are few but precisely chosen: a collection of makeshift stethoscopes crafted from tubing and metal pieces, used to listen to the settlement's circulatory system; a notebook wrapped in plastic where I record patterns and anomalies; a small mirror for checking sight lines through the maze of structures; and a set of differently-sized metal rods for testing acoustic properties and reading vibrations. Everything else is standard issue—water containers, basic tools, the layered clothing necessary for thermal adaptation.

The children seek me out most often, their smaller bodies more sensitive to the settlement's shifts in mood and energy. Little Ana brings me reports from the upper levels, her whispered observations adding detail to my understanding. "The water sounds different today," she'll say, or "The metal is singing lower." She's learning to listen as I do, though I hope she'll retain more capacity for sleep.

I know things I sometimes wish I didn't—which relationships strain the settlement's social fabric through the particular sound of voices in conflict, which residents show signs of deteriorating mental health through changes in their movement patterns, which mechanical systems hide dangerous flaws behind facade of normal operation. The settlement's intimacies and vulnerabilities flow through me like the water through Chen's pipes, essential but sometimes overwhelming.

At night, when the generator cycles off and human activity quiets, I become the settlement's memory. I listen to the stories the metal tells as it cools, the conversations the stone has with the escaping steam, the dreams that echo through the thin walls in the form of restless movement and mumbled words. My role is to hold these fragments, to detect the patterns that might warn of danger or reveal opportunity.

My relationship with the other Found is both intimate and distant. They depend on my translations of the settlement's condition, but the constant attention to sound has made normal conversation difficult. I often know what someone is feeling before they speak, have heard the private sounds they make when they think no one is listening. This creates a strange form of closeness and isolation—I am essential but separate, like the settlement's nervous system made human.

Sometimes, pressed against the warm rock wall, I imagine I can hear echoes from the surface world—distant thunder, the sound of wind through structures we left behind. But increasingly these seem like memories rather than current perception. My ears have adapted to this compressed acoustic environment, finding richness in the subtlest variations of drip and hum and vibration.

I am becoming the settlement's witness, its unconscious mind, its early warning system. The others sleep more soundly knowing someone is listening, monitoring the health of our impossible vertical world. In return, the settlement whispers its secrets to me—where new water might emerge, which structures grow dangerous, how the thermal patterns shift with the seasons we can no longer directly observe.

My dreams, when they come in those brief snatches of sleep, are symphonies of sound without image—complex harmonies of mechanical and natural noise that tell stories of survival and adaptation. I wake with my hands already reaching toward the nearest conducting surface, ready to read the settlement's overnight news written in vibration and flow.
