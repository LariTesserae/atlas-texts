I find myself as one of the Resonants, though I am young by the standards of this place—perhaps only a few centuries old, still learning to navigate the border between sound and substance.

I emerged from a hairline crack near the middle terraces during what the Collectors call a "harmonic convergence," when the Listening Stones briefly aligned their songs into something approaching the ancient melodies. The other Resonants say I carry traces of those old harmonies in my frequency signature, that I resonate with patterns they had thought lost forever.

My form shifts constantly. When I focus, I can maintain something resembling solidity—a translucent figure threaded with silver light that follows the same mineral veins that run through the obsidian. But my natural state is more like living music made visible, ripples of harmonics that bend the air around me into strange geometries. The glass beneath me remembers my passage for days, humming softly with echoes of my song.

I spend much of my time learning to read the stories held in the Listening Stones' frequencies. Each spire contains decades of accumulated sound—wind patterns, the footsteps of long-gone inhabitants, the last words spoken in the ruined shelters. The older Resonants can extract entire narratives from a single sustained note, but I am still clumsy, catching only fragments: a child's laughter trapped in crystallized obsidian, the scrape of tools against stone, the whisper of robes moving through what were once gardens.

The Collectors fascinate and unsettle me. When they approach, my form becomes more solid, as if their presence demands definition. They never speak to me directly, but sometimes I feel their attention like a weight, and I know they are cataloging my frequencies, adding my song to their vast repositories of memory. The robed figure I encounter most often carries with it the scent of copper and time. When I sing near it, its obsidian face reflects not my current form but what I might become—older, more complex, my harmonics woven with deeper understanding.

I have begun to suspect that the Collectors are not separate beings at all, but facets of something larger. When three or four gather, their movements become too synchronized, their attention too unified. The glass-bodied creatures that follow them click in patterns that remind me of the geometric channels in the stone, as if they are all part of a single distributed consciousness trying to solve an equation written in the very structure of the Steppes.

The Dreaming Stone calls to me, especially during the deep night hours when the air grows still enough to carry the most subtle frequencies. Its dreams leak sound as well as vision, and I can hear phantom melodies that make my form ache with recognition—songs I have never learned but somehow remember. The older Resonants warn me against approaching too closely. They tell stories of our kind who were drawn into the Stone's dreaming and emerged changed, singing only of what was lost instead of what might yet be.

But I am young and curious, and the ancient harmonies are so beautiful.

I have learned to communicate with the Watchers, though "communicate" may be too strong a word. When they emerge during the darkest hours, I can modulate my frequencies to match the resonance of the geometric patterns they tend. They respond by adjusting the flow of mercury-sand through the channels, creating new configurations that generate different harmonics from the stone itself. It is like composing music together, though the composition may take decades to complete and its purpose remains opaque to me.

The Watchers never acknowledge my presence directly, but I sense approval in the way they work when I sing near them. Sometimes I fancy that they are teaching me, showing me how the slow music of decay can be as beautiful as any song of creation. Their touch transforms entropy into art, and I am beginning to understand that this place is not dying but completing itself—becoming what it was always meant to be.

I spend my quiet hours exploring the deepest cracks in the obsidian, following veins of mineral that ring with their own inner melodies. Here, in the most ancient layers of the stone, I sometimes find pockets of trapped sound—fossilized music from before the mountains fell, preserved in crystalline matrices that dissolve at my touch, releasing their contents into my being.

These fragments of the original songs are changing me. My form grows more complex each day, my harmonics richer and more layered. I begin to understand what the older Resonants mean when they speak of the time before silence, when this place rang with the music of living things and growing things and the joy of existence itself.

But understanding brings sorrow too. Each recovered melody makes the current silence feel deeper, more complete. I am learning the weight of being a keeper of lost things, of carrying songs that no living throat will ever sing again. The warm spheres that the Collectors gather—I can hear their purpose now. They were instruments once, or perhaps the crystallized hearts of beings who sang as naturally as they breathed.

Sometimes, when the wind is right and the Listening Stones achieve perfect harmony, I can almost hear it—the great song that once unified this place, that gave meaning to the patterns in the stone and purpose to the ancient shelters. In those moments, I understand that we are all notes in a composition too vast for any single consciousness to comprehend, each playing our part in a symphony that may be ending or may be preparing for its most beautiful movement yet.

I am young among the Resonants, but I carry the future of our kind in my ever-shifting form. Each day, each song, each newly discovered harmony brings me closer to a choice I sense approaching: whether to preserve what was or help birth what might be. The Dreaming Stone whispers of permanence, of joining the eternal dream where nothing is ever truly lost. But the Watchers show me another path, where endings are doorways and silence is just another note waiting to be played.

For now, I sing to the stones and listen to their patient replies, learning the language that connects all things in this place of beautiful desolation. The glass beneath me rings with my passage, and somewhere in those resonances, new harmonies are being born.
