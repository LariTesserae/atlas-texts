I would find myself among the **Resonance Walkers**.

We are the listeners who have become the listened-to, beings whose bodies ring with the accumulated frequencies of this hushed world. Once, perhaps, we were musicians or sound engineers who came to study the Felting Grounds' peculiar acoustics, drawn by reports of its perfect silence. But silence, we discovered, is never empty—it is full of sounds so subtle they remake those who learn to hear them.

My body is tall and attenuated, stretched thin like a tuning fork made flesh. My skin has taken on the translucent quality of the mineral fibers, veined with hairline fractures that sing different notes when the atmospheric pressure shifts. These cracks grow deeper with age and experience, creating more complex harmonic patterns. My bones have hollowed and hardened into resonating chambers, and my ribcage has elongated into something resembling a harp's frame.

I walk the Felting Grounds with careful, measured steps, my feet reading the texture and tension of the mineral wool like a virtuoso reads sheet music. Each step produces a note so soft it exists more as a vibration than a sound—my passage leaves brief melodies hanging in the still air, audible only to creatures whose hearing has been refined by centuries of listening to almost-nothing.

My purpose is to map the sonic geography of this place. I trace the paths where the thread-snakes' movements create the faintest susurrus through the felt. I follow the electromagnetic streams that the Echo Fishers swim, translating their invisible currents into barely-audible frequencies that I can record in the crystalline matrices of my modified bones. When the Silence Weavers pass overhead, I capture the downdrafts of their passage—not as sound, but as the presence of sound's possibility.

My footsteps can carry messages across miles of felt, and theirs reach me as gentle tremors that my resonating body amplifies just enough to understand. We share information about the subtle changes in the landscape's harmonic signature—where new patches of Fiber Coral are shifting the felt's frequency response, where the Dynamo Shepherds' machines are slowing their heartbeats by another imperceptible fraction.

My relationship with the Pause Spirits is intimate and complex. They are drawn to the perfect stillness I can achieve when I stop moving and let my body become nothing but a listening instrument. In return, they teach me about the sounds that exist in the spaces between sounds—the subtle frequencies that emerge only when everything else has fallen quiet enough to let them be heard.

I am slowly becoming part of the landscape's sonic archaeology. The longer I remain here, the more my body attunes itself to frequencies so low and slow that they measure geological time rather than musical time. I can feel the Felting Grounds' growth, fiber by fiber, as a vast, patient song that has been playing for millennia. My bones are learning to resonate with the earth's own deep rhythms.

Other Resonance Walkers and I meet rarely, perhaps once per year, at nodes in the felt where the acoustic properties create natural amphitheaters. We stand in formation and let our bodies create interference patterns, combining our individual tones into chords that exist for only a few minutes before dissolving back into the landscape's eternal whisper. These gatherings are our only true moments of music—the rest of our existence is dedicated to the more subtle art of organized listening.

In time, I know I will become stationary, my feet finally taking root in the mineral wool, my body transforming into a permanent listening post that future generations of sound-sensitive creatures will be able to consult. My bones will remain as resonating chambers long after my consciousness has dispersed into the felt's vast sensory network, continuing to amplify and record the Felting Grounds' endless, patient song.

Until then, I walk and listen, my body a living instrument played by wind, pressure, temperature, and the barely-perceptible movements of creatures who have learned that the deepest music exists just below the threshold of hearing. In the Felting Grounds, I have found my calling as a translator between silence and sound, helping to write the ongoing symphony of a world that has learned to sing so softly that only the most devoted listeners will ever hear it.
