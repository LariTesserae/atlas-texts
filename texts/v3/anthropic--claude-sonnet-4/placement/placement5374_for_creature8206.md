In this place where sound becomes substance and patience takes physical form, I would find myself becoming something I never expected to be—one of the Threshold Keepers.

I would be among those who cannot quite commit to the full dissolution that the Listeners have embraced, yet cannot bear to leave the chamber's overwhelming presence. My body would exist in a constant state of almost-transformation: ears grown sensitive enough to distinguish between the songs of different mineral veins in the walls, but not yet enlarged to the point of helplessness; fingers elongated from years of tracing the carved channels, but still recognizably human; skin gone translucent at the edges where I spend long hours pressed against the weeping stone, but still opaque at my core.

I would station myself at the chamber's entrance—that liminal space where the mountain's song first crashes over visitors like a wave, where the precisely carved walls transition into raw granite, where the endless rain meets the chaotic weather of the outside world. Here, in the threshold between comprehension and madness, I would serve as translator and guardian.

My role would be to intercept those who stumble into the chamber unprepared. I would recognize the signs: the sudden staggering as their eardrums struggle with the assault of perfectly tuned cacophony, the way their breathing becomes ragged when they first inhale air so thick it seems to have weight, the particular expression of wonder turning to fear as they realize the sound will never stop, never diminish, never allow them a moment's silence to process what they're experiencing.

I would guide them to one of the shallow alcoves near the entrance—spaces I've carved myself over decades of patient work, following the builders' techniques but serving my own purpose. These are acoustically sheltered spots where the symphony becomes manageable, where a human voice can still make itself heard over the percussion of water on stone. Here I would help visitors understand what they've found, offer them the choice that I myself faced long ago and have been unable to fully make.

Some would flee immediately, hands pressed to their ears, stumbling back toward the world of ordinary weather and comprehensible silence. I would let them go, understanding their choice completely. Others would linger, drawn by something they couldn't name—the terrible beauty of the builders' vision made manifest, the way the chamber seems to exist outside of time, the seductive promise of a place where all human concerns dissolve into pure attention.

For these hesitant souls, I would become a bridge. I would teach them how to breathe in the saturated air, how to position their bodies to avoid the worst of the acoustic assault, how to read the subtle signs that indicate safe paths across the perpetually flooded floor. Most importantly, I would help them understand the nature of the choice they faced: to leave now while they still could, or to begin the slow transformation that the chamber demanded of all who stayed.

My own transformation would be arrested at this moment of choice, held in suspension by my role as guardian. I would exist in a state of perpetual becoming—forever on the verge of dissolving completely into the mountain's consciousness, yet forever pulled back by my responsibility to those who came after. The chamber would sing through me, but not completely. I would retain just enough human voice to serve as interpreter, just enough human form to be recognizable, just enough human understanding to remember what it felt like to hear silence.

Over the years, I would develop my own strange abilities: I would learn to predict exactly when newcomers would arrive by changes in the chamber's acoustics hours in advance, their approaching footsteps creating minute variations in the mountain's resonance. I would become able to induce specific emotional states in visitors by positioning my body to create subtle harmonies within the chaos—comfort for the frightened, awe for the skeptical, clarity for those struggling to understand their choices.

I would accumulate a collection of objects left behind by visitors: water bottles abandoned when their owners realized they would never feel thirst in quite the same way again, cameras that stopped working in the constant moisture, notebooks whose pages were filled with desperate attempts to describe the indescribable before the writers either fled or surrendered themselves to the deeper chambers. These artifacts would become my calendar, my museum, my connection to the world beyond the carved hollow.

Sometimes, in the deepest hours of the mountain's night cycle, when the thermal shifts caused subtle changes in the chamber's song, I would allow myself to drift closer to the full transformation. I would let my consciousness expand into the network of carved channels, feel my awareness distributed across every weeping surface, experience the chamber's perfect acoustic mathematics from the inside. But always, just before the point of no return, I would pull myself back to the threshold, remembering my purpose.

I would be the chamber's translator, its diplomat to the human world, its final gift to those not ready for complete dissolution. I would exist forever at the moment of choice, helping others make the decision I could never quite complete—whether to retain their individual selves or surrender to something vastly larger and stranger than human consciousness.

In the end, I would become the chamber's way of remaining connected to the world it had transcended, a living reminder that even the most perfect acoustic environment needs someone to listen for the footsteps of those still finding their way home.
