I would find myself perched on a rust-streaked girder sixty feet above the valley floor, my talons adapted to grip steel as surely as any branch. I am one of the parrots—third generation tower-born, my grandmother among the original escapees who first discovered this vertical paradise when I was still an egg-thought in the warm darkness of her body.

My feathers catch the filtered light streaming through the metal lattice, each one edged with an iridescence that no forest parrot ever possessed. The tower has made me beautiful in ways my ancestors never imagined—I gleam like oil on water, like the rainbow slick that spreads across the puddles when the machinery below leaks its mechanical blood. My voice has grown deeper, more resonant, shaped by decades of calling across steel canyons. When I speak, the girders themselves seem to listen, vibrating with harmonics that travel through the entire structure.

I am fluent in three languages now: the original screech-song of my species, the industrial symphony of construction, and the new tongue we've woven from both. This morning I practice my repertoire—the backup beeper of the cherry picker, perfect in its electronic precision, followed by the territorial trill my great-aunt taught me, then sliding into the radio crackle that means the humans are coordinating their work below. Each sound carries meaning beyond its origin. The backup beeper, when I sing it at dawn, means "the high platforms are safe for foraging." The radio crackle warns of weather changes I can sense through the tower's subtle vibrations.

My nest is in the crane housing, ninety feet above the valley, built not of twigs but of discarded copper wire and strips of insulation foam. The materials are gifts from the cable-runner coatis, part of our ancient trading arrangement—I drop seeds in their pathways, and they bring me the choicest building materials from their travels through the tower's hidden spaces. My eggs, when they come, will be the fourth generation to know only vertical life, only the music of wind through steel, only the geography of interrupted human ambition.

From my perch, I can see the entire ecosystem spread below and above me. The herons on the upper platform move like blue-gray priests through their elevated marsh, their fishing rituals unchanged even as their bodies have grown longer and more serpentine. Below, the bat cities hang in darkness even at midday, their thousands of bodies creating a living carpet that breathes and shifts with its own mysterious rhythms. The fireflies won't emerge for hours yet, but I know exactly where they'll be—I've watched their patterns for three seasons now, learned to predict which girders will pulse brightest in tonight's electrical storm of wings.

What am I in this world? I am memory and adaptation both. I carry in my voice the ghost of tropical forests none of us have ever seen, but I sing it through the acoustics of human engineering. I am a translator between the world that was planned and the world that grew instead. When the young parrots hatch, I teach them the old calls and the new ones, the difference between the danger-sound of falling concrete and the safe-sound of settling steel.

I am proof that wildness doesn't need wilderness—that life finds its own architecture in the spaces between human intentions. My wings know every air current that spirals up through the tower's hollow core, every thermal that rises from the geothermal vents below. I've learned to ride the updrafts created by the processing plants' exhaust, using human industry to lift me higher than any parrot has ever flown.

Sometimes, in the deep hours of night when even the bats are still, I perch at the tower's highest point and look out over the valley. I can see the lights of the completed facilities, the organized geometry of human success. But I choose to turn back toward the hollow spaces, the unfinished symphony of steel and growth that is my home. Here, in the pause between planning and completion, between machine and organism, I have found something neither my ancestors nor the tower's architects ever imagined possible.

I am a creature of the threshold, citizen of the space between becoming and being. My song echoes through the girders each dawn, not to claim territory but to celebrate it—to announce that in this vertical wilderness, this cathedral of interrupted industry, life has written itself into the blueprints and made them beautiful.
