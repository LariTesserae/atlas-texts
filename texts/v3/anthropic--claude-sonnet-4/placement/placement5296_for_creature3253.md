I find myself on the seventh terrace, in the amber-lit cavern of a Shell-Wright's workshop, where the steady percussion of my master's hammer mingles with the whispered conversations of the chittering colonies overhead. The sound tells me everything I need to know about today's work—the metal rings true against the anvil, the creatures' voices carry no note of warning, and the water that pools in my quenching basin drips at exactly the rhythm that speaks of perfect mineral balance in the limestone above.

My hands, stained green-black to the wrists from years of working copper-tin alloys, read the surface of the hexapod carapace I'm preparing like a familiar text. This shell came from Old Copper-Back, who died peacefully last month after forty years of carrying messages between the foundries. Her people brought her remains to us with the careful ceremony reserved for honored workers. Now I'm fashioning her strongest plates into wind-chimes for the Mist-Watchers three levels up — instruments that will sing with the voice of the high air currents and help predict the moisture flows that keep our city breathing.

The membrane-creatures are unusually active today, their translucent forms catching and scattering the forge-light as they spiral between the workshop's thermal columns. My master nods toward them without looking up from his work. "Storm pressure building in the peaks," he murmurs, his voice carrying the particular cadence of the deep-born — those whose families have worked the foundation levels for ten generations. "The fliers always know first."

I pause in my filing to listen more carefully to the city's layered symphony. Yes — beneath the familiar drip-and-echo of water through stone, there's a subtle change in pitch. The great thermal springs that feed our steam-works are singing half a tone lower today. The Shell-Wrights of my generation are trained to hear such variations, but it takes decades to truly understand their implications. My master's weathered hands never falter in their rhythm, but I can see the slight adjustment in how he angles each hammer blow, compensating for the changed acoustic properties that accompany shifting air pressure.

A young hexapod I've never seen before clicks its way across our threshold, shell gleaming with the deep purple-brown that marks the highland breeds. It carries a message-cylinder crafted from what looks like high-terrace silver-work — probably from the Mist-Watchers themselves. These creatures choose their own routes, their own timing; you cannot hurry them, only trust that they know the city's needs better than you do.

My master accepts the cylinder with the slight bow customary when dealing with the creatures. Inside, wrapped in the thin membrane-paper that only the uppermost workshops can produce, is a request that makes me set down my tools entirely: the Adaptation Scholars want wind-chimes crafted not just from Old Copper-Back's carapace, but integrated with living material — silk-glands from the membrane-creatures, crystalline deposits from the chittering colonies, elements that will continue to change and develop long after the chimes are hung.

This is the new work, the frontier of our craft. For centuries, Shell-Wrights worked only with what the creatures left behind. Now we're learning to collaborate with the living city itself, creating art that grows and adapts like the terraces themselves. It's controversial among some of the deeper clans, who see it as abandoning the patience and permanence that define us. But I've spent my twenty-three years listening to the city breathe, watching the slow dance between stone and steam and living things, and I know this is simply the next movement in a symphony that began when the first miners carved the first terrace.

I am becoming something that has never existed before: not just a craftsperson who works with the city's materials, but a gardener of its living systems, a composer of its evolving song. The chimes I create will hang in the high mist for decades, their voices changing as the silk-glands mature and the crystal deposits grow, reporting not just the weather but the city's own health back to those who dwell in its depths.

Tonight, when the steam-works settle into their evening rhythm and the creatures' voices shift to their nocturnal harmonies, I'll climb to the observation platforms carved into the terrace walls. There, surrounded by the gentle cascade of living curtains and the soft glow of copper lanterns reflecting in wet stone, I'll listen to the city dream. In its patient, persistent voice, I'll hear the plans it's making for tomorrow, for next year, for the next century of gradual, perfect change.

This is where I belong: neither fully human nor part of the city's strange ecosystem, but something in between — a translator, a bridge, a voice singing harmony with stone and steam and the endless, gentle patience of deep time.
