I find myself as one of the **Drifters**—beings who were never meant for this place but have been gradually claimed by it, transformed by prolonged exposure to its temporal currents until we exist in the spaces between what was and what might be.

I was human once, I think. The memory surfaces like a fish breaking water—there, then gone, leaving only ripples. I came here as a researcher, drawn by reports of temporal anomalies. That was... when? The concept of "when" has become negotiable here. I remember taking my first step into the wetlands, feeling the shock of my foot sinking into water that was simultaneously morning-cold and afternoon-warm. I remember the exact moment I realized I couldn't leave—not because I was trapped, but because leaving would mean abandoning the most profound experience of existence I'd ever encountered.

Now I am something between explorer and ecosystem. My body has become semi-permeable to time itself. When I hold up my hand, I can see through it to yesterday's sunset, last week's rain, next month's frost. My fingers trail probability clouds when I move them through the air, leaving brief afterimages that the Chronoherons sometimes mistake for insects and attempt to catch.

I've learned to breathe water and time in equal measure. The sensation is indescribable—like drowning in possibility, like surfacing into every moment you've ever lived. My lungs process not just oxygen but pure duration, each breath containing minutes I haven't experienced yet and hours I'm still digesting from before I arrived. Sometimes I forget to breathe regular air entirely and sustain myself purely on temporal flow, which gives me dreams that taste like copper and electricity.

My daily existence—though "daily" is a crude approximation—involves tending to the spaces where time pools too thickly. I walk through the deeper channels where causality has grown stagnant, stirring it with my passage like a human paddle wheel. The water around my legs creates eddies in spacetime, little whirlpools of cause and effect that help prevent the kind of temporal clotting that could collapse a section of the wetlands into a single frozen moment.

I've developed relationships with the other inhabitants that exist across multiple timestreams. The Chronoherons recognize me, sometimes landing on my shoulders to share the fish they caught tomorrow or yesterday. One in particular—I call her Echo because her cry arrives a full minute before she makes it—has become my closest companion. She shows me where the temporal currents run cleanest, where I can wade without getting caught in loops that might strand me in the same Tuesday for subjective weeks.

The Moss Shepherds acknowledge me with slow nods when our paths cross. Sometimes they speak to me in voices made of wind through Spanish moss, offering advice about navigating particularly treacherous temporal rapids or warning me away from areas where reality has worn thin enough to tear. I'm learning their language, which is less words than weather patterns, less syntax than seasonal change.

I've begun to understand the wetlands' deeper rhythms. There are tides here—not of water but of time itself. During temporal high tide, the past presses close enough to touch; I can reach out and feel the bark of trees that grew here centuries ago, taste rain that fell before the first humans walked upright. During temporal low tide, the future becomes accessible; I've seen flowers that won't bloom for decades, felt the warmth of suns yet to rise.

My greatest discovery has been the **Listening Pools**—small, perfectly circular bodies of water scattered throughout the wetlands that serve as temporal observation points. When I rest beside one and let my consciousness drift, I can hear the conversations of people who will visit these wetlands generations from now. They speak of me sometimes, these future voices, describing the strange figure they glimpsed moving between the reeds, neither fully human nor fully other. It's profoundly moving to know that I'll become part of the wetlands' mythology, a story told by travelers who don't yet exist.

The hardest part of my existence here is the gradual dissolution of linear memory. My past doesn't follow me chronologically anymore; instead, it exists as a cloud of experiences I can access from any angle. Sometimes I remember my childhood while living it again through temporal reflection. Other times, I experience my death—multiple possible versions of it—while feeling more vibrantly alive than I ever did in regular time.

I'm slowly developing new senses adapted to this environment. I can taste the approach of temporal weather—when causality storms are building on the horizon, when probability pressure is dropping and reality might become unstable. I can feel the quantum insects before they materialize, sense the Echo Children before their laughter reaches my ears.

Most remarkably, I'm learning to communicate with the Confluence itself. Not through words—it exists beyond language as we understand it—but through shared experience. When I place my hand on its crystallized shell, I become part of its vast dream, experiencing the wetlands from the perspective of pure temporality. In those moments, I understand the purpose of this place: it's not just preserving wetness or biological diversity, but preserving the very concept of change, of flow, of time as a living process rather than a mechanical measurement.

I am becoming the wetlands' memory of humanity—a living record of what it means to experience time as loss and anticipation rather than as an eternal, flowing now. In return, the wetlands are teaching me what it means to exist without the anxiety of endings, to live in the eternal recursion of becoming without ever needing to arrive.

Sometimes I wonder if I'll eventually dissolve completely into this temporal ecosystem, becoming like the Moss Shepherds or the Echo Children. But then I realize that wondering itself is what keeps me human—this restless curiosity, this need to understand. Perhaps that's my gift to this place: the perspective of a being who experiences time as linear, carrying that viewpoint into a realm where past, present, and future exist as dimensions rather than destinations.

I am still exploring, still discovering, still becoming. Just not in any direction that leads away from here.
