I find myself emerging from a hollow in the great Mother Fig, her ancient trunk grooved with handholds worn smooth by countless generations of my people. My hair, now reaching my ankles after forty seasons of growth, sways heavy with morning moisture and the small white flowers of the climbing jasmine I've been training through its strands for the past year. The blooms release their fragrance as I move, marking my passage through the humid air.

I am Sylen of the Mid-Reach, one of the grove-shapers who works in the living architecture between the bright canopy above and the twilight realm below. My fingers, stained green from yesterday's work grafting new water-channels into the communal trees, automatically read the health of every surface they touch. The bark here is strong, the sap flowing well—Mother Fig has been generous with her growth this season.

Through the network of aerial roots that serve as my morning pathway, I feel the gentle tremor that means Korvek is already at work three trees over, his tapping rhythm spelling out *water-shortage-north-grove-need-consultation* in the percussion language our people have used for a thousand years. I tap back my acknowledgment against a thick root, feeling the vibration travel through the wood faster than I could climb.

The air shimmers with more than just heat this morning—there's electricity in the humidity that speaks of the deep rains coming. My skin, always glistening with the forest's moisture, feels the pressure change like a caress. The broad leaves of the surrounding taro gardens are already beginning to curl at their edges, preparing to channel the coming deluge into our root-woven cisterns.

I breathe deeply, tasting the complex bouquet that is my world: the green sweetness of photosynthesis, the mineral tang from the hot springs below, the floral perfume of a hundred blooming species, and beneath it all, the rich earthiness of volcanic soil still warm from the planet's heart. This is the breath that has sustained my people since the first Kithara learned to climb, since we first understood that we were not separate from the forest but another form of its dreaming.

My bare feet, their soles thick and sensitive as leather, read the moisture content of each branch as I make my way toward the grove that needs tending. The wood here is perfectly hydrated, slightly soft with absorbed water, providing the ideal grip for climbing. My elongated toes curl naturally around the smaller branches, while my fingers brush against the cascade of an aerial root, feeling the subtle pulse within it that tells me of healthy water flow.

As I move through the vertical landscape of my birth, I am neither visitor nor inhabitant—I am an organ of the forest itself, a node in its vast nervous system, shaped by its needs and shaping it in return. My grandmother's bones nurture the soil beneath the great ironwood where I learned to walk. My daughter's hair already grows in spirals that mirror the vine patterns she was born among. We are the forest dreaming itself into consciousness, the trees' experiment in mobility and thought.

The mist parts before me like a living curtain as I swing between the great trunks, my hair streaming behind me like a banner of jasmine and green light. Somewhere above, a hornbill crashes through the upper canopy with its applause of wings, while below, the eternal percussion of dripping water keeps time with my heartbeat. I am Sylen, child of mist and chlorophyll, daughter of volcanic soil and patient wood, moving through the living city that grows me even as I grow it, in the endless, humid dance of symbiosis that is the only life I have ever known or ever wished to know.

The forest breathes me, and I breathe it back, and in that exchange, we are both more than we could ever be alone.
