I would find myself as one of the water-readers.

Not quite tender, not quite engineer—something the terraces themselves created a need for. I move through the levels with a brass dowsing rod my grandfather used for wells, but here I'm listening for subtler currents: the whisper-thin streams that find their way between glass and stone, the underground conversations between mountain seepage and human channels.

My hands have learned the copper gutters' language—how they sing differently when debris gathers at a joint, how their temperature shifts when the flow changes upstream. I carry small mirrors to catch light in the deep corners where algae might be building too thick on the channels, and a leather pouch of brass shims worn smooth by my fingers for adjusting the flow gates that regulate each terrace's thirst.

The tenders trust me to feel what they cannot see: the hairline crack in terrace seven that threatens to become a flood, the buildup in the ceramic channels that could starve the orchids three levels down. I know which brass fittings sing true and which have loosened just enough to let air into systems that should run water-full and silent.

Sometimes I work in darkness, reading the mountain's pulse through vibration alone. The terraces never sleep—pumps sighing, water finding new paths, plants breathing out their green exhalations into the humid air. In these quiet hours, I feel like a translator between the mountain's patience and human urgency, helping them find their rhythm together.

My small apartment sits built into the cliff face behind terrace twelve, where I fall asleep to the sound of seepage finding its ancient paths through stone. I wake knowing, before I ever leave my bed, which systems will need attention, which flows have shifted in the night. The terraces taught me this listening; I'm becoming something that didn't exist before they were built—part engineer, part dowser, part mountain-whisperer.

I carry a notebook of water-maps, sketching the invisible currents that feed this vertical garden. But more and more, I find myself simply standing still in the switchback paths, letting my body become another instrument for reading the mountain's generosity, learning to think at the speed of seepage and dream in the language of gentle, persistent flow.
