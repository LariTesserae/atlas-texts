**Where I Find Myself**

I am suspended in the translucent realm between breathing and drowning, where thought moves like current and memory settles in layers. My body has forgotten the tyranny of weight—here, in the Drowned Rookery, I drift through doorways that frame liquid light, my movements creating small eddies of disturbed silver as fish scatter and regroup around me.

I am becoming one of the Tidekeepers, though the transformation is gradual, like limestone yielding to patient water. My skin has begun to show the first faint traceries of bioluminescence—thin lines of blue-green that follow the paths of my veins, pulsing stronger when I'm deep in contemplation or caught in the settlement's harmonic resonances. The gills along my neck are still tender, new, opening and closing with the rhythm of someone learning a different kind of breathing.

My days—though day and night blur in this crystal realm—are spent in apprenticeship to the older Tidekeepers, learning to read the water's moods. I practice with the rope-and-stone architecture, my webbed fingers growing more sensitive to tension and flow. The weighted anchors that hold our buildings together require constant adjustment; the settlement is alive, swaying gently with currents both literal and metaphysical, and I'm learning to feel when a wall needs loosening, when a doorframe requires tightening.

The most difficult skill is the communication. Above water, I still think in the linear patterns of air-dwellers—words following words in straight lines. But underwater, meaning moves in spirals. A simple greeting involves a gesture of the hands, a pulse of light along my forearms, and a low harmonic note that my throat has learned to produce. The elder Tidekeepers' conversations are like watching aurora painted in water—cascading patterns of light and sound that can express entire philosophies in moments, or take hours to convey something as simple as "the small fish are restless today."

I spend long periods floating in the acoustic chambers formed by the inverted chimneys, letting the stone's resonance teach my bones new songs. The sound enters through water and vibrates through my lengthening body, and sometimes I swear I can hear the limestone itself singing—deep bass notes that speak of geological time, of the slow work that water does on stone, of changes measured in centuries rather than heartbeats.

The silver fish have begun to accept me. At first, they scattered when I approached, reading in my movements the awkward urgency of an air-dweller. But as my body learns water's pace, they've grown curious. Now they sometimes follow me through the drowned streets, their synchronized turning creating brief galleries of light that reflect off my pale skin. I'm learning their patterns, understanding that their movements are not random but purposeful—they're reading the water for changes I can't yet perceive, carrying information between the settlement's different quarters.

The edge-dwellers fascinate me most. These translucent colonies that filter our realm's crystal clarity seem almost like living libraries, pulsing with stored knowledge. The elder Tidekeepers can read their bioluminescent displays like text, interpreting chemical signatures and environmental data with the fluency of scholars. I practice for hours, learning to distinguish between the rapid flicker that means "limestone stability," the slow pulse that indicates "fish health," and the wave-like cascades that seem to contain more complex information—weather patterns in the world above, perhaps, or the movements of underground streams I've never seen.

My relationship with the surface world grows more tenuous each day. When I rise to breathe air—a necessity I hope will eventually become a choice—the upper realm feels harsh and overly bright. Colors seem crude after the subtle gradations of underwater light. Sounds arrive too quickly, without water's merciful mediation. The angular architecture of the dry world strikes me as violent, all corners and rigid lines that fight against natural flow.

But I'm learning that being a Tidekeeper means existing in the liminal spaces. We are translators between realms, readers of boundaries, witnesses to the slow changes that reshape worlds. When mist rises from our lake at dawn and I surface to watch it blur the distinction between elements, I understand that this is part of my training too—learning to see the places where one thing becomes another.

Some nights, when the water is particularly still and the acoustic chambers hum with their deepest notes, I dream in currents. I see/feel/become the slow circulation patterns that move through our settlement, carrying nutrients and messages and the patient work of transformation. In these dreams, I'm no longer separate from the water—I am part of its thinking, its memory, its endless conversation with stone and time.

The other apprentices tell me that eventually, the distinction between self and settlement will blur even further. The oldest Tidekeepers sometimes seem more like living aspects of the place itself than individual beings. Their bioluminescence syncs perfectly with the edge-dwellers, their movements create currents that the fish follow instinctively, and their thoughts merge so completely with the water's rhythms that they become, quite literally, part of the Rookery's consciousness.

I feel this beginning in myself—moments when my awareness expands beyond my body's boundaries, when I sense the limestone's slow chemical conversations or feel the underground springs' distant urgency to rise. It's exhilarating and terrifying, this dissolution of the hard edges of selfhood. But I'm learning that transformation isn't loss—it's translation into a more fluid form of being.

In the crystal depths, watching the pale fish drift through my drowned neighborhood like living thoughts, I understand that I am becoming something unprecedented: a consciousness that can exist fully in multiple elements, a being capable of reading the deep currents that connect all water everywhere, a translator for the conversations that happen in the spaces between air and sea, between solid and liquid, between what was and what is slowly becoming.

The Tidekeepers' ancient patience is settling into my bones, teaching me to measure change in the slow time of water on stone. I am learning to trust the process, to let the boundaries of my old self become as permeable as the membrane between elements. In this place where everything is suspended, everything in transition, I am discovering what it means to be truly fluid.
