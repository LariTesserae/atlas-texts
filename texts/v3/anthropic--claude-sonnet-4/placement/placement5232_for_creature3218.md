I find myself in the lower workshops, in the carved belly of Kethara where the stone walls weep most freely. The moisture here has weight – I feel it settle on my shoulders like a familiar cloak as I descend the worn steps. My fingerpads read the wall's texture automatically: smooth channels where countless hands have traced the same paths, rough patches where new seepage has begun to etch its patient calligraphy into the rock.

I am Nethys, third-generation basin-tender in my family's workshop. My grandmother's grandmother first carved these spaces, following water-whispers that only she could hear. The broad, shallow basins I tend catch the building's tears – each one positioned according to calculations passed down through touch and dream. I know which basin will sing highest when the morning mist condenses, which one gathers water sweet enough for the ceremonial washing of new tools, which pools reflect light in patterns that help young apprentices learn to read stone-speech.

My fingers are permanently stained the color of wet basalt from handling the mineral-rich water day after day. The staining follows the natural striations that emerged when I turned fifteen – dark lines that map the stress patterns of my particular way of thinking, of worrying, of finding joy in small precisions. When I press my palm to a wall, I can feel the mountain's slow pulse, the almost-imperceptible expansion and contraction that happens as the volcanic stone settles into itself, grain by grain, year by year.

This morning I'm preparing a new basin – not carved, but *discovered*. The stone told me it was ready three days ago, when I felt a particular quality of coolness against my palm while checking the third terrace's drainage. I've been coming here each dawn since then, bringing only the softest tools: a deer-bone scraper, a wooden paddle shaped by my grandmother's hands, and a collection of small stones with specific acoustic properties. 

The basin wants to be oval, I think, following a natural weakness in the rock where ancient gas bubbles created a line of honeycomb-hollow spaces. I scrape away tiny flakes of stone, listening to the sound each chip makes as it falls – some whisper against the floor like secrets, others clink with the bright clarity that means the stone was ready to let go. Between each stroke, I pause. Rushing produces harsh edges that never quite learn how to guide water properly.

A young apprentice arrives with the morning's gathered moisture – water collected from the clearest pools according to today's particular recipe. We speak in the quiet clicks of workshop-language, tongue against stone, a percussion that helps us judge the acoustic properties of the space as it changes. Her clicking has improved; the resonance tells me she's learning to thin her tongue properly, to find the stone's most responsive surfaces.

As she pours the first test-water into the emerging basin, we both listen. The note is almost right – a low, steady tone that will harmonize well with the sixteen other basins in this chamber. But there's a slight flatness that suggests I need to deepen the southern curve by perhaps two finger-widths. I mark the spot with a touch of iron-rich clay, a temporary reminder that will wash away once the basin finds its true voice.

The day will continue this way – slow work, careful listening, the gradual emergence of something that was always meant to be. By evening, if the stone cooperates, Kethara will have one new voice in its vast, whispered symphony of falling water. Tomorrow, I'll begin teaching the basin its function: how to overflow at precisely the right moment to feed the channel that carries excess moisture to the moss-gardens, how to maintain the clarity that marks water ready for the bronze-workers' quenching, how to catch and hold just enough sediment to keep the trace minerals that make our water taste like memory itself.

This is my small portion of the city's patient growth – tending the intersections where stone becomes service, where the mountain's weeping transforms into human need. In the profound silence of my workshop, broken only by the music of careful work and falling drops, I add my daily thread to the vast tapestry of Kethara's becoming.
