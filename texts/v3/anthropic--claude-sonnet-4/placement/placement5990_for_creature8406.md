In this world, I would find myself as one of the **Channel Readers**—a peculiar profession that emerged as Millhaven adapted to its waterlogged existence.

I spend my mornings tracing the granite runnels that thread between buildings, a worn leather satchel at my hip containing brass instruments of my own design: delicate gauges that measure not just flow and pressure, but the subtle vibrations that travel through water when the mist carries news from the upper valleys. My fingers, permanently wrinkled from constant moisture, can read the temperature of water to within degrees, can feel the difference between rainwater, groundwater, and the precious condensation that weeps from the ancient stones.

The townspeople rely on me to predict which terraces might flood during heavy mist, which channels need clearing, and—most crucially—when the fish are running messages. For the channel fish aren't merely decorative; they're part of an communication network that evolved naturally in Millhaven's watery embrace. Certain species swim upstream only when atmospheric pressure drops significantly. Others school in specific patterns when distant weather approaches. The smallest, silver as mercury, cluster near channel junctions when mechanical problems upstream send vibrations through the water network.

I've learned to read their liquid script: three dart-shaped fish circling counterclockwise near the market bridge means the steam pressure in the hillside workshops is running too high. A solitary copper-colored fish swimming against the current indicates a blockage in the upper channels that will cause overflow by evening. When the translucent ones—nearly invisible except for the light they bend—gather in unusual numbers, it means the mist will lift briefly tomorrow, providing a rare opportunity for the solar collectors hidden among the ferns to capture enough energy for the emergency systems.

My workshop occupies a narrow building wedged between two water channels, its lower level partially flooded by design. Here, I maintain detailed charts tracking the correlation between fish behavior, mist density, industrial rhythms, and seasonal changes. The walls are covered with intricate maps showing not just Millhaven's physical layout, but the invisible currents of pressure, temperature, and humidity that flow through the settlement like arteries.

In the evenings, I join the other technical specialists in the old mill's upper chambers, where we coordinate the town's complex systems. The steam engineers consult my readings to adjust pressure levels. The gardeners plan their plantings based on my moisture predictions. The bridge keepers use my reports to know when to expect higher water levels that might require temporary channel diversions.

But my work isn't merely practical. In this place where water connects everything, I've become something of an oracle, reading the settlement's moods in the behavior of its liquid networks. When the children's paper boats consistently veer toward the eastern channels, I know the community's attention is turning that direction—perhaps toward news from the outside world that arrives with the morning mist. When the fish gather in the deepest pools, it signals a period of introspection and maintenance ahead.

I wear clothes dyed the deep blue-green of deep channel water, with brass buttons that have developed their own patina patterns—a personal weather record written in verdigris. My boots are specially crafted with soles that grip wet stone but can also sense vibrations through the granite, letting me feel the pulse of the settlement's hydraulic heartbeat as I walk.

On foggy mornings when the mist is so thick that the terrace levels above disappear entirely, I move through Millhaven's channels like a navigator in an intimate sea. The water speaks differently in this weather—more urgent, carrying sounds and scents from farther away. These are my favorite working days, when the boundary between air and water becomes meaningless, and I can feel most clearly how the settlement breathes as a single living system.

The townspeople have learned to read me as I read the channels. They know that when I move quickly between stations, checking my instruments with sharp, efficient motions, some change is coming that will require community preparation. When I spend long minutes crouched beside a particular channel, my hand trailing in the water while I listen to something only I can hear, they understand that Millhaven is telling me one of its deeper secrets—information that will shape decisions in the days ahead.

In this world where rust and roses collaborate, where industrial rhythm harmonizes with water song, I am the translator between human intention and elemental wisdom, ensuring that our careful civilization continues to dance gracefully with the forces that shaped this misty valley home.
