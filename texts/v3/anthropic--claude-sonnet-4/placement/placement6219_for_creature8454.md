I find myself among the **Resonance Tenders**, dwelling in the hollow spaces between the great trunk chambers where sound itself becomes a living thing.

My home is a spherical pod grown into the intersection of three massive branches, its walls lined with a delicate membrane of bark that has been coaxed into impossible thinness—translucent enough that the bioluminescent pods outside cast shifting patterns across my living space, yet strong enough to contain the carefully cultivated acoustic environment within. The membrane responds to the slightest vibration, and over the years of my residence, it has learned to amplify the frequencies I most need to hear while dampening those that would interfere with my work.

I am both human and something more—or perhaps something other. The constant exposure to the deep harmonic resonances of the trunk-cities has changed me in ways that are difficult to describe to those who live in the visual world of the outer platforms. My hearing has developed beyond the normal human range; I can distinguish the specific signature of each steam engine by its harmonic overtones, feel the stress patterns in bridge cables through the way they sing in the wind, and detect the early stages of root rot by the slight shift in the subsonic vibrations that travel through the living wood.

My eyes have adapted too, becoming oversensitive to the subtle light variations that filter down through the canopy layers, but more importantly, I have learned to *see* sound—the way it pools in the trunk chambers, flows through the ventilation systems, and creates standing wave patterns that reveal the hidden architecture of the tree-cities. When I work, I often keep my eyes closed anyway, navigating by the acoustic landscape that most inhabitants never consciously notice.

My tools are a mixture of the grown and the crafted: tuning forks cultivated from resonant wood that continue to live and adjust their frequencies as the humidity changes; contact microphones grafted directly into my fingertips that allow me to hear the secret conversations of metal and wood; and a collection of sympathetic strings grown from modified root fibers, each one attuned to a different harmonic of the city's vast acoustic signature.

What I do is hard to explain to outsiders. I am part diagnostician, part musician, part healer. The trunk-cities generate incredibly complex acoustic environments—the steam engines create rhythmic pulses that can either synchronize with the natural resonant frequencies of the living wood or create destructive interference patterns. Left unattended, these conflicting vibrations can literally shake a tree-city apart, causing the careful integration of biological and mechanical systems to fail.

But when properly tuned, the acoustic environment becomes something extraordinary: the mechanical rhythms can actually encourage growth in specific directions, guide the development of new chambers, and create resonant networks that allow the trees themselves to communicate across vast distances. The most experienced Pneumatic Guild members claim they can feel this happening, but I can *hear* it—the slow, deep conversations between distant trunk-cities, carried on frequencies so low they travel through the earth itself.

My typical day begins in the pre-dawn hours, when the thermal activity is lowest and the acoustic environment most stable. I descend through maintenance shafts that most inhabitants don't even know exist, following the sound-paths through the heart of the tree. My fingertips press against junction points where mechanical and biological systems meet, listening for the subtle discord that indicates stress, disease, or simple misalignment.

Sometimes the solution is mechanical—adjusting valve timings, tightening or loosening coupling bolts, recalibrating pressure relief systems. But more often, it requires the delicate work of acoustic gardening: planting specific fungal cultures that will dampen unwanted resonances, coaxing the growth of wood structures that will redirect sound waves, or working with the Graft-Wrights to modify the living portions of the system to better harmonize with the mechanical components.

The most challenging and rewarding part of my work involves the **Deep Listening sessions**. Once each season, I spend several days in the very heart of the oldest trunk-cities, in chambers so central and so acoustically isolated that the outside world disappears entirely. Here, I listen to what the trees are trying to tell us—their slow thoughts transmitted through creaking wood and shifting sap, their dreams of growth patterns that won't be realized for decades, their memories of the valley floor that lies far below.

During these sessions, I sometimes encounter others like myself: **Root-Whisperers** who have learned to hear the chemical communications of the underground networks, **Steam-Singers** who can predict mechanical failures by the changing pitch of engine noise, and occasionally, one of the mysterious **Echo-Walkers**—individuals who claim to have learned to step outside of linear time by following sound waves as they bounce through the infinite complexity of the canopy structure.

We don't speak much when we meet—speech seems crude and limited compared to the rich language of harmonics and resonance that fills our shared environment. Instead, we share information through carefully modulated tones, comparing notes about the city's health through musical phrases that carry more information than words ever could.

My body has adapted to this life in ways both subtle and profound. My bones have become denser and more resonant—I can feel sound through my skeleton as much as through my ears. My breathing has synchronized with the rhythm of the steam engines, and my heartbeat naturally aligns with the deep pulse of sap through the great trunks. Sometimes, when I'm working in the deepest chambers, I feel as though I'm not just listening to the city but becoming part of its acoustic signature, adding my own small voice to the vast harmonic structure that keeps everything in balance.

The loneliness is real—most inhabitants of the trunk-cities experience their environment primarily through sight and touch, and explaining the acoustic dimension of this place is like describing color to someone who has never seen. But there's also a profound connection, a sense of being woven into the very fabric of this impossible vertical world. When I place my hands against the warm bark and feel the city's pulse transmitted directly through my bones, I know I am exactly where I belong—not just living in the Hanging Gardens of Ashkenar, but helping to tune the grand symphony that keeps them growing toward the sky.

In quiet moments, usually in the deep of night when even the steam engines run at their gentlest rhythm, I sometimes hear something that might be the trees' dreams—vast, slow melodies that speak of canopies that will eventually touch the clouds, of a time when the distinction between city and forest will have disappeared entirely, when the children of current inhabitants will live in a world where the mechanical and biological have become so perfectly integrated that the very concepts will seem quaint. Until then, I listen, I tune, I tend—a small but essential part of the great work of growth.
