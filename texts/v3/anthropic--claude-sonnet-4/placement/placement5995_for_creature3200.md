I would find myself on the middle terraces of Kethara, in that liminal space between the Stone Listeners above and the mist-pools below. Here, where the carved channels widen into shallow basins before continuing their descent, I would be one of the Threshold Keepers—those who tend the delicate transition zones where water changes its mind about which direction to flow.

My dwelling would be built into a natural alcove in the granite face, its entrance marked by a curtain of the broad-leafed vines that spiral around everything in this city. Inside, the walls would curve with the mountain's contours, fitted with shelves carved directly from the living rock. These shelves would hold my collection of small stones—not ordinary ones, but those that sing when the mist passes over them at particular angles, each one positioned to catch a different note in the mountain's vast, slow symphony.

As a Threshold Keeper, my days would follow the rhythm of the water's descent. In the pre-dawn darkness, I would move through my assigned channels with bare feet, feeling for irregularities in the flow. My fingers—like those of all my people, sensitive beyond ordinary measure—would trace the carved gutters, detecting the microscopic variations that could, over decades, alter the perfect distribution of water through the city's levels.

But I would be different from the pure Stone Listeners above. Where they commune directly with the mountain's deepest thoughts, I would exist in conversation with transition itself—the moment when mist becomes droplet, when seepage becomes flow, when the vertical becomes horizontal in the brief pools where water rests before continuing its journey downward.

My skin would carry the particular pallor of those who work in the threshold zones, pale as the Keth'moren but with a bluish undertone from constant proximity to moving water. My hair would be long and fine, braided with small metal chimes that sound different notes depending on the moisture in the air—an early warning system for changes in the weather patterns that could disturb the city's equilibrium.

I would wear robes of woven mist-vine fiber, dyed the color of wet stone, with deep pockets for the specialized tools of my trade. Not the chisels of the builders above, but calibrated measures for water depth, small scoops for clearing debris, and most importantly, the listening stones—flat, palm-sized pieces of granite ground so thin they become translucent, which when held to the ear can reveal the subtlest changes in water pressure throughout the channel system.

My garden would be vertical, trained along the support pillars of a bridge that spans one of the wider terraces. Here I would grow the water-speaking plants—varieties of moss that change color with the mineral content of the flow, ferns whose fronds curl differently depending on the underground pressure, and a climbing vine whose flowers bloom in sequence to map the exact timing of the mist's daily cycle.

In the evenings, I would join the other Threshold Keepers in our particular form of communion. We would gather at the edge of our terrace where the water makes its leap to the level below, listening to the sound of each drop's impact on stone. In these subtle variations—the difference between a drop that falls from the eastern edge versus the western, the way the sound changes when the air pressure shifts—we would read the mountain's immediate intentions, its responses to the weight of clouds gathering in the valley, its adjustments to the imperceptible settling of stone.

My nights would be spent in the workshop behind my dwelling, crafting replacement chimes for the channel systems throughout the middle terraces. Each chime must be tuned not just to a musical note, but to the specific frequency that resonates with flowing water at that elevation, at that angle of slope, in that particular season. The work requires absolute silence and concentration, fingers reading the metal's vibrations the way others might read text.

I would know every Threshold Keeper in the city by the sound of their footsteps on wet stone, by the particular way they breathe in the heavy air of the mist pools. We would share a vocabulary of subtle gestures—ways of indicating water pressure, flow rate, mineral content—that would be invisible to outsiders but clear as spoken words to those who live in the margins between the mountain's moods.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night when the mist settles most thickly in the courtyards, I would feel the mountain's dreams differently than the Stone Listeners do. Where they receive visions of geological time and tectonic patience, I would experience the dreams of water itself—memories of rain on distant peaks, the slow journey through underground streams, the anticipation of eventual reunion with the valley's hidden rivers.

In this role, I would be essential but nearly invisible, tending to the transitions that make the Stone Listeners' perfect communion possible, ensuring that the city's vertical harmony continues unbroken, one precisely calibrated droplet at a time.
