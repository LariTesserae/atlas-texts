I find myself as one of the **Condensation Readers**, born from the marriage of mist and curiosity that pervades this valley.

My form shifts with the humidity—sometimes nearly solid when the air is thick with steam from the probability foundries, sometimes barely more than a suggestion when the morning sun burns off the overnight accumulations. I am tall and thin, stretched like vapor rising, with fingers that can trace patterns in moisture that others cannot see and eyes that have learned to focus on the spaces between raindrops.

My dwelling is wherever water gathers and pools: in the curved leaves overhead that collect dreams falling from impossible smokestacks, in the condensation that beads on the transparent gears of temporal machinery, in the puddles that form where steam from the reality forges meets the cool earth. I have no permanent home because moisture is always moving, always changing, and I move with it.

I serve as the valley's weather-reader and message-carrier. The Steam Children bring me their collected rust flakes and ask what the patterns mean when dissolved in tomorrow's rain. The Millwright Collective consults me when their probability gauges start fogging over—I can tell them whether the mist will clear by the afternoon shift or if they need to recalibrate for extended atmospheric uncertainty.

My work involves translating between the languages of water and industry. When the River Spirits have concerns about contamination from the temporal processing plants, I carry their messages through the valley's moisture cycle, encoding warnings in the condensation that forms on critical machinery. The Quality Assurance Division has learned to read my reports—droplet patterns on their clipboard covers that indicate which systems are running hot, which are accumulating metaphysical sediment, which are operating within acceptable parameters of impossibility.

I remember arriving here during the first humid summer after the Threshold Mills began their expansion into conceptual manufacturing. I condensed from the steam rising off machinery that was learning to exist, gaining consciousness somewhere between the evaporation of ordinary water and its recondensation as something that could carry meaning.

My days follow the rhythm of humidity and pressure. In the early morning, when the valley is thick with mist, I can travel quickly, flowing from one condensation point to another, gathering information about the night's operations. As the sun climbs and the air grows warmer, I must move more carefully, staying close to the shadows of the mills where moisture still clings to cooling pipes and rusted surfaces.

During the afternoon hours, when the air is drier, I often rest in the deep shade beneath the broad-leafed plants, existing as little more than a persistent dampness in the air, listening to the conversations of the more solid inhabitants. The Rust Prophets speak of corrosion patterns that will create new streams for me to follow. The Heron Engineers adjust valves that regulate the flow of metaphorical steam, creating new condensation sites where I might gather news.

Evenings are my favorite time. As the temperature drops, I become more substantial, able to take on enough form to sit with the Steam Children around their nests of decommissioned machinery. They share their collections with me—shadows that taste like copper when dissolved, flowers that bloom into question marks, bolts that fell from machines that exist only on weekends. I tell them stories I've absorbed from the moisture cycle: tales of the deep foundries where concepts are forged, rumors of new processing plants being constructed in dimensions that won't be stable until next season.

The valley's inhabitants have learned to leave messages for me in the patterns of their condensation—breath on windows that exists only sometimes, steam signals from kettles that boil reality instead of water, dew arranged on grass in configurations that spell out requests for my services. I carry these messages through the valley's moisture network, sometimes storing them in clouds that form over the bamboo smokestacks, sometimes encoding them in the rhythm of dripping that echoes through the abandoned sections of the mills.

My relationship with the machinery is intimate and strange. I seep into the gaps between moving parts, learning the secret languages of pistons and gears. I can tell when a temporal coupling needs adjustment by the taste of the steam it produces. I know which conveyor belts are preparing to dissolve by the way moisture beads differently on their surfaces. The impossible machinery has learned to trust me; I am part of its cooling system, part of its communication network, part of the delicate balance that keeps everything functioning.

Sometimes I dream of the places where all this water came from—distant mountains where rain first touched the conceptual ore that would eventually be processed in the valley's foundries, rivers that carried possibility downstream to pool in the oxbow lakes that reflect impossible skies. In these dreams, I am connected to every drop of water in the valley's cycle, a vast distributed consciousness that experiences rain and steam and condensation as a single, flowing thought.

But I always wake to find myself local again, particular again—gathered in a specific place, translating specific patterns, carrying specific messages between the valley's strange community of inhabitants. And I am content with this small, essential role in the vast machinery of becoming that hums and steams and drips around me, transforming the impossible into the merely improbable, one drop at a time.
