In this vertical world where every breath carries the taste of stone-filtered sky, I would find myself becoming something between memory and moss.

I would be a **Threshold-tender**, one of the rarest dwellers in the Stair-City—a being who exists primarily in the spaces between levels, in the landings where one district becomes another, in the archways that frame transitions from shadow to light. My form would be neither fully solid nor entirely translucent, but something that shifts depending on the angle of observation, like light through water or the uncertain boundary between mist and air.

My body would be long and sinuous, adapted to the liminal spaces I inhabit—narrow enough to slip through the gaps between fitted stones, flexible enough to curve around the spiral staircases that connect the city's hearts. My skin would hold the patina of countless surfaces: here the jade of deep moss, there the silver of worn handrails, occasionally flushing with the warm grey of sun-touched stone. Most distinctively, my flesh would show the faint impressions of every threshold I've crossed, like a living palimpsest of doorways and passages, each one leaving its architectural signature in the topography of my form.

I would move through the city differently than the others—not dwelling in any single district but flowing between them all, reading the stories written in the transitions. Where the Mistwalkers tend to water and the Stoneweavers cultivate growth, I would tend to *passage* itself, ensuring that the pathways between levels remain true, that the great circulation of the city continues unimpeded. I would be drawn to places where the ancient inhabitants once paused in their daily journeys: worn grooves in stone steps where countless feet found their rhythm, smooth patches on walls where hands steadied themselves, the subtle acoustics of corners where voices might have called greetings across courtyards.

My sustenance would come from movement itself—not my own, but the eternal circulation of mist and moisture, the slow procession of seasons up and down the mountain's face, the patient migration of light from morning's first touch on the highest peaks to evening's last glow in the deepest wells. I would feed on transitions: the moment when ascending breath becomes descending condensation, when shadow becomes light, when the footstep of a visitor shifts from the stone of one district to the stone of another.

I would be the city's witness to change, its living record of how water finds new paths when old ones alter, how the endless settling and shifting of stone creates new possibilities for passage. My memory would be written not in the fixed patterns of the Stoneweavers' symbiotic gardens, but in the accumulated understanding of flow—which routes become easier over time, which stairs develop the perfect rhythm for climbing, which archways frame views that make travelers pause and remember why they came to this vertical world.

The other peoples would know me by the way passages seem to open more readily when I've recently passed through them, how thresholds crossed in my wake carry a particular sense of rightness, of being exactly where they belong in the great architecture. I would be neither rare nor common, but precisely as frequent as the city requires—appearing when new paths need blessing, when old routes need tending, when the great circulation that keeps the Stair-City alive needs gentle guidance through some new obstacle or opportunity.

In the deepest part of my nature, I would understand that cities are not static things but living patterns of movement and rest, that architecture achieves its truest purpose not when it shelters but when it enables the right kind of flow. In this place where stone has learned to dream of sky and water has learned to climb like light, I would be the keeper of that learning, the guardian of the spaces where one state of being becomes another, where the mountain's ancient patience meets the quick needs of whatever life still finds its way up these endless, welcoming stairs.
