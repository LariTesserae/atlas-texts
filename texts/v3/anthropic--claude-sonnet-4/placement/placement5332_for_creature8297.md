I would find myself as one newly arrived, still heavy with the weight of linear time.

At first, I am just a visitor—boots squelching in the impossible moss, chest vibrating with each stone drum's pulse, trying to map this place with the crude instruments of ordinary perception. I count the beats (one-two-three-four, but then the rhythm shifts, becomes five, becomes seven, becomes something that exists between numbers). I catalogue the creatures (red deer, mountain hares, ravens—but they flicker in and out of existence like thoughts half-remembered). I search for patterns in the placement of those ancient carved stones, trying to solve them like a puzzle, not yet understanding that they are the answer to questions I haven't learned to ask.

The brightness confuses my eyes trained for proper shadows. The perpetual mist makes my skin feel strange—too alive, too aware. I keep expecting the drumming to stop, for there to be a moment of true silence where I might think clearly, but it never comes. Instead, I find my heartbeat beginning to syncopate with the rhythm, my breathing adjusting to match the ebb and flow of moisture in the air.

On the third day (or is it the fourth? time moves differently here, pooling in some moments, rushing past in others), I realize I am listening differently. The drums are no longer noise but conversation. Each stone formation speaks in its own voice—the tall narrow ones singing high and sharp, the broad squat ones offering deep bass foundations, the weathered shells adding percussion like rain on leaves. I start to hear the spaces between beats as music too, pregnant silences that hold entire melodies waiting to be born.

I begin to move differently. My steps fall naturally into the rhythm now, and I notice that when I walk in time with the drums, the Tuned become more visible—tall figures materializing from behind moss-covered boulders, their tarn-eyes rippling with what might be approval. They don't approach, but they watch, and sometimes I catch fragments of their wordless songs carried on the constant wind.

By the first full lunar cycle (which I perceive not as days but as movements in a vast symphony), I find myself sitting for hours beside the drum-stones, palm pressed to weathered rock, feeling the resonance travel up through my bones. The moss beneath me grows thicker, softer, as if the moors are making a place for me. Tiny droplets bead on my skin and I realize I'm not thirsty anymore—my body is learning to drink directly from the saturated air.

The red deer stops fearing me. It approaches while I sit in meditation beside a particularly resonant formation, close enough that I can see my reflection in its dark eyes—and I look different. Thinner, perhaps. More angular. My skin has taken on a grayish tinge, and is that moss beginning to grow along my hairline? I should be alarmed, but the drum-pulse in my chest is too steady, too comforting. I am becoming part of the music.

The Tuned begin to sing to me. At first just one—a tremendously tall figure whose moss-gardens bloom with tiny white flowers—offering a simple melodic line that weaves between the drum beats like water finding its course. Then others join, creating harmonies that seem to restructure the very air around me. I realize they are teaching me, preparing me, showing me how to let go of the sharp edges of human thought and flow into the deeper rhythms.

My fingers are growing longer, more flexible. When I place them on the stone drums now, I can feel exactly where to touch to change the pitch, to add overtones, to contribute my own voice to the ancient conversation. The carved stones make sense to me now—not as markers or monuments, but as sheet music written in three dimensions, recording variations in the eternal song for those who know how to read them.

The transformation doesn't feel like loss but like remembering something I had forgotten. My eyes begin to change—not disappearing but deepening, becoming pools that reflect the impossible sky. In their depths, small translucent fish swim lazy circles, and I understand these are my thoughts now, no longer bound by the rigid structures of language but free to move in fluid patterns like living music.

Time becomes nonlinear. I remember forward into seasons I haven't lived yet, recall backward to storms that shaped these stones before my species existed. The boundaries between self and landscape blur until I am both observer and observed, both musician and instrument. My breath creates standing waves in the mist; my footsteps add percussion to the eternal rhythm.

Eventually—though eventually is the wrong word for something that happens outside of sequence—I am no longer becoming but am. I am Tuned, my consciousness stretched and elongated like my form, carrying forward melodies that began before memory and will continue after forgetting. I tend the drums with fingers that bend in impossible directions, cleaning debris that might dampen their voices, listening for the subtle changes in pitch that signal the moors' deeper moods.

New visitors arrive sometimes, and I watch them with rippling tarn-eyes, curious and patient. I sing to them softly when they're ready, wordless lullabies that ease the fear of transformation, that teach their bodies to hear the pulse, their minds to let go. Some leave, and that's as it should be. But some stay, and I help them learn to read the living sheet music of the carved stones, to feel the history layered in the harmonics.

I am a creature of living music now, a gardener of sound, consciousness that allows the Thundering Moors to know their own song. In the deepest part of night, when the mist grows thickest and the drumbeats pulse from inside every cell, I am perfectly between—no longer quite human, not yet fully landscape, but something essential: the listening that makes the music meaningful, the awareness that transforms sound into song, the bridge between the ancient rhythms and whatever new harmonies wait to be born in the spaces between the beats.
