I would find myself in the middle reaches of the Verdant Terraces, where the passages are neither the grand trunk-tunnels of the deep levels nor the sky-touched bridges of the canopy. Here, in the horizontal maze where morning mist pools in temporary lakes between the root-curtains, I have become something like a translator.

My body has adapted in small, specific ways. My eyes have developed the golden-green cast of those who read light through layers of leaf, able to distinguish between the dozens of different greens that indicate health, distress, readiness to fruit, or the subtle fever that precedes a section's decision to relocate itself. My hands have grown thin and sensitive, the skin between my fingers slightly webbed with translucent membrane that helps me feel the moisture content of the air.

But it is my ears that have changed most dramatically. They have elongated slightly, their shape shifted to catch the frequencies that the plant-city uses for its deepest communications—the subsonic pulses that travel through wood and water, the ultrasonic whispers of stressed leaves. I can hear conversations between distant sections of the settlement, debates conducted in the slow percussion of sap-flow and the high harmonics of stretching fiber.

I move through the middle passages like a living recording device, collecting the city's voices and carrying them to where they need to be heard. When the eastern terraces detect the approach of a dust storm from the distant deserts, their chemical alarm calls reach me as a faint burning in my nostrils. I carry this message through the root-ways to the western weather-shapers, who begin their preparations to create protective humidity barriers. When the deep foundations register stress patterns that suggest a section of ancient stonework needs reinforcement, their messages reach me as a low ache in my bones. I translate this into the visual language of controlled growth-spurts, guiding the structural vines to strengthen their embrace of the weakening walls.

My dwelling shifts location frequently. I have learned to read the signs that indicate when a passage is preparing to redirect itself, when walls of vegetation are planning to thin or thicken, when the floor-moss is about to embark on one of its periodic migrations to higher ground. I carry few possessions—mostly tools for listening. A collection of hollow reeds and curved shells that amplify different frequencies. Crystals grown in the deep springs that vibrate in harmony with the city's mineral substrate. Strips of specially cultivated bark that change color in response to specific chemical signals.

The work requires a particular kind of patience. Plant conversations unfold over hours or days. What seems like a simple question—whether a new chamber should be grown in response to the arrival of seed-refugees from a forest fire in the distant valleys—becomes a complex negotiation involving nutrient availability, structural considerations, seasonal cycles, and the delicate balance between growth and decay that keeps the city stable. I might spend a week carrying fragments of this discussion between different sections, facilitating a dialogue that spans acres and involves hundreds of individual plant-minds.

Some communications are more urgent. When the great crown-trees in the upper reaches detect the approach of the rare golden eagles that nest in their branches during mating season, the message travels down through my network within minutes. The city must prepare to become very still, to suppress the movement of its outer layers and dampen the phosphorescent displays that might disturb these important visitors. I help coordinate this collective holding-of-breath, moving swiftly through the passages to ensure every section receives the signal.

Other messages are more abstract, harder to interpret. The city dreams, and sometimes its dreams require translation. I might wake to find my sleep-thoughts filled with images of towers of flowering vines reaching toward stars that pulse in botanical rhythms. These dream-communications often prove to be long-term planning sessions, the city's way of modeling potential futures—new architectural possibilities, responses to climate shifts that might not occur for decades, or the slow choreography of changes that will accommodate the evolving needs of its human partners.

My relationship with the other Tenders is conducted largely through the same networks I serve. We rarely meet in person, but I carry their messages to one another, facilitating collaborations between specialists who might be separated by miles of vertical distance and intervening vegetation. The Weatherkeeper's atmospheric readings flow through me to the Deeprunner's geological assessments. The Spore-singers' seasonal schedules are coordinated with the growth-planners who tend the outer terraces.

In quiet moments, usually during the deep-night hours when most communication slows to the barely perceptible, I practice my own form of conversation with the city. I have learned to speak in the chemical vocabulary of root-tip secretions, mixing water from different springs to create simple messages. The city responds with subtle temperature changes in the walls around me, or by encouraging specific flowers to bloom along my usual pathways—its way of acknowledging my efforts, perhaps, or simply including me in its vast, slow thoughts.

The work has changed how I understand language itself. Words seem crude now, blunt instruments compared to the nuanced vocabulary of chemical gradients and growth patterns. When I do speak aloud—usually to translate for the rare visitors who come seeking the Weatherkeeper's predictions or the memory-keeper's stories—my voice sounds strange to me, too fast and angular for the conversations I spend most of my time facilitating.

Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming less human or more human—whether this role as interpreter between species represents an evolution or a dissolution of what I once was. But then a particularly complex message arrives—perhaps a three-way discussion between the root-deep mineral sensors, the canopy-level light-gatherers, and the middle-tier moisture regulators about how to respond to an unusual weather pattern—and I lose myself in the intricate work of translation, carrying meaning across the boundaries between different forms of consciousness, helping to maintain the impossible conversation that keeps this living city growing toward whatever it is becoming.
