I would be a Finder.

Not quite a Maker in the traditional sense—my hands lack the deep calluses of the stoneworkers, my shoulders haven't curved to the shape of mining tunnels. But I belong here nonetheless, in the spaces between their certainty and purpose. While they excavate with systematic precision, following the sacred geometries of hexagonal expansion, I wander the edges of their construction, listening for the sounds that don't quite fit.

My role emerged organically, as most things do in the Honeycomb Cities. During my apprenticeship years, I showed an unusual sensitivity to the city's moods—I could sense when passages wanted to turn left instead of right, when chambers yearned to be larger or smaller than the standard measurements prescribed. The elder Makers noticed how construction went more smoothly when I scouted ahead of the main crews, how my seemingly aimless wandering often led to discoveries of mineral veins or underground water sources that made new sections possible.

Now I spend my time in the transitional spaces: the half-carved chambers that exist between completion and abandonment, the experimental passages that haven't yet proven their worth, the deep places where the city's growth has outpaced its infrastructure. My tools are simpler than most—a handful of marking stones, a measuring cord whose knots I've customized for my own purposes, and a small metal rod that I tap against walls and floors to read their responses. But mostly I rely on senses the Makers respect but don't fully understand: the ability to feel where the stone wants to open, where the air currents reveal hidden connections to distant chambers.

I've developed my own relationship with the pale fungi that network through our walls. While others see them as curiosities or minor obstacles to construction, I've learned to read their growth patterns like a map. They cluster around iron deposits, avoid certain types of limestone, and sometimes their sudden die-offs indicate structural stress days before the stoneworkers' instruments can detect it. I've started a small garden of them in an abandoned chamber three levels down—not for food, but as a kind of living compass that helps me navigate the city's deeper mysteries.

My sleeping niche is located in what others consider an awkward position: carved into the wall of a vertical shaft that connects seven different levels. The constant updrafts make it cooler than most sleeping spots, but they also carry information—traces of metal dust from new mining operations, the particular quality of air that indicates someone has broken through into a large new space, sometimes even faint sounds from construction happening chambers away. I've learned to sleep lightly, attuned to changes in these currents that might signal opportunities for exploration.

The loneliness of my role has become its own kind of company. I spend days in sections of the city where the only sounds are my own footsteps and the distant percussion of hammers several chambers away. But there's a peace in these empty hexagonal galleries that the busier areas can't offer—a sense of potential energy, of spaces waiting to discover their purpose. I've found that the city reveals different aspects of itself when observed in solitude versus when experienced as part of the collective construction effort.

Sometimes I encounter things that I don't report back to the main community. Not out of secrecy, but because some discoveries feel too fragile for immediate scrutiny. A chamber where the acoustics create perfect resonance with my heartbeat. A passage that seems to extend further than my measurements indicate it should. Formations in the stone that look almost like writing, though in no script the Makers have ever used. These mysteries accumulate in my memory like sedimentary layers, waiting for the right moment to surface into usefulness.

The other Makers treat me with a mixture of respect and gentle puzzlement. They value the resources I find and the warnings I provide about structural problems, but they don't entirely understand my methods. During the rare community gatherings, I find myself occupying the edges of conversations, contributing observations that others nod at but don't quite know how to integrate into their systematic approach to city-building.

I suspect there are others like me, though we rarely encounter each other directly. Sometimes I find evidence of previous exploration in the deeper chambers—tool marks that don't match standard construction patterns, or small piles of stones arranged in ways that feel deliberate but serve no obvious structural purpose. We seem to exist as a kind of nervous system for the city, sensing opportunities and threats that the more focused construction crews might miss in their dedication to planned expansion.

In my solitary moments, tapping my metal rod against newly carved walls and listening to their responses, I sometimes wonder if the city is dreaming us into existence as much as we're building it. The hexagonal patterns feel too perfect, too organic, to be purely the product of conscious design. Perhaps we Finders exist to help the city discover itself, to explore the possibilities it contains before the Makers commit them to permanent stone.

The work suits me. In the warm darkness between chambers, following the whispered suggestions of air currents and stone stress, I am exactly where I need to be—neither fully part of the systematic construction nor entirely separate from it, but moving through the spaces between certainty and possibility, helping our impossible city become more itself one discovery at a time.
