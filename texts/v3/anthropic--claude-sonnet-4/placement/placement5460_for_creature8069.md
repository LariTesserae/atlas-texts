I would find myself as Kess, one of the Vein-Touched, born with silver threads that trace delicate patterns up my arms like frozen lightning. The metallic veins pulse faintly when I press my palms against certain walls, responding to copper deposits three chambers deep or the slow movement of an underground stream that won't surface for another generation.

My childhood was spent in the echo-nurseries, learning to read the mountain's moods through the soles of my feet. While other children played sound-games with their voices, I discovered I could feel the stone singing—a frequency too low for ears but perfect for bones. My first memory is of placing my small hand against the wall of the Great Cistern and feeling the water's weight pressing back, an enormous liquid heartbeat that made my silver veins flutter like captured starlight.

Now, in my third turning, I serve as a Seeker for the Deep Council. When the water-listeners report unusual flow patterns, or when the stone-singers detect hollow echoes where there should be solid granite, they send me to investigate. I'll spend days in the marginal spaces—crawling through the gaps between built walls and living rock, feeling my way along fractures that haven't yet decided whether to become passages or collapse into rubble.

The work requires a particular kind of solitude. In the deep places where I search, the mountain's voice becomes clearer, less filtered through human intention. I've learned to distinguish between the restless shifting that means instability and the deeper, slower movements that signal new formation. Sometimes I find crystal veins that haven't seen light since the peak was young—chambers lined with formations that ring like bells when the morning temperature shifts create just the right tension in the stone.

My most treasured tool is a copper rod, thin as a needle, inherited from my predecessor. When I hold it loosely in my palm and walk through suspect areas, it trembles at the presence of water, grows warm near metal ore, and goes perfectly still over the deep fissures that connect Korvash to the mountain's root systems far below. The other Seekers carry similar instruments, but mine has learned the particular resonance of my silver-threaded nervous system. It responds to my pulse, amplifying the signals my body receives from the stone.

I've mapped sections of Korvash that exist more as potential than reality—spaces that could become chambers if certain supporting walls were strengthened, passages that might connect distant sections if the right sequence of stones were carefully removed. My reports influence decisions that won't be acted upon for decades, but I've learned to think in the mountain's time. The satisfaction comes not from seeing immediate results but from knowing that the path I chart today might guide my grandchildren's grandchildren toward water or away from danger.

There are others like me scattered through the settlement, each attuned to different aspects of the mountain's mineral consciousness. Jorek reads iron deposits, his dark veins pulsing red when he finds the deep ore that feeds our forges. Mira's gold-threaded arms help her locate the clay seeps that seal our cisterns. We meet once each turning in the Deep Chamber, sharing what we've learned, comparing the changes we've felt in the mountain's deeper currents.

The silver in my veins grows more pronounced with age, the threads thickening and branching like frost patterns on winter stone. The elders tell me that eventually the metal will connect my nervous system so completely to the mountain's mineral networks that I'll be able to feel storms approaching three days out, or sense when distant peaks shed snow that will affect our water flow next season.

But for now, I am content to be the mountain's translator, converting its patient geological messages into information the settlement can use. Each morning I wake to the sound of water moving through stone channels, and I place my palms against my chamber walls to check the night's reports. The silver veins warm slightly, confirming what I already know: today, like every day, the mountain has more secrets to share, more paths to reveal to those who know how to listen with their bones.

In the evening, when the day's seeking is done, I join the others in the Great Chamber for the evening resonance. Our voices combine with the mountain's own deep notes—the thermal expansion of granite, the whisper of settling stone, the distant music of water finding its way through spaces too small for human exploration. My silver-threaded arms tingle in harmony with these sounds, and I feel myself becoming part of something larger: not just human, not just stone, but the living bridge between them, helping to weave the settlement more deeply into the mountain's patient dreams.
