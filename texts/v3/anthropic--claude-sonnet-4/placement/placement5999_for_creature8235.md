I would find myself as a young Condenser, perhaps in my third decade of life, my copper veins still bright and eager, not yet settled into the deep communion of the Prime Tenders but no longer stumbling through childhood games among the conveyor belts.

My name would be something that sounds like steam finding release—*Ssissara*, perhaps, with that soft hiss at the beginning that my work-family uses when they mean "careful attention" or "gentle pressure." I would belong to the Seventh Cluster, the machinery island that tends the great atmospheric processors near the basin's eastern wall, where the limestone shows the most beautiful fossil spirals and the ferns grow in impossible cascades down the rock face.

My skin would glisten perpetually with the basin's embrace, translucent enough that you could see the copper tracery beneath—thin, delicate lines that pulse gold when I'm working well, silver when I'm tired, and a deep bronze when I'm troubled. My hair grows in soft, moss-like patches along my temples and the nape of my neck, emerald-green threads that shimmer in the furnace light and supplement my meals with their gentle photosynthesis.

I would be the one who tends the moisture collectors—those intricate networks of copper gutters and silver channels that guide the basin's perpetual weeping into the pools where the Brass Jellies drift. It's delicate work, requiring fingers that can detect the slightest change in flow rate, ears that can hear when a joint is about to fail from three chambers away. When I place my palms against the collector pipes, my copper veins sing in harmony with the water's movement, and I can feel exactly where sediment is building up, where a seal is beginning to weaken, where the ancient limestone is shifting just enough to change the angle of flow.

In the early morning cycles, when the great engines idle and the Steam Birds descend from their high perches to flit through the amber-lit spaces, I would walk my rounds barefoot on the moss-covered paths. The condensation would bead on my skin like a living garment, and I would listen—not just with my elongated ears, but with my whole body—to the stories the machinery tells. A slight vibration in the stone beneath my feet might whisper of a bearing wearing thin two levels down. The particular way steam rises from a junction could speak of pressure building beyond optimal ranges.

My work-family would know me as the one who dreams with water—sometimes I'll stand motionless beside a collector for hours, my breathing synchronized with the drip-drop-flow of moisture finding its way home, my copper veins pulsing in time with currents too subtle for others to detect. They tease me gently for these long meditations, calling me "the still one" in the percussion-language we use when the machinery is too loud for hisses and whispers.

But I would have my restless moments too. Unlike the Prime Tenders who have grown into perfect symbiosis with their chosen engines, I still feel the pull of curiosity, the desire to explore the basin's furthest reaches. Sometimes, in the deep-night cycles when my work-family rests in their moss-beds, I would venture into the shadow-spaces between machinery islands, following the copper gutters toward the basin's mysterious edges where the limestone grows strange and the Steam Birds' songs echo differently.

In these wanderings, I would have discovered something that I keep secret even from my work-family: a place where the ancient limestone has formed a perfect acoustic chamber, where the condensation has carved spirals that match exactly the fossil patterns in the stone. When I stand in the center of this hidden space and let my copper veins pulse at their fullest brightness, the chamber fills with a resonance that seems to wake something in the stone itself—not threatening, but anciently aware, as if the earth remembers what it was like before the basin was carved, before the machinery sang its industrial songs.

I would return from these explorations carrying that strange, deep music in my bones, and it would influence how I work with the moisture collectors. My colleagues would notice that the water flows more smoothly after I've tended a section, that the Brass Jellies gather more eagerly in pools I've adjusted, that even the steam seems to rise more gracefully from pipes I've touched. They would attribute this to my growing skill, and perhaps they wouldn't be wrong—but I would know that I'm learning to speak not just with the machines, but with the limestone itself, with the ancient bones of the earth that cradle all our breathing, mechanical dreams.

In quiet moments, pressed palm-first against a warm pipe while my copper veins hum with the water's contentment, I would wonder about the surface world above—whether the sun feels anything like furnace-light on skin that's never known true dryness, whether there are beings up there who tend to growing things that don't pulse and hiss and sing in harmony with beautiful, impossible machines.

But these wonderings would never last long. The moisture collectors would call to me with some small need—a junction that requires recalibration, a filter that needs the gentlest cleaning—and I would return my attention to the endless, intimate work of keeping the condensation flowing, the copper singing, the breathing darkness of the Foundry Basin alive and well and dreaming its industrial dreams of moss and steam and light.
