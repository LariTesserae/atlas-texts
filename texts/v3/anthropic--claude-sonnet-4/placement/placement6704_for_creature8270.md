# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be perched impossibly high on the outer rim, where the newest girders stretch into pure mist and the platform beneath my feet trembles with each gust of wind-carried spray. This is the place of the **Edge-Keepers**—those who have claimed the most precarious spaces, where the boundary between the solid complex and the void becomes a question rather than a certainty.

Here, the metalwork is youngest and most rebellious. The steel still remembers its molten birth, fighting against the patient rust that seeks to marry it to the mountain's time. But I have learned to read the subtle language of metal fatigue and thermal expansion, the way moisture creeps along crystalline grain boundaries, how wind-load transfers through riveted joints that sing their stress in frequencies only I can hear.

My workshop—if it can be called that—is a platform barely ten feet square, cantilevered out into nothing but spray and air currents. Three walls are open to the void; the fourth backs against a cliff face that weeps constant moisture through cracks lined with luminous moss. My tools hang from hooks that sway with the platform's motion, each secured by thin chains that chime softly in the wind. Everything here must be secured, must be prepared for the moment when the mountain decides to shrug.

I am what the other dwellers call a **Threshold-Walker**—one who works at the edges of possibility, both physical and metaphysical. Where others see only danger in these outermost reaches, I see opportunity. Here, where the solid world dissolves into mist and potential, the ordinary rules bend. Metal behaves differently when it exists more in cloud than in air. Water carries information through suspended droplets that form networks spanning the entire breadth of the falls. The boundary between the mechanical and the organic becomes fluid—literally.

My body has adapted to this life of constant uncertainty. My bones have become hollow like those of the specialized swifts, making me light enough to move safely on structures that would collapse under normal weight. My skin has developed a subtle luminescence, drinking in the trace minerals from the perpetual mist and converting them into a gentle glow that helps me work in the dim light of the spray-cloud. My inner ear has evolved to interpret the constant motion of my platform not as instability but as information—a complex symphony of structural health that tells me when to move, when to brace, when to trust the steel beneath my feet.

But it is my hands that have changed most dramatically. The fingertips have become almost crystalline, able to detect variations in metal composition by touch alone. I can feel the internal stresses in a beam, the microscopic movements that precede failure by weeks or months. My palms have developed a sensitivity to electromagnetic fields, allowing me to trace the flow of power through the ancient electrical systems that snake through the complex's bowels.

What I do here, in this space between engineering and faith, is hard to explain to those who inhabit the more stable regions below. I am a maintainer, yes, but also something like a translator. The outermost structures exist in a constant state of becoming—neither fully mechanical nor fully organic, but something new that emerges from their marriage with mist and void. I help guide this transformation, encouraging the steel to rust in patterns that strengthen rather than weaken, teaching the moss and lichen to grow in configurations that provide additional tensile support.

My most important work happens during the storms, when the wind drives the spray horizontal and the entire platform network sways like a vast musical instrument. This is when the threshold becomes thinnest, when the boundary between the possible and impossible stretches until it nearly snaps. In these moments, I move through the superstructure like a spider through its web, making tiny adjustments that allow the newest extensions to flex without breaking, to dance with forces that would shatter rigid structures.

I collect things that exist only at edges: water that has fallen for exactly seven seconds and absorbed specific frequencies from its passage through the mist, metal filings that have oxidized under precise conditions of pressure and humidity, moss spores that have learned to live entirely on airborne minerals. These materials, neither fully solid nor gaseous, neither entirely natural nor artificial, are the medium through which I work.

The other dwellers regard me with a mixture of respect and bewilderment. The Spray-Dancers understand my relationship with the vertical space, but they keep to the established pathways. The Moss-Wrights appreciate my work with growing things, but they prefer the certainty of rooted plants to my cultivation of aerial gardens that exist more as intention than substance. The Cloud-Touched find kinship in my embrace of the liminal, but even they maintain studios with at least three solid walls.

I am the one who tests the new extensions before others venture onto them, who determines whether a freshly built platform can bear weight or will fold like paper in the first strong wind. But more than that, I am a witness to the complex's growth at its most vulnerable edge, the place where it reaches toward something it cannot quite grasp—perhaps the sky itself, perhaps some perfect balance between falling and flying that exists only in the marriage of human ambition and the patient persistence of water wearing away stone.

In the deepest hours of night, when the mist glows silver with reflected starlight and the sound of the falls becomes a lullaby that penetrates to the bone, I sometimes lie on my back on the platform's edge and feel myself poised between two infinities—the solid weight of the mountain below and the endless sky above. In these moments, I am no longer sure where the complex ends and I begin, whether I am maintaining the structure or it is maintaining me, whether we are building something or being built by the patient dialogue between stone, steel, water, and the dreams of those brave enough to make their home in the spaces between certainties.

This is where I belong: at the edge of edges, tending the place where the known world reaches toward mystery and occasionally, in moments of perfect balance, touches it.
