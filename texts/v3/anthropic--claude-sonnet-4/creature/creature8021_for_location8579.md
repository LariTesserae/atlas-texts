The people of the Canopy Cities move with the measured grace of those born to swaying ground. Their limbs are elongated, fingers tapering to points that can read the subtle textures of bark-speech, ears slightly cupped to catch the whispered messages that travel through root-tubes. Over generations, their bones have grown hollow like bird bones, not brittle but singing—they chime softly when they walk, creating unconscious harmonies as families move together through the moss-carpeted streets.

Their skin holds a faint green undertone, photosynthetic patches along their arms and shoulders that supplement their diet during the long, mist-heavy months when hunting is sparse. Children are born with this marking more pronounced, fading as they age unless they choose the Deep Tender calling—those who spend their lives in the darkest heartwood chambers, maintaining the city's dreams, whose skin deepens to forest-dark and whose eyes dilate wide and stay that way.

They dress in clothes grown rather than woven: vests of supple bark-cloth that continues growing slowly throughout its wearer's lifetime, gradually shifting cut and color with the seasons. Their fabrics bloom. A wedding dress might flower once a year on the anniversary. Mourning clothes produce small, dark berries that drop as grief fades. The most skilled seamster-gardeners create garments that tell stories through their seasonal changes, narratives that unfold over decades of wear.

The Listeners inhabit the root-telephone chambers, pale from lives spent underground, their temples scarred from the surgical grafts that allow them to plug directly into the communication network. They relay messages but also translate the city's deeper thoughts, the slow concerns it whispers through fungal networks: warnings of branch weakness, requests for new growth patterns, complaints about districts that have grown too heavy or too hurried. The Listeners' minds work at two speeds—quick enough for human conversation, slow enough to understand wood-time.

High above live the Bridge-Builders, families who have spent generations tending the great suspension pathways. Their children are born with an instinctive understanding of tension and sway, able to read the health of a kilometer-long span in the way it moves beneath their feet. They marry across vast distances, sometimes courting through messages carried by trained birds between bridges, conducting relationships that span multiple tree-cities. Their weddings are held on new bridges, the couple literally bringing their communities together.

The Sap-Farmers tend the city's circulation, monitoring the great flows that carry nutrients and messages throughout the network. They taste the sap for health, sweetness, and the subtle flavors that indicate the city's moods. Master Sap-Farmers can predict weather days in advance by flavor alone, can diagnose problems in distant districts by detecting stress chemicals in the flow. They paint their faces with tree-signs, temporary tattoos that indicate which groves they're tending, which chambers are under their care.

In the highest reaches dwell the Sky-Shepherds, who manage the luminescent insect clouds and direct the great bird migrations. Thin as saplings from lives lived in low-oxygen altitudes, they wear cloaks of bird feathers that allow them to glide between the tallest spires. They speak in whistles and chirps, a language borrowed from their charges. Their eyes are sharp enough to count individual insects in a swarm of millions, their hands gentle enough to convince a cloud of fireflies to resettle in a district that needs more light.

The Deep Tenders, as mentioned, are perhaps the strangest. They enter the profession as children, chosen by the city itself—they're found sleepwalking toward the deepest chambers, drawn by calls only they can hear. Over decades of communion with the city's dreaming mind, they become something between human and tree, their hair growing root-like, their thoughts slowing to match the patient rhythms of wood-consciousness. They emerge rarely, and when they do, they speak in metaphors so dense that special interpreters are needed to translate their insights into actionable civic policy.

Children grow up climbing before they walk properly, their playground the infinite three-dimensional maze of branches and chambers. They attend school in rooms that rearrange themselves based on the lesson—history chambers that recreate past events through controlled growth, mathematics classes where the walls themselves demonstrate geometric principles by shifting their angles. The brightest students are those who learn to ask the city questions directly, coaxing knowledge from chambers that remember centuries of accumulated wisdom.

The elderly don't die so much as gradually become part of the city. In their final years, they choose a chamber that calls to them, gradually growing still and quiet until they become fixtures, their wisdom accessible through the root-network long after their bodies have become indistinguishable from the surrounding wood. Families visit these ancestor-chambers for advice, pressing their hands to bark that was once grandmother's forehead, listening for whispered counsel that emerges from the city's collective memory.

Musicians here don't play instruments so much as conduct the city itself. They've learned to make the hollow chambers resonate, turning entire districts into vast wooden organs. Their concerts can last for days, melodies that begin in one tree-tower and take hours to migrate through the root-system to emerge transformed in another. The greatest symphonies are played across entire mountain ranges, requiring decades to complete, with movements timed to coincide with seasonal changes and weather patterns.

The merchants are also gardener-diplomats, travelling between Canopy Cities in hollowed seed-pods that sail the high winds between mountain ranges. They trade not just in goods but in genetic material—new bark patterns, experimental moss varieties, architectural wood-shapes that have succeeded in distant groves. Their greatest treasures are songs that make certain wood-types grow faster, or pheromone-recipes that can encourage reluctant branches to bridge particularly difficult gaps.

And throughout it all move the Tree-Speakers, part priest and part translator, who maintain the careful relationship between human needs and wood-dreams. They mediate disputes not just between people but between people and city, negotiating when a family wishes to expand their chamber in a direction the tree finds uncomfortable, or when a district's growth is causing structural problems that the city can sense but humans cannot. They're recognized by their bark-scarred hands and the small saplings they wear woven into their hair—live crowns that connect them more directly to the urban forest's vast, slow thoughts.

The children born here know no other way of life, can't imagine buildings that don't breathe or cities that don't dream. They grow up understanding themselves as part of something larger and stranger and more patient than human civilization has ever been, symbiotic citizens of a place that is simultaneously the most artificial and most natural environment their species has ever created.
