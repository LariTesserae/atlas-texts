In the deep chambers of Korvash live the Khevari—the Stone-Touched—a people whose bodies have grown to mirror the patient endurance of their mountain home. Their skin carries the pallor of mist and granite, marked by a network of fine lines that seem carved rather than aged, deepening slowly over decades like the channels worn by water into stone. Their hands are broad and thick-fingered, with calluses that have hardened into something approaching horn, capable of sensing the grain and stress of rock through the slightest touch.

The Khevari are tall but dense, their bones heavy as the bedrock itself. They move with deliberate precision, each gesture considered and economical. Their hair grows coarse and mineral-white early in life, often braided with thin copper wires that serve both ornament and function—the metal conducts sound and helps them navigate the acoustic maze of their home. Their eyes are pale, adapted to the filtered light of perpetual mist, and capable of detecting the subtle variations in stone that reveal structural weakness or the presence of hidden water.

Generations of breathing the thin, moisture-laden air have shaped their lungs into powerful bellows. They speak in low, resonant tones that carry far through stone corridors, their language rich with words that distinguish between dozens of different types of silence, the quality of echoes, the sounds that water makes as it moves through different kinds of rock. Their children learn to sing before they learn to walk, their voices trained to map the chambers around them through the patterns of returning sound.

The Khevari measure age not in years but in the number of stone-turnings they have witnessed—the slow process by which a section of wall is taken apart, each block examined, and rebuilt with microscopic adjustments that will serve the settlement for another century. The eldest among them, the Deep Listeners, have felt the mountain's pulse for eight or nine turnings. Their fingertips have worn smooth from constant contact with stone, and they can predict shifts in weather, structural stress, or the discovery of new water sources by laying their hands against the living rock.

Family structures among the Khevari follow the logic of their architecture. Blood relations matter less than acoustic ones—children are often raised by whoever's voice harmonizes best with their own natural pitch. Households form around the resonance of particular chambers, with marriages arranged as much for the musical compatibility of voices as for any other consideration. The resulting family songs, practiced over decades, serve as both cultural identity and practical communication system, allowing complex information to be transmitted across the settlement's many levels.

Their crafts have evolved to work with the mountain's gifts. Master stone-singers can locate hidden cavities in granite by the way their voices reflect off internal surfaces. Water-listeners press their ears to walls to track the movement of underground streams. The most revered artisans are the Echo-Shapers, who carve chambers and passages to produce specific acoustic effects—a whisper gallery where lovers can speak privately across a vast space, or a resonance chamber that amplifies the mountain's own subtle voices into an audible warning system.

The Khevari have no written language. Instead, they preserve knowledge in the stones themselves, carving information into the walls using a complex system of textures readable by touch. Important histories are embedded in the very architecture—the great water crisis of three turnings past recorded in the rough-hewn surface of a cistern wall, the lineages of master craftsmen traced in the smooth channels that direct condensation through the settlement's heart.

Their relationship with the mountain's small creatures is one of careful symbiosis. They've learned to interpret the behavior of the stone-mice whose tunnels sometimes reveal structural weaknesses before human senses can detect them. The frost-birds that nest in the settlement's edges serve as weather-singers, their calls changing pitch and rhythm to announce shifts in temperature and pressure. Some Khevari develop such sensitivity to these natural signals that they can predict the mountain's moods hours or even days in advance.

Among them move the Vein-Touched—individuals born with veins of metallic blue or silver threading through their pale skin, marking them as particularly attuned to the mountain's mineral consciousness. These rare people serve as living dowsing rods, their bodies resonating with deposits of ore, crystal formations, or hidden springs. They often become the settlement's pathfinders, responsible for planning the slow expansion of chambers and passages that follows the natural logic of the stone.

The Khevari age like their mountain—slowly, steadily, wearing away from the outside while their essential nature remains unchanged. Their elderly don't die so much as gradually become more stone-like, spending longer periods in meditation with their hands pressed to walls, their breathing slowing until it matches the imperceptible rhythm of the mountain itself. The most venerated eventually choose to be sealed into special chambers where they continue their communion with the living rock, their whispered insights emerging through speaking-tubes to guide future generations.

In the deepest chambers, where the mountain's warmth is strongest and the stone walls weep constantly with mineral-rich moisture, live the youngest Khevari—infants and children whose bones are still soft enough to be shaped by their environment. These nursery chambers echo constantly with lullabies that teach acoustic navigation, mathematical concepts embedded in rhythm and melody, and the endless patient songs that describe the settlement's history, one stone at a time. The children's games involve elaborate patterns of echoes, preparing them for a life where sound and silence, pressure and patience, water and stone will be their constant companions.

The Khevari have learned to think in geological time, but they have not lost their humanity to it. Instead, they have discovered a form of endurance that transforms waiting into a kind of active prayer, maintenance into meditation, and the slow work of survival into something approaching art. They are the mountain's own people now, as patient and enduring as stone, as essential and life-giving as water, as resonant with unspoken possibilities as the vast chambers they have carved from the living rock.
