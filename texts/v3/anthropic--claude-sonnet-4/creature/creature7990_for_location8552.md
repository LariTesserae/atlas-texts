The Khethi move through their terraced world with the unhurried precision of those who measure time in growing seasons rather than heartbeats. Their bodies have adapted across generations to their unique environment—they tend to be shorter and more compact than surface dwellers, with broad, capable hands whose fingers have developed an almost preternatural sensitivity to soil moisture and plant health. Their skin carries a perpetual hint of green beneath its natural tones, as if the constant proximity to chlorophyll has left its mark, and their eyes have grown large and keen in the filtered light of the canopy.

They dress in layers of finely woven plant fiber that can be adjusted throughout the day as humidity shifts—loose outer garments that breathe in the thick air, with tighter underlayers that wick moisture without restricting the constant bending and reaching their work requires. Their clothing is never quite one color, dyed with plant materials that shift subtly between green, brown, and gold depending on the light. Many garments show deliberate tears and patches where living vines have been encouraged to grow through the fabric, creating garments that are partially alive.

The Khethi speak in voices that have learned to carry without echoing, their language rich in terms for gradations of moisture, soil consistency, and plant health that would sound like poetry to outsiders. They have a tendency to pause mid-sentence, not from hesitation but from a habit of listening—to the whisper of settling earth, the particular rustle that indicates a vine ready for harvest, or the almost inaudible shift that means a terrace wall needs attention. Their conversations weave around work like water around stones, interrupted and resumed so naturally that the rhythm feels inevitable.

Children here are raised as much by the city itself as by their parents. They learn to walk on the yielding, uneven surfaces of the terraces, developing an extraordinary sense of balance and an intuitive understanding of which plants can bear their weight. The youngest spend their days following the gardener-architects, learning to read the subtle signs that indicate where new growth should be encouraged or where intervention is needed. They play games that would mystify surface children—contests of identifying plants by touch alone, races through the canopy walkways that require as much botanical knowledge as agility, and elaborate versions of hide-and-seek that can last for days as children find sleeping nooks among the roots and branches.

The Khethi elderly are revered as living libraries of growing knowledge. Their fingers can assess the health of soil by touch, predict weather patterns by the behavior of insects, and judge the perfect moment for harvest by signs invisible to younger eyes. They move slowly but are never rushed, understanding that their measured pace reflects the rhythms of their city itself. The oldest among them bear intricate tattoos created from plant dyes that shift color with the seasons, marking them as master gardeners whose knowledge has become literally embodied.

Their governance, if it can be called that, functions more like ecosystem management than traditional politics. Decisions emerge from long consultations between the master gardeners, with discussions that can span weeks as various factors are considered—the needs of different crop cycles, the structural requirements of expanding terraces, the delicate balance of water flow. Authority flows to those who demonstrate the deepest understanding of the city's needs, and leadership changes as gradually and naturally as the seasons.

The Khethi have no concept of rushing. Their entire culture has adapted to the pace of growth itself—they plan in decades, think in generations, and move with the deliberate care of people who understand that hasty actions can disrupt systems that took lifetimes to establish. Yet this is not laziness or inefficiency; when urgent action is needed—a terrace wall threatening collapse, a pest infestation, or the rare severe weather—they respond with coordinated precision that speaks to their deep interconnectedness with their environment.

They sleep in small alcoves woven directly into the living architecture, surrounded by the gentle sounds of growth and the slow breathing of their city. Dreams here are said to be particularly vivid, influenced by the rich, oxygen-heavy air and the constant, subtle movement of the earth beneath them. Many Khethi report dreams of flying through the canopy or of seeing through the eyes of the plants they tend, experiences that blur the line between metaphor and symbiosis.

Their relationship with the wildlife of Kheth-Morah exemplifies their approach to life itself—not dominance or even partnership, but a kind of negotiated coexistence. They've learned to read the territorial markers of the ground-dwelling rodents and plan their planting accordingly. They know which trees the fruit bats prefer and ensure those remain undisturbed during the creatures' peak seasons. The birds that nest in the living walls are considered neighbors, their needs factored into the endless subtle adjustments that keep the city thriving.

To an outsider, the Khethi might seem to lack ambition or drive, but this would misunderstand their deepest values. Their ambition is vast—it simply operates on a timescale that encompasses centuries. They dream of terraces that spiral down to depths not yet imagined, of fruit varieties not yet bred, of harmonies between human need and natural process not yet achieved. They are building something unprecedented, but they're building it at the speed of growth itself, with the patience of soil and the persistence of seeds.
