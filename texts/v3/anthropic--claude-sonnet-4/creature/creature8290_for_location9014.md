**The Kethrani**

They are a people shaped by altitude and glass—tall and narrow like their city, with bones that hollow slightly in adolescence, a genetic gift that makes them lighter without sacrificing strength. Their fingers are longer than those of lowlanders, ending in subtle hooks of keratin that grew over generations from gripping smooth glass surfaces and thin rope. When they gesture while speaking, their hands move like dancers', flowing in curves that mirror their architecture.

The Kethrani breathe differently. Their lungs have adapted to extract every molecule of oxygen from the thin air, and their speech comes in measured cadences—not from any cultural restraint, but because they have learned to make each breath count. Children born here never know the luxury of breathless laughter; instead, their joy manifests in the soft clicking sounds they make with their tongues against their teeth, a music that carries beautifully through the hollow core.

Their eyes have changed too, developing a nictitating membrane to protect against the glare of sun on obsidian and an enhanced ability to see in the gentle bioluminescence that bathes their nights. In certain light, their pupils reflect with an opalescent sheen, and they can spot the thermal currents that their flying companions ride long before outsiders notice the shimmer.

**The Glasswright Families**

At the city's heart live the most ancient bloodlines—the glasswrights who know the spire's moods. They feel vibrations through their feet that warn of stress fractures forming stories above or below. Their workshops occupy the prime chambers, where natural acoustics allow them to hear the almost-musical note the glass makes when carved just so. They work with tools inherited through twenty generations: diamond-tipped chisels, sonic cutters that sing at frequencies only they can hear properly, and measuring devices calibrated to the spire's own geometry.

The glasswrights' children learn by placing their small palms against the walls and feeling for the spire's heartbeat—the deep, slow pulse of thermal expansion and contraction that marks the difference between day and night, season and season. By their teens, they can predict weather changes by the way the obsidian hums.

**The Windkeepers**

On the outer platforms, the windkeepers tend their aerial gardens. These are people of the edges, comfortable with space and emptiness, who have developed an almost supernatural balance. They can sleep on ledges no wider than their shoulders, and their children take their first steps on swaying platforms suspended over thousand-foot drops.

The windkeepers have formed the deepest bonds with the city's flying creatures. Each keeper knows the individual call of hundreds of the jewel-bright fliers, can predict their migration patterns with the seasons, and speaks to them in a language of whistles and hand gestures that mimics the creatures' own communication. Some keepers claim they dream in flight, sharing the thermal visions of their charges.

**The Vesselwrights and Engineers**

In the middle reaches, practical people maintain the mechanical heart that keeps Kethros alive. They are stockier than other Kethrani—their bodies not having undergone the full vertical adaptation because their work demands strength over lightness. Their hands are stained permanent colors: brass-green from the winch mechanisms, copper-blue from the heating pipes, and silver-black from the lubricants that keep everything moving.

These are the most talkative of the Kethrani, their conversations a constant background chatter of technical details, maintenance schedules, and good-natured complaints about the impossibility of hauling replacement parts up three miles of rope lifts. They measure time not by the sun but by the rhythm of the steam engines, and their children learn to sleep through the mechanical heartbeat that would keep any lowlander awake.

**The Symbiotic Companions**

The leathery, cat-sized wing-riders are not pets or servants but genuine partners. They have their own social structures within the spire, their own territories and hierarchies. The largest and oldest among them—creatures who may live for centuries—serve as informal ambassadors between their species and the Kethrani. These ancient ones have learned to understand human speech, though they rarely choose to respond in kind, preferring their rain-on-glass chittering.

The wing-riders remember things differently than humans—they navigate by magnetic fields and air pressure changes, and they can sense the approach of storms days in advance. Some Kethrani families have lived in partnership with the same wing-rider lineages for so many generations that the creatures have become repositories of family history, carrying messages not just between floors but between decades.

**The Children of Glass**

Perhaps most remarkable are the children born and raised in Kethros. They grow up thinking vertically, navigating three-dimensional space with an intuition that amazes visitors. They play games that would terrify lowland adults—racing along narrow ledges, swinging between platforms on gossamer-thin cables, diving through the hollow core to catch updrafts that carry them safely to lower levels.

These children develop a deep, almost mystical connection to their city. They can feel its moods through the soles of their feet, taste changes in the wind that signal shifts in the aerial ecosystem, and hear harmonics in the mechanical heartbeat that reveal the health of distant systems. As they mature, many discover they can influence the city's living systems—coaxing bioluminescent cultures to grow in new patterns, encouraging the jewel-bright fliers to nest in specific locations, even sensing stress points in the glass before cracks become visible.

**The Elders**

Those who have lived longest in Kethros become something approaching oracles. Their bones are light as bird bones, their movements economical and precise. They speak little but watch constantly, their opalescent eyes tracking thermal currents invisible to younger residents. They know the spire's deepest secrets—which chambers were carved in desperation during harsh winters, which routes through the hollow core carry sounds from the outside world, and which of the ancient glasswrights' tools still hum with power after centuries of use.

The elders often spend their final years on the highest platforms, tending the most delicate bioluminescent cultures and maintaining the signal beacons that guide lost wing-riders home through storms. When they die, their ashes are incorporated into new glass vessels for the light-cultures, becoming part of the gentle radiance that makes their city a pillar of soft luminescence against the dark sky.

In their collective existence, the people of Kethros have become something more than human—not through magic or divine intervention, but through the patient accumulation of adaptation, tradition, and symbiosis. They are the children of altitude and glass, shaped by their impossible city into creatures perfectly suited for life on the edge of earth and sky.
