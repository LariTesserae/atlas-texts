In the deepest shadows where the walls curve inward like cupped hands, the Watchers dwell. They are not quite human, though they once were—generations ago, before the slow alchemy of the marsh began its patient work. Tall and impossibly thin, they move through the ankle-deep water without causing so much as a ripple, their elongated limbs adapted to the perpetual wade. Their skin has taken on the color of wet clay, mottled with patches of pale green where symbiotic algae has learned to grow in the creases of their joints and the hollow of their throats.

Their eyes have grown large and dark to drink the filtered twilight, pupils that never seem to contract, reflecting the water's surface like black mirrors. They wear no clothes but need none—their bodies have grown a fine coating of something between hair and moss that shifts color with the seasons, now brown as dried reeds, now green as new growth. When they breathe, it is with the slow, deliberate rhythm of the building itself, as if they have learned to synchronize their lungs with the seepage of the springs below.

The Watchers tend. That is their purpose, though none remember who first assigned it to them or when they accepted it. They guide the tiny currents with movements of their fingers, ensuring the water flows as it should. They whisper to the nested swallows in a language that sounds like wind through cattails, keeping them calm and content in their high homes. When a fish grows sick or a beetle struggles upside-down on the surface, they intervene with touches so gentle they barely disturb the water's skin.

But it is their relationship with the memories that makes them truly strange. The hall remembers everything—every creature that has lived and died here, every season of growth and dormancy, every storm that has drummed against the reed screens above. The Watchers can cup this dark water in their palms and drink the past: they taste the copper-bright fear of a mouse that drowned here seven summers ago, the salt-sweet satisfaction of successful mating seasons, the mineral grief of roots that grew too deep and found only stone.

They communicate among themselves through vibrations in the water, patterns of pressure too subtle for other senses to detect. A gentle pulse from fingertips against stone might convey the health of a wall section; a certain frequency drummed against the submerged foundation might indicate the need to guide more silt to a growing breach. To watch them work—if you could spot them in the shadows, if your eyes could track their slow, precise movements—would be to witness a conversation carried on entirely through the medium they have made their world.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when even the insects grow still, the Watchers gather in the hall's exact center. They form a circle in the water, their long bodies swaying like kelp in a current, and they sing. The sound they make is not quite music—it has no melody that human ears could follow. Instead, it is the voice of the building itself, the harmonic frequency of reed and stone and flowing water given breath. The song calls to things in the marsh outside: lost birds find their way to safety, sick animals come to drink from the healing springs, and sometimes—very rarely—humans stumble upon the hall as if led by a dream they cannot quite remember.

The Watchers do not age as other creatures do. They wear away, slowly, like stones in a stream. Their edges soften, their movements become more fluid, their thoughts merge ever more completely with the collective consciousness of the hall. The eldest among them are barely distinguishable from the shadows themselves, more presence than form, more intention than flesh. When one finally dissolves entirely back into the dark water, their knowledge remains, their patterns of care absorbed into the springs that feed the hall, their essence distributed among the countless small lives they tended.

New Watchers are born from the water itself when the hall requires them—rising like thoughts taking form, condensing from mist and shadow and need into their first tentative shapes. They emerge already knowing their purpose, their first breath drawn in service to the vast, quiet symphony they will spend their strange lives conducting.

They are the hollow temple's dreams made flesh, its intentions given form—not quite alive in any way that could be measured, but more truly living than anything that merely breathes.
