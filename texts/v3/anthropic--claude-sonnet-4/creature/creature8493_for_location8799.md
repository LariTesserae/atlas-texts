**The Keth-Morans**

They move like water itself—fluid, unhurried, reading the rhythms of their vertical world in ways outsiders never master. The people of Keth-Mora have adapted to their impossible city over generations, their bodies shaped by constant navigation of swaying bridges and slick stone paths. Their hands are strong and calloused, fingers long and sensitive for finding purchase on wet flowstone. Their feet are bare more often than not, toes splayed wide for gripping the moss-cushioned terraces.

Their skin holds a subtle green undertone, not quite human pale nor truly verdant, as if the eternal filtered light has slowly tinted them. Hair grows thick and often bears small flowers or leaves caught from the overhanging gardens—decorations that are part accident, part fashion. They smell of stone and growing things, of mineral water and the sweet decay of abundant life.

The Keth-Morans speak in whispers when they speak at all, their voices roughened by the constant moisture. But mostly they have developed an intricate sign language that flows like dance, hands weaving meanings through the green air. Children learn to read the subtle grammar of finger-flutter and palm-turn before they can properly walk the treacherous bridges. Their signs are beautiful, incorporating the shapes of local flora—the unfurling of ferns, the droop of heavy fruit, the cascade of falling water.

They are gardener-builders, tending the city as much as inhabiting it. Each family maintains their section of wall, guiding the growth of vines and the formation of new flowstone. They know which mineral deposits will calcify into stable bridges and which pools will yield the sweetest water. Their tools are half-implement, half-plant: staffs wrapped with climbing vines for leverage, bowls grown from gourds trained to specific shapes, rope woven from strong canyon fibers.

The children are born climbers, as comfortable hanging upside-down from a vine bridge as walking on level ground. They play games that would terrify outsiders—leaping between hanging gardens, racing up near-vertical walls, diving through curtains of falling water. By adolescence, they can navigate the entire city with their eyes closed, reading their path through touch and sound and the shift of air currents.

The Keth-Morans dress practically in layers that can be shed or added as they move between the steamy canyon floor and the cooler upper reaches. Their clothes are woven from canyon fibers and dyed with mineral stains—deep greens, earth browns, and the occasional brilliant flash of flower-bright color. Everything is made to dry quickly and resist the constant damp.

They are traders and craftspeople, their goods prized by the outside world for their unique beauty. They carve vessels from the living flowstone while it's still soft, creating pieces that continue to grow and change after purchase. Their woven goods incorporate living elements—belts that bloom, blankets that release the scent of mountain flowers when warmed by body heat.

But perhaps most remarkably, they have become part of the canyon's ecosystem in ways beyond the merely practical. They serve as gardeners to the vertical wilderness, their cultivation having guided the development of new species found nowhere else. Some of the fruit-bearing vines exist only because of careful human breeding over generations. Certain flowers bloom in response to their caretakers' presence, opening when approached by familiar hands.

The Keth-Morans have also developed an almost supernatural ability to predict the canyon's moods. They can sense when new flowstone formations will emerge, when certain bridges of living vine are growing weak, when the mineral content of the weeping walls is changing in ways that might affect the growth patterns. This knowledge is passed down through family lines like precious heirlooms, each generation adding to the accumulated wisdom of reading stone and water and growing things.

In the evening, when what little direct sunlight penetrates the canyon has faded, they gather in the moss-deadened alcoves where conversation is possible. Here, in soft voices, they share the day's observations—which pools have grown warmer, where new ferns have appeared, how the bird populations are shifting with the seasons. They are chroniclers of their vertical world, maintaining an oral tradition that maps every change in their ever-changing city.

There are perhaps two hundred of them in total, living in a density that should be claustrophobic but somehow isn't—they have learned to exist in three dimensions like the plants they tend, using every level of their narrow world. They are neither hermits nor isolationists; traders visit regularly, and some young Keth-Morans venture out into the wider world. But invariably, they return, drawn back by the song of falling water and the embrace of walls you can touch from either side.

They are a people who have learned to thrive in impossible space, who have made abundance from constraint, who have become something new while remaining utterly human—gardeners of a city that gardens itself, inhabitants of a place where the boundary between living and dwelling has dissolved entirely.
