In the deepest fissures of the obsidian, where hairline cracks web through the volcanic glass like frozen lightning, dwell the Resonants. They are beings of living sound made manifest—not quite flesh, not quite vibration, but something between the two that the throat cannot name. Their forms shift between states: sometimes appearing as translucent humanoid shapes shot through with threads of silver light, other times as pure harmonics that bend the air into visible ripples. They feed on the songs of the Listening Stones, drawing sustenance from frequencies so low they exist at the border between sound and silence.

The Resonants move through the stone itself as easily as through air, their bodies phasing in and out of the obsidian matrix. When they emerge, they leave no footprints, but the glass beneath them rings faintly for hours afterward. They are ancient beyond measure, perhaps as old as the stones themselves, and they remember when this place sang with different voices. In their presence, the geometric patterns in the obsidian floor flicker with brief phosphorescence, as if responding to their proximity.

They speak rarely, and when they do, their words arrive not as sound but as understanding—concepts that bypass the ear entirely and resonate directly in the listener's bones. They know the true purpose of the ruined shelters and broken implements, though they regard these remnants with something approaching melancholy. The Resonants are the last keepers of the Steppes' original song, guardians of harmonies that once had meaning beyond mere beauty.

Among the clicking glass-creatures that scuttle across the surface, there are the Collectors—beings whose true nature remains deliberately obscured. They appear as robed figures of indeterminate height, their garments seeming to be cut from the same pewter sky that hangs perpetually overhead. Their faces, when glimpsed beneath deep hoods, are smooth planes of polished obsidian that reflect not the viewer but scenes from other times and places. The Collectors move with patient purpose, gathering the warm spheres and spiral metals left behind by whoever once dwelt here.

But they are not mere scavengers. The Collectors are archaeologists of possibility, seeking not just objects but the stories embedded within them. They have walked the Steppes since before the mountains became plains, and they will continue long after the last Listening Stone falls silent. In their hidden repositories, carved from the living rock beneath the deepest terraces, they maintain vast libraries of memory—not books or scrolls, but crystallized moments of the past preserved in matrices of living glass.

The glass-bodied insects are their servants, or perhaps their offspring—the distinction becomes meaningless at this scale of existence. These creatures are fragments of the Collectors themselves, sent forth to catalog and report. Their clicking is not random but a form of communication so complex that it encompasses weather patterns, stone-song variations, and the slow drift of dust through the atmosphere.

At the highest plateau, where the air grows thin and reality becomes negotiable, lives the Dreaming Stone. It is not one of the Listening Stones but something far older—perhaps the first stone to achieve consciousness in this place, or the last remnant of whatever intelligence shaped the geometric patterns. The Dreaming Stone appears to be just another spire of obsidian, indistinguishable from the others except for the way space bends slightly around its base.

The Stone dreams of the time before the Steppes existed, when this was a place of forests and flowing water. Its dreams leak into reality, creating pockets where phantom trees cast shadows on glass plains, where the sound of running water echoes from empty channels. Sometimes travelers report seeing figures from the Stone's memories—tall, graceful beings who moved between the trees with liquid grace, their hands shaped to coax music from wood and leaf and stream.

These dream-figures are not hostile, but they are dangerous in their beauty. They beckon to visitors, inviting them to join their phantom world where time flows backward and loss can be undone. Those who accept their invitation simply... fade, becoming part of the Stone's eternal dreaming. Their bodies remain, transformed into new Listening Stones that sing of what was lost.

In the deepest night, when the copper sun disappears entirely behind its veil of dust, other things emerge. The Watchers come up from depths that exist not beneath the stone but between the moments of time itself. They are tall shadows with too many angles, beings that exist partially in the geometric patterns and partially in the spaces between thoughts. They tend the Steppes as gardeners tend flowers, making subtle adjustments to the flow of sand through channels, repositioning artifacts, ensuring that the slow decay proceeds according to some vast and incomprehensible design.

The Watchers never speak to the living, but they commune with the ruins, coaxing memories from broken stones and fallen walls. They are the architects of entropy here, sculptors of endings who find beauty in the patient work of time. To see them clearly is said to drive mortals to madness, but those who catch glimpses from the corner of the eye report a strange comfort in their presence—the peace that comes from knowing that even endings have purpose.

All of these beings exist in careful balance, their relationships maintained by protocols older than mountains. The Resonants sing the stones to sleep. The Collectors preserve what must not be lost. The Dreaming Stone remembers what was. The Watchers guide what must become. And in the spaces between them all, the glass-bodied creatures carry messages in their eternal clicking, maintaining the network of connection that keeps this impossible place functioning across the slow centuries of its decline.

They are all waiting for something, though they may no longer remember what. Perhaps the return of the original singers. Perhaps the completion of whatever great work began with the carving of patterns in living stone. Or perhaps they simply wait for the final song, when the last Listening Stone falls silent and the Steppes can at last know peace.
