# The Inhabitants of the Stillwater Rookery

## The Chorus Eternal

The birds are not quite what they appear to be from a distance. Yes, there are gulls and terns and cormorants—recognizable species that cry out with familiar voices—but look closer, and wrongness creeps in at the edges. Some of the gulls have too many joints in their wings, allowing them to fold their appendages into impossible origami configurations as they settle into their eternal positions. Others sport feathers that don't quite catch the light correctly, as if they're made of something that only resembles down and quill.

The cormorants dive and freeze, suspended in their hunting posture for decades at a time, but their eyes continue to move—tracking invisible fish through the motionless water with the patient intensity of creatures who have all eternity to wait for the perfect strike. When they do occasionally shift position, it's with a fluid grace that seems to bypass the intervening space entirely, simply appearing in their new stance without the journey between.

Among the more familiar species nest the endemic birds of the Rookery—creatures found nowhere else, evolved or perhaps created by the temporal suspension itself. The Echoing Herons stand twice as tall as their mundane cousins, their necks extending and contracting like telescopes as they peer into the water's depths. Their calls arrive several seconds after their beaks open, the sound traveling through layers of frozen time to reach the ear. When multiple Echoing Herons call at once, their voices weave together in complex temporal harmonies, creating melodies that seem to fold in on themselves.

The Mist Swallows are perhaps the strangest—tiny birds that seem to be constructed entirely from the rising water vapor, their forms constantly shifting between solid flesh and wispy condensation. They nest in the geometric moss patterns, their young hatching as little more than thoughts of birds before gradually accreting enough substance to take flight. Their songs sound like distant wind chimes, and they feed on the suspended droplets of mist, growing fatter and more real with each ethereal meal.

## The Persisters

But it would be wrong to assume the human inhabitants are truly gone. They're still here, in a fashion—woven so deeply into the Rookery's temporal fabric that they've become something else entirely. The Stillwater Folk, as they might have called themselves once, linger in the spaces between moments.

You might glimpse them sometimes: a shadow moving behind the birds on a distant rooftop, the suggestion of a figure mending nets that were broken centuries ago, hands that appear briefly in the water before dissolving back into reflection. They exist in the margins of the frozen moment, going about their daily routines in a time that no longer moves forward. A woman eternally casting her line into water that will never ripple, her face serene with the patience of someone who knows the fish will always be just about to bite. A child forever reaching for a dropped toy that hovers inches below the water's surface, their laughter caught mid-breath in their throat.

The Persisters retain their forms only when observed peripherally. Look directly at one, and they scatter like mist, only to reform moments later in another location. They've learned to exist in the spaces that eyes don't quite focus on, in the gaps between attention. Sometimes they'll brush against a living visitor with fingers that feel like cool air, trying to communicate through touch what voices can no longer express.

They tend the Rookery still, in their way. The geometric moss patterns grow according to their guidance, invisible hands shaping living fractals that serve as perches and nesting sites for the stranger birds. They move the boats sometimes—visitors will swear a coracle was in one place yesterday, only to find it fifty yards away today, still bearing its cargo of roosting birds, as if the vessel had been gently relocated by caretakers who no longer cast shadows.

## The Watchers

In the deepest parts of the flooded settlement, where the water reaches chest-high and the oldest buildings stand, dwell the Tide Keepers. These were once the settlement's leaders, shamans, or perhaps simply those unlucky enough to be standing in the wrong place when the temporal freeze occurred. They've become something like living statues, their bodies partially submerged and perfectly still, their faces turned upward toward the unchanging sky.

The Tide Keepers breathe once every few hours—a slow, deliberate inhalation that draws mist into their lungs and releases it in perfect spirals. Their eyes never close, and they track every movement in the Rookery with ancient awareness. They remember the moment before the suspension, and they're waiting—have been waiting for centuries—for the moment that will release it. Visitors sensitive to such things report that approaching a Tide Keeper feels like standing next to a dam that's about to burst, all that collected time pressing against the barriers of the present moment.

Birds nest in their hair and on their shoulders, treating them like particularly reliable perches. The Keepers don't seem to mind; in fact, there's evidence they've developed a symbiotic relationship with their feathered tenants. The birds bring them gossip from other parts of the Rookery, and in return, the Keepers provide perfect stability and shelter. Sometimes, very rarely, a Tide Keeper will speak—their voice emerging like distant thunder, words stretched across hours of time: "The... flood... comes... soon..."

## The New Arrivals

The temporal suspension has begun to attract creatures from outside the normal flow of time. Chronophages—beings that feed on temporal distortions—have discovered the Rookery and taken up residence among the ruins. They appear as shifting, half-transparent shapes that might be mistaken for unusual birds if glimpsed quickly. But they have too many angles, and they cast shadows that move independently of their bodies.

The Chronophages feed on the frozen moments themselves, delicately harvesting droplets of suspended time like nectar from flowers. Where they've fed heavily, small pockets of normal time have been restored—a single ripple might expand across a few inches of water surface before running into the greater temporal wall and stopping abruptly. The Persisters seem to tolerate them, perhaps even welcome them, sensing that the Chronophages might eventually consume enough suspended time to free the Rookery from its eternal moment.

But for now, they're simply another layer of inhabitant in this impossible ecosystem, adding their own alien cries to the endless cacophony. Their voices sound like breaking glass played in reverse, and they sing only during the brief periods when the mist forms particularly complex patterns—as if they're reading music written in water vapor and responding with songs that make the geometric moss pulse with bioluminescent light.

The Rookery endures, tended by its strange community of the living, the half-living, and the never-quite-dead, all united in their patient wait for time to begin moving again—or perhaps, after so many centuries, hoping that it never will.
