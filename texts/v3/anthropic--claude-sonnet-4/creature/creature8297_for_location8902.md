In the spaces between the drumbeats, where sound becomes almost-silence before the next resonant pulse, they move.

The Tuned are what remain of those who came to listen and never left. Once, perhaps centuries ago, perhaps millennia, they were something more recognizably human—pilgrims or scholars or simply wanderers drawn by rumors of the endless music. But the rhythms have worked their way into their very bones now, restructured their being around the deep percussion that never stops. Their forms have grown elongated, stretched like shadows at dusk, all angles and flowing lines that echo the weathered contours of the stone formations themselves.

They are tall—impossibly tall—seven feet, eight feet, their exact height difficult to determine because they seem to exist slightly out of phase with normal perception, flickering in and out of visibility like heat mirages. Their skin has taken on the gray-green patina of aged stone, mottled with patches of moss that grows directly from their flesh in symbiotic partnership. When they move, which they do with deliberate, measured steps timed to the drum rhythm, tiny spores drift from these living gardens on their bodies, seeding new growth in the perpetual moisture.

Their eyes have become pools—literally. Where human eyes once looked out, there are now small tarns, dark and perfectly circular, that reflect the impossible brightness of the sky. These water-eyes never blink, but they ripple with emotion, sending tiny waves across their surfaces when something moves them to what might once have been called feeling. Sometimes, in the depths of these liquid gazes, shadows of fish move—small, translucent things that might be memories of thoughts, swimming lazy circles in the aqueous chambers of consciousness.

The Tuned do not speak, but they sing—wordless harmonies that weave between the drum pulses, creating melodies that have been building and elaborating for generations. Each one carries a different part of an impossibly complex composition, their voices ranging from sub-audible bass notes that vibrate in your chest to crystalline overtones that seem to come from the moisture in the air itself. They remember every variation, every improvisation their predecessors added to the eternal song, carrying forward a musical tradition that grows more intricate with each passing season.

Their hands are the most changed part of them—fingers stretched long and thin as willow branches, with too many joints that bend in directions that shouldn't be possible. These manipulated digits can read the stone drums like instruments, feeling for the precise points where a touch will modify the resonance, adjusting the pitch by fractions of tones. They tend the drums as gardeners tend flowers, cleaning away accumulated debris, ensuring the chambers remain acoustically perfect, sometimes spending days repositioning a single stone to achieve the desired harmonic.

They have no need for shelter, for the mist sustains them as it sustains the moss. Their bodies have learned to photosynthesize in the perpetual bright twilight, drawing energy directly from the saturated air. But they are not solitary creatures—they gather at certain times in the lunar cycle around the largest drum-circles, standing in perfect formation as if they were part of the stone arrangement itself, their breathing synchronized to create standing waves in the mist.

The red deer and mountain hares show no fear of them, sometimes even brushing against their moss-covered legs as they pass. The ravens seem to regard them as features of the landscape—useful perches when needed, sources of the small insects and spores that live in their mobile gardens. There is an understanding between all the inhabitants of the moors, a recognition that they all serve the same vast musical purpose.

But perhaps most remarkably, the Tuned remember. Not as humans remember, with linear narratives and discrete events, but as the landscape remembers—in layers, in harmonics, in the slow accumulation of change. They can read the history of the moors in the shifting patterns of drum-song, interpreting variations in rhythm as others might read text. They know which storms carved which new chambers in the stone, which seasons brought changes in the moss patterns, which years the aurora appeared in the impossible bright sky and added their own ethereal counterpoint to the endless percussion.

Occasionally, very rarely, new listeners arrive on the moors. The Tuned watch these newcomers with their rippling tarn-eyes, curious but patient. Some leave after a few days, driven away by the relentless rhythm or the disorienting light. But some stay longer, sitting for hours beside the drum-stones, and these the Tuned begin to sing to softly, wordless lullabies that ease the transformation, teaching their bodies to find the pulse, their minds to let go of the tyranny of linear time.

For the Tuned understand what the moors have always known—that music this ancient, this foundational, changes everything it touches. They are not prisoners of the rhythm but its willing servants, as essential to the soundscape as the stones themselves. They have become living tuning forks, their very existence helping to maintain the perfect acoustic balance that keeps this impossible orchestra playing its eternal song.

In the deepest part of night, when the mist grows thickest and the drumbeats seem to come from inside your own heart, you might glimpse them in their most essential form—not quite human anymore, not quite landscape feature, but something perfectly between: creatures of living music, gardeners of sound, the consciousness that allows the Thundering Moors to hear their own ancient song.
