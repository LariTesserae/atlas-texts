The Keth'miran have lived so long within the breathing stone that their footsteps have worn grooves into pathways, their hands have polished handholds to silk, their very presence has shaped the mesa as surely as water shapes riverbeds. They are a people of gradual movement and eternal patience, their lives measured not in seasons but in the slow cycle of stone-sweat and the deeper rhythms of the mesa's underground streams.

Their skin holds the pale luminescence of filtered sunlight, taking on the faint mineral sheen of the limestone they live within. The children are quicksilver—small and lithe, with elongated fingers perfect for gripping stone, their eyes large and dark to catch every reflection of light from the water pools. They navigate the vertical city with an ease that seems almost gravity-defying, reading the stone's texture like others might read faces, knowing instinctively which surfaces will hold their weight and which conceal the entrance to hidden chambers.

The adults move with the measured deliberation of people who understand that haste achieves nothing in a place where the rock itself sets the pace. The women often wear their hair in complex braids threaded with small carved stones—each stone marking a significant moment in the maintenance of their particular section of the settlement. Their clothing is woven from the silk of cave-dwelling spiders and the down of the cliff swallows, dyed in subtle earth tones that shift color in different lights like the stone itself.

The men have developed an extraordinary sensitivity to water—they can detect by touch the health of a seeping wall, predict by the sound of dripping when a channel needs clearing. Their hands are perpetually damp from their work with the settlement's circulatory system, and they move through the levels with the quiet confidence of people who understand that the city lives only as long as the water flows properly.

The elders are perhaps the most remarkable, for they have begun to physically merge with the settlement itself. Their joints have calcified in ways that echo the stone's natural formations, and they spend their final decades positioned near the most important water sources, becoming living monitors of the mesa's health. When they finally die, their bodies are sealed into alcoves within the stone, where the mineral-rich water slowly transforms them into part of the mesa's structure—honored ancestors who literally become the bones of the city.

Among them move the Listeners—individuals born with an unusual sensitivity to the mesa's deep rhythms. They can hear the underground streams changing course, feel the minute settling of stone that might threaten a dwelling, predict the severity of the seasonal rains by placing their palms against certain walls. The Listeners are both guides and guardians, their warnings heeded absolutely, their judgment trusted in all matters relating to the settlement's relationship with the living rock.

The settlement also shelters the Carved Ones—people whose bodies bear the ritual scarification that maps the mesa's hidden water channels. Each line and curve on their skin corresponds to a passage or cistern within the stone, making them living archives of the settlement's most crucial knowledge. In times of crisis, when channels become blocked or new sources must be found, the Carved Ones serve as guides through the mesa's interior labyrinths.

Children are born into a complex web of relationships—not just with their biological families, but with their level-families, water-families, and stone-families. Each child learns to tend a particular section of wall-weeping, to care for specific animals, to maintain certain pathways. Their education unfolds over decades, as they slowly acquire the intricate knowledge needed to read the mesa's moods and needs.

The animals that share this vertical world have become almost as specialized as their human neighbors. The cliff swallows have developed an unusually sophisticated relationship with the inhabitants—different families of birds nesting in specific areas, their movements serving as early warning systems for changes in weather or the structural integrity of the rock. The small mammals have learned to time their appearances to the humans' daily routines, emerging to drink when the water pools are freshest, disappearing into hidden passages that even the human inhabitants don't fully map.

In the deepest chambers of the mesa live the Deepmost—elderly Keth'miran who have chosen to spend their final years in the settlement's heart, tending the great cisterns and the most ancient water channels. They rarely emerge into daylight, their eyes having adapted to read the subtle variations in darkness, their hands grown extraordinarily sensitive to the mesa's underground currents. They speak in whispers that carry strangely through the stone passages, and younger inhabitants sometimes make pilgrimages to consult them about matters of great importance.

The Keth'miran have no written language as such—instead, they carve their history and knowledge into the stone itself, creating an ever-evolving text that spreads throughout the settlement. Important decisions are marked by new carvings, significant events commemorated by subtle alterations to existing passages. The city itself becomes their library, their constitution, their collective memory—a living document that grows and changes with each generation while remaining as permanent as stone.

In the highest reaches of the settlement dwell the Skyreaders—those whose role is to watch the distant horizons and the changing patterns of weather, but also to maintain the settlement's connection to the world beyond the mesa. They are often the most far-sighted of the inhabitants, their eyes adapted to long distances, their patience legendary even among a patient people. They track the movements of distant storms across the steppes, predict the arrival of the seasonal rains, and watch for the rare travelers who might approach across the vast landscape.

At night, when the stone releases the day's absorbed warmth and the water sounds grow more pronounced in the cooling air, the entire settlement seems to exhale. The Keth'miran gather in the broader terraces, their quiet conversations mixing with the eternal music of dripping water. Children curl up in alcoves carved specifically for sleep, while adults check the water levels one final time before rest. The mesa settles around them like a vast, protective organism, holding them safe within its patient embrace until dawn filters once again through the carved windows and another day begins in the slow rhythm of stone and water and time.
