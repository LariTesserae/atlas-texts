The **Seepage Folk** have made this district their domain—a guild of artisans whose bodies bear the mineral stains of their calling like badges of mastery. Their skin carries a subtle metallic sheen, not quite human in its luster, accumulated through years of working with the mountain's iron-rich weeping. The most senior among them sport elaborate rust-bloom patterns across their hands and forearms, intricate as lacework, prized as marks of experience. They dress in leather aprons that have absorbed so many mineral traces they've become living maps of the district's water flow, each stain a record of a particular spring or seepage point.

The Seepage Folk move with the patient, methodical rhythm of their adopted landscape. They speak in measured cadences that echo the chunk-hiss of the steam engines, their conversations punctuated by long, comfortable silences. Their guild hierarchy follows the water's path: apprentices work the lower terraces where the seepage first emerges, learning to read the mountain's moods in the color and flow of each spring, while masters claim the upper reaches where the most complex mineral concentrations require the finest touch.

They are joined by the **Steam Shepherds**—a loose confederation of engineers and tinkerers who tend the district's mechanical heartbeat. These folk have developed an almost supernatural ability to diagnose problems by sound alone. They walk the streets with heads cocked, listening to variations in the engine rhythms that would be imperceptible to outsiders. Many have modified their hearing through careful exposure to the harmonic frequencies of well-tuned machinery, claiming they can hear the mountain itself breathing through the network of pipes and channels. Their workshops cluster around the primary steam outlets, where they've learned to harness not just the mechanical power but the mountain's own geothermal gifts.

In the spaces between industry, the **Threshold Dwellers** have carved out their own existence. These are the descendants of the district's original construction crews who chose to stay, adapting to life in the perpetual damp. They've developed a culture around transience and impermanence—their homes are engineered to be easily moved as seepage patterns shift or new industrial needs arise. They raise families in structures that can be dismantled in a day and rebuilt by the following sunset, teaching their children to read the mountain's intentions in the subtle changes of steam density and mineral taste.

The children themselves form packs that navigate the district with feral efficiency, their bare feet hardened to the warm, damp stone. They hunt the clouds of insects with improvised nets, turning pest into protein with casual expertise. These young ones know every hidden passage where the water runs warm enough for bathing, every overlooked niche where mountain birds nest. They speak a pidgin of technical jargon inherited from their parents and the secret language of places where adult concerns don't reach.

Among the machinery, families of **Iron-Touched** maintain the district's more delicate mechanisms. Generations of working with mineral-rich water and metal have left them with an intuitive understanding of oxidation and preservation. Their touch can slow rust, their breath can fog metal in patterns that reveal stress points invisible to normal eyes. They intermarry almost exclusively with each other, believing that diluting their gift would be a betrayal of both craft and calling.

The district's **Water Witches** form the closest thing to a governing body—elderly women and men who remember the first springs and can predict, with uncanny accuracy, where new seepages will emerge. They carry carved staffs of mountain stone that weep mineral water from hidden reservoirs, tools both practical and ceremonial. When disputes arise about water rights or territorial boundaries, the Water Witches adjudicate by reading the patterns of stain and flow, their decisions accepted because everyone knows the mountain speaks through them more clearly than through law or custom.

In the heights where the stone is newest and sharpest, hermit **Stone Listeners** have taken up residence in caves barely larger than coffins. They claim to hear the mountain's dreams in the settling and shifting of fresh-cut rock. Some say they're mad from breathing too much mineral-laden steam; others insist they're the only ones who truly understand what the district is becoming. They emerge occasionally to offer cryptic warnings about structural integrity or to guide rescue efforts when someone becomes lost in the maze of channels and foundations.

Finally, there are the **Understudies**—migrants and refugees drawn by rumors of steady work and clean water, who arrive expecting familiar urban rhythms and instead find themselves inducted into this strange marriage of mountain and machinery. Some adapt, their bodies and expectations gradually reshaping to match the district's demands. Others move on, carrying stories that ensure the foundry district's reputation grows in ways both accurate and wildly embellished.

Together, these overlapping communities have created something unprecedented: a society that treats the living mountain as both partner and employer, where human ambition has learned to flow along the channels that geology provides, and where the rhythm of daily life matches the patient, endless breathing of stone and steam.
