In this perpetual twilight between stone and sea, the Vaelthari have become something neither fully land-dwelling nor aquatic, but something altogether new. Their bodies tell the story of countless generations spent in the eternal embrace of warm, mineral-laden waters.

Their skin has taken on a subtle translucency, pale as cave pearls with an underlying luminescence that mirrors the phosphorescent algae they tend. Generations of diving through flooded passages have enlarged their lung capacity until they can hold their breath for twenty minutes or more, their ribcages expanded like those of deep-water pearlers. Their eyes have grown large and dark-adapted, pupils dilated permanently to catch every flicker of bioluminescent light. When they surface into the brighter pools, they often wear carved shell visors or simply squint behind their long, dark lashes.

Their fingers and toes have lengthened, webbed with thick membranes that can fold tight when gripping stone or spread wide when swimming. Calluses from generations of stone-carving have created intricate patterns on their palms and fingertips—raised ridges that help them read carved surfaces in absolute darkness. The eldest among them can run their hands over a wall and recite its history, feeling the subtle differences in chisel work that mark different craftsmen, different eras.

Children are born already adapted to this amphibious life. They swim before they walk, their mothers nursing them in shallow pools while the warm current rocks them to sleep. By adolescence, their ear canals have developed specialized membranes that seal against water pressure while still allowing them to hear the subtle acoustic cues they use for navigation. They communicate as much through clicks of the tongue and taps of stone against stone as they do through whispered words—sound carries differently through water-heavy air, and silence is often more valuable than speech.

The Vaelthari have divided into subtle castes based on their relationship to the depths. The Brightkeepers tend the luminescent gardens in the upper terraces, their skin retaining more pigment from regular exposure to the filtered light. They cultivate strains of moss and algae, crossbreeding them for maximum luminescence, and their fingernails often glow faintly green from handling phosphorescent cultures. The Deepsingers work in the flooded chambers far below, their pale forms ghostly as they carve new passages by feel alone. Their voices have developed an uncanny ability to read the acoustics of flooded spaces—they can sing a note and tell you the exact dimensions of a chamber, the texture of its walls, whether its passages are clear or blocked.

Between them are the Currentkeepers, the engineers and architects who maintain the endless flow systems that are the city's lifeblood. Their arms and shoulders have grown powerful from hauling stone and working wooden pulleys in the humid air. They know every channel, every sluice gate, every place where the water gathers speed or slows to a contemplative trickle. They sleep to the sound of specific flows, and can wake instantly when something changes in their assigned section's rhythm.

The elderly among the Vaelthari continue working until their bodies simply merge with the place they've spent their lives shaping. Their bones, heavy with accumulated minerals from the waters, eventually calcify into living extensions of the carved walls. Some choose to end their days in meditation pools in the deepest chambers, allowing the phosphorescent algae to colonize their skin until they become living lanterns, their slow breathing the only sign they haven't yet become part of the stone itself.

There are stories—whispered in the deeper chambers where sound travels strangely—of Vaelthari who have gone entirely into the depths, who swim in the flooded caverns below the city where larger things than pale fish move through the darkness. These Deep Ones supposedly tend gardens of bioluminescent coral in chambers that haven't seen air in a thousand years, their forms so changed by pressure and time that they're barely recognizable as human. Whether they're ancestors or descendants, guardians or prisoners of their own transformation, no one can say. But sometimes, in the deepest pools, children claim to see shapes that might be faces looking up at them through the glowing water, expressions patient as stone, wise as the slowly rising sea.

The Vaelthari as a whole move with fluid grace, their gestures economical and precise. They waste nothing—not motion, not breath, not the faintest glimmer of light. They are sculptors of water and time, patient as the mineral deposits that slowly transform their bodies, inexorable as the tide that claimed their mountain and gave them, in return, a world where the boundary between self and city, between flesh and flowing stone, grows thinner with each passing year.
