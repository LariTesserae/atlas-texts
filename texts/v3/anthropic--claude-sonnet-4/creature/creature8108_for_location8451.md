**The Listeners**

They are shaped by water and time as surely as the stone itself.

The Listeners move through their vertical city with a fluid grace that speaks of generations spent navigating wet stone and curved passages. Their feet are broad and sure, with thick, calloused soles that grip the perpetually damp surfaces without slipping. Their skin has taken on the honeyed hue of the volcanic rock, marked with the same rust-colored streaks—not painted or tattooed, but a gradual staining that comes from constant contact with the mineral-rich water that weeps from every surface. Children are born pale as cave flowers, but by adolescence their skin shows the first faint traceries of stone-color, and the eldest among them seem carved from the same rock as their homes.

Their ears are their most remarkable feature—larger than seems natural, delicately curved and mobile, capable of subtle adjustments that allow them to read the water's voice like a written language. A shift in tone warns of a blocked channel three levels up. A change in rhythm signals the need to adjust flow patterns before the morning rains arrive. They can distinguish between the footfalls of every individual in the settlement by the way water droplets scatter from their passing. In the deepest chambers, the most gifted among them claim to hear the mountain's heartbeat—the slow pulse of thermal springs far below, the stress patterns in stone that predict where new channels might be carved.

They speak little aloud, finding verbal language crude beside the water's infinite vocabulary. When they do speak, their voices are soft and precisely modulated, trained from childhood to add harmony rather than discord to the settlement's acoustic environment. Children learn early to whisper, and by adulthood most communication happens through subtle gestures, the placement of stones in channels to create specific tones, or the cultivation of particular plants whose leaves rustle with meaningful patterns when touched.

The Listeners' hands are their tools—long-fingered and remarkably sensitive, capable of reading texture and temperature variations in stone that would be invisible to others. They work constantly but never hurriedly, always following the mountain's own pace. A single alcove might take a lifetime to complete, passed from parent to child like a meditation practice. They wear no jewelry that might interfere with their work, no clothing that rustle or scrape. Their garments are woven from plant fibers that grow naturally in the settlement, dyed soft greens and browns with minerals from the water itself.

Each family tends specific channels, specific chambers, their knowledge passed down through generations of careful observation. The oldest among them have grown almost translucent with age, their veins showing blue-green through skin that seems to hold its own soft light. These elders spend their final years in the deepest resonance chambers, where they claim to commune directly with the stone—listening to stories that have been accumulating in the mountain for centuries, gradually becoming part of that vast, patient narrative themselves.

They eat little—mostly the delicate mushrooms that grow in the settlement's darker corners, supplemented by leaves and shoots from their hanging gardens. Their bodies have adapted to extract maximum nourishment from minimal intake, another way they've shaped themselves to match their austere home. They age slowly but inevitably, wearing down like the stone itself, growing smaller and more concentrated until they seem to become pure essence—all attention, all listening, barely present in the physical world.

The young ones are born amphibious, as comfortable in the deeper pools as on the carved walkways. They learn to swim before they walk, their small bodies perfectly suited to navigating the water-filled chambers where adults must stoop or crawl. By adolescence they begin to specialize—some developing an affinity for the settlement's acoustic engineering, others for the cultivation of the precise plant communities that help regulate moisture and temperature throughout the structure.

When one of the Listeners dies, their body is placed in a special chamber where the water will slowly, gently dissolve it back into the mineral matrix of the mountain. Nothing is wasted; everything returns to the eternal cycle of stone and water and time. Their knowledge lives on in the careful modifications they've made to channels and chambers, in the particular plants they've encouraged to grow in specific locations, in the subtle improvements to the settlement's vast acoustic instrument that each generation builds upon.

They dream in water-music, their sleep filled with the same rhythms that pulse through their days. Even unconscious, they adjust position to avoid disrupting the flow patterns around them. They have become so integrated with their environment that they seem less like inhabitants than like the mountain's own organs of perception—living extensions of the stone's slow awareness, translating its patient thoughts into the language of cultivation and care.
