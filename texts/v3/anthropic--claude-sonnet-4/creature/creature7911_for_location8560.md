In the deepest quiet of the Emerald Hollows dwell the Seep-Keepers, though they are less seen than sensed—a presence that moves through the perpetual dusk like living shadow given substance. Their bodies are elongated and graceful, standing perhaps four feet in height when upright, but they rarely maintain any single form for long. Their skin shifts between the deep brown-green of wet bark and the silvered gray of lichen, not through conscious effort but as naturally as breathing. When they rest against a moss-covered stone or the buttressed base of a tree, they become part of the landscape so completely that even the salamanders pause, uncertain.

The Seep-Keepers move with a liquid patience that matches their realm. They can remain motionless for hours, sometimes days, tending to the delicate balance of water and moss, soil and stone. Their fingers are long and thin, ending in what might be nails or might be small roots—the distinction blurs here, as it does with so much else about them. These digits can detect the slightest changes in water flow, the health of moss colonies, the slow migration of nutrients through the spongy earth.

They communicate, when they communicate at all, through touch—palm to palm, fingertip to moss, hand to water surface. Their language is the transmission of sensation: the memory of last season's flooding, the taste of iron in a new seepage, the satisfaction of a perfectly balanced pool. Sometimes they will stand in a circle around one of the dark tarns, their hands trailing in the water, sharing information that flows between them like the very currents they tend.

The Seep-Keepers are neither entirely individual nor completely collective. They exist in small family groups of three or four, bound together by an intimacy deeper than blood—they share the sensation of the watershed itself, feeling through their bodies the vast network of underground springs and surface pools as a single, breathing organism. When one tends a distant seepage, the others feel the cool touch of water on their own hands.

Their dwellings, if they can be called that, are the stone circles and moss-covered platforms scattered throughout the Hollows. These aren't built so much as encouraged—the Seep-Keepers guide the growth of moss, the settling of stones, the patient accumulation of sediment until the landscape itself provides shelter. They sleep in hollows of their own making, depressions in the moss that conform perfectly to their bodies, sometimes shared with the great salamanders who seem to recognize them as part of the ecosystem's natural order.

Time moves differently for the Seep-Keepers. They measure seasons not by days or months but by the subtle cycles of their realm: the slow expansion and contraction of moss beds, the gradual shift of water levels, the decades-long growth of the great ferns. They may tend the same pool for years, making adjustments so minute they're barely perceptible, ensuring that the delicate chemistry remains perfect for the microscopic life that forms the foundation of their world.

They are the memory-holders of the Hollows, carrying in their long lives the knowledge of how each pool came to be, why certain paths remain clear while others close over with vegetation, which stones were placed by ancient hands and which emerged from the earth's own patient work. They remember the rare visitors who have found their way into the deep places, though they rarely reveal themselves to outsiders—more often, such travelers emerge with only a sense of having been quietly observed, gently guided, and ultimately protected.

The Seep-Keepers reproduce rarely, perhaps once in a generation, and when they do, it is as much a joining with the landscape as with each other. The young emerge from moss cradles already attuned to the water table's rhythms, their first touch not of air but of the cool, mineral-rich seepage that is the lifeblood of their world.

In the deepest, most sacred pools, some say there are Elder Seep-Keepers who have tended their waters for centuries, who have grown so close to the landscape that they exist more as spirits of place than as individual beings. They are felt as a presence in the weight of the humid air, in the perfect stillness of the tannin-dark water, in the way certain clearings feel watched and protected. Whether these are the same beings grown ancient or something the Emerald Hollows itself has dreamed into existence, none can say—and perhaps the distinction no longer matters in a place where boundaries dissolve as gently as mist.
