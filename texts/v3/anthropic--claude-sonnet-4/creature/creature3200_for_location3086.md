In the upper terraces of Kethara live the Keth'moren—the Stone Listeners. They are a people shaped by generations of mountain silence, their forms tall and lean, adapted to the vertical pathways and narrow passages of their city. Their skin carries the pale luminescence of those who live perpetually in mist, with a subtle gray undertone that mirrors the granite around them. Most striking are their hands—long-fingered and extraordinarily sensitive, capable of reading the mountain's moods through the slightest vibrations in stone.

The Keth'moren move with deliberate grace, each step placed with the same precision their ancestors used to set the city's foundation stones. They speak in soft voices that seem to emerge from the mist itself, and their language contains long pauses—spaces where they listen to responses from the mountain that others cannot hear. When they work, carving new channels or tending their impossible gardens, they pause frequently, fingers pressed to stone, heads tilted as if receiving instruction.

Their clothing reflects their environment: woven fabrics in shades of granite and cloud, cut in flowing lines that never catch on the narrow passages. They wear soft shoes of worked leather that make no sound on stone, and many carry small tools of dark metal—chisels and awls that seem to shape stone through gentle persuasion rather than force.

The Keth'moren age differently than other peoples. Rather than growing frail, they become more translucent, more integrated with their mountain home. The eldest among them are barely distinguishable from the mist at first glance, their presence felt more than seen. These ancients serve as the city's memory, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps the cisterns at their perfect level and the gardens in their eternal arrangement.

Children are born rarely but treasured deeply. Each infant arrives already attuned to the mountain's rhythms, sleeping and waking with the mist's rise and fall. They learn to walk on the steep stairs before they can speak in full sentences, and their first words are often responses to stone-whispers only they can hear.

The Keth'moren rarely leave their mountain cities. When they do venture to the valley roads connecting the peaks, they move in small groups, maintaining constant contact with the stone paths beneath their feet. They seem to navigate by an internal compass aligned not with magnetic north but with the deep structures of the mountains themselves.

Among the terraces, each family tends specific gardens and water channels passed down through generations. They know precisely which stones hold morning moisture longest, which vines will shade a particular terrace at mid-day, which fish in the pools are the great-great-grandchildren of fish their great-great-grandparents once knew. This intimate knowledge creates a living map of the city written in muscle memory and whispered tradition.

Their nights are spent in quiet communion with the mountain. They gather in the deeper courtyards where the mist pools thickest, sitting in patient circles around the cisterns. Here they listen to the water's subtle songs and share dreams that arrive not from sleep but from the stone itself—visions of the mountain's slow thoughts, memories of its birth from the earth, previews of changes that might come in centuries yet to unfold.

The Keth'moren possess no written history because they need none. The mountain remembers for them, and they have learned to remember with it. Their past lives in the precisely placed stones, in the eternal arrangement of gardens, in the unchanging patterns of mist and water. They are both the mountain's caretakers and its living memory, a people who have achieved perfect equilibrium with their vertical world.
