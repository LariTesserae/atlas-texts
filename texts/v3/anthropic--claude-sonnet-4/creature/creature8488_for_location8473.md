In this threshold city, the inhabitants have become something between carved and born, shaped by millennia of mist and the mountain's slow exhalations. 

The **Condensers** move through the perpetual twilight like living architecture—their skin has taken on the blue-grey patina of weathered stone, mottled with patches that shimmer faintly when moisture beads upon them. They are tall and angular, their limbs elongated by generations of reaching into high crevices and narrow channels. Their fingers end not in nails but in shallow grooves, evolved to channel water with the same efficiency as the carved gutters they tend. When they stand still—which they do often, for hours at a time—they become indistinguishable from the relief carvings that cover the city's walls, breathing so slowly that moss begins to gather in the hollows of their shoulders and the creases of their robes.

But it is their eyes that mark them as creatures of this impossible place: large, opalescent, adapted to read the subtle variations in mist density that predict weather days in advance. They see in gradients of moisture what others might see in color, watching the mountain's breath paint invisible maps across the air. Their voices, when they speak, carry the hollow resonance of wind through stone passages—conversations that echo between buildings and can be heard three levels up or down, though the speaker may be nowhere visible.

The **Seep-touched** are smaller, more numerous, their bodies compact and rounded like water-worn pebbles. They cluster in the warmer microclimates where thermal springs meet the surface, their skin glistening with a natural oil that beads moisture rather than absorbing it. Children of these people can hold their breath for impossible lengths of time and swim through flooded lower passages that connect distant parts of the city in an aquatic network known only to them. They speak in whispers and clicks, sounds that carry perfectly through water-filled channels but dissipate quickly in open air.

Most mysterious are the **Stone-current**—beings that seem to flow between solid and liquid states. They inhabit the deepest foundations where the city roots itself directly into living rock. Rarely glimpsed, they appear as shifting patterns in the stone itself, suggestions of faces or limbs that resolve and dissolve like figures seen in rushing water. They are the city's true architects, guiding the slow process by which buildings grow rather than are built, nudging thermal expansion and the creep of settling foundations to gradually reshape passages and chambers across decades. When they manifest more fully, they appear as tall, impossibly thin figures whose surfaces ripple with the pattern of flowing water frozen mid-motion.

Smallest but perhaps most essential are the **Breath-shepherds**—creatures no larger than a child's fist, with translucent bodies shot through with veins of silver. They tend the city's respiratory system, crawling through the network of air passages that honeycomb the settlement, ensuring proper circulation and clearing blockages of ice or debris. Their presence is felt more than seen: a sudden warmth in a previously stagnant corner, a fresh current of air where none should flow. They sleep curled in the carved collection basins, their bodies barely distinguishable from drops of mercury-bright condensation.

All of these inhabitants move according to the mountain's rhythms rather than the sun's. They are most active during the deep mists of early morning and evening, when the boundary between air and water grows thin. During the brief, harsh clarity of midday, they retreat to the shadowed passages and interior chambers, waiting for the city to exhale its ghosts again.

They do not build fires—the mountain provides what warmth they need through thermal springs and the slow heat of stone heated by day and released by night. Their food grows in the careful gardens of moss and lichen they tend, supplemented by what they glean from the mist itself: microscopic organisms and minerals that accumulate in their collection systems.

Most remarkably, they seem to share a collective memory encoded in the city's stones. Touch a wall, and the Condensers can tell you which generation carved this passage, what weather patterns shaped this particular groove. The Stone-current remember every adaptation the mountain has made to human habitation, while the Seep-touched know the stories held in every collected pool—tales that flow from basin to basin like an aquatic oral tradition.

They are not a civilization that conquered this hostile place but one that surrendered to it so completely that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has worn away like stone under patient water. They have become the mountain's dreams made manifest, the embodied memory of everything that water has learned about finding its way home.
