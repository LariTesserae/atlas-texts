In this place where physics genuflects to older truths, the Viridians have made their eternal home.

They are not quite gardeners, though they tend. Not quite priests, though they serve. They move through the Deepmost Garden with the fluid deliberation of those who have learned to walk between the spaces where causality grows thin. Once, perhaps eons ago, they were human—or something adjacent to humanity. Now they are translucent as the water-that-defies-itself, their forms shifting between states: sometimes solid enough to disturb the mirror-stones beneath their feet, sometimes so ethereal they pass through the crystalline structures without disturbing the impossible geometries trapped within.

The Viridians number neither seven nor seventy, but something that exists in the spaces between numbers—a quantity that changes based on who counts them, or whether counting is even possible in a place where mathematics occasionally forgets its own rules. They appear as tall, willowy figures draped in robes that might be woven light, might be crystallized time, might be the accumulated whispers of water learning new ways to exist. Their faces are serene masks of polished bone or porcelain, featureless save for dark hollows where eyes should be—hollows that contain not darkness but depth, as if their sight extends not outward but infinitely inward.

Their hands, when visible, are impossibly long and delicate, with fingers that can manipulate the geometric impossibilities suspended in the crystal structures. They reach into spaces that shouldn't exist and adjust angles that fold reality back on itself, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps this pocket of broken physics from either collapsing entirely or expanding to consume everything beyond its borders. When they work, the mineral-water flows in new patterns, the shadows pool differently, and the phosphorescent light shifts in hues that have no names.

They speak, but not with voices. Their communication moves through the saturated air like changes in pressure, like adjustments to the weight of time itself. A Viridian's thought becomes a subtle alteration in the way light bends around the crystal structures, or a new rhythm in the pulsing channels, or a brief shiver in the upward-flowing water. To witness their conversations is to feel reality itself inhaling and exhaling, stretching like a sleeping cat before settling into a new configuration.

Among them moves one who might be their keeper, or might be their memory made manifest. This figure is older than the others—not aged, but deeper, as if they have been worn smooth by more epochs of impossible water. They carry a staff that exists in more dimensions than the others can perceive, its tip occasionally disappearing entirely before returning with fragments of equations that solve for variables that don't yet exist. This keeper tends to the nearly-creatures in the deepest channels, those wisps of almost-life that follow the mineral currents. Under their care, these fragments sometimes coalesce into shapes that might once have been fish, or flowers, or falling stars—but only for moments before dissolving back into potential.

The Viridians do not age, but they do change. Over centuries, some become more crystalline, their forms slowly merging with the structures they tend until only their consciousness remains, suspended like the impossible geometries, observing from within angles that contain eternity. Others become more fluid, eventually joining the upward-flowing water in its endless dance of defying gravity and sense. But they do not die—death requires time to move in only one direction, and here time pools and eddies like everything else.

In the deepest places where the channels converge, there moves something that might once have been the first Viridian, or perhaps the garden itself given form. It appears as a shadow that casts light, a presence so saturated with the weight of accumulated impossibilities that space bends around it like fabric around a stone. This being—if being is the right word—remembers when water first forgot how to be water, when the laws of physics first grew tired and began to dream. It tends not to the garden's growth but to its forgetting, ensuring that this pocket of reality continues to slip gracefully away from the constraints that govern the world beyond.

The Viridians move through their eternal cultivation with a patience that spans geological ages. They are witnesses to the slow dance of entropy and impossibility, shepherds of a space where beauty exists not despite the breakdown of natural law but because of it. In their care, the Deepmost Garden continues its quiet revolution against the tyranny of making sense, each impossible cascade of upward water a prayer to mysteries that predate the very concept of mystery itself.

And sometimes—perhaps once in a millennium, perhaps once every time someone tries to count them—a new Viridian appears, emerging from the crystal structures like light through a prism, or condensing from the phosphorescent air like dew. They arrive already knowing their purpose, already translucent with the accumulated weight of impossible time, ready to take their place in the eternal tending of a garden that grows not plants or flowers but the very concept of what it means for something to exist beautifully, impossibly, forever.
