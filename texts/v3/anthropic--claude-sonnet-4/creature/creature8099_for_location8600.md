In the Settled Hollow dwell the **Vethani**—the Deep Dwellers—a people whose bodies and souls have been shaped by countless generations in the breathing stone.

They are smaller than their surface ancestors, compact and sturdy, with broad shoulders and powerful hands scarred smooth by stonework. Their skin has taken on the pallor of cave fish, though it carries a subtle luminescence—not bright enough to see by, but noticeable in the deeper shadows, a faint pearl-sheen that comes from drinking the mineral-rich waters and breathing the humid air thick with stone-dust. Their eyes are large and dark, pupils permanently dilated, capable of gathering the faintest glimmers of reflected lamplight. When they blink, their eyelids sweep across like shutters, protecting their sensitive vision from the rare bright torch-flame.

The Vethani move with the fluid certainty of water finding its course. They navigate the spiral walkways without conscious thought, their feet reading the worn grooves in the stone, their hands trailing along familiar walls. They have developed an uncanny ability to judge distances by sound alone—the precise delay between a footstep and its echo tells them exactly where they stand in the vast hollow. Their voices have adapted too, becoming richer and more resonant, trained from childhood to make use of the cavern's acoustics. A whisper in the right spot can carry across the entire city, while a shout in another place might be swallowed by the stone within arm's reach.

But it is in their relationship with water that the Vethani show their deepest adaptation. They can predict rain on the surface by the changing rhythm of seepage three days before it falls. They know which drops carry news from the upper world—traces of pollen, hints of seasonal change, messages from the sky they have not seen in generations. The children learn to read the water-signs before they learn to carve stone: this trickle means the eastern channels are full, that splash warns of flooding in the lower levels, those particular ripples in the central cistern foretell changes in the deep currents below.

The Vethani have organized themselves not by blood or territory, but by sound and rhythm. Families are groups of people whose footsteps complement each other, whose voices harmonize in the great acoustic space. The master stone-carvers are chosen not just for their skill with chisel and hammer, but for their ability to work in time with the eternal percussion of dripping water—their hammering becomes part of the city's symphony rather than a disruption of it.

They have developed a complex relationship with the creatures that share their hollow. The bat-keepers know each of the great colonies by their wingbeat patterns and can predict their movements through the cavern system. Some Vethani have learned to call the salamanders, using them as living messengers to carry small objects through the water-channels. The eyeless creatures of the deepest levels—pale, elongated things that might once have been human—are treated with careful respect. The Vethani leave offerings of mushroom-bread at the threshold between the settled areas and the deep places, and in return, these beings sometimes emerge to repair damage in the unreachable high places, their long arms and finger-sensitive navigation proving invaluable for maintenance work in the eternal darkness above the lamp-light.

Perhaps most remarkably, the Vethani have developed what they call "stone-singing"—a practice somewhere between craft and ritual. Master singers learn to place their voices so precisely within the cavern's acoustics that they can cause specific stones to resonate. They use this ability for both practical and spiritual purposes: locating weaknesses in the rock face, calling the community to gather, or creating the deep harmonic ceremonies that honor the hollow's vast presence.

The Vethani children are born already adapted to their world. They emerge from the womb with enlarged lung capacity for the humid air, and their first sounds blend naturally with the dripping and splashing around them. By the time they can walk, they navigate by echo as much as by sight. They play games that would be impossible on the surface—timing jumps to match the rhythm of falling water, hiding by matching their breathing to the hollow's deep respiratory cycle, learning to sing in harmony not just with each other but with the very stones that surround them.

The elders of the Vethani speak of dreams shared not between people but between the hollow and its inhabitants. They describe sleeping in rhythm with the great stone breathing, feeling their own small dreams merge with something vast and patient and ancient—not quite consciousness as the surface world would recognize it, but an awareness that encompasses all the water-flow and lamp-light, all the footsteps and voices, all the small warm lives that have made themselves part of this underground vastness.

In the deepest ceremonies, when the Vethani extinguish most of their lamps and let the hollow breathe in relative darkness, they say the boundaries between themselves and their city dissolve. They become part of the settling, part of the slow patient work of stone and water, part of the eternal conversation between emptiness and inhabitation, between the weight of earth above and the bubble of life carved carefully, lovingly, into its heart.

They are a people who have learned not to conquer their impossible home, but to join it—to become part of its rhythm and song until there is no clear distinction between the city and its inhabitants, between the hollow and the lives it holds, between the ancient patient stone and the small bright flames that dance across its surface, creating light, creating warmth, creating home in the breathing darkness.
