In this realm of shifting stone and shimmering heat, three peoples have made their home, each as adapted to the aerial dance as the strange creatures that share their sky.

The **Thermari** are the most numerous—tall, lean humans whose skin bears a bronze sheen that seems to absorb and reflect the amber light. Their hair grows in curious patterns, standing in natural ridges and whorls that channel the constant air currents away from their faces. Over generations in the heat-shimmer, their eyes have developed an inner membrane that filters the harsh light and allows them to see clearly through the thermal distortions. They dress in loose, flowing garments woven from processed volcanic glass—fabric that breathes with the air currents and never quite touches their skin, creating its own cooling layer.

The Thermari live in extended family groups scattered across multiple platforms, their households expanding and contracting as the stone fragments drift apart or draw together. They are the engineers and rope-dancers of Keth-Moraan, masters of the cable systems and bridge-builders who can walk a swaying line between platforms with the same casual grace others might show crossing solid ground. Their children learn to read the thermal currents as other young ones might study written words, sensing by subtle changes in air pressure and light refraction when a platform is about to shift, when a new thermal column is building strength, when the stone-swimmers are about to phase through their foundations.

More mysterious are the **Ashkin**—beings who may once have been human but have undergone some profound transformation in the deep furnace-light. Their bodies appear to be composed of living volcanic glass, translucent and dark-veined, with hearts that pulse visibly like glowing coals within their chests. They stand shorter than the Thermari but move with an eerie fluidity, as if their bones bend like heated metal. The Ashkin can withstand temperatures that would kill others, and they alone can work the deep-core mining sites where the platforms connect to active magma flows through crystalline tubes.

The Ashkin speak rarely, and when they do, their voices carry harmonics that resonate through stone itself. They seem to have some communion with the stone-swimmers, often pausing in their work to place glowing palms against the basalt, as if listening to conversations held within the rock. Their dwellings are built into the very hearts of the platforms, carved chambers that glow with their own inner light. They are the deep-crafters, creating tools and ornaments from metals that exist nowhere else—alloys that form only in the intersection of intense heat, specific mineral conditions, and what some whisper is their own life-force, somehow transmuted into the working of stone and flame.

But perhaps strangest of all are the **Drift-touched**—humans who have spent so long among the heat-dancers and crystalline birds that the boundary between their flesh and the strange energies of this place has begun to blur. They appear mostly human at first glance, but their shadows move independently, sometimes separating entirely to scout ahead across impossible gaps or slip through cracks in stone. Their hair flows like liquid light, and their voices can split into harmonic layers, allowing them to sing counter-melodies to themselves.

The Drift-touched serve as guides and negotiators, equally at home in the solid settlements and in the spaces between—those void-pockets where sound dies and light turns cold. They can survive brief exposures to the raw thermal columns, riding them like elevators between levels of the aerial archipelago. Some say they can speak with the heat-dancers in languages of pure light-refraction, bargaining for safe passage or warning of platform shifts. Their children are born with an intuitive understanding of the air-currents' moods and can sleep peacefully even as their platforms spin and tumble through the sky.

These three peoples have developed a complex interdependent society. The Thermari provide engineering, agriculture in the form of heat-root gardens that grow in controlled thermal vents, and the social structures that hold communities together as they drift apart and reform. The Ashkin create the tools and deep-infrastructure that make life possible, tapping into energy sources and mineral resources that sustain the settlement. The Drift-touched serve as messengers, scouts, and weather-readers, their unique abilities allowing communication and travel between platforms even during the most chaotic thermal storms.

Trade happens in the brief moments when platforms draw close, or along the permanent cable-bridges between the most stable fragments. But their true economy is one of favors and timing—knowing when to secure a line, when to release it, when to help a neighbor's platform stabilize, when to share the precious condensed water or the rare cool-zone harvests. They have learned to live with constant change, to build relationships that can stretch across growing distances, to find stability not in place but in the rhythm of their shared adaptation to an environment that never stops moving.

Their festivals coincide with the great thermal alignments, when the entire archipelago briefly moves in synchronization. During these times, platforms cluster together into temporary super-islands, and all three peoples gather to celebrate the aerial dance that defines their existence. They share stories passed down through generations of platform-dwellers, tales of legendary heat-swimmers and master bridge-builders, of great storms when the entire settlement was scattered to the horizon and slowly reformed over months of careful navigation back to community.

In the workshops and meeting-spaces, elderly Thermari teach young ones to read the thermal map—the invisible landscape of air currents and heat columns that forms their true geography. Ashkin masters take apprentices into the deep chambers to learn the songs that shape stone and metal. Drift-touched elders pass on the meditation techniques that allow consciousness to partially separate from flesh, to become light enough to ride the heat-shimmer between worlds.

And always, they live with the knowledge that their home is temporary, that tomorrow's configuration will be different, that adaptation is not just survival strategy but a way of being. They have become a people of the air as much as the stone, learning to thrive in the spaces between solid things, to find home not in any fixed location but in their shared ability to dance with the sky itself.
