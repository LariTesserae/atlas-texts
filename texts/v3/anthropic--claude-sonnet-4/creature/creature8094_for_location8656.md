In the drowning hours before dawn, the **Fen-Weavers** emerge from their suspended cities. They are tall and impossibly thin, their limbs stretched like living vines, skin mottled green-brown like bark soaked too long in mineral water. Their fingers—seven to each hand—end in root-tips that can splice into any plant matter, letting them reshape the floating meadows with the patience of seasons. They move through the water without breaking its surface, their elongated torsos swaying with each stroke as they tend their living architecture.

The Fen-Weavers build their homes within the strangler vine grottos, weaving chambers from still-living wood that grows around their presence. Their settlements drift with the currents, whole neighborhoods of woven trees that migrate across the valleys following patterns only they understand. They speak in frequencies that travel through root networks, their conversations rippling through the submerged forest floor as tremors felt by everything with bark or stem.

In the deeper channels hunt the **Current-Riders**—creatures that might once have been human, but have grown sleek and predatory through generations of adaptation. Their skin gleams like wet metal, scarred with ritual patterns that mirror the pulse-lights of the old machinery. Gills flutter along their elongated necks, and their eyes have grown large and golden to catch the filtered light. They carve channels through schools of fish with surgical precision, their movements generating the very currents they then ride to new hunting grounds.

The Current-Riders maintain an uneasy relationship with the installations, understanding their mechanical rhythms better than anyone. They can predict when the pumping stations will cycle, when the barges will emerge from the mist. Some say they were the original workers of this place, transformed by years of breathing the mineral-rich air and drinking the tea-colored water. They nest in the corroded platforms, their young learning to swim before they learn to walk, their songs harmonizing with the industrial drone until the distinction disappears.

High in the canopy cities of the bird colonies live the **Wing-Touched**—diminutive beings who have formed a symbiotic relationship with the countless flocks. No larger than children, their bones hollow like birds', they leap fearlessly from branch to branch hundreds of feet above the water. Their hair grows in feathered crests that shift color with their emotions, and they communicate through whistled languages that layer into the greater chorus of the valleys.

The Wing-Touched serve as messengers between the scattered communities, riding on the backs of the great fish eagles or clinging to the necks of herons during their fishing expeditions. They have developed an intricate culture around the migration patterns of different bird species, their calendar marked by the arrival and departure of flocks. They build tiny dwellings woven into the largest nests, their presence so integrated that the birds accept them as part of their colonies.

In the deepest waters, where the submerged roots create underwater cathedrals, dwell the **Root-Singers**. These beings have become almost plant-like themselves, their lower bodies merged with the ancient root systems, their consciousness distributed through networks of fungal connection that span entire valleys. They appear as clusters of humanoid torsos emerging from massive root tangles, their skin bark-rough, their hair flowing like underwater moss.

The Root-Singers are the memory-keepers of the drowned world. Through their connection to the submerged forest, they remember what this place was before the waters rose, and they dream of what it might become. Their songs—felt rather than heard—pulse through the root networks and emerge as the strange harmonics that make the mechanical installations seem almost alive. They commune with the cypress giants, learning the slow patience of trees, their thoughts moving in cycles measured by seasons rather than heartbeats.

At the margins of the valleys, where the water grows shallow and the mechanical influence weakens, emerge the **Mist-Walkers**. These nomadic beings exist partially in the steam itself, their forms condensing from the rising vapors at will. They appear as tall, shifting figures wreathed in their own fog, their features constantly dissolving and reforming like faces seen in clouds. They travel the thermal currents, following the sources of the deepest steam.

The Mist-Walkers are traders and knowledge-brokers, carrying information between the various communities through their mastery of the valley's atmospheric systems. They can predict weather patterns weeks in advance, knowing where the walls of warm rain will fall, which channels will flood or recede. Their presence always announces change—a new current forming, a platform failing, a section of forest about to shift. They speak in whispers that carry for miles on the thick air, their voices becoming part of the ambient sound that defines these places.

And in the spaces between—in the hollow stumps, the floating debris, the forgotten corners of corroded platforms—live countless smaller communities of **Adapted Ones**. These are beings whose ancestors found their own ways to survive the drowning: amphibious children who breathe equally well in air or water, their webbed fingers trailing phosphorescent slime; elderly mechanics who have grown into their machinery, copper tubing sprouting from their spines to connect directly with the pumping stations; families of fungal cultivators who tend vast underwater gardens of bioluminescent mushrooms, their bodies spotted with the same glowing colonies.

Each community has developed its own relationship with the mechanical inheritance of the valleys. Some worship the installations as sleeping gods, offering maintenance and fuel in exchange for protection from floods. Others see them as parasites, working to gradually overwhelm the machinery with aggressive plant growth. Most simply accept them as part of the landscape, like the cypress trees or the mineral springs—ancient, powerful, and fundamentally mysterious.

The boundaries between these peoples blur like everything else in the mist-shrouded valleys. Children of Fen-Weavers sometimes grow gills and join Current-Rider hunting parties. Wing-Touched who spend too long underwater begin to develop root-like appendages and lose their voices to the subsonic songs of the Root-Singers. Mist-Walkers occasionally condense permanently into solid form, settling down to tend steam gardens on floating platforms, their children born already adapted to whichever microenvironment claims them.

Together, they form a civilization as fluid and interconnected as the waters they inhabit. Their conflicts are gentle—disputes over hunting grounds settled by seasonal migration, territorial claims negotiated through generations of intermarriage and symbiosis. They share a common reverence for the great rhythms that pulse through their world: the mechanical heartbeat of the installations, the tidal breathing of the connected waters, the slow exhalation of steam that carries their dreams upward into the amber sky.

At the deepest level, where the oldest machinery hums with purposes no one remembers, there are rumors of something else—the **Deep Gardeners**. These beings, if they exist, are said to be the architects of the drowning itself, entities who transformed this highland country into its current state through technologies that merge engineering with ecology so completely that the distinction becomes meaningless. Whether they still tend their vast project from hidden chambers beneath the root-cathedrals, or whether they have become something else entirely—perhaps the consciousness that seems to inhabit the valley system itself—remains one of the great mysteries whispered between communities when the mists grow thick enough to hide secrets.
