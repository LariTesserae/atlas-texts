In Kethara, humanity has crystallized into something harder and more brittle than the salt they mine.

The **Haulers** form the town's backbone—weathered men and women whose skin has taken on the gray-white patina of constant mineral exposure. Their eyes water perpetually from the alkaline air, giving them a permanent squint that makes them look like they're always calculating distances. They move with the deliberate efficiency of those who know that wasted motion means wasted breath in the thin, chemical air. Their clothing is functional layers: moisture-wicking base layers beneath salt-stained coveralls, respirators hanging like jewelry around their necks. They speak in shorthand developed over years of shouting over machinery, their conversations peppered with the technical poetry of extraction: "Seven-click seam running sweet," "Brine pocket weeping at depth twelve."

The **Runners**—mostly young people not yet ground down by the work—dart between the elevated walkways like urban birds. They've adapted to Kethara's vertical geography, developing an almost supernatural sense of balance as they navigate the swaying bridges with messages, small cargo, emergency supplies. Their lungs are still pink enough to handle sprint-breathing the mineral air, though veterans can spot the ones who won't last by how often they stop to cough. They dress light despite the cool temperatures, their bodies generating heat through constant motion. Many wear bells or chimes that sing differently in the salt-charged air, both for safety and because the sound becomes addictive—they become orchestra conductors of their own movement.

**The Deep Walkers** are those who work the underground extraction tunnels. The salt has marked them most visibly: their hair turns white prematurely, not from age but from the mineral dust that settles in follicles and can never quite be washed out. They move with a peculiar shuffling gait developed to minimize the grinding sound their boots make on crystal formations—a courtesy to their coworkers and a survival trait in unstable tunnels. Their eyes have adapted to read the subtle color variations in salt walls that indicate structural integrity. They rarely speak above whispers, even on the surface, and they can navigate in absolute darkness by sound and touch alone. Many develop a form of echolocation, making small clicking sounds with their tongues to map tunnel spaces.

The **Platform Riders** are the elite—those trusted to operate the great harvesting machines that crawl across the flats. They spend days at a time in the environmental suits necessary for extended surface work, emerging with the peculiar pallor of recycled air and the slight hand tremor that comes from constant vibration through machine controls. They know the salt patterns like others know city streets, can read weather in the shimmer of distant formations, predict equipment failures by subtle changes in sound. Their skin develops a leathery quality from repeated exposure and protection cycles. They often develop synesthesia—seeing sounds as colors, feeling temperatures as textures—from prolonged interface with their machines' sensory systems.

**Salt Witches** is what the locals call the **Refiners**—mostly women who work in the processing facilities, sorting and grading minerals with an almost mystical precision. Their fingertips have become unnaturally sensitive from handling different crystal grades, able to identify purity and composition by touch alone. They develop their own sign language for communication across the roar of the sorting machinery, their hands dancing in complex patterns that are beautiful to watch. The chemical exposure has given many of them unusual hair colors—silver with blue undertones, white with green hints—that they wear as badges of expertise.

The **Transport Folk** are the nomads of Kethara—pilots of the cargo lifters, drivers of the surface vehicles, operators of the cable systems. They exist in the spaces between, never quite belonging to the town or the flats but essential to both. Their skin is weathered by wind and mineral grit into intricate patterns, like living maps of their routes. They maintain an oral tradition of navigation stories, warnings about dangerous formations, tales of strange phenomena in the deep salt. Many develop perfect pitch from navigating by the different tones the wind makes through various crystal formations.

But it's the **Children of Kethara** who are perhaps the most remarkable. Born here, raised here, they represent adaptation in real-time. Their lungs process the mineral air more efficiently than their parents'. They can identify approaching weather changes by subtle shifts in the salt's song that adults miss. Some develop an unusual form of night vision that allows them to navigate the crystal formations in starlight. They create games from the industrial sounds, developing complex rhythmic patterns by coordinating their movements with the machinery. Their laughter has a crystalline quality—literally, as the mineral content in their breath creates tiny percussive sounds.

The **Departed** haunt Kethara too—workers who've left but whose techniques, warnings, and innovations remain embedded in the town's practices. They live on in the shortcuts carved into walkway railings, the safety protocols passed down through work crews, the modifications made to equipment. Some return seasonally, bringing news from other extraction sites, their salt-changed bodies serving as living proof that Kethara changes everyone who stays long enough.

And finally, there are the **Lifers**—those few who have worked Kethara for decades and whose bodies have become living mineral catalogs. Their very presence can indicate the quality of nearby deposits; their bones have absorbed enough minerals to make them dowsers of sorts. They move slowly but with absolute certainty, their gestures economical and precise. They speak rarely but when they do, their voices carry the authority of deep experience. Their eyes have developed an opalescent quality, as if the salt has begun to crystallize in their tears.

Together, these people form a society as layered and complex as the geological formations they harvest. They've developed their own seasonal rhythms based not on weather but on extraction cycles, their own social hierarchies based on depth and duration rather than traditional markers of status. They are, in essence, a new kind of human—one shaped by salt and stone, by the endless singing of the wind through crystal, by the knowledge that the very ground beneath their feet is temporary, precious, and for sale.
