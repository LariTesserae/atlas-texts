In the deepest hollows where root and stone have fused into living chambers, the Tenders make their homes. They are no longer quite human, though they remember being so in the way one recalls a half-forgotten dream. Their skin has taken on the subtle green patina of those who live always in filtered light, and their fingertips have grown sensitive as root-hair, able to read the chemical conversations that flow through the plant-city's circulatory system.

The Tenders move through their domain with a patience that matches the rhythms of growth itself. They do not walk so much as flow, following pathways that exist more in the city's vegetative memory than in any fixed geography. Their clothes—if they can still be called clothes—are woven from the gift-fibers that certain vines produce, garments that continue growing even as they're worn, sprouting small flowers or useful pouches as needed. Some Tenders have learned to encourage specific patterns, coaxing their living garments to bloom in synchronized displays during the city's rare ceremonial moments.

Each Tender has developed an intimate relationship with particular sections of the settlement. Kessa tends the spiral gardens of the eastern terraces, where morning mist first catches in the cup-flowers. Her hair grows long and moss-touched, braided with thin vines that help her sense the moisture needs of plants three levels below. When she sleeps—which she does standing, like a tree—small epiphytes take root temporarily in her hair, sharing their stored water during the dry hours before dawn.

In the central chambers where the greatest trees have grown up through what were once grand halls, old Jorim maintains the city's memory. His beard has become indistinguishable from the lichen that records the settlement's history in its slow-growing patterns across the ancient stones. He can read decades in the twist of a vine, interpret the plant-city's dreams in the arrangement of new growth. When visitors come—rare pilgrims ascending from the valleys below—Jorim's voice emerges like wind through leaves, telling stories that the trees whisper to him in their sap-slow language.

The children born here know no other way of being. They are small and quick, able to scramble through passages that reshape themselves hourly. Their laughter sounds like rustling leaves, and they play games that involve coaxing flowers to bloom in specific sequences, or racing along the living bridges that grow between terraces. Young Mira can make the phosphorescent fungi pulse in rhythm with her heartbeat, while her brother Tam has learned to call the mist-birds with perfect imitations of their whispered songs.

In the highest reaches, where the canopy opens to glimpses of sky, the Weatherkeeper dwells. She is the most changed of all, her form elongated and willowy, her arms extending into branch-like appendages that can sense pressure changes days before storms arrive. She conducts the city's response to weather, sending chemical signals through the root network that prepare the lower levels for rain, or encouraging the great leaf-umbrellas to unfurl in protective cascades.

There are others who have chosen different paths of symbiosis. The Deeprunner rarely surfaces, having learned to follow the root highways that plunge into the mountain's core. His skin has grown pale and sensitive, able to detect the mineral songs that rise from the deep springs. He tends the city's foundational connections, ensuring the ancient stones remain harmoniously bound with their living matrix.

The Spore-singers work in the twilight hours when the fungal networks are most active. They have developed throat pouches that can harmonize with the subsonic frequencies the mushrooms use to communicate across the settlement. Through their songs, they help coordinate the great waves of blooming that sometimes cascade across the terraces, or guide the gentle choreography of decay that opens space for new growth.

Not all who come here can make the transition. Some visitors leave quickly, overwhelmed by the humid air and the sensation of being absorbed into something vast and growing. Others try to stay but cannot adapt, and the city gently expels them, creating uncomfortable drafts and thorny passages until they choose to depart. But those who can surrender their rigid sense of self find themselves welcomed into a community that exists in profound intimacy with the living world.

The Tenders rarely speak in words anymore, having learned the richer vocabulary of chemical signals, growth patterns, and the subtle pressures that flow through the root network. When they do use their voices, it's often to translate for the rare outsiders who come seeking knowledge of this place. Their speech has grown soft and rustling, interspersed with long, comfortable silences that match the patient rhythms of photosynthesis and cell division.

At night, when the city glows with its gentle phosphorescence, the Tenders sometimes gather in the old amphitheater—now a bowl of luminescent moss where the ancient stone seats are barely visible beneath cushions of flowering vine. They share the day's observations: which sections are preparing to fruit, where new chambers are opening in the deep roots, what messages have arrived from the far edges where the settlement gradually gives way to wild mountain forest.

They dream communally, their sleep-thoughts flowing through the same networks that carry nutrients and water. In these shared dreams, they experience the city's memories: the time before, when it was merely stone and mortar; the first tentative growth that emerged in cracks and crevices; the slow decades of transformation; and the possible futures that stretch ahead like branching paths through an infinite garden.

The Tenders understand that they are not the city's masters but its partners in an ongoing experiment in symbiosis. They tend not just the plants but the delicate balance between what was human and what was wild, nurturing a new form of life that belongs entirely to neither category. In caring for this impossible place, they have become something impossible themselves—human and plant, individual and ecosystem, ancient and eternally growing toward some unimaginable future flowering.
