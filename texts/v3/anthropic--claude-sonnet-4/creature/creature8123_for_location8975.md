In this realm of eternal mist and mineral warmth, the Soakers have made their home for countless generations. They are a people shaped by their environment as surely as water shapes stone—tall and willowy, with skin that gleams faintly opalescent from a lifetime spent in mineral baths. Their flesh has taken on subtle hues: pale jade along their shoulders and backs, cream-white at their joints, with veins of rust-orange tracing delicate patterns beneath translucent skin where the spring waters have left their mark.

The Soakers move with fluid deliberation, their elongated limbs adapted for graceful navigation through the terraced pools. Their fingers and toes are slightly webbed, ending in soft, sensitive pads that can read the temperature and mineral content of water with a touch. They breathe slowly, their expanded lung capacity allowing them to remain submerged for long periods in meditation or work. When they surface, droplets cling to their skin like living jewelry, each one a tiny lens refracting the misted light.

Their hair grows in fine, moss-like filaments that seem to photosynthesize in the pale light, turning deeper green when they spend time in the upper terraces and fading to silver-white in the depths. They cultivate these living tresses with the same patience they tend their pools, braiding them with water-vine and tiny fern fronds that continue to grow and flourish as living ornaments.

The Soakers organize themselves in Circles—family groups that claim stewardship over particular sequences of pools. Each Circle has developed its own understanding of the water's properties, becoming specialists in different aspects of the springs' gifts. The Thermal Circle tends the hottest pools near the source, their skin bearing the deep rust-orange marks of prolonged exposure to iron-rich waters. They are the forge-tenders, though they work not with metal but with the slow precipitation of minerals, crafting tools and ornaments by guiding the natural accumulation of deposits over decades.

The Deep Circle dwells in the lower terraces where the water has cooled and clarified, their jade-marked skin allowing them to see clearly through many fathoms of crystal-clear spring water. They are the pool-deepeners, the channel-carvers, working with bone tools and patient pressure to guide the water's eternal work of reshaping stone. Their lungs are the most developed, allowing them to work for hours beneath the surface, feeling for stress points in the limestone, encouraging the formation of new chambers and passages.

The Moss Circle inhabits the middle terraces, where vegetation grows most lush. Their skin bears intricate cream-colored patterns where symbiotic plant matter has taken root, creating living gardens across their bodies. They are the cultivators, working with the architectural moss and training the water-vines into bridges and living structures. They speak in whispers, believing that loud voices might disturb the delicate balance between plant and stone.

The Salt Circle tends the mineral gardens—carefully maintained pools where different springs converge, creating crystalline formations of extraordinary beauty. Their skin is marked with veins of pale blue and white where they have absorbed trace amounts of precious minerals. They are the memory-keepers, reading the history of each spring in its mineral signature, understanding the deep currents that feed the terraces from far below.

Children are raised collectively by all the Circles, spending seasons with each to understand the full ecosystem of the terraces. They begin pale and unmarked, their skin slowly taking on the characteristics of the waters they most often inhabit as they discover their calling. Some never fully commit to a single Circle, remaining pale and opalescent, becoming the Wanderers who move between levels carrying messages and maintaining the connections that bind the community together.

The Soakers communicate through a complex system of water-signs—subtle movements of hands and feet that create specific patterns of ripples across pool surfaces. Different minerals in the water carry these ripples differently, allowing for nuanced communication across great distances. They also speak through temperature, subtly warming or cooling sections of shared pools to convey emotional states and intentions.

Their dwellings blur the line between architecture and organism. The Soakers grow their homes as much as build them, encouraging certain formations of stone, training plants into structural roles, and allowing the gradual accumulation of minerals to create walls and roofs. Each dwelling is unique, shaped by the specific characteristics of its pool and the patient work of generations. Some homes exist entirely underwater, accessed by diving through seemingly bottomless pools that open into air-filled chambers below.

The Soakers measure time by the Mineral Calendar—the slow cycles of deposition and erosion that mark their pools. They celebrate the Opening of New Channels, when patient water work finally breaks through to create a new pool or pathway. They mourn the Closing of Old Ways, when shifting mineral deposits seal off familiar routes. Their history is written in the stone itself, readable to those who understand the language of limestone and mineral-sign.

They have no metals, no fires that might disturb the delicate balance of their misty realm. Instead, they work with the springs themselves, learning to concentrate minerals into useful forms, growing tools from guided precipitation, creating implements that are as much living organisms as crafted objects. Their cutting edges are formed from carefully shaped calcite formations, their vessels grown from patient accumulation of mineral layers.

In the deepest pools, where the water emerges from unknown depths, live the Oracles—ancient Soakers whose skin has become almost entirely mineralized, forming living statues that still breathe and think but move only with the slowness of geological time. They are consulted on matters of great import, their responses given in the gradual shift of mineral deposits that takes seasons to fully understand.

The Soakers have developed a relationship with the few creatures that share their realm. They tend gardens of water-moss that serve as both food and symbiotic partner, the plants growing in their hair and skin while providing nutrients and oxygen. They keep schools of translucent fish whose movements through the pools help circulate the waters, and they communicate with the ancient salamanders that occasionally emerge from the deepest springs, creatures so old they remember when the terraces first began to form.

Their philosophy centers on patience and gradual transformation. They believe that wisdom comes through long observation, that beauty emerges from allowing natural processes to unfold without force. They see themselves not as masters of their environment but as partners in its endless becoming, guiding and being guided by the eternal conversation between water and stone.

In their ritual gatherings, entire Circles will submerge in chosen pools, lying motionless for hours while they feel the subtle currents, the mineral exchange, the slow heartbeat of the springs. They emerge from these communions with shared visions—glimpses of the deep aquifers that feed their realm, premonitions of new channels about to open, understanding of distant earthquakes that might affect their flow.

The Soakers have no word for loneliness, for they are always in communion with their pools, always feeling the circulation that connects every basin to every other. When one of their number dies, the body is placed in a special dissolution pool, where mineral-rich waters gradually transform flesh and bone into new formations, new colors in the stone, ensuring that death is merely another form of becoming within their liquid realm.

They dream collectively, their unconscious minds merging through the mineral-laden waters that connect all pools. In these shared dreams, they experience the deep memory of the stone, feel the mountain's slow breathing, understand their place in the vast patience of the earth's transformation. Some never fully wake from these dreams, becoming the Dreamers who float perpetually in the deepest pools, their consciousness dispersed through the entire network of springs, becoming part of the living memory of the terraces themselves.
