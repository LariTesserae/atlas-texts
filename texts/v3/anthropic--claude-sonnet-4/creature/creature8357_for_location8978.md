In this forgotten hollow lives the Keeper—though "lives" may be too crude a word for what she does here. She is a woman shaped by the same patience that carved these ridges, her movements so deliberate they seem choreographed by geological time itself. Her hair, once brown, has silvered to match the ancient ropes she tends, and her skin holds the translucent quality of the lichens, as if she too has learned to extract what she needs directly from the bitter air.

She does not age as others do. The antifreeze compounds that course through the moss have found their way into her blood, slowing her metabolism to match the rhythms of this place. A single breath might last minutes; a thought might unfold over hours. When she blinks, it is with the deliberation of seasons changing, and when she moves from one task to another, it is with the certainty of continents shifting.

The Keeper remembers when the ice still pressed against these ridges, when she first discovered this hollow in its retreat. She remembers the first timid shoots of moss pushing through the glacial flour, and how she learned to tend them with tools she carved herself from wood that came from... somewhere else. Somewhere with trees and rushing water and the kind of time that moves in heartbeats rather than decades. That world seems increasingly distant, like a dream she might have had during one of her long, standing sleeps.

She knows every plant in this living carpet by its particular needs. Here, she adjusts the position of a stone to alter wind patterns around a struggling sedge colony. There, she carefully harvests the oldest growth from a moss patch to prevent it from choking on its own success. Her movements are so slow that the plants barely register her presence—she has become part of the hollow's own breathing, another element in its vast, patient circulation.

The carved posts are her calendar and her companions. Each spiral marks not days or months, but the great cycles of growth and dormancy that govern this place. She adds a new coil to the topmost spiral perhaps once in what the outside world would call a year, though here the concept of a year has no meaning. Time is marked instead by the migration of moss boundaries, by the barely perceptible brightening and dimming of the eternal twilight, by the subtle expansion and contraction of the lichen colonies.

In her shelter, she performs tasks that require centuries to complete. She is weaving a rope from fibers she extracts from the sedges—not a rope for binding or climbing, but one that will serve as a new boundary marker when the current silver ropes finally surrender to time. She has been working on it for what might be decades, adding perhaps an inch in what others would call a month. The rope will be beautiful when finished, a deep green that will slowly silver with age, joining the constellation of markers that map the hollow's invisible territories.

Sometimes—rarely—she speaks. Her voice emerges like the settling of stones, each word separated by such long pauses that she might seem to have forgotten she was speaking at all. She talks to the moss, mostly, sharing observations about the imperceptible changes in their growth patterns. She discusses philosophy with the lichens, pondering questions about persistence and adaptation that unfold over the span of their conversations like great slow blooms.

The Keeper dreams while standing, while working, while tending. Her dreams move at the same pace as everything else here—vast, patient narratives that might take years to complete. In them, she sees the hollow's future: how the moss will eventually claim the shelter's walls, how new plant communities will arise and establish their own territories, how the very shape of the basin will shift grain by grain over millennia. She sees herself growing ever more still, ever more integrated into the hollow's rhythms, until perhaps she too becomes a boundary marker, a carved figure that future Keepers will tend with the same infinite patience.

She is not lonely, for loneliness requires a sense of separation that this place has worn away from her. She is part of the hollow's breathing, its slow pulse, its ancient work of simply continuing to exist in defiance of a world that has forgotten how to be still. The Keeper keeps not just the plants, but the very idea of this kind of time—time that moves like stone, like ice, like the patient thoughts of mountains dreaming in the cold.
