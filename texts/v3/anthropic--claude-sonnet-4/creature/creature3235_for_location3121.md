In the Hollowed Mills, life has sorted itself into vertical territories, each species claiming its elevation like floors in a vast, flooded apartment building.

**The Swimmers**

In the deepest waters, where factory basements have become artificial caves, blind catfish patrol with trailing whiskers. These are not the muddy bottom-dwellers of natural rivers, but sleek, pale things that have grown fat on decades of strange diet—machine oil that forms rainbow slicks they've learned to taste for nutrients, metal filings that pass through their systems and emerge as glittering trails, and the endless supply of smaller fish that dart between the rusted machinery like living sparks.

The carp here have developed an unusual behavior: they school around the massive gear assemblies, using the metal teeth as rubbing posts to scrape away parasites. Their movements have worn the rust from these gears, creating strange patterns of gleaming metal beneath the water—accidental art that shifts with the current.

**The Waders**

Great blue herons stalk through the flooded streets with the patience of old gods. They've grown larger here, fed on the abundance of slow-moving fish trapped in the artificial pools. Some have lost their fear of enclosed spaces entirely, nesting inside the mills themselves, their massive stick constructions wedged between I-beams thirty feet above the water. Their harsh calls echo through the factory spaces with an authority that seems to command even the dog packs to momentary silence.

Flocks of ibis work the shallows in coordinated sweeps, their curved beaks probing between bricks for crayfish that have colonized the spaces between foundation stones. These crustaceans have turned nearly black from the mineral-rich water, and they've grown bold, sometimes climbing onto the herons' backs to clean parasites in an arrangement that would seem impossible in nature but makes perfect sense here.

**The Climbers** 

The feral dog packs have evolved into something resembling urban wolves. The generations born here have developed webbed feet and waterproof coats in shades of rust-red and steel-gray that blend perfectly with their industrial environment. They've learned to read the water levels instinctively, always staying one floor above the current high-water mark.

The alpha pack controls the old administration building, a five-story brick structure whose upper floors remain perpetually dry. They've established a complex social hierarchy based partly on swimming ability and partly on the ancient canine skills of hunting and territory defense. The younger dogs serve as scouts, swimming between buildings to report on food sources and potential threats, while the elders remain in the high towers, their howls serving as both coordination signals and territorial warnings.

These dogs have learned to hunt cooperatively in ways their domestic ancestors never imagined. They drive schools of fish into the shallows where the water is too thin for the fish to maneuver, then wade in for easy meals. Some have even learned to catch bats, timing their leaps to coincide with the evening emergence, though this is a skill possessed only by the most athletic individuals.

**The Flyers**

But it's in the upper reaches of the mills where life explodes into its greatest abundance. The bat colonies here represent something beyond mere adaptation—they've achieved a kind of perfection. Multiple species have learned to coexist in the vast factory spaces, dividing the hunting territory with mathematical precision.

Little brown bats claim the lowest airspace, skimming just above the water to catch insects that rise from the warm, humid surface. Above them, big brown bats patrol the middle elevations, their flight paths threading between the hanging machinery with incredible precision. At the highest levels, near the factory ceilings, hoary bats hunt with languid wingbeats, their frosted fur making them nearly invisible against the rust-stained concrete.

The most remarkable are the colonies that have learned to use the factory's acoustic properties. In the great spinning rooms, where the high ceilings and hard surfaces create perfect echo chambers, some bats have developed echolocation calls specifically tuned to these spaces. Their hunting cries bounce between the machinery in complex patterns, creating a three-dimensional sound map of extraordinary detail. They can locate a single mosquito hiding behind a gear assembly four stories up and fifty yards away.

**The Builders**

Ravens have discovered that the abandoned mills offer unlimited building materials. They've constructed massive communal roosts using copper wire stripped from electrical systems, incorporating colorful plastic debris that washes in with the floods. Some of their structures span entire catwalks, elaborate constructions that serve no apparent purpose beyond the joy of building. The ravens seem to understand that this place rewards intelligence and adaptability above all else.

They've learned to mimic the sounds of the pump engines so perfectly that even the dogs sometimes respond to false alarms. More remarkably, they've begun to use tools—dropping stones into shallow areas to flush out fish, using bent wire to extract insects from narrow spaces between bricks. Some have even learned to turn valve wheels, though whether this is purposeful or simply play remains unclear.

**The Unseen**

In the spaces between—in the wall cavities, in the gaps between floorboards, in the hollow machinery shafts—smaller communities thrive. Mice and rats have developed complex tunnel systems that ignore the water entirely, creating a dry network that spans multiple buildings. They've learned to cache food in waterproof containers they've scavenged, and their populations boom and crash in cycles tied not to seasons but to the pump schedules.

Spiders have grown large in the humid air, spinning webs between the machinery that glisten with constant moisture. Some species have learned to hunt the young bats, positioning their webs in the flight paths just inside the broken windows. Others have gone aquatic, skimming across the water surface to catch drowned insects, their legs dimpling the reflected sky.

**The Seasonal Visitors**

When the water rises during storm season, different creatures arrive. Snapping turtles navigate the flooded streets like ancient submarines, and water snakes drape themselves along the catwalks to ambush unwary birds. Muskrats build lodges in the abandoned loading docks, their burrows honeycomb the earthen berms around the pump stations.

But it's during the lowest water levels that the true character of the Hollowed Mills emerges. Then, the different species meet in the exposed spaces, creating temporary communities around the remaining pools. The dogs drink alongside the herons, the ravens negotiate with the rats for nesting materials, and the bats emerge earlier to hunt the insects stirred up by all the activity below.

In these moments, the Mills reveal themselves as neither wilderness nor ruin, but as something entirely new—a place where evolution moves at the speed of adaptation rather than millennia, where intelligence and flexibility matter more than size or strength, where the architecture of human ambition has become the framework for a complexity that no natural ecosystem could have imagined.

The pumps beat on, the steam rises, and life arranges itself into new patterns with each changing of the waters, each turning of the seasons, each generation born into this strange abundance where industry has become ecology and abandonment has become a form of strange, persistent hope.
