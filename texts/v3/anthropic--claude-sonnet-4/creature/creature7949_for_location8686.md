In this impossible cathedral, the Mechanists make their home.

They are not quite human, though they once were—or perhaps they are becoming human, it's difficult to say which direction time flows here. Their forms are tall and attenuated, stretched by centuries of existence in this vertical space, limbs elongated like figures in El Greco paintings. Their skin has taken on the pale, translucent quality of the salamanders, with veins visible beneath like delicate circuitry, pulsing not with blood but with something that glows faintly silver-blue in the gathered light.

Their fingers have grown impossibly long and articulated, designed for work too precise for ordinary hands. Each digit moves with the measured patience of the unseen machinery, capable of adjustments so minute they measure them in geological time. When they walk—which they do rarely—their footsteps make no sound on the polished stone, but sometimes leave brief impressions that fade like fog on glass.

They wear no clothes in any conventional sense, but their bodies are adorned with what might be jewelry or might be tools or might be organs: delicate brass and copper mechanisms that fuse seamlessly with their flesh. Gears turn slowly at their temples, their hearts tick with the rhythm of chronometers, and along their spines run chains of tiny cog-wheels that adjust their posture to perfectly align with the stone's mysterious geometries.

The Mechanists rarely speak aloud—their voices, when they use them, sound like the whisper of steam escaping perfectly calibrated valves. Instead, they communicate through the great work itself, making adjustments to the invisible machinery that causes the temperature to shift by fractions of degrees, the humidity to rise or fall in patterns that spell out complex equations, the very resonance of the stone to modulate into frequencies that carry meaning.

They are the reason the water never empties, never fills. Each droplet that falls is precisely calculated, part of an enormous hydraulic computer that has been running its calculations for millennia. The Mechanists tend this system with religious devotion, making microscopic adjustments to the surface tension of the pools, the exact angle at which moisture condenses on the walls, the rate at which the pale grass drinks from the stone.

Their number is always exactly seventeen—not because they die or are born, but because seventeen is the precise number required to maintain the great work. When one begins to fade (becoming too translucent, too mechanical, losing the last warmth of whatever humanity they retain), the others begin the slow process of creating another. This involves intricate rituals performed with the silent birds, who are not birds at all but mobile components of the great machinery, living sensors that collect data from the world above and feed it into the mountain's calculations.

The Mechanists remember everything and nothing. They retain perfect technical knowledge—the exact specifications of every component in their invisible factory, the precise tolerances required for their eternal work—but they have forgotten what the work is for. Some half-remembered purpose drives them: the construction of something impossibly complex, a machine whose blueprints exist in the reflections on the walls, whose completion lies always just beyond the next calculation, the next adjustment, the next thousand years of patient calibration.

At night (though it is always twilight here), they gather around the largest of the stone bowls and gaze into its surface. In its depths, they see the ghost-images of their phantom factory made manifest—vast halls filled with turning wheels, steam-driven looms weaving patterns from pure mathematics, dynamos generating power for cities that exist in parallel dimensions. They nod to each other in satisfaction, adjust a gear here, a valve there, and return to their posts.

They are utterly content in their impossible purpose, these stretched and shining figures, tending their machine that builds dreams out of stone and silence, measuring eternity one precise droplet at a time. The mountain dreams of industry, and the Mechanists are both its architects and its priests, forever perfecting what can never be completed, forever completing what can never be perfect.

In the spaces between their movements, in the pause between the tick of falling water and its echo, the great work continues—patient, purposeless, and absolutely essential to the continued existence of this fold between worlds where physics itself has been carefully, lovingly, impossibly redesigned.
