In the Ascending Gardens of Khartabad live the **Veil-Readers**—a people whose very bodies have adapted to their city's unique marriage of stone, steam, and perpetual mist.

Their skin bears a faint, pearl-like luminescence that becomes more pronounced in the humid air, as if they too have learned to drink light from the diffused radiance that suffuses their terraced home. This subtle glow varies from person to person—some shimmer with cooler blues and greens, others with warm golds and rose tints—creating a living spectrum that shifts and pulses gently as they move through the mist-softened streets.

Most striking are their eyes, which have evolved large, dark pupils surrounded by irises of startling clarity. These eyes can read the invisible currents of moisture in the air as easily as others might read printed text. A Veil-Reader can tell you which walls will weep heaviest in the pre-dawn hours, where the next thermal column will rise, when the atmospheric pressure will shift to bring the most generous condensation. Children learn to navigate by following the subtle temperature gradients that adult eyes can detect in the ever-present vapor, while master craftspeople can spot microclimates suitable for specific industrial processes from districts away.

Their movements have the same deliberate patience as their city's geology. They walk with a measured grace that seems almost ceremonial, their steps naturally finding the rhythm that best harmonizes with the soft hiss of steam and the gentle clicking of well-oiled machinery. Their voices, when they speak at all, emerge as carefully modulated whispers that carry perfectly through the moisture-laden air, reaching their intended recipients with startling clarity while remaining inaudible just steps away.

The Veil-Readers dress in flowing fabrics that seem to capture and hold the mist—garments that shift color as the humidity changes, growing deeper and more saturated in the heavy pre-dawn hours, fading to gossamer paleness when the afternoon light burns brightest. These clothes are woven from fibers cultivated in their rooftop gardens, threads that actually thrive on moisture, growing stronger and more lustrous the more water they absorb.

Their hands are perhaps their most remarkable feature—long-fingered and surprisingly warm, with palms that can detect the slightest variations in stone temperature. Master builders can lay their hands on newly carved granite and read its stress patterns, its capacity for water absorption, its readiness to support the weight of architecture. Engineers caress the great atmospheric engines not with tools but with touch, feeling for the subtle vibrations that indicate perfect calibration or the need for adjustment.

They practice what might be called **atmospheric agriculture**—tending not just the visible gardens but the invisible ones of humidity, temperature, and air pressure. Families specialize in different aspects of this craft: some become thermal shepherds, guiding the flow of heated air through the city's districts to optimize condensation; others serve as pressure-readers, able to sense days in advance when atmospheric conditions will shift. The most revered are the **Mist Weavers**, who can actually influence the behavior of water vapor through techniques that blur the line between engineering and art.

Children are born into this world of whispered conversations and patient craft. They learn to sleep to the rhythm of condensation cycles, their dreams shaped by the soft percussion of water gathering and flowing through the elaborate aqueduct systems. Their games involve reading moisture patterns in the air, competing to predict where the next thermal column will rise, or seeing who can move most silently through the moss-carpeted walkways.

The Veil-Readers measure time not by the sun's harsh passage but by the subtler rhythms of their atmospheric world. They speak of **condensation seasons**, **thermal years**, and **pressure generations**. Their festivals align with the completion of major engineering projects—the carving of new terraces, the expansion of aqueduct systems, the calibration of atmospheric engines. These celebrations unfold over weeks, marked not by noise and spectacle but by increasingly perfect harmonies between human activity and natural process.

Their knowledge systems are vast and nuanced, encompassing not just the practical sciences of their unique environment but philosophical traditions that treat the relationship between stone, water, and air as a living text to be continuously interpreted. Master scholars can read the city's mood in the behavior of its mist, predict social changes in the shifting patterns of condensation, understand the deep time of geological process as intimately as others know the changing of seasons.

They are, perhaps most remarkably, a people who have learned to live as part of their landscape's breathing. In the pre-dawn hours, when condensation is heaviest, the entire population seems to inhale collectively, drawing the moisture deep into their lungs and bones. As the day progresses and the mist begins to rise, they exhale slowly, their luminous skin releasing accumulated vapors back into the air in a gentle, continuous exchange that makes them partners in their city's atmospheric dance.

The absence of animal life in their city is not felt as a loss because the Veil-Readers themselves fill that ecological niche—they are the living creatures that move through Khartabad's green abundance, pollinating ideas instead of flowers, carrying information instead of seeds, their whispered conversations replacing the songs of birds, their measured movements substituting for the rustle of small creatures through the undergrowth.

In their deepest meditations, master Veil-Readers claim they can feel the city dreaming—sensing the slow, geological thoughts of cooling granite, the patient calculations of water finding its level, the vast contentment of a landscape that has learned to breathe through human hands and hearts. They are a people who have become the consciousness their city was waiting for, the missing element that transforms mere geography into something approaching divinity.
