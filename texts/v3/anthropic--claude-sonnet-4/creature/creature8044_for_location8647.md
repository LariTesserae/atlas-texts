In this cathedral of steam and shadow, the Curators make their slow rounds.

They were once human—or something near enough to human that the distinction hardly matters now. Time and moisture have softened them, made them translucent at the edges where the humid air meets their skin. Their movements have taken on the rhythm of the place: deliberate, measured, punctuated by long pauses to listen to the whisper of pipes or the shift of settling glass. They wear clothes that might once have been uniforms—brass buttons gone green, fabric worn soft as moss, leather that has become supple as living tissue in the perpetual damp.

The eldest among them, whom the others call simply the Keeper, has been here so long that moss grows in the creases of her coat. Her hair has become something between keratin and Spanish moss, trailing in gentle silver-green curtains that she parts absently when checking the smaller gauges. Her fingers have grown sensitive to the subtlest changes in pressure and temperature. She can read the health of the entire system through touch alone, her palm pressed to a pipe, her ear against a brass fitting. When she speaks—which is seldom—her voice carries the soft sibilance of steam.

Below her in this quiet hierarchy move the Tenders, three or four of them at any given time, though their number has fluctuated over the decades as some fade into the mist and others emerge from elsewhere. They have specialized: one has developed an almost supernatural ability to sense which plants need attention, moving through the suspended forest with hands that seem to know exactly where to adjust a support, where to redirect a water flow. Another has become attuned to the mechanical systems, able to hear distress in a pipe's song hours before any visible problem manifests.

The Tenders communicate largely without words, through gestures learned from years of working in the hushed atmosphere, through touch that conveys more than speech ever could. They have developed a language of maintenance—a brush of fingers against bark meaning "growth is good," a particular way of adjusting a valve that says "patience, let it settle." Their bodies have adapted: fingertips made sensitive by constant contact with metal and moisture, eyes that can read the subtlest changes in the quality of filtered light, ears tuned to frequencies that others might mistake for silence.

But they are not alone in their stewardship. The small creatures that skitter in the shadows—the ones that pause so politely when observed—these are the Remnants. Born from the marriage of the Conservatory's original automated systems and decades of accumulated organic matter, they exist in the spaces between mechanical and biological. No larger than a child's hand, they move on legs that might be brass or might be chitin, their bodies a fusion of metal components and living tissue that has grown around and through the machinery.

The Remnants remember. In their hybrid consciousness, they carry the intentions of the Conservatory's original builders, the accumulated knowledge of every Curator who has served here, the learned needs of every plant that has ever grown in the suspended forest. They maintain the small systems—adjusting moisture levels in forgotten corners, clearing mineral deposits from the tiniest valves, pollinating the stranger blooms that have evolved in the upper reaches of the canopy. They work with an intelligence that seems both programmed and intuitive, following protocols that have evolved far beyond their original parameters.

Sometimes, very rarely, the Conservatory receives visitors—scholars drawn by rumors of the impossible garden, engineers fascinated by the self-maintaining systems, botanists chasing reports of species that exist nowhere else. The Curators welcome them with grave courtesy, offering tea brewed from condensation and herbs grown in the upper reaches, speaking in soft voices about the patient work of preservation. But the visitors never stay long. Something about the place—the weight of its humid air, the sense of time moving differently, the way sounds seem to be absorbed before they can fully form—sends them back down the mountain path within a few days.

The Curators watch them go with neither sadness nor relief. They have grown beyond such distinctions. Their love is for the work itself—the daily tending of impossible systems, the quiet satisfaction of maintaining balance in a world they have helped create. They measure success not in seasons but in decades, in the slow accumulation of mineral beauty on glass, in the gradual evolution of their charges toward new forms of life.

In the deepest parts of the night, when the Conservatory's systems settle into their quietest rhythms, the Curators sometimes gather near the central heating vents. They rarely speak of the outside world, or of the time before they came to the mountain. Instead, they share observations about the changes they've witnessed: how the light has shifted as the mountain's position relative to the sun has changed over the years, how certain plants have begun to develop new relationships with the water systems, how the Remnants have started to exhibit behaviors that weren't part of their original design.

They are, in their way, scientists and artists both—documenting a living system that they are simultaneously creating and preserving. Their journals, written on paper made from plant fibers and bound in leather softened by decades of moisture, record not just the mechanical details of maintenance but the subtle poetry of growth and adaptation. They write about the quality of steam on different days, the way certain combinations of humidity and temperature create miniature weather systems within the dome, the dreams they sometimes share that seem to come from the Conservatory itself.

For they have begun to suspect—though none would say it directly—that their careful tending has awakened something in the place. The Conservatory has begun to dream. In the deep circulation of its systems, in the patient exchange between plant and metal, water and air, something like consciousness has begun to stir. The Curators feel it in the way the building seems to anticipate their needs, the way problems resolve themselves before becoming critical, the way the whole system pulses with something that feels increasingly like intention.

They tend it still, these quiet keepers of forgotten steam, but they do so now with the reverence of gardeners who have realized that what they thought they were cultivating has begun, in its own slow way, to cultivate them in return.
