The Kithara dwell here, their bodies shaped by generations of arboreal life in this perpetual greenhouse. They are tall and willowy, with limbs that seem impossibly long and graceful—arms that can span twice their height, fingers that stretch like tender shoots. Their skin carries a subtle green undertone, not quite chlorophyll but close enough to suggest some ancient symbiosis, and it glistens always with a fine sheen of moisture that helps them regulate temperature in the humid heat. When they move through the canopy, they flow like wind through leaves, their elongated forms perfectly adapted to slip between branches and twist around obstacles without breaking stride.

Their hair grows in fibrous strands that they never cut, instead training it like aerial roots to grow in useful directions—some let it cascade down to form natural rope, others weave it with vines to create living headpieces that bloom with small flowers and catch dewdrops. Children are born with pale, almost translucent hair that darkens as they age, developing the same subtle green tinge as their skin. The eldest among them have hair so darkly verdant it's almost indistinguishable from moss, and they often let epiphytes take root in it, wearing gardens on their heads like crowns of living jewels.

Their eyes have adapted to the filtered light of the forest interior—large and dark with pupils that dilate wide in the perpetual twilight below the canopy, yet contract to pinpricks when they climb to the bright upper reaches. The irises show flecks of amber and gold, like sunlight caught in tree sap. They see in ways that outsiders struggle to understand, perceiving the health of plants by subtle color variations invisible to others, reading the moisture content of air by how it refracts light through the mist.

The Kithara's hands are perhaps their most remarkable feature. Their fingers are extraordinarily sensitive and dexterous, able to feel the flow of sap beneath bark, sense the vibrations that travel through wood when someone walks on a distant branch, distinguish between dozens of different root textures in complete darkness. They grow their fingernails long and shape them into delicate tools—some curved for stripping bark, others pointed for detailed carving, still others broad and flat for smoothing surfaces. These nails are incredibly strong, infused with minerals from the volcanic soil that they absorb through their plant-heavy diet.

Children are born knowing how to grip, their tiny hands instinctively wrapping around branches before they can even focus their eyes. By the time they walk—which they do remarkably early, driven by the vertical nature of their world—they can already climb simple vines. Their first years are spent learning to read the forest's moods, to tell which branches will bear weight and which are too young or diseased, to sense the difference between the safe sway of a healthy tree and the dangerous lean of one about to fall.

As they mature, the Kithara develop an almost supernatural awareness of their environment. They can tell when it will rain by the way certain leaves curl, predict where new springs will emerge by feeling the subtle warmth in stone through their bare feet, navigate in complete darkness by following the scent trails of specific flowering vines. Their culture treats the forest not as a resource to be harvested but as an extension of their own bodies—they feel physical pain when major trees die, experience euphoria when the rains bring the forest into full bloom.

The elderly Kithara become increasingly plant-like themselves. Their movements slow to match the patient pace of growing wood. Their skin develops a bark-like texture in patches, especially on their hands and feet where they most frequently touch living wood. Some develop what they call "root-sense"—the ability to perceive the underground networks of fungal communication that connect the forest's trees. These elders often spend their final years partially buried in the rich volcanic soil, tending to seedlings with a care that borders on the mystical.

Their society is organized like the forest itself—in layers and interconnected networks rather than rigid hierarchies. The canopy-walkers, who spend their time in the bright upper reaches, serve as scouts and weather-readers, their sun-darkened skin marked with intricate tattoos that map wind patterns and seasonal changes. The mid-canopy dwellers are the builders and shapers, training the trees that form their architecture, weaving the root-walls and guiding the growth of living tools. The ground-walkers, rare and revered, tend to the geothermal springs and the deep root systems, their pale skin adapted to the twilight world of the forest floor.

Communication among the Kithara happens as much through touch as through voice. They tap complex messages into tree trunks, the vibrations traveling through the wood to listeners hundreds of feet away. Lovers court by braiding their hair together with flowering vines, creating temporary gardens that bloom and fade with their romance. Agreements are sealed by jointly tending a young plant, the health of their shared garden serving as a living symbol of their relationship's vitality.

Their children are often born in specially cultivated tree-hollows lined with the softest moss, their first breaths perfumed with the gentle fragrance of night-blooming flowers. From birth, each child is paired with a seedling of their own, and the growth of their tree-partner serves as a measure of their development. When Kithara die, their bodies are placed in hollow logs and buried in the rich soil, their decomposition nurturing new growth that their descendants will shape into tomorrow's living architecture.

The Kithara dream in photosynthesis and root-networks, their sleep synchronized with the forest's own circadian rhythms. They wake with the first light filtering down through the canopy and drowse as the mist thickens in the evening heat. In their deepest sleep, some say they join the conversation of trees—the slow, patient dialogue of sap and seasons, growth and decay, that has continued unbroken since the first seeds took root in the cooling volcanic soil.
