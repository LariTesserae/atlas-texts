The people of Kythara move with a peculiar grace born of necessity—bodies that have learned to read the subtle tremors that precede new growth, feet that instinctively find purchase on stone still deciding its final shape. They are small in stature, most no taller than a child of twelve summers, with hands grown broad and sensitive from constant work with living rock. Their skin bears a faint luminescence, as if years of breathing the mountain's mineral-rich exhalations have left them touched by its inner light.

Their eyes have adapted too—larger than they should be, with irises that shift between deep amber and volcanic black depending on the light. They see patterns in stone that others would miss: the hairline fractures that tomorrow will become handholds, the subtle color changes that herald a new spring's emergence. When they watch the mountain's movements, their pupils dilate like those of creatures accustomed to reading meaning in shadow and thermal glow.

The Kytharans dress practically in garments woven from the fibers of thermal-spring plants—clothing that breathes and flexes with the humid air, that doesn't mind being soaked by sudden mists or warmed by unexpected vents. Their fabrics shimmer slightly, threads shot through with volcanic glass so fine it's nearly invisible, creating garments that seem to hold light within their weave.

They are a people who speak in whispers and gestures, their language evolved for a world where sound carries consequence. Their words are soft, sibilant things that mimic the sounds of their environment—the hiss of steam, the whisper of settling stone, the gentle percussion of water on rock. But their hands speak volumes, fingers dancing in complex patterns that convey not just meaning but emotion, urgency, even poetry. A master builder can conduct an entire construction project through gesture alone, their hands describing architectural intentions while their ears remain tuned to the mountain's deeper voice.

Children here learn to crawl by following the warm channels where new stone emerges, their small bodies naturally seeking the comfort of the mountain's heat. They play games of prediction—guessing where tomorrow's growth will appear, racing to spot the first signs of a forming thermal pool. Their laughter is rare but musical when it comes, quickly hushed by parents who've learned that joy, like sorrow, can resonate through stone in unexpected ways.

The eldest among them carry themselves with a particular stillness, as if they've learned to match their internal rhythms to the mountain's patient pulse. These are the Listeners—individuals who've developed an almost supernatural sensitivity to Kythara's moods. They can predict major shifts days in advance, sensing the deep preparations the mountain makes before significant growth. Their hair has gone white early, bleached by years of mineral springs, and their hands shake slightly—not from age, but from constant attunement to tremors others cannot feel.

Among the Listeners is Veth, whose fingers have grown so sensitive she can read the mountain's intentions through touch alone. She places her palms against newly formed walls and knows whether they'll hold or shift by morning. Her eyes have gone completely black, pupils expanded permanently to catch every nuance of the settlement's strange light. When she speaks—which is rarely—her voice carries harmonics that seem to resonate with the stone itself.

The builders work in fluid partnerships, their movements choreographed by years of shared response to the mountain's changes. They've developed an intuitive understanding of volcanic glass, shaping obsidian tools that sing softly when they cut true, that grow warm in the hand when the stone they're working is ready to yield. Master builder Kiros has hands scarred by decades of work, but his scars form patterns—maps of every major growth cycle he's witnessed, each mark a memory of adaptation and survival.

The children show a different kind of adaptation altogether. The youngest generation displays an almost symbiotic relationship with their environment—they seem to know instinctively where new passages will form, where thermal pools will appear. Seven-year-old Mira can predict the emergence of new springs with uncanny accuracy, her small body drawn to spots that will bubble with warm water before dawn. She speaks less than others her age, but when she does, her words carry the odd resonance of someone who's learned language from stone as much as from human voices.

There are the Tenders, too—individuals who've taken responsibility for the settlement's stranger inhabitants. They care for the translucent organisms in the thermal pools with tools made from crystallized spring minerals, instruments that won't disturb the delicate balance these creatures require. Tender Kyth moves through the settlement like a choreographer of life itself, anticipating which pools will appear, preparing suitable environments, even coaxing the mysterious organisms to adapt when their homes shift too quickly.

The not-quite-marmots have formed their own relationships with the Kytharans. These creatures, with intelligence bright in eyes too knowing for ordinary animals, have learned to work alongside the builders. They point with tiny paws toward instabilities in stone, chatter warnings before major shifts. Some of them seem to understand human gesture-language, responding to complex instructions with an accuracy that suggests true comprehension rather than mere training.

At night, when the mountain's growth slows to its deepest rhythm, the Kytharans gather in their shifting plaza. They don't sing—sound is too dangerous—but they move in patterns that create a kind of visual music, bodies swaying in harmony with the thermal currents, hands painting stories in the phosphorescent air. These evening gatherings are part meditation, part planning session, part celebration of another day survived in partnership with their impossible home.

They are dreamers who've learned to dream in stone, pragmatists who've made peace with impermanence, and above all, they are a people who understand that sometimes the greatest act of human will is knowing when to surrender to something larger than yourself. In their patient tending of Kythara's birth, they've become something new themselves—not quite human in the way their ancestors were, but something more suited to a world still learning its own shape.

Each morning, as new ridges of basalt shoulder up through the earth, the Kytharans wake not with fear but with curiosity, ready to discover what their mountain has decided to become overnight, ready to adapt once again to the endless, beautiful surprise of living inside a landscape that refuses to hold still.
