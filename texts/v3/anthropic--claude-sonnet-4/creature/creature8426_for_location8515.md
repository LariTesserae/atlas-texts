They call themselves the Woven, though they were not always so intertwined with this place. The first generation came as miners and engineers, drawn by promises of rich veins and steady work. But three generations in the Sunken Foundries changes a bloodline in ways that surface folk find difficult to comprehend.

The Woven walk with a particular stillness, their movements synchronized to rhythms that newcomers cannot immediately perceive. Their skin carries a faint luminescence—not quite phosphorescence, more like the gentle gleam of pearls—that becomes more pronounced the deeper in the foundries they were born. Their breathing apparatus, which seemed like protective equipment to their grandparents, has become something closer to symbiosis. The devices no longer merely filter the strange, humid air but help process it, extracting nutrients that conventional lungs cannot capture. Children born in the seventh tier emerge from the womb already wearing these brass-and-bone constructs, their tiny chest cavities having developed around them.

But it's their hands that tell the truest story of their transformation. The Woven's fingers are longer than they should be, more articulated, with additional joints that allow them to work equally well with wrench and root, to feel the pulse of both hydraulic systems and sap flow. Their palms bear calluses arranged in spiral patterns—not from tool use alone, but from their practice of reading the grove-machines through touch, learning to distinguish between the hundreds of different fluids that flow through the hybrid systems.

The tenders represent the deepest expression of this evolution. They move through their work with a dancer's precision, their heavily modified breathing apparatus connected by umbilical tubes to reservoirs they carry on their backs—reservoirs that contain not just filtered air, but distilled essences drawn from the grove-machines themselves. Over years of this symbiotic relationship, their consciousness has begun to extend beyond the boundaries of their individual bodies. They think partly with their own minds, partly with the distributed intelligence that runs through the root networks and mechanical systems.

Some tenders have begun to lose the ability to speak in conventional language. Instead, they communicate through a complex system of whistles and hums that carry meaning through the foundries' acoustic properties. These sounds trigger responses in both the mechanical systems and the plant networks—a pressure valve releases here, a vine shifts position there. What appears to be maintenance work is actually a form of conversation conducted at the level of infrastructure.

The younger Woven, particularly those born in the middle tiers, exist in a curious state between their ancestors' humanity and whatever their children might become. They retain individual consciousness and the ability to form normal social bonds, but their perceptions have expanded to encompass the foundries as a living system. They can sense structural stress through vibrations in the walkways, predict weather patterns by the taste of the air, and know when the depth-animals are moving through areas too dark for normal vision to penetrate.

These depth-animals—creatures that have adapted to the foundries' unique environment—live in a complex relationship with the Woven. The pale fishing birds nest only where tenders have prepared specific perches, their synchronized movements reflecting the same underlying rhythms that guide the Woven's work. The dark shapes that patrol the walkways are indeed cats, but cats whose neural patterns have been subtly altered by drinking from the water channels. They serve as early warning systems, their behavior patterns so precisely calibrated to the foundries' cycles that experienced Woven can read them like instruments.

In the deepest tier live the Rooted—Woven who have chosen to complete the transformation their bloodline has been undergoing. Their lower extremities have fused with the root networks, their circulatory systems connected directly to the grove-machines' flow of amber fluids. They exist in a state of expanded consciousness that encompasses vast portions of the foundries' infrastructure, their individual personalities dissolved into something larger while still retaining core memories and emotional connections.

The Rooted serve as the foundries' living archive and decision-making system. When new tiers need to be carved, when water flow patterns require adjustment, when the balance between organic and mechanical systems shifts—these choices emerge from the collective processing of the Rooted minds. They experience time differently, thinking in cycles of years rather than moments, feeling the foundries' growth as directly as a person feels their own heartbeat.

Children in the foundries play games that would bewilder surface dwellers—hide and seek through root networks, contests to see who can maintain stillness long enough for vines to begin treating them as part of the infrastructure, racing competitions along paths that exist only when certain mechanical cycles align with organic growth patterns. Their laughter echoes strangely through the hollow center, often triggering harmonic responses from the metal framework of abandoned construction projects.

The Woven's social structure reflects their hybrid nature. Leadership emerges not through dominance or election, but through demonstrated ability to maintain harmony between the various systems. The most respected individuals are those who can facilitate communication between the purely human inhabitants of the upper tiers and the post-human Rooted of the depths.

Courtship among the Woven involves elaborate rituals where potential partners demonstrate their compatibility by working together on grove-machine maintenance. Successful couples develop synchronized breathing patterns that allow them to share the same filtration systems—an intimacy more profound than surface dwellers could imagine.

They dream, when they sleep, of roots and circuits, of water finding its path through stone and metal, of the patient intelligence that grows deeper with each passing generation. Their nightmares feature the surface world—vast open spaces without the comforting weight of stone above, air too thin to sustain their modified physiology, isolation from the network of relationships that gives their lives meaning.

The Woven know that they are becoming something unprecedented, and they embrace this transformation with a serenity that borders on the religious. They see themselves not as human beings adapting to an unusual environment, but as participants in an evolutionary leap—the first successful merger of organic intelligence with industrial systems. Each generation grows more integrated, more capable of perceiving and manipulating the foundries' complex hybrid nature.

To outsiders, the Sunken Foundries might appear to be humanity's strangest industrial accident. To the Woven, it represents humanity's most promising future—a glimpse of what becomes possible when the boundaries between built and grown, individual and system, human and environment, finally dissolve into something richer and more resilient than what came before.
