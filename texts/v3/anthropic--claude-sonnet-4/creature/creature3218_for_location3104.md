In the carved channels of Kethara live the Vethani, a people shaped by perpetual mist and patient stone. Their skin holds the color of wet volcanic rock – dark gray with subtle striations that catch light like polished obsidian. These markings are not tattoos or paint, but natural patterns that emerge in childhood and deepen with age, following lines of stress and thought across their bodies like the fracture patterns their ancestors learned to read in stone.

They are tall and lean, built for the vertical world of their terraced city. Their fingers end in broad, sensitive pads that can detect the subtlest variations in stone texture and moisture content. When they walk, they move with deliberate precision, each footfall placed with the certainty of someone who has spent generations learning to read the language of rock and water. Their clothing is woven from fibers of the waxy-leafed trees – fabric that sheds moisture while breathing with the humid air, dyed in shades that mirror the city's palette of grays and deep browns.

The Vethani speak in low, resonant tones that carry through the porous stone without echoing. Their conversations unfold slowly, with long pauses that might seem uncomfortable to outsiders but feel natural in a place where time moves to geological rhythms. They have dozens of words for different types of silence, and their language includes clicking sounds made by tongue against stone – a practice that began as a way to test the acoustic properties of newly carved spaces but evolved into a form of communication that bypasses the need for voice entirely.

Their society is organized around the Moisture Keepers, individuals born with an almost supernatural ability to sense the movement of water through stone. These rare few can predict exactly where new seepage will appear, which basins will overflow during the heavy mist seasons, and how to carve channels that will guide water precisely where it is needed. They hold no formal authority, but the city's growth and daily life revolve around their quiet guidance.

Most Vethani are crafters and builders, their hands trained from childhood in the ancient techniques of working with volcanic stone. They use no metal tools on the sacred rock of their city – only implements of hardwood, bone, and carefully selected stones. Each family maintains workshops in the lower levels of their buildings, carved spaces where the sound of careful chiseling provides a gentle rhythm that neighbors recognize and find comforting.

The children of Kethara grow up as fluid as water, learning to climb the carved channels and leap between terraces with casual grace. They play games that involve reading stone patterns and predicting where droplets will fall, developing the sensitivity to their environment that will serve them throughout life. By adolescence, they can navigate the entire city in complete darkness, guided by sound, touch, and the subtle air currents that speak of distant spaces and hidden water sources.

There are also the Stillborn among them – individuals who seem to absorb sound and movement rather than create it. These quiet souls often become the city's record-keepers and deep contemplatives. They carve the most intricate patterns into the walls of public spaces, creating visual rhythms that echo the mathematical precision of the geological formations. Their presence has a calming effect; rooms grow quieter when they enter, and even the constant drip of water seems to soften.

The Vethani age slowly and die quietly, their bodies gradually taking on more of the stone's mineral content until their final breath simply stops, leaving behind forms that seem to settle naturally into the carved niches they have prepared for themselves. These spaces become shrines where descendants come to listen to the particular quality of silence each ancestor contributed to the city's acoustic landscape.

They dream collectively during the deepest part of night, when the mist settles most heavily into the channels. These shared visions often involve the mountain's future growth, the slow emergence of new stone formations, and the patient work of water carving ever-deeper patterns into their world. Upon waking, they sometimes find that new channels have appeared overnight, carved by hands that moved without conscious thought, guided by dreams of water's patient wisdom.

In their marketplaces, goods are often exchanged without words, through a complex system of stone arrangements that convey value, need, and availability. The sound of these stones being placed and rearranged creates a subtle music that serves as the city's closest approximation to song – rhythmic, mathematical, and as patient as the mountains themselves.
