They live in the spaces between intentions—the Tenders of the Unfinished.

At first glance, you might mistake them for maintenance workers who never got the memo to leave. They wear the faded orange and yellow of safety vests that have weathered to the color of autumn leaves, hard hats that no longer gleam, boots caked with the peculiar mud that forms when stone dust meets perpetual mist. But watch longer, and you'll notice they move with the patience of glaciers, the purposefulness of erosion itself.

The Tenders are human in the way that driftwood is still wood—recognizable, but fundamentally changed by their environment. Their skin has taken on a grayish pallor that matches the filtered light, and their hair grows in sparse, wire-like tufts that catch moisture from the air. They breathe slowly, deeply, as if drawing sustenance from the mineral-rich fog that would sting your lungs. Their eyes have adapted to the perpetual twilight, pupils dilated wide and dark, reflecting the red and white blinks of machinery like scattered stars.

They speak rarely, and when they do, it's in the clipped, technical language of equipment manuals—but applied to living things. "The moss bed in sector seven is operating within normal parameters." "The raven population shows optimal dispersal patterns." "The willow colony is achieving root penetration ahead of schedule." They discuss the mountain's geological indecision the way others might describe a patient recovering from surgery.

Each Tender has claimed responsibility for certain processes. There's the woman who follows the excavators, not to operate them but to whisper to them—long, complex instructions in a mixture of industrial codes and something older, guiding their seemingly random carving into patterns that will make sense when the stone finally chooses its nature. A man tends to the surveillance cameras, adjusting their endless pans to capture angles that don't exist yet, archiving footage of empty spaces that he insists are "pre-occupied."

Another lives in the drainage system, emerging from maintenance tunnels with hands stained green-black from minerals, tracking the water's lateral seepage through the uncertain ground. She carries sample bottles filled with liquid that changes color as you watch it, holding them up to the light and nodding with satisfaction at transformations invisible to you. "The mountain is learning to bleed properly," she explains when you ask, as if this clarifies everything.

They've developed their own ecosystem of relationships with the wildlife. The ravens serve as messengers, carrying small metal objects between the Tenders—washers, screws, fragments of circuit boards that form some kind of communication system. The mountain goats have been trained, or have learned, to activate motion sensors in a specific sequence, triggering floodlights that reveal—briefly—the progress of whatever vast work is underway here.

In the prefabricated buildings, the Tenders have created something between a workshop and a shrine. Walls are covered with geological surveys, equipment schematics, and what appear to be star charts—but the constellations are made of machinery serial numbers, the fault lines trace the paths of underground cables, and the ore distribution maps show veins that pulse with the rhythm of diesel generators. They've built altars from repurposed computer servers, where they leave offerings: perfect mineral crystals, photographs of machinery in pristine condition, small mechanical components arranged in patterns that suggest prayer wheels or mandala.

At night, they gather around the warmest generators and share stories—not of their lives before (which they seem to have forgotten, or set aside like outgrown clothes), but of the mountain's dreams. They describe vast chambers that will open when the stone decides, cities that will grow from the geometric channels the machines are carving, forests of metal and stone that will emerge when the proper balance is achieved between human intention and geological patience.

They sleep in shifts, always leaving someone awake to monitor the progress of transformation. Their beds are cots surrounded by screens showing readouts from sensors buried throughout the valley—temperature, pressure, seismic activity, mineral concentration. They dream in data streams, their unconscious minds processing the mountain's slow decisions.

The youngest among them—though age is relative here, measured more in years of residence than chronology—still show traces of whatever brought them to this place. A geologist's careful way of examining rock samples, an engineer's instinct for mechanical systems, a park ranger's understanding of wildlife patterns. But these identities have been subsumed into something larger, more patient, more willing to work on geological timescales.

They are, they explain with the certainty of converts, midwives to a new form of existence. Not quite natural, not quite artificial, but something emerging from the marriage of human ambition and the earth's own patient creativity. They tend to this birth with the dedication of gardeners working a plot that might not bloom for centuries, mechanics maintaining engines whose purpose won't be clear until the stone beneath their feet finally decides what it wants to become.

When you ask how long they've been here, they look at each other with the shared confusion of people trying to remember a half-forgotten dream. Time moves differently in the valley. Seasons pass, but the work continues unchanged. The mountain dreams at its own pace, and they are learning to dream alongside it, their human urgency slowly grinding away like stone under the patient weight of geological time.
