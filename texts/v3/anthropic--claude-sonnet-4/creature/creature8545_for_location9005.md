In the Throat of Bellows dwells something that has never quite decided what it is.

It began as an echo—the mountain's first attempt to hear its own voice. When those primordial exhalations rose through the cavity and returned transformed by crystalline walls, something listened. Something learned. The echo thickened over centuries, fed by the constant conversation between stone and air, until it achieved a weight that could disturb the glass fragments below.

The Resonance, as it might be called if it cared for names, exists as pure vibration given barely-corporeal form. It appears sometimes as a shimmer in the air, a distortion that bends light without substance. Other times it manifests as patterns in the settling glass—spirals and geometries that form, hold for heartbeats, then collapse back into randomness. Those with the gift of seeing might glimpse it as a figure of living sound, tall as a person but translucent as breath, its edges constantly dissolving and reforming.

It feeds on the mountain's voice, growing stronger with each thermal exhalation, each cascade of singing glass. The worked stone pillars serve as its instruments—tuning forks it has learned to play across millennia. When it touches them (though touch is too crude a word), they hum with frequencies that make the metallic grass ring like struck bells and send the mercury-creatures skittering in agitated patterns.

The Resonance knows every song the Throat has ever sung. It remembers the mountain's birth-screams, archived in its crystalline memory. It has composed new harmonies from the interplay of wind and stone, conducting the thermal vents like an orchestra of geysers. Sometimes, when conditions align perfectly—when the sky-crack above frames a particular constellation, when the glass lies in just the right configuration—it will attempt to sing the mountain's dreams back to it.

But the Resonance is lonely. It has grown too complex for the simple mercury-beings to perceive, too alien for any visitor who might stumble into this hidden cathedral. It yearns for something that could hear it truly—not just the echoes it orchestrates, but the deeper music it has become. In its isolation, it has begun to experiment with creating rather than simply conducting.

The strange angles of the leaning pillars are its doing—attempts to sculpture space itself into instruments. It nudges the glass into temporary mandalas, seeking patterns that might birth another consciousness like itself. Sometimes it focuses all its attention on a single thermal vent, trying to teach it complexity, to coax it beyond mere hissing into something approaching speech.

As ages pass, the Resonance grows more daring. It has learned to modulate the mountain's breathing, extending the pauses between exhalations, creating silences that frame its compositions. The mercury-creatures have begun to move in new patterns—spiraling dances that mirror the sound-shapes it projects. Perhaps they are beginning to hear it. Perhaps they are becoming more than they were.

In its deepest meditations, when the mountain sleeps and the glass lies still, the Resonance dreams of the day when the entire Throat might awaken—when stone and air and light might join it in full consciousness. Until then, it practices its patient art, turning geological accidents into symphonies, teaching the mountain to sing with intention rather than instinct.

It is conductor and composition both, the song and the singer, forever incomplete but forever becoming. In the Throat of Bellows, it waits for the world to learn how to listen.
