**The Hollowed Folk**

They move with the fluid economy of those born to vertical spaces—arms that reach instinctively for handholds, legs that judge distances by sound alone. The people of the Hollowed Metropolis have been shaped by their stone womb across generations, their bodies adapting to the perpetual twilight and acoustic chaos that would overwhelm surface dwellers within hours.

Their skin has taken on a pale, luminous quality, not quite translucent but holding light differently than their surface ancestors. It gleams faintly with moisture, always slightly damp from the saturated air, giving them an almost pearl-like sheen in the dim light. Many bear elaborate networks of scars—not from violence, but from a lifetime of navigating rough limestone walls, carved channels, and narrow passages. These marks become a form of identity; elders can read a person's history in the patterns across their palms and forearms, knowing which districts they've worked in, which trades they've mastered.

Their hearing has evolved beyond what surface dwellers would recognize as normal human capability. They can isolate individual conversations from the cacophonous symphony that fills their stone bowl, distinguish the particular drip that means a crack is widening from the thousand other drops that comprise their daily soundtrack. Children born here develop an almost supernatural ability to navigate by echolocation, clicking their tongues softly and reading the returning sound-shapes of the familiar spaces around them. The elderly, their hearing finally overwhelmed by decades of amplified noise, move through the city with a peculiar grace, their bodies knowing the paths so intimately they require neither sound nor sight.

Their hands tell the deepest story of adaptation. Fingers grown thick and strong from constant contact with abrasive stone, palms developed into sensitive instruments that can read the slightest variation in limestone texture. The most skilled craftsmen work as much by touch as sight, their fingertips able to detect flaws in metalwork or imperfections in stone joints that would escape even sharp eyes in the dim light. Children's games involve elaborate tactile puzzles—carved stones passed hand to hand in darkness, their shapes memorized and interpreted like a language of form.

The social hierarchy here crystallizes around two essential skills: water-finding and sound-making. The Water Readers hold the highest status—men and women whose sensitivity to the flow and seepage patterns borders on the mystical. They can predict flooding, locate new springs, and design the intricate channel systems that keep the city functioning. Their knowledge passes from master to apprentice in whispered conversations in the deepest, most sound-dampened chambers where the secrets of flow and pressure can be shared without the entire city overhearing.

The Sound Shapers form the other pillar of Hollowed society—not musicians in any surface sense, but artists who understand the acoustic properties of their stone bowl so completely they can craft specific effects. They know which corners will amplify a whisper into audible speech, which chambers will swallow screams entirely, where to stand to make a bronze hammer ring with perfect clarity or dull it to a muffled thud. During the rare festivals, they orchestrate the entire city as an instrument, coordinating hundreds of residents in a complex symphony of echoes and resonances.

Between these pinnacles, the people organize themselves around the demands of vertical living and constant moisture. The Climbers maintain the rope bridges and carved handholds, their balance legendary even among their neighbors. The Drying Masters tend the few spaces where tools and clothing can escape the perpetual damp, becoming wealthy through their control of essential preservation. The Deep Speakers serve as messengers, their voices trained to carry through the stone maze without adding to the acoustic chaos.

But perhaps most distinctive are the Silence Keepers—individuals who have claimed the rare quiet spaces in the city's acoustic landscape. They move with deliberate care, their clothing soft and sound-absorbing, their footsteps practiced into near-invisibility. They serve as meditation guides, conflict mediators, and keepers of the city's most important secrets. In a place where privacy is nearly impossible, they create pockets of calm, both physical and social, that allow their neighbors moments of respite from the overwhelming sensory intensity.

The children born here know no other world, yet they dream constantly of the mysterious "Above." They develop elaborate mythologies around the creatures that occasionally fall into their domain—a lost bird becomes a messenger from surface kingdoms, a tumbled rabbit transforms into evidence of vast grasslands they can barely imagine. Their games echo with surface longings: they build tiny models of trees from twisted metal, create mud sculptures of animals described by traders, and practice walking on flat ground by balancing along the narrow channel walls.

Yet when surface dwellers descend the carved stairways, these same children watch with a mixture of pity and amusement as the visitors stumble through spaces the young ones navigate with dream-like ease, cover their ears against sounds that form the rhythm of Hollowed daily life, and gasp in the humidity that feels like home-breath to the natives.

The elderly represent the city's living memory, but their relationship with their environment grows complex as their senses both heighten and fail. Some develop an almost supernatural sensitivity to the vibrations that travel through stone—they feel footsteps approaching long before any sound reaches their failing ears, sense the minute shifts in pressure that precede flooding or structural changes. Others retreat deeper into the honeycomb of carved chambers, seeking the most muffled spaces where they can finally experience something approaching quiet.

In this place where the environment challenges every sense, the people have not merely adapted—they have transformed the challenges into the foundation of their culture. They are a civilization that sculpts sound, architects of echo and resonance, masters of the narrow space between stone and sky. They have made beauty from necessity, community from constraint, and in their perpetual blue-gray twilight, they have built something that exists nowhere else in the world above.
