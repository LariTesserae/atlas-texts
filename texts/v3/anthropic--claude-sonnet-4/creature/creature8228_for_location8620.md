In the Sunken Groves dwell the Moss-Tenders, a people who have become as much a part of the ecosystem as the bryophytes they cultivate. They move through their waterlogged domain with an unhurried grace that matches the rhythm of slow-growing things, their footsteps so practiced on the slick stones that they barely disturb the surface tension of the shallow channels.

Their bodies have adapted to this realm of constant moisture and gentle cold. Their skin holds a subtle greenish undertone, not quite human pale but not entirely other, as if they have absorbed something of the lichen they live among. Their hair grows thick and slightly coarse, naturally resistant to the perpetual dampness, often wound with living strands of moss that they tend as carefully as gardeners might tend roses. When they breathe, small puffs of vapor escape their lips even in the warmer hours, their metabolism having adjusted to extract every bit of warmth from the cool, humid air.

The Moss-Tenders dress in garments woven from processed plant fibers—soft fabrics made from the inner bark of the water-adapted trees that ring their settlement, dyed in subtle earth tones using the very fungi that spread across their walls. Their clothing is designed to work with moisture rather than against it: loose-fitting tunics and trousers that dry quickly, boots with soles molded from tree resin that grip the wet stones, and cloaks that shed water while allowing their bodies to breathe. They wear no metal ornaments, for metal rusts too quickly here, but instead adorn themselves with polished stones, carved bits of calcified mineral deposits, and living jewelry—small air plants and delicate ferns that they coax to grow in their hair and around their wrists.

Their eyes have developed a particular sensitivity to the pearl-soft light that filters through the mist. Where others might squint in the dim conditions, the Moss-Tenders see clearly, able to distinguish between dozens of different types of growth on a single wall, to spot the subtle signs that indicate where a new spring might emerge, or where a building's foundation is beginning to shift. Their pupils remain slightly dilated even in brighter light, giving them an otherworldly, contemplative appearance.

The children of the Groves grow up amphibious, as comfortable wading through knee-deep channels as walking on dry ground. They learn early to read the language of water—which streams are safe to drink from, which pools harbor useful microorganisms, where the current indicates a new fissure opening in the bedrock below. Play becomes learning as they chase patterns of mold growth across building faces, or compete to see who can guide a floating leaf through the most complex route of interconnected waterways.

The adults organize their society around the careful stewardship of their transformed city. They have Watchers who monitor the structural integrity of buildings, noting which ones are settling too quickly and might need to be evacuated or reinforced with carefully placed stones. Others serve as Stream-Guides, maintaining the channels that bring fresh water through their settlement, clearing blockages of fallen debris or redirecting flow when the natural course threatens to undermine a foundation.

Most specialized are the Growth-Weavers, masters of the symbiotic arts who understand exactly which combinations of moss and lichen will best preserve a crumbling wall, which fungi can be encouraged to strengthen wooden beams from within, and how to cultivate the particular species of bacteria that help prevent harmful decay. They work with their hands deep in the living matrix of their city, feeling for the pulse of growth and decline, making adjustments so subtle that the changes seem entirely natural.

The elders of the Moss-Tenders are living libraries of their people's slow wisdom. Their memories stretch back through generations of gradual change, holding knowledge of which buildings first began to list, where the earliest springs emerged, what the water levels were in seasons of unusual rain or drought. They speak in voices soft as the whisper of settling stones, their words chosen with the same patience their people apply to all things. When they gather to make decisions that will affect their community, they do so in council meetings that may last for days, each perspective considered as carefully as a Stream-Guide considers the placement of a single stone that might redirect a waterway.

Some among them, called the Deep-Listeners, have developed an almost mystical connection to the underground water systems. They can sense changes in pressure and flow through vibrations that travel up through the limestone bedrock, predicting where new springs will emerge or old ones fail. These individuals often spend long hours in meditation, standing motionless in the shallow pools with their bare feet on the stone, feeling for the heartbeat of their aquatic world.

The Moss-Tenders rarely venture beyond their transformed valley. The dry lands above feel harsh and brittle to them, lacking the gentle give of water-softened stone and the constant music of trickling streams. When they must travel to trade for goods they cannot produce—perhaps special stones from distant quarries, or seeds from plants that might adapt to their unique conditions—they do so quickly and return gratefully to their realm of mist and growing things.

They have no written language, but instead maintain their culture through patterns cultivated in the moss itself. Important stories, genealogies, and technical knowledge are encoded in the arrangements of different bryophyte species across designated walls. A trained reader can walk along these living texts and absorb generations of accumulated wisdom, though the reading requires years to master and must be refreshed constantly as the living words grow and change.

Their music echoes the sounds of their environment—wordless melodies that mimic the falling of water, the settling of stones, the almost inaudible whisper of expanding moss. They create instruments from hollow reeds and resonant stones, but often prefer to sing unaccompanied, their voices blending with the ambient sounds of their city until it becomes difficult to tell where human music ends and the song of the place itself begins.

The Moss-Tenders understand themselves not as conquerors of their environment but as partners in an ongoing collaboration. They see their role as guiding rather than controlling the transformation of their ancient city, encouraging beneficial changes while gently redirecting those that might prove destructive. They measure success not in the accumulation of wealth or the speed of progress, but in the health and stability of the living system they help maintain.

In their hands, ruin becomes renewal, and what others might see as decay, they cultivate as a new form of flourishing. They are gardeners of time itself, tending to changes that unfold across decades with the same careful attention others might give to the turning of the seasons.
