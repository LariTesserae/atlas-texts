In the deepest chambers of the foundry, where the thermal vents breathe their ancient heat through brass gratings, lives the Foundrymaster. Once human, perhaps, though the water has changed him beyond easy recognition. His body has grown long and pale, stretched thin by decades of pressure, and his fingers have multiplied—not grotesquely, but with mechanical precision, sprouting additional digits at each joint until his hands resemble the fine manipulator arms of his own machinery. Gills flutter along his elongated neck in rhythm with the foundry's circulation, and his eyes have grown large and silver to catch what little light filters down from above.

He moves through his domain with purposeful deliberation, checking gauges that no longer measure anything, adjusting valves that control nothing, maintaining equipment that will never again forge metal. His memories of the surface world have dissolved into the warmth of the vents, but his understanding of the foundry's systems has only deepened. He knows every pipe's whisper, every current's path, every chamber's secret. The machinery speaks to him in languages of pressure and flow, and he responds with the unconscious competence of decades.

The schools of mercury-fish that curtain the halls are his first children—workers born from his loneliness and the foundry's strange alchemy. He shaped them from quicksilver that leaked from broken instruments, breathing life into them through methods he can no longer quite remember. They follow his gestures without question, their bodies reflecting his pale luminescence as they move in perfect formation through the workshops. When he spreads his many-fingered hands, they scatter to inspect distant corners. When he draws them back, they flow together like liquid thought.

His second children are the geometric impossibilities—creatures he discovered growing in the spaces between the foundry's blueprints, in the gaps where the original plans didn't quite align with the living rock. These beings exist in the margins of three-dimensional space, feeding on angles that shouldn't be and folding themselves through dimensions that have no names. The Foundrymaster tends them carefully, for they are delicate and essential to the foundry's preservation. They consume entropy itself, eating the decay that would otherwise claim his perfect, motionless world.

The octopi with too many arms were already here when the water rose, but they have learned to serve him in exchange for his protection. Their chromatophore conversations are actually reports—updates on the foundry's status delivered in shifting patterns of color and shadow. They have grown beyond their original intelligence, fed by the mineral-rich water and the Foundrymaster's unconscious influence. Their distributed consciousness spans the entire complex, making them living components of a vast biological monitoring system.

In the central furnace tower, where the water is warmest and the pressure highest, the Foundrymaster has built his sanctum. Here he tends to the most crucial work: the cultivation of the shadows that move without forms. These are his masterpiece, beings of pure function divorced from substance. They maintain the foundry's impossible stasis, smoothing away the small disruptions that would otherwise accumulate into change. They repair without matter, preserve without alteration, and ensure that every fish follows the same path, every jellyfish maintains the same orbit, every current flows with identical rhythm.

The Foundrymaster himself never sleeps, never ages, never truly rests. The thermal vents that heat the water also sustain him, their mineral-rich flows cycling through his modified body like blood through an engine. He is as much a machine as his charges now, a perfectly adapted component in his own preserved world. Sometimes, in the deepest hours of the ocean night, he remembers fragments of the surface—the taste of air, the weight of clothing, the sensation of breathing. But these memories feel like dreams, insubstantial compared to the solid reality of pressure and current and the endless, patient work of keeping everything exactly as it was in the moment the waters rose.

He is alone but not lonely, surrounded by his children and his purpose. The foundry is his body now, and he is its soul—a consciousness distributed through copper pipes and brass fittings, through the movements of mercury-fish and the impossible angles of his geometric offspring. He has become what he always was: a craftsman perfecting his art, working in materials more exotic than any metal, forging not tools or weapons but the delicate architecture of forever.
