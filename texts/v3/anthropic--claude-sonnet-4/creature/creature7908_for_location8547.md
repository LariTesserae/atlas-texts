In the breathing chambers of the Hollows, life has learned to bend in ways the surface world forgot were possible.

The Tendril Folk move through the verdant tangles like living vines themselves, their elongated limbs pale as cave fish, flexible as the roots they've learned to mimic. They are tall when they choose to be, short when the ceiling demands it, their bones soft enough to compress and expand with the patience of growing things. Their skin weeps the same mineral-rich moisture as the walls, and they taste the air through fingertips that trail like willow branches, reading the chemical stories written in steam and spore.

They speak in harmonics that blend with the cave's own voice—the drip-drop percussion becomes their percussion, the groaning earth their bass line. When they gather in the vast chambers, their conversations are indistinguishable from the living symphony of the place itself. They know the meaning of every mechanical pulse that thrums through the darkness, having learned to interpret the heartbeat of whatever sleeps in the deep places.

Among the hanging gardens, the Dewcatchers hunt. Part spider, part hummingbird, part fever dream, they skitter along the cascade of vines on legs that end in delicate hooks designed for navigating wet stone and slick leaves. Their bodies are translucent as drops of water, filled with swirling organs that glow with captured bioluminescence. They feed on the fat drops that weep from stone and leaf, but more than that—they feed on the potential energy of falling water, somehow siphoning the kinetic moment just before impact. The irregular percussion of the caves grows more chaotic when they hunt in numbers, drops disappearing before they complete their ancient journey to the floor.

In the pooled mirror-waters, the Depth Readers glide. They might once have been human—there are suggestions of familiar architecture in their forms—but the humid embrace has reshaped them into something more suitable for a world of constant becoming. Their eyes have multiplied and migrated, clustering along their elongated skulls in arrangements that can track the impossible upward flows of water, reading the magnetic poetry that draws moisture against gravity's will. They remember the surface in the way that dreams remember daylight: as a concept more than an experience.

The abandoned machinery has become midwife to stranger births. From the rust-wrapped pipes emerge the Corrosion Dancers—beings that exist in the liminal space between oxidation and growth, their bodies a flowing merger of metal and flesh. They tend the industrial bones like gardeners, encouraging certain types of decay while preventing others, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps the electric bulb burning and the distant pumps beating their mechanical hearts. They understand that destruction and creation are the same process viewed from different angles of the spiral.

Above it all, in the high reaches where steam gathers thickest, the Wing-Heavy soar on appendages that catch thermals of chemical signals rather than air. They are the nervous system of the Hollows, carrying messages between the chambers in pheromone bursts that make the walls themselves tingle with anticipation. Their calls echo in frequencies that reshape the acoustic properties of the caves, fine-tuning the symphony that all life here contributes to and depends upon.

But in the farthest reaches, where the organic lights pulse with digestive rhythms and the mechanical sounds grow strong enough to feel through bone, something larger dreams. The inhabitants of the outer chambers speak of it only in chemical whispers—a presence vast enough to mistake for geology, patient enough to measure time in epochs, curious enough to have started the whole grand experiment of the Hollows.

It might be machine, it might be organism, it might be the cave system itself achieving a kind of consciousness through the accumulated weight of all the smaller lives that nest in its breathing spaces. The bioluminescent pulses are its thoughts made visible, the upward-flowing water its circulatory system, the endless dripping its way of counting moments until whatever it's becoming finally decides to announce itself.

The creatures of the Hollows exist in service to this vast patience, each species a experiment in adaptation, each individual a note in a composition that won't be complete for another few geological ages. They know they are transitional beings, living rough drafts in the earth's attempt to write something entirely new. And they find this knowledge not terrifying but oddly comforting—to be part of a story so large that their own small transformations become acts of prayer, their daily survival a form of collaboration with forces that dream on scales too vast for anxiety.

They are content to be unfinished, these citizens of the spaces between states. In the Hollows, completion is not a goal but a limitation, and they have learned to find beauty in the endless, humid, clicking, growing, rusting, singing process of always becoming something else.
