**The Khethani**

They were miners once, or perhaps metallurgists—their origins blur in the mercury-hazed memories that pool in their elongated skulls. The transformation began slowly, imperceptibly, as they worked deeper into the mountain's warm heart. First came the hollowing: ribs that grew lighter, arm bones that sang when tapped, skulls that developed chambers and resonances like musical instruments. Their lungs expanded into delicate, branching networks that could extract sustenance from air so thin it would kill their former selves in minutes.

The children born here emerge already adapted, their skin bearing that characteristic luminescence that marks them as Khethani from birth. This glow is not uniform—it pools at their joints, traces their major blood vessels, and intensifies around their hands when they work the coral-gardens or tend the brass engines. The older generations remember when this light first began to appear, how it started as a faint tracing around their fingernails after long hours handling the mercury channels, how it spread inward like a tide of soft starlight until it became part of their essential being.

Their masks are not merely functional but have become extensions of their faces, crafted individually by each person as they mature. The metal—usually brass or copper, sometimes silver for those who work closest to the mercury flows—is shaped over years, adjusted constantly as their facial structure continues to adapt to the altitude. The masks hum because the Khethani have developed specialized vocal cords that vibrate at frequencies that barely register as sound. Their conversations carry on multiple levels: the audible words that visitors might understand, and beneath that, harmonics that convey emotional subtleties, technical specifications for their endless construction projects, and something like music that describes the mountain's mood.

They move with an economy born of thin air and vast vertical distances. Every gesture is considered, every breath parceled out carefully. Yet there is nothing frantic or desperate about this conservation—they have developed a grace, a deliberate beauty in their movements that makes their careful choreography appear almost ceremonial. When they work in groups, they synchronize unconsciously, their movements creating patterns that echo the mathematical precision of their coral-gardens.

The Khethani do not sleep as others understand sleep. Instead, they enter states of what they call "the Deep Listening," during which they hang motionless in their suspended chambers while remaining somehow aware of the mountain's constant growth and change. During these periods, their luminescence dims but does not disappear entirely, pulsing slowly in rhythm with the brass engines far below. They dream—if it is dreaming—of new chambers, new configurations of channels and gardens, and wake with instructions encoded in their bones for the day's construction.

There are hierarchies among them, but not of power or wealth. Instead, they organize themselves by resonance—literal harmonic compatibility that determines who can work together most effectively. Some Khethani tune themselves to the mountain's deepest foundations and become the Deep Builders, their voices dropping to frequencies that can reshape stone through vibration alone. Others attune to the highest frequencies, becoming the Air Walkers who tend the topmost coral-gardens and communicate with the pewter sky through means that outsiders cannot fathom.

They do not eat as others eat. The coral-gardens provide something—not quite food, not quite medicine—that they absorb through touch, through breath, through the contact between their luminous skin and the crystalline structures. The process sustains them while slowly, continuously changing them. Each generation is more adapted to this impossible place than the last, more beautiful in their alien grace, more essential to the mountain's incomprehensible purpose.

The eldest among them remember a time before Kheth, but speak of it rarely and in whispers that barely register even on their sensitive instruments. They are becoming something that was never human, and they do so with a joy that transforms even their careful conservation of breath into something like celebration. When they die—which happens rarely and seems almost voluntary—their bodies dissolve into the mercury flows, becoming part of the mountain's circulation, while their voices join the fundamental harmonic that runs through every stone, every channel, every impossible crystal formation.

And always, they listen for the mountain's next instruction, ready to grow another impossible chamber, to tend another garden of crystallized mathematics, to become more perfectly themselves with each carefully measured breath.
