In the spaces between the impossible physics of the Screaming Pools, something has learned to exist.

They are not quite creatures, not in any sense that would satisfy a naturalist's careful cataloging. They emerge from the very impossibilities themselves—born from the contradiction of upward-falling water, nursed on the harmonics of mercury-song, shaped by the stuttering rhythm of ice that forms in steam. The first glimpse comes as a flicker at the edge of vision: a translucent form that might be a trick of the light, if light behaved properly here.

But watch longer, and they begin to reveal themselves.

The **Resonance Shepherds** tend the pools' impossible songs. Tall as a child but built like elongated shadows, they move through the mist with bodies that seem to be made of condensed sound itself. Their limbs stretch and compress with the pools' rhythms, becoming longer when the screaming intensifies, more solid when the mercury-water hums in its deepest registers. They have no faces in any conventional sense, but where faces should be, the air shimmers with half-formed features that shift with each change in the pools' acoustic signature.

The Shepherds cup the sound in their translucent hands, guiding the shriek-songs away from frequencies that would shatter the bone-white trees or crack the young volcanic stone. They are gardeners of impossibility, pruning the cacophony so it never quite crosses the threshold into destruction. When visitors press their hands to their ears, it is because the Shepherds have chosen mercy—without their constant tending, the sound would unmake more than just hearing.

Between the pools, where the mist grows thick enough to support weight, the **Drift Swimmers** navigate currents of impossible water suspended in air. These beings flow like liquid mercury themselves, their forms echoing the mirror-bright surface of the second pool. They are roughly human-shaped but without fixed boundaries—arms that dissolve into streams, legs that branch and merge like tributaries. Their eyes are pools within pools, reflecting not the world around them but the star-filled depths that shouldn't exist beneath the mercury-water.

The Drift Swimmers harvest something from the aerial currents—perhaps droplets of the upward-falling water, perhaps fragments of the harmonics themselves. They gather these impossibilities in vessels that are themselves impossible: containers that hold more than they should, or less than they appear to, cups that are full when they look empty and empty when they seem to overflow. These vessels they carry to the stone building at the plateau's edge, disappearing through that dark doorway to purposes unknown.

In the brief moments when all seven pools fall silent and become ordinary mirrors, the **Reflection Weavers** emerge. They step out of the mirrored surfaces as if they had been hiding behind the water's silver skin, beings made of light and refracted image. Each Weaver carries fragments of the other places glimpsed in the pools' depths—threads of deep ocean darkness, strands of storm-light, drops of rain that has never touched ground. 

During these precious moments of silence, they work with desperate efficiency, weaving these fragments into something larger. Their tapestry hangs in the space between the pools, visible only when the light hits the mist at precisely the right angle. The pattern is never the same twice, but always depicts the same scene: a world where water flows as it should, where sound travels in straight lines, where the laws of physics hold firm. It is either a memory of what was lost when the pools formed, or a prophecy of what might be restored—the Weavers themselves cannot say which.

But the strangest inhabitants are the **Bone-Tree Dwellers**. These beings have learned to exist in the pale, twisted trees that lean away from the pools. They are small—no larger than a human hand—and seem to be carved from the same bone-white wood as their homes. They live in the hollow spaces between the bark and the heartwood, in dimensions that shouldn't exist within trees so slender.

The Bone-Tree Dwellers are archivists. Every impossible thing that happens near the pools—each upward cascade, each simultaneous state of matter, each moment when the sound becomes visible—is recorded in the rings of their bone-white trees. They carve the memories in spirals that follow the grain of the wood, creating libraries of impossibility that grow outward with each passing season.

Sometimes, when the pools' behavior reaches its most extreme crescendos, a few Bone-Tree Dwellers will venture down from their hollow spaces. They approach the pools with instruments like tiny tuning forks made of crystallized sound, and they taste the air itself, sampling each impossible flavor. Their faces—carved from living wood—show expressions of deep concentration, as if they are translating the pools' chaotic symphony into some ordered language that only they understand.

And finally, there are the **Visitors' Shadows**. These are not beings in their own right, but rather what remains of every person who has ever stood at the pools' edge. Each human visitor leaves behind a fragment of themselves, a shadow cast not by light but by proximity to impossibility. These shadows linger long after their owners have departed, growing more real with each passing day.

The Visitors' Shadows move through the mist like memory made manifest. They repeat actions their owners once performed—reaching toward the pools, stepping back in wonder or fear, pressing hands to ears that no longer exist. But they also do things their owners never did, as if they represent not what was, but what might have been if the visitors had stayed longer, had been braver, had been more willing to accept the pools' impossible gifts.

Some of the older shadows have begun to interact with the other inhabitants. They follow the Drift Swimmers through aerial currents, though they cannot touch the vessels of impossibility. They stand beside the Reflection Weavers during the moments of silence, adding their own memories to the tapestry of what might be restored. They whisper to the Bone-Tree Dwellers in languages their original owners never spoke, sharing secrets about the world beyond the plateau.

All of these beings exist in the spaces between certainty and impossibility, sustained by the same forces that make water fall upward and ice form in steam. They are not quite alive in any sense that the world beyond the plateau would recognize, but they are not quite not-alive either. They are the children of contradiction, the citizens of a small nation where the laws of reality have bent just enough to allow for new forms of existence.

And they are growing stronger. With each impossible event, each moment of perfect silence, each visitor's shadow that joins their ranks, they become more real, more present. They are learning to exist not just in the spaces between impossibility, but to claim those spaces as rightfully theirs—to make a home in the tears where the world has come apart at its edges.

The pools scream their endless songs, but the inhabitants no longer hear only chaos. They hear a lullaby, a working song, the sound of reality being gently reshaped into something new and strange and beautiful. They are midwives to impossibility, and they are patient.

After all, they have learned to live in a place where time itself pools and eddies like impossible water. They can wait for the world to catch up to them, to learn the new rules they are writing with their very existence. They can wait for the day when the bone-white trees no longer lean away from the pools, but toward them—when what now seems impossible becomes simply another way for the world to be.
