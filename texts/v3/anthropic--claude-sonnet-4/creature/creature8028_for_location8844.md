In this vertical sanctuary, the **Kethani** have made their home—a people whose very beings have become attuned to the mountain's slow pulse and the eternal conversation between stone and water.

They are tall and lean, these dwellers in the mist, their limbs elongated by generations of reaching across chasms and climbing the treacherous paths between terraces. Their skin has taken on a pale, almost translucent quality, like fine porcelain held to candlelight, with a subtle green undertone that speaks of lives spent breathing the exhalations of countless growing things. Veins show faintly beneath this skin, not blue but silver-green, as if their very blood has learned to carry the essence of moss and mountain water.

Their hair grows in soft, fibrous strands that seem to drink moisture from the air—never quite wet, never quite dry, but perpetually dewy. Colors range from deep moss-green to the silver-grey of weathered granite, and it moves with an almost plant-like quality, stirring in the slightest breeze like the fronds of the ferns they live among. The eldest among them wear their hair long, braided with small stones and living moss that continues to grow, creating crowns of green that shift and change with the seasons.

But it is their eyes that mark them most clearly as children of this place. Large and luminous, they have adapted to see in the perpetual twilight of the gorge. The irises have taken on colors that mirror their world—deep amber like pools of resin, grey-green like lichen on stone, or the pale silver of mist itself. These eyes seem to hold depths, as if one could peer into them and see the layered terraces of their home reflected endlessly inward.

The **Kethani** move with a peculiar grace, their bare feet finding purchase on the most treacherous moss-slick stones. Their soles have thickened and grown sensitive, reading the texture of rock and the stability of each step through touch alone. They walk with arms slightly extended, always ready to catch themselves or steady against a wall, their balance perfect and unconscious after lifetimes of navigating their vertical world.

Their clothing is woven from the fibers of plants that grow only in this moist microclimate—soft, water-resistant fabrics that never quite dry but never rot, dyed in the muted colors of their environment. Cloaks of woven moss-fiber drape from their shoulders, growing slightly throughout the seasons as living fibers continue their slow growth. Boots of felted fungal material, cultivated in the deep caves, provide warmth while allowing them to feel the subtle communications of the stone beneath.

In their daily lives, the **Kethani** have become gardeners of verticality. They tend the hanging gardens that cascade down the cliff faces, knowing which fungi to encourage and which to gently redirect. Their hands, long-fingered and gentle, seem to coax growth from bare stone. They speak to their plants in soft, humming voices that blend with the constant sound of dripping water, and the plants respond, turning toward their touch like cats seeking affection.

Children among the **Kethani** begin life in the deeper alcoves, their infant cries echoing strangely in the stone chambers. They learn to crawl on surfaces most lowlanders would find impossibly treacherous, their small hands instinctively finding the safest holds. As they grow, they develop an almost mystical connection to the paths between terraces, navigating them even in the thickest mist as if the stone itself whispers directions.

The adults work in harmony with their environment's rhythms. Some tend the cascade gardens, others craft tools and clothing from the materials the mountain provides. The finest artisans among them are the **Stone Listeners**—individuals who can place their ear against the rock and hear the movement of water within, predicting where new chambers might be carved or where dangerous instabilities might develop.

Their society has no rigid hierarchy, but natural leaders emerge—those whose harmony with the mountain's moods allows them to guide others safely through the treacherous beauty of their vertical world. The **Mist Speakers** serve as weather-readers and path-finders, able to interpret the subtle changes in air pressure and moisture that herald shifts in their mountain microclimate.

In the twilight hours, when the mist grows thickest, the **Kethani** gather in the larger alcoves for their communal rituals. They hum together in harmonies that resonate through the stone, their voices joining with the natural acoustics of their mountain home to create music that seems to emerge from the very walls. These songs tell the stories of water and stone, of the slow dance between growth and decay that governs their world.

Their cuisine consists of the pale mushrooms that grow in fairy rings, bitter greens that thrive in perpetual dampness, and the small, silver fish they catch in the deepest pools. They brew teas from moss and lichen that warm the body from within and seem to help them see more clearly in their dim world. On rare feast days, they prepare delicacies from the crystalline formations that grow in the deepest caves—minerals that dissolve on the tongue like flavored ice, carrying the pure taste of the mountain's heart.

The **Kethani** age beautifully, their connection to their environment deepening with each passing year. The eldest become almost spectral, their translucent skin and flowing hair making them seem like spirits of mist and moss given form. These ancients retreat to the deepest chambers, where they tend gardens of luminescent fungi and commune with the mountain's slowest, deepest thoughts.

Death, when it comes, is gentle among the **Kethani**. They sense its approach like a change in the mountain's breathing and retreat to prepared alcoves where their bodies can return to the stone and moss, becoming part of the endless cycle of growth and renewal that sustains their vertical paradise. In time, the most beautiful moss gardens are said to grow where the wisest of their kind have chosen to rest, their essence feeding the eternal green cascade that defines their world.

The **Kethani** have no desire to leave their mountain sanctuary. To them, the outside world seems harsh and flat, lacking the intricate vertical relationships that give meaning to existence. They are complete within their realm of mist and stone, each generation more perfectly adapted to their unique niche between earth and sky, where water writes poetry on granite and moss dreams in shades of green that have no names in the languages of the lowlands.
