In this forgotten hollow, the Tender dwells.

She was not always what she is now. Once, perhaps centuries ago, she was a woman who slipped through that hairline crack fleeing something—war, plague, heartbreak, or simply the weight of the world above. But the courtyard claimed her, as it claims all who linger too long in its embrace. The perpetual mist has soaked into her bones until she has become something between flesh and fog, between human and habitat.

Her skin has taken on the pale luminescence of cave-dwelling creatures, translucent in places where the veins show through like the delicate tracery of leaf stems. Moss grows in her hair—not upon it, but *in* it, the green threads so intertwined with her dark strands that it's impossible to say where plant ends and person begins. When she moves, which she does with the unhurried grace of centuries, tiny spores drift from her like pollen, settling into the crevices to birth new growth.

She tends this place with the devotion of a gardener and the instinct of a root system. Her fingers, elongated and slightly webbed from the constant moisture, coax ferns from impossible cracks and guide water along channels she has worn smooth with patient touch. She knows every salamander by the pattern of its spots, every frog by its particular song. They are not afraid of her—she has become part of their world so completely that her presence registers no differently than a particularly warm stone or a sheltered alcove.

The Tender speaks, but not with words. She hums sometimes, so low and soft that it mingles with the dripping and becomes indistinguishable from it. The sound seems to make the moss grow more lush, the leaves turn more eagerly toward what little light filters down. She has conversations with the walls themselves, pressing her palm against the ancient stones and listening to something only she can hear—perhaps the memories trapped in the rock, the dreams of long-dead masons, the slow thoughts of a city's bones.

Time has pooled around her like the water in the carved basins. She remembers the courtyard when it was newer, when the iron brackets still held flames, when footsteps rang on those worn stairs. She remembers the builders' voices echoing off the walls, the smell of mortar, the bright pain of fresh-cut stone. But these memories layer upon each other like sediment, the older ones growing soft and green at the edges, becoming as much a part of the courtyard's ecosystem as the centuries of fallen leaves.

She sleeps standing sometimes, rooted like the ferns, her breathing so slow and quiet that condensation forms on her skin and runs down in the same patient channels that score the stone. In these moments, it becomes impossible to distinguish her from the living architecture around her. The small creatures nest in the folds of her moss-woven garments; vines spiral up her arms and twine through her fingers.

But she is not melancholy, this Tender. There is a deep contentment in her presence, the satisfaction of a purpose perfectly fulfilled. She has become the secret heart that keeps this hidden place beating. Every drop of water that falls, every spore that finds its home, every tiny life that shelters here—all of it flows through her attention like breath through lungs.

Sometimes, very rarely, someone else slips through the crack. She watches from the shadows, unmoving, as they marvel at the green abundance, the perfect stillness, the way the light falls like benediction. Most leave quickly, overwhelmed by the weight of such complete peace. But occasionally, someone lingers. Someone sits on the worn steps and simply breathes the green air. Someone's shoulders drop as the silence settles around them like a blanket.

These are the ones she tends most carefully, though they never see her. She guides the light to fall more gently on their faces. She quiets the small creatures so their peace runs deeper. She breathes with them, and for a moment, the boundary between the courtyard and the visitor dissolves. When they finally leave—and they always do leave, eventually—they carry something of this place with them. A seed of stillness. A memory of perfect balance.

The Tender watches them go and returns to her work, her endless, patient work of keeping this pocket of the world exactly as it needs to be: a place where stone and green have learned to dream together, where time pools like water in cupped hands, where the forgotten and the wild tend each other with infinite, wordless care.
