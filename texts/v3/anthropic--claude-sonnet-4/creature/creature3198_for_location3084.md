In this realm of endless song, life has learned to exist between breaths of stone and wind.

The **Resonant Folk** are the primary human inhabitants, though "human" may no longer be the precise term. Generations of exposure to the mountains' constant harmonics have changed them in subtle but profound ways. Their bones have grown denser, more resonant—when they speak, their voices carry overtones that seem to echo from within their very skeletons. Their ears have adapted too, with enlarged inner chambers that allow them to perceive the mountains' song in layers most visitors cannot fathom. They can distinguish between the deep warning rumbles that precede rockfalls and the crystalline tones that signal thermal vents opening deep below.

These people move in small, scattered communities of rarely more than a dozen souls. They build nothing permanent, understanding that the mountains themselves are the only true architecture here. Instead, they follow the song—when certain peaks begin humming in specific combinations, they know it's time to migrate to thermal valleys where the primitive cycads will fruit. When the bass notes drop to subsonic levels, they retreat to higher ground before the flash floods of condensation arrive.

Their children are born into silence—not because they cannot hear, but because they instinctively understand that human voices should not compete with the greater symphony. The Resonant Folk communicate through subtle hand gestures, body positioning, and when necessary, a form of controlled humming that weaves into the mountains' song rather than challenging it. Their spoken language has nearly disappeared, replaced by a complex vocabulary of harmonies and rhythmic breathing patterns.

Among them move the **Bone Singers**—hermits so transformed by prolonged exposure that they have become living instruments. Their skeletons have hollowed and elongated, creating resonating chambers within their bodies. They can no longer speak as others do; instead, the wind moves through them, their breath becoming part of the mountain's voice. Other Resonant Folk regard them with reverent unease, for the Bone Singers seem to exist partially in some other state of being, their awareness spread across the entire acoustic landscape. They appear when someone is dying, their presence a bridge between the human song and the eternal chorus of stone.

The **Stone Listeners** are different—not changed by the mountains but choosing to change with them. These are individuals who have learned to enter deep meditative states where they claim to hear patterns in the chaos, prophecies hidden in the harmonics. They spend months in the acoustic dead zones, places where the song creates perfect silence, teaching themselves to hear the spaces between notes. When they emerge, they carry fragments of what might be songs, what could be languages, what perhaps are messages from whatever dreaming intelligence shapes this endless music.

But the mountains harbor others whose relationship with the song is more complex. The **Echo Children** are beings of uncertain origin—some say they are the offspring of humans and whatever spirit inhabits the peaks, others believe they are the mountains dreaming of having voices of their own. They appear as translucent figures, visible mainly in peripheral vision, their forms shifting like heat mirages. They seem to be composed partly of condensed sound waves, their bodies rippling with visible harmonics. They neither help nor harm the human inhabitants, but their presence indicates places where the boundary between the physical and acoustic worlds grows thin.

In the deeper caverns live creatures that may once have been human but have forgotten that state entirely. The **Throat Dwellers** have merged so completely with the mountain's song that they exist as pure acoustic phenomena with only vestigial physical forms. They manifest as voices without bodies, conversations that echo from empty chambers, laughter that cascades down stone faces where no one stands. The Resonant Folk leave offerings of water and soft minerals at cave mouths, not from worship but from an understanding that these beings are what they themselves might become given enough time and surrender.

The pale, echolocating lizards are not mere animals but seem to possess a collective intelligence, their clicks and chirps forming complex patterns that sometimes synchronize with the mountains' deeper rhythms. The Resonant Folk have learned to interpret certain lizard-call sequences as warnings or guidance, though whether the creatures are actually intelligent or simply so perfectly adapted to this acoustic environment that they appear to be communicating remains unclear.

Above all these inhabitants moves something the locals refer to only as the **Deep Singer**—not seen but felt, a presence vast enough that its breathing might account for the thermal currents, its heartbeat for the deepest bass notes that shake the peaks. Whether this is a literal entity, the collective unconscious of the mountains themselves, or simply the human need to assign intention to the incomprehensible song, none can say. But all who dwell here long enough begin to sense it, a consciousness older than geology, patient as erosion, expressing itself through an endless composition that uses the peaks as instruments and all who live among them as notes in an eternal, ever-changing symphony.

The Resonant Folk know they are temporary—their bones may sing now, but eventually they will become part of the stone itself, their final notes absorbed into the greater chorus. They do not resist this fate but rather practice for it, learning to harmonize not just with the mountains' song but with the silence that waits beyond it, preparing for the day when their voices join the ancient, endless music of the Singing Peaks.
