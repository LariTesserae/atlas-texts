## The Quarrymen

They move like water themselves, these people who have learned to think in stone. Their bodies carry the particular posture of those who spend their days bent over resistant material—shoulders broad and slightly rounded, forearms thick with the accumulated strength of a thousand thousand hammer swings. Their hands tell stories in calluses and scars, each finger mapped with the geography of their trade. The stone has marked them as surely as they have marked it.

The oldest among them is Kerra the Deep-Reader, whose grandmother's grandmother first found the spiral grain that runs through the quarry's heart. She moves through the courts with a walking stick carved from ironwood, tapping walls and listening to the hollow spaces that sing back. Her eyes have gone milky with age, but her fingertips read stone like scripture. She can tell you which wall will weep heaviest when the rains come, which workshop will need new channels cut before winter. The younger quarrymen bring her samples—fragments of strange-colored stone, pieces that split wrong—and she holds them to her ear like seashells, listening for their secrets.

Tam the Cleaver works the straightest cuts, the ones that will become lintels and foundation stones. His workshop sits where two natural channels meet, so water flows past his feet while he works. He has trained his twin daughters, Mira and Nira, to hold his measuring rods just so, to call out the angles in the old numbers that follow the stone's own mathematics. The twins finish each other's sentences and work in perfect synchronization—one tapping the practice rhythm while the other sets her chisel, both counting beats under their breath. They are seventeen and have already developed the deep chest and strong wrists of true quarryfolk.

In the workshop that catches the most mist, old Hadric shapes the circular basins that catch and hold water throughout the settlement. His work requires the most patience—sometimes he spends days on a single curve, following the stone's willingness to become hollow. His apprentice, young Jorik, has just begun to understand that the stone must want to be shaped, that forcing it leads only to cracks and waste. Jorik's hands still shake slightly when he sets his chisel, but Hadric remembers his own trembling youth and says nothing, only demonstrates again the proper breathing that steadies both heart and hand.

The children born here learn to walk on wet stone before they learn to walk on dirt. They play games of balance on the narrow workshop eaves, dare each other to cross the courts on the thinnest ledges. By age seven, they can identify different grades of limestone by touch alone. The youngest, barely old enough to hold tools, help sort stone chips by color and size—blue-grey from the deep layers, cream from the surface cuts, the rare veined pieces that hold ancient sea-creatures in their hearts.

There are no marriages here in the way outsiders understand them, but partnerships form naturally around complementary skills. Wren the Channel-Cutter works always with Davi the Measurer; their workshop produces the gutters and water-ways that keep the settlement from flooding. They have been together fifteen years and move around each other with the fluid precision of dancers. Neither could imagine working without the other's rhythm.

The settlement feeds itself through careful trade with the valley folk below. Every month, they send down finished stone—carved blocks, shaped basins, decorative pieces marked with the spiraling patterns that follow the natural grain. In return, they receive grain and oil, dried fruits and the occasional bit of metal for tools. But they are not entirely dependent on trade. They have learned to trap the fat grey crayfish that live in the deeper pools, to gather the pale mushrooms that grow where stone dust and water meet in the darkest corners.

Some among them develop what they call "stone-dreams"—a peculiar sensitivity to the rock's deep memory. These dreamers sometimes wake in the night knowing exactly where to cut, or which section of wall will reveal a perfect spiral chamber. Mael the Dreamer sits often in meditation by the oldest pool, his weathered hands resting on stone worn smooth by countless touches. He speaks little, but when he does speak, the others listen. He was the one who found the deep vein of blue-grey limestone that has supplied them for the past decade.

They mark time not by seasons but by projects—the year they cut the great spiral stair that leads to the upper workshops, the year they discovered the chamber that sings when water flows through it just so. Their longest memories are held in stone itself: every carved surface records decisions made by hands now decades buried, techniques passed down through careful observation rather than written word.

In the evenings, when the day's cutting is done, they gather in whichever court has the most pleasant acoustics that night and share the day's small discoveries. Someone has found a new kind of spiral grain. Someone else has perfected a technique for cutting curves without waste. The conversation moves like water itself, flowing from workshop to workshop, carrying knowledge the way their channels carry stone dust and rain.

They sleep lightly, these people, trained to wake at the sound of shifting stone or changing water. Their dreams are filled with the geometries of their trade—perfect circles, spiraling cuts, the satisfying sensation of chisel meeting stone at exactly the right angle. When they wake, their first instinct is to listen: to the quality of echo in the courts, to the sound of water in the channels, to the subtle percussion of their neighbors beginning another day's patient conversation with the willing stone.
