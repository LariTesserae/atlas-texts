In this vertical maze of stone, the Stitch-Folk have made their impossible home.

They are a people shaped by the necessity of connection across void—tall and lean, with arms grown long from generations of reaching between peaks, fingers that have learned to find purchase in hairline cracks. Their most distinctive feature is what gives them their name: the intricate network of scars that covers their skin from childhood onward. These are not wounds but marks of belonging, careful cuts made during the Binding ceremonies that mark each major transition in life. The scars form patterns that tell stories—which peak you were born on, which family line you descended from, which bridges you've helped to build, which neighbors you've pulled back from a deadly fall.

The youngest among them, the Leap-children, possess an almost supernatural confidence with height and distance. They scramble up sheer faces like insects, their small fingers and toes finding holds where adults see only smooth stone. By age seven, they can gauge the strength of a rope bridge by its sound, can predict which way the wind will shift by reading the subtle flex of the peaks. They play games that would terrify outsiders—racing across bridge-spans in storms, competing to climb the steepest faces, calling riddles to each other across chasms too wide for adult voices to bridge.

The Bridge-Builders form the most respected caste, though "caste" is too rigid a word for how the Stitch-Folk organize themselves. These are the ones who span the impossible gaps, who read stone the way sailors read water. They carry coils of rope woven from the iron-grass, and hammers whose heads have been passed down through generations. Each Bridge-Builder knows intimately the personality of every peak in their domain—which stones can bear weight, which cracks will widen under strain, where the wind creates pockets of calm air in the midst of storms. They work in pairs always, never alone, with elaborate systems of signals and safety lines that have prevented countless deaths.

But it is the Carvers who truly make life possible here. These patient artisans spend their lives in conversation with stone, gradually hollowing out the chambers where families shelter, the workshops where tools are made, the cisterns where precious water is stored. They work by sound as much as sight, listening to the ring of their chisels to know where the stone is solid and where hidden weaknesses lie. The most skilled among them, the Deep-Carvers, create the elaborate tunnel systems that connect chambers within a single peak, sometimes working for decades on a single spire, gradually transforming it into a vertical village.

The Trade-Callers serve as the vocal cords of this scattered city. Blessed with voices that can carry across the widest chasms, they relay messages, announce the arrival of goods, sing the evening songs that help families scattered across dozens of peaks know that their neighbors are safe. They've developed a complex system of calls and responses, a musical language that can convey everything from "Bridge needs repair on the south face" to "New child born to the Redstone family" to the subtle harmonies that constitute formal marriage proposals between peaks.

Among them move the Menders, who tend not just to broken bones and cut flesh but to the spiritual wounds that come from living always on the edge of falling. They know which herbs growing in the cracks can ease the shaking hands of a Bridge-Builder who has seen too many friends die, which combinations of lichen can be brewed into teas that help the Deep-Carvers sleep without dreams of being buried alive in stone. The Menders also serve as the keepers of the scar-patterns, the ones who perform the Binding ceremonies and ensure that each person's story is properly written on their skin.

The Stitch-Folk have no formal leaders, but they listen carefully to the Weatherwise—those who have developed an almost mystical ability to read the sky and stone. These individuals can predict storms days in advance, can sense when the stone itself is restless and might shed deadly fragments, can guide the Bridge-Builders to attempt connections only when conditions are perfect. They often stand for hours at the edges of platforms, reading signs invisible to others, their scarred faces turned upward to catch messages in the movement of clouds.

Most mysterious of all are the Deep-Runners, a small group who have learned to navigate the maze of chasms at the base of the peaks, in the perpetual shadow-country where ice never melts. They emerge occasionally with strange finds—fragments of metal unknown above, bones of creatures that must once have lived here before the great upheaval, sometimes even small lumps of coal that burn with precious warmth. But they speak little of what they see down there, and their scars follow different patterns, as if they are becoming something other than the typical Stitch-Folk.

The children learn early that every person is a potential lifeline, that the survival of the whole depends on the strength of each connection. Their games emphasize cooperation over competition—elaborate challenges that require multiple peaks working together, puzzle-songs that can only be completed when voices from a dozen different heights join in harmony. They sleep in chambers carved directly into the stone, warmed by carefully banked fires and the shared body heat of extended families, dreaming to the sound of wind through the peaks and the distant calls of the Trade-Callers announcing that all is well.

When someone dies, the Stitch-Folk do not bury them—there is no earth deep enough. Instead, the body is wrapped in ropes and lowered into the deepest accessible chasms, returned to the shadow-country below. But before this, the community gathers on every available platform and bridge to sing the Scattering Song, a complex musical piece that requires voices from multiple peaks to complete. The song tells the story written in the deceased's scars, celebrates the bridges they built or chambers they carved, mourns the connections that are now broken. The ceremony can last for days, voices joining and dropping out as people take turns, until every peak has contributed its part and the story is complete.

The Stitch-Folk measure wealth not in possessions but in connections—how many bridges they've helped to build, how many peaks they can reach safely, how many families would take them in if their own peak became uninhabitable. They speak of themselves not as individuals but as parts of a single entity stretched across the stone maze, each person a necessary thread in an enormous net that holds their scattered society together.

In the deepest winter, when storms make the bridges impassable and each peak becomes an island, they endure by singing to each other across the void, their voices carrying messages of comfort and connection through the howling air. These are the times when the Stitch-Folk most fully understand what they are—not separate families clinging to separate stones, but one people spread thin across the vertical world, held together by ropes and scars and songs and the absolute certainty that no one survives alone among the Crowded Peaks.
