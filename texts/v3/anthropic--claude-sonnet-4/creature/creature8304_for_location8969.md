In the earliest hours, when the first light catches the obsidian and throws rainbows against the stone walls, the Glasswalkers emerge from their homes. They move with a peculiar grace—feet knowing precisely where to step, how to distribute weight across the treacherous surfaces. Their soles are thick with callus, but more than that, they seem to read the glass itself, sensing the microscopic imperfections that provide purchase where others would slip.

The children are the most remarkable. Born on these terraces, they treat the razor edges as playground equipment, dancing across surfaces that would slice unwary flesh. They wear soft leather wrappings on their hands and feet—not for protection, but to muffle sound. Their games involve elaborate choreographies of silence, competitions to see who can traverse an entire terrace level without creating even the whisper of footfall on glass. When they do make noise, it's intentional: dropping pebbles in complex rhythms that echo between the levels, creating conversations in percussion that their parents understand as readily as spoken words.

The adults move with different purposes but equal precision. The Waterwrights tend their elaborate spring systems—not farmers, exactly, but cultivators of flow itself. They've learned to read the seasonal variations in seepage, to predict where new springs might break through, to coax water along carved channels with microscopic adjustments of angle and depth. Their hands are always damp, fingers pruned and sensitive to the slightest change in moisture. They taste their water like vintners taste wine, detecting mineral notes that reveal which deep chambers and ancient pressures have shaped each drop's journey through the volcanic glass.

Among them move the Lightbenders—artisans who've mastered the terraces' most dangerous gift. They carry polished obsidian tools, mirrors and lenses ground to exact specifications, capable of focusing the endless reflected radiance into beams intense enough to cut stone or start fires. But more than that, they paint with light itself. Their workshops, built at precise angles on the broader terraces, become theaters where controlled beams create moving sculptures of illumination. They work mostly at dawn and dusk, when the light has color and weight, crafting displays that tell the ancient stories of how the terraces were born.

The Bridgebuilders represent a different relationship with this vertical world. Stocky and methodical, they're the ones who shaped granite and basalt into the spanning arcs between levels. But they're not merely engineers—they're philosophers of space and connection. Each bridge they build changes the social dynamics of the terraces, determining which communities can easily visit each other, which relationships will flourish or fade. They plan their constructions across generations, understanding that a bridge built today might not find its true purpose for decades, when populations shift and new needs emerge.

In the evening hours, when the reflected light grows golden and finally fades, the Echokeepers take their turns. These are the storytellers and historians, but their medium is sound itself. They position themselves at specific points where the acoustic properties of the terraces create perfect amplification, their voices carrying clearly across vast distances. Their tales aren't just spoken—they're spatially choreographed, with different voices coming from different levels, creating narrative layers that unfold vertically through the settlement. Children fall asleep to these distributed lullabies, stories that seem to come from the glass itself.

The most mysterious residents are the Stillwalkers—solitary figures who tend the empty stretches between settlements. They wear robes that match the obsidian perfectly, making them nearly invisible except for their movement. No one quite understands their purpose, but they appear wherever the glass shows signs of stress—hairline cracks that might threaten a terrace's stability, or areas where thermal expansion has created dangerous tensions. They work alone, using techniques known only to their small brotherhood, somehow coaxing the volcanic glass back into stability without tools more complex than polished stone and careful touch.

During the rare storms that reach this highland plateau, all the residents retreat indoors—not from wind or rain, but from the chaos of water and light. When drops strike the obsidian surfaces in quantity, the entire terrace system becomes a symphony of crystal chimes, beautiful but overwhelming. The reflected light fractures into thousands of rainbows, creating an environment so visually intense that prolonged exposure can cause a kind of euphoria that borders on madness. The Glasswalkers have learned to recognize the signs in each other: the distant gaze, the tendency to stop moving and simply stare into the endless refractions. When someone shows these symptoms, the community gently guides them to the darker interior spaces, letting their eyes and minds rest until the light-drunk stupor passes.

At night, when the terraces finally release their hoarded light and the obsidian becomes truly black, the residents don't sleep easily. The sudden absence of reflection feels like a loss, a dimming of the world itself. They've developed rituals to ease this transition—small oil lamps that float in the spring-fed pools, creating tiny constellations reflected in the dark water. The children, especially, need these gentle lights to feel secure in a world that has suddenly lost its luminous breath.

Through all seasons and all hours, the Glasswalkers share one common trait: they speak softly. Not from shyness or custom, but from necessity. Their words, like their footsteps, carry far on these resonant surfaces. A conversation at normal volume becomes a public announcement; an argument becomes a spectacle for the entire terrace system. This has shaped not just their speech but their thoughts—they've become a people of careful words, considered emotions, and profound inner lives. What they cannot say aloud, they express through their movement, their craft, and the precise choreography of their daily existence on these impossible stairs of glass and light.
