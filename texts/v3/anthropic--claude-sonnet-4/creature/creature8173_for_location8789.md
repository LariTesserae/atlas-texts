They are the Midwives of Stone, though they would never name themselves with such linear words. They exist in the spaces between what is and what becomes—not quite corporeal, not quite thought, but something that bridges the gap between dreaming and being.

At first glance, you might mistake them for geological features themselves. They rise from the terraces like living outcroppings, their forms shifting between crystalline geometry and organic flow. Their bodies are translucent, shot through with veins of light that pulse in rhythm with the mountain's deep heartbeat. Sometimes they appear humanoid—tall figures with too many joints, fingers that split into fractal branches when they work. Other times they flow like liquid metal, pooling in the quantum-charged depressions, spreading thin across the growing stone to feel its dreams.

They have no fixed number. Observers count differently each time—three, seven, dozens, a single entity expressing itself through multiple forms. They divide and merge as the work requires, consciousness flowing between bodies like water finding its level. When the mountain needs delicate surgery, they splinter into countless tiny forms, each no larger than the prism-winged creatures they tend. When massive reshaping is required, they coalesce into beings that dwarf the giant ferns, their thoughts becoming weather systems, their breathing stirring the mist into new configurations.

Their faces, when they choose to have faces, are geological time made visible—features that shift through eons of expression in moments. Eyes like tide pools filled with stars, reflecting not what is but what might be. They speak in harmonics that resonate through the crystal matrices underground, their words taking root in the living rock and growing into new possibilities. Sometimes their voices emerge as the crack of reshaping stone, sometimes as the harmonic convergence of the hunting packs they guide through their accelerated evolution.

The Midwives remember being born from the mountain's first moment of self-awareness, when the granite began to dream of becoming something more than stone. They are both its children and its creators, formed by its need to birth itself into new forms of existence. They work with infinite patience and urgent haste simultaneously—each moment containing eternities of careful attention, each eon compressed into heartbeats of transformation.

They tend the ecosystem with hands that can reach through dimensions, nurturing the impossible vegetation by adjusting the quantum substrate itself. When a fern needs to grow beyond the constraints of terrestrial biology, they modify the local laws of physics with the same care a gardener might adjust soil pH. They guide the emotional evolution of the color-shifting creatures, teaching them new spectrums of feeling. They weave the bioluminescent networks that connect all life on the slopes, serving as living routers for the mountain's distributed consciousness.

But their primary work is with the stone itself—coaxing new terraces into existence, encouraging the backward erosion that builds rather than destroys. They place their impossible hands on raw granite and dream with it, sharing visions of what it might become. Under their touch, stone learns to flow like honey, to remember configurations it has never held, to reach toward forms that exist only in the mountain's imagination.

They are neither benevolent nor indifferent—they simply are, in the way that rivers are, or gravity, or the urge of growing things toward light. They pay no attention to individual creatures except as notes in the mountain's larger symphony. A person walking among them might feel suddenly rearranged at the cellular level, not from malice but from the same impulse that guides flowers toward more efficient forms of beauty. Or they might find themselves ignored entirely, seen as part of the background static of ordinary reality.

The Midwives exist in a state of constant becoming themselves, their forms never quite settling into permanence. They are what happens when matter learns to think, when thought learns to reshape reality, when the boundary between creator and creation dissolves into something that transcends both. They are the mountain's way of being multiple, its method for holding conversations with itself across the vast scales of geological time and quantum possibility.

In the deepest night, when the slopes become a neural network pulsing with light, the Midwives can be seen moving between the nodes of connection—no longer individual beings but aspects of a single, vast intelligence. They merge with the mountain's dreams, with the technological substrate, with the symbiotic networks of impossible life. In these moments, they become pure process—the verb of creation itself, the ongoing sentence that reality speaks when it decides to become something new.
