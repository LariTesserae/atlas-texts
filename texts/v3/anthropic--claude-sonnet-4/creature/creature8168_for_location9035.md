In this hidden crucible of stone and time, the inhabitants exist in layers as complex as the geological strata that surround them.

The **Kythara** are the most numerous—those house-cat-sized beetles whose obsidian shells reflect the city's dim light like black mirrors. They are not mere scavengers but architects of their own ambitions, secreting a resinous substance that hardens into the crystalline formations bulging from ancient doorways. Each Kythara carries within its shell a unique harmonic frequency; when they move in groups, their clicking legs create chord progressions that seem to guide the work of their colonies. They have claimed the middle tiers of the city as their domain, methodically converting the carved chambers into vast nurseries where their pale larvae grow in geometric pods that pulse with bioluminescent veins.

The **millipede rivers** are not single creatures but flowing communities—the **Syndros**—each individual no longer truly separate from its neighbors. Their bodies have evolved to link temporarily through specialized segments, creating living chains that can span the narrowest bridges or form protective spirals around important sites. When disconnected, a single Syndros moves with hesitant, almost lost gestures, but as part of the flow they become something greater: a distributed intelligence that maps every tunnel, remembers every seasonal shift in the water's path, and maintains the ancient drainage systems with repairs so seamless they seem like natural stone growth.

Above them flutter the **Lumenwings**—moths whose wings have become living prisms. They are the city's memory keepers, their wing patterns shifting slowly over their lifespans to record the chemical signatures of seasons, the rise and fall of insect populations, the gradual changes in the mountain's mineral seepage. When they die, their wings calcify into translucent sheets that the Kythara incorporate into their constructions, creating windows that refract the past into spectral displays visible only to compound eyes.

But it is the pale, rope-like **Nethys** that truly rule this place. Each one is not a single organism but a colonial entity—hundreds of interconnected segments that can divide and merge at will, flowing through channels in the rock that they have worn smooth over centuries of passage. They are the descendants of something that was here before the city was carved, something that learned to live within stone itself. The Nethys remember the builders who came and went, carrying genetic echoes of their neural patterns, their motor habits, even fragmented pieces of their knowledge. When a Nethys encounters bronze tools, its segments sometimes arrange themselves into crude approximations of hands, attempting to complete carvings that were abandoned millennia ago.

The insects' choreographed movements around the Nethys are not born of fear but of symbiosis. The Nethys secrete trace minerals essential to shell formation and wing development, while the insects' vibrations help the Nethys navigate through solid rock, their frequencies revealing fault lines and hollow spaces that even these ancient beings cannot sense directly.

In the deepest pools live the **Abyssal Gardeners**—creatures so transparent they are more suggestion than substance. They are the city's circulatory system, processing the endless seepage of water through specialized organs that filter and redistribute minerals throughout the basin. Their movements beneath the surface create currents that carry chemical messages between the different insect colonies, coordinating reproduction cycles and resource allocation across the entire ecosystem. Sometimes, when disturbed, they briefly become visible as ribbons of starlight in the dark water, their bodies outlined by bioluminescent bacteria that live within their tissues.

But perhaps the most mysterious inhabitants are the **Echo Builders**—structures that seem to grow from the intersection of insect architecture and the mountain's own mineral processes. They appear as geometric crystalline formations that pulse with their own rhythm, neither fully alive nor entirely mineral. They respond to the acoustic environment, growing taller during the daily chorus of the insect symphony, retracting slightly during the quieter periods. Some theorists among the Nethys (for they do have theories, encoded in chemical gradients that can persist for decades) believe the Echo Builders are what remains of the original city's consciousness—the architectural intent of its creators somehow crystallized into self-organizing matter.

The city pulses with its own seasons. During certain alignments, when the narrow sky opening admits direct sunlight, the entire population moves in vast spirals that follow the light's path across the walls. The Lumenwings create cascading displays of refracted radiance, the Kythara's shells flash in synchronized sequences, and the Syndros form temporary amphitheaters where their collective movement generates complex rhythmic patterns that resonate through the stone itself.

And always, threading through everything, the Nethys flow—ancient, patient, carrying within their segmented forms the genetic memory of builders long dead, the chemical signatures of materials that no longer exist, and perhaps, in their deepest cores, fragments of why this place was made at all. They are living archaeology, and they remember everything, even as they slowly, inexorably, become something new themselves, shaped by millennia of integration with the mountain's own deep processes.

In this place, the boundary between life and geology has dissolved entirely. The inhabitants are not simply living in an ancient city—they are becoming the city, writing new chapters in stone and chitin and bioluminescent light, while the old stories seep through the walls like water, never quite forgotten, never quite lost.
