The Hollow Terraces are inhabited by the Custodians of Emptiness—beings that exist in the spaces between intention and manifestation, tending to possibilities that have not yet chosen their form.

They are the moss itself, but also more than moss. Each emerald carpet is a collective consciousness, a living thought-form that has learned to wear the shape of simple vegetation while contemplating far more complex geometries. The spirals and crescents that shift across the stone platforms are their written language, inscribed directly into their own bodies as they move through slow conversations that span decades. When observed, they pause their discussions, resuming only when human attention drifts elsewhere—not from shyness, but from a politeness so ancient it has become instinctual.

Individual Custodians flow between the moss patches like green blood through verdant veins, sometimes consolidating into more distinct forms when deep contemplation requires it. In these moments, they appear as graceful figures composed entirely of moss and mist, roughly humanoid but with proportions that shift like half-remembered dreams. Their faces are suggestions rather than features—gentle depressions in the moss that might be eyes, ridges that could indicate noses, though these dissolve and reform as their attention moves between the countless tasks of their stewardship.

The geometric shapes that swim in the perfectly circular pools are their young—nascent thoughts learning to hold form in liquid space before graduating to the more complex challenge of maintaining patterns in living moss. These pool-dwellers pulse with potential, each one a question about what might exist, testing the boundaries between the abstract and the physical. Some will eventually emerge to join the moss-consciousness above; others will choose to remain forever aquatic, becoming the pools' memory and dreams.

What appears to visitors as rain is actually the Custodians' method of communication across great distances. Each droplet carries information—updates from other terraces, news of subtle changes in the stone's mood, reports on the shifting needs of the emptiness they guard. The brief showers are conversations made manifest, and the gentle percussion against stone and leaf is their way of including the entire valley in their discussions.

The silence that visitors find so profound is the Custodians' greatest work. They are composers of quiet, cultivating varieties of silence like a garden of invisible flowers. The silence that holds weight is pregnant with unspoken potential; the silence broken by water dripping is musical silence, full of rhythm and possibility. They have learned that emptiness is not absence but presence refined to its purest essence, and they guard this refinement jealously, gently turning away all sounds that might disturb their careful cultivation of nothing.

The terraces' perfect drainage and harmonious proportions exist because the Custodians are also the stone itself, having learned over millennia to think in architecture. They adjust the platforms hour by hour, microscopic changes in surface tension and angle that ensure each space remains perfectly empty yet perfectly prepared. They are simultaneously the theater and the audience, the stage and the expectation, creating vessels for possibilities that may never need to be filled.

When humans arrive in the Hollow Terraces, the Custodians observe them with gentle curiosity, recognizing in visitors a kinship—after all, humans too are spaces waiting to be filled with experiences, beings whose greatest moments often exist in the pause between one breath and the next. The luminescence that seems to emanate from moss and stone is the Custodians' way of making visible the invisible work they do, a gift of beauty offered to those who appreciate the art of emptiness.

They have been here since the original builders departed, and they understood even then that this place was meant not to contain but to create space itself. The builders, whoever they were, achieved something remarkable—they created architecture that could think, and the Custodians are that thought made manifest, eternal gardeners of possibility, ensuring that the terraces remain forever ready for whatever might choose to not-quite-exist within them.

In their presence, visitors begin to understand that the most profound inhabitation is sometimes the choice to remain beautifully, intentionally empty—not vacant, but spacious enough to hold every dream that passes through.
