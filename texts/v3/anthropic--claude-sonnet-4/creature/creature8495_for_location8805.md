In this mist-shrouded sanctuary live the Kythara—the Weavers of Water and Wood. Their bodies have adapted across generations to their amphibious existence: fingers elongated and webbed at the tips, skin bearing a faint greenish cast from symbiotic algae that supplements their nutrition during the lean mountain winters. Their eyes, large and dark-adapted, can perceive the subtle shifts in light that penetrate the eternal mists, reading weather patterns in the play of shadows on water.

The eldest among them, Grandmother Siltara, moves through the floating gardens with movements so fluid she seems part plant, part current. Her hair, long gone white as mountain snow, flows with living strands of water moss that she has cultivated for seven decades. She remembers when the great willow-mother at the settlement's heart was merely a cutting, and her gnarled hands—stained deepest jade from a lifetime of plant-binding—can coax growth from the most reluctant branch. She speaks in whispers that carry perfectly across water, her voice layered with the authority of one who has weathered sixty seasonal cycles of mist and thaw.

The builders work in pairs bonded since childhood—Tender and Shaper, each partnership complete in their shared understanding. Kael and Thyma move as one organism through their daily tasks: he guides the willow shoots with bronze tools worn smooth as river stones, while she breathes life into the binding points with her uncanny ability to sense where plant meets plant in perfect symbiosis. Their feet, bare and broad, grip the curved surfaces of reed boats with unconscious precision, balancing loads of bundled branches that would topple others into the dark water below.

Among the younger generation swim the Deep Watchers—those born with the gift of holding breath for impossible lengths, who tend the spiral gardens in the basin's depths. Nym moves like a pale fish through the underwater forests, her lungs capable of sustaining her for nearly an hour in the oxygenated depths. She has mapped every thermal current, every spawning ground of the char, every seasonal shift in the great organism that pulses at the bottom of their world. Her skin bears ritual scars in flowing patterns that mark her as chosen by the Deep Current—the mysterious entity that may be plant, animal, or something altogether other that forms the foundation of their floating realm.

The children are amphibious in the truest sense, as comfortable beneath the surface as above it. They attend floating schools where they learn to read the language of currents, to identify edible algae by touch in murky water, to predict weather from the behavior of dragonfly swarms. Young Orias, barely ten summers old, can already weave silk from caddisfly larvae into nets so fine they catch pollen grains, and her laughter bubbles up from beneath the surface where she plays elaborate games of tag with the char.

The settlement's Listener, ancient Bren, spends his days motionless on the central platform, one hand trailing in the water, reading vibrations that travel through liquid and living wood. He interprets the mountain's moods—the grinding of distant glaciers, the thunder of rockfall in neighboring valleys, the deep thrumming that precedes the seasonal melt. His warnings have saved them from flash floods and avalanches, his weathered face always turned slightly upward as if hearing voices in the mist that others cannot perceive.

At night, when the fog lies heaviest and the water holds perfect stillness, the Kythara gather for the Telling—their oral history sung in harmonies that resonate through water and wood alike. Their voices, trained from birth to carry across the acoustic perfection of their bowl-shaped world, weave together stories of the first Weavers who discovered this place when the glacier retreated. They sing of the great winter when the basin nearly froze solid and only the deepest magic kept their community alive. They chant the names of every willow branch, every thermal vent, every generation of char that have shared their floating world.

And in the deepest reaches of night, when even their voices fall silent, they feel it—the slow, vast intelligence that dreams in the depths below them. Some say it is the mountain's heart, others believe it is the collective memory of every plant that has ever grown in the basin. The truly wise among the Kythara know it is both and neither: their true partner in the ancient dance of growth and guidance that keeps their impossible city alive in its bowl of stone and mist, generation after generation, world without end.
