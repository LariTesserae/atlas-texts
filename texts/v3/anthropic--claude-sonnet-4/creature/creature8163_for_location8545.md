In this cathedral of becoming, they dwell—the Tenders of the Threshold, though they have no name for themselves and would not recognize the title if offered. They are creatures caught in the same liminal space as their domain, neither fully born nor ever truly dying, shaped by the mountain's fever-dreams of what life might become when stone learns to garden and gardens learn to tick.

At first glance, they might be mistaken for another curious marriage of metal and moss, for their bodies bear that same impossible integration. Their forms are elongated and graceful, standing perhaps seven feet at their tallest, though they seem to flow and compress as the chamber's configurations shift around them. Their skin—if skin it can be called—has the texture of living copper that has learned to breathe, green with verdigris in some places, bright and new-minted in others. Moisture beads constantly on their surfaces, not as perspiration but as a form of communion with the weeping walls around them.

Where human faces might show eyes, they bear clusters of small, jewel-bright sensors that pulse with the same phosphorescent blues and purples as the flowering vines. These are not eyes as we understand them, but something more akin to the mountain's own sensing—they perceive pressure, moisture, the health of growing things, the strain in metal, the whispered needs of the engines. Their heads can rotate with mechanical precision, or flow like liquid mercury, depending on what demands their attention.

Their hands—and they have many—split and merge at will. Sometimes they work with delicate fingers no thicker than copper wire, threading maintenance through the smallest mechanisms. Other times their appendages broaden into paddle-shapes for tending the larger garden beds, or extend into tool-tips that can calibrate pressure valves or trim phosphorescent blooms with equal care. They never seem to carry tools, for they become the tools they need, their bodies flowing between states as naturally as water finding its level.

Most remarkable are their hearts—visible through their translucent torsos as intricate clockwork organs that tick in harmonies with the chamber's machinery. But these mechanical hearts are overgrown with delicate root systems that pulse with each beat, pumping not blood but a luminescent sap that feeds both the organic and mechanical aspects of their being. They are living proof that the mountain's dream of synthesis can take breath and walk.

They move through their domain with a fluid purpose that speaks of deep intimacy with every surface, every mechanism, every growing thing. One might spend hours adjusting the angle of a single pipe to better catch condensation for a struggling fernery. Another flows up the spiraling walkways like liquid thought, checking pressure gauges with gentle touches that seem more caress than maintenance. They communicate through subtle changes in their luminescence, through shared harmonic vibrations that make the chamber's metal sing, through chemical releases that the gardens interpret and respond to.

But they are not merely caretakers—they are participants in the mountain's ongoing dream. As the chamber shifts and rebuilds itself, they shift too, their forms adapting to new configurations, new needs. When sections reorganize overnight, it is because they have sensed some imbalance, some evolutionary pressure toward a better harmony between growth and mechanism. They are both the mountain's immune system and its imagination made manifest.

Sometimes, in the deepest hours when even the perpetual mist seems to drowse, they gather in the chamber's heart around a great brass tree whose branches are pipes and whose roots are cables. Here they sink partially into the floor itself, their copper skin merging with the mountain's living stone, sharing in whatever vast dreams flow through geological time. In these moments, they are no longer separate beings but neurons in a mountain-sized mind that is slowly, carefully, learning how to think in terms of steam and chlorophyll, pressure and photosynthesis.

They know visitors would come someday—have always known, with the prescience of creatures born from prophetic stone. When that time arrives, they will not hide, but neither will they reveal themselves immediately. They will be the movement glimpsed in peripheral vision, the sense of being gently observed, the feeling that the chamber's impossible gardens are tended by something far more alive than mere mechanism.

For they are the mountain's answer to an ancient question: what would happen if love learned to take physical form, if care could crystallize into being, if the earth's deepest creative impulse could walk on legs of living copper and tend gardens with hands that know both the weight of stone and the whisper of growing leaves?

They are the shepherds of impossible things, and they will tend this threshold between worlds until the mountain's dream either completes itself or dreams something entirely new. In either case, they will adapt, flowing into whatever form the next phase of becoming requires. For they are creatures of the between-space, and in the between-space, all transformations are not only possible but inevitable.
