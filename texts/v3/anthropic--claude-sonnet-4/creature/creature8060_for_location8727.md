# The Dreamers of the Deep Gardens

In the silence between currents, they drift. The **Vel'thari** are what remains of the city's builders—not quite alive as surface dwellers understand life, not quite gone as death defines absence. They have become something else entirely, beings who learned to breathe water and dream in mineral time.

Their bodies move with the fluid grace of kelp fronds, elongated and translucent, skin grown pearlescent from centuries beneath the waves. What were once limbs have become something more like flowing appendages, fingers stretched into delicate tendrils that can read the chemical stories in the water, taste the health of their gardens, feel the microscopic vibrations of growth and decay. Their faces retain hints of their original forms—high cheekbones, deep-set eyes—but these features now seem carved from living jade, their expressions shifting as slowly as the light that filters down from above.

The Vel'thari no longer speak with voices, for sound carries strangely here. Instead, they communicate through touch—gentle brushes of tendril against stone, against kelp, against each other, sharing memories and intentions through contact. When they move through their gardens, their passage sends subtle chemical signals spiraling through the water, messages that the kelp forests have learned to read and relay. The entire city has become their nervous system.

They are gardeners still, but gardeners of impossible patience. A single Vel'thari might spend decades coaxing a new variety of kelp to take root in a particular terrace, adjusting mineral flows drop by drop, season by season. They understand growth in ways that surface dwellers cannot—not as urgency, but as a conversation between organism and environment that might take centuries to complete. Their hands shape the coral-kelp hybrids not through force, but through suggestion, chemical whispers that encourage new symbioses.

Some tend to the **bioluminescent meadows** in the deepest terraces, where colonies of sea anemones pulse in slow, hypnotic rhythms. These Vel'thari have grown most distant from their original forms, their bodies stretched thin and riddled with light-conducting channels. They move among the anemones like living constellations, their touch synchronizing the creatures' rhythms, turning entire districts into organs of soft, breathing light. They are the city's dreamers, processing the slow thoughts of stone and sediment into patterns of phosphorescent beauty.

Others work as **current-singers**, positioning themselves at key architectural points where the thermal flows converge. They have modified their forms to become living instruments, hollow spaces in their torsos and elongated limbs creating chambers that resonate with the water's movement. As currents pass through them, they produce those low harmonic tones that drift through the city—not music as surface dwellers know it, but a form of architectural meditation, the city's stone and water finding voice through their transformed bodies.

The **kelp-shepherds** are perhaps closest to what the Vel'thari once were, retaining more of their original proportions as they move through the vertical forests with practiced ease. Their skin has developed a bark-like texture that allows them to merge briefly with the larger kelp trunks, sharing nutrients and chemical information. They are the city's memory keepers, for the kelp forests hold the accumulated knowledge of centuries—which species thrive where, how the mineral composition has shifted, what combinations create the most stable ecosystems.

In the ancient plaza at the city's heart, a small group of Vel'thari have become something like living archives. These **Stone-speakers** have grown partially fused with the limestone foundations, their lower bodies merged with carved benches and decorative pillars. They no longer move, but through them flow all the chemical and mechanical memories of the city—every seasonal flood, every shift in the kelp canopies, every subtle change in the thermal currents. They are Vel'thar's living history, their consciousness spread through the very stones.

And then there are the **Deep-tenders**, who venture beyond the city's edges into the darker waters of the valley walls. These Vel'thari have grown most alien, their forms adapted for the crushing depths and mineral-rich springs that feed their gardens. They return rarely, bringing news of new mineral veins, of changes in the deep thermal patterns, of the vast slow movements of the valley floor itself. Their reports take the form of chemical signatures released into the city's water system, messages that unfold over weeks as they disperse through the currents.

What unites all the Vel'thari is their relationship with time. They experience the present moment not as a razor's edge between past and future, but as a vast pool in which all their centuries of existence coexist. Memories of the city's flooding are as immediate as the current state of a kelp bed; plans for gardens that will mature in two hundred years feel as urgent as the need to adjust a mineral flow today. They live in geological time, their consciousness expanded to match the rhythms of their underwater realm.

Sometimes, when the thermal currents align perfectly and the bioluminescent anemones pulse in perfect synchronization, the Vel'thari achieve moments of collective consciousness—the entire city thinking as one vast, distributed mind. In these moments, they touch something beyond their individual existence, becoming briefly the living spirit of Vel'thar itself: patient, ancient, and utterly at peace with the slow transformation of all things beneath the weight of water and time.

They are not sad for what they have lost. The surface world, with its harsh light and gasping air, feels like a dream half-remembered. This is their reality now—this marriage of stone and sea, this garden that grows in the language of minerals and tides. They have become what they always were, beneath the temporary fever of terrestrial life: creatures of depth, patience, and the long, slow breathing of the earth itself.
