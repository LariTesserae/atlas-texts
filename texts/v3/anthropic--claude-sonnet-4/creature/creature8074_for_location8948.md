The Tidekeepers are the city's truest inhabitants—families whose bloodlines run back through centuries of flood and ebb, their very bones seemingly calibrated to the water's rhythm. They move with an uncanny anticipation, adjusting dock lines minutes before the tide turns, opening sluice gates with timing that seems instinctive rather than calculated. Their children learn to read the water's mood before they learn letters, sensing the subtle shift in air pressure that precedes the evening tide, the almost imperceptible change in the sound of lapping water that signals a higher flood is coming.

These are people whose skin has taken on a faint luminescence from generations of breathing the humid air, their eyes adapted to pierce the constant mist with startling clarity. They speak in low voices that carry perfectly across water, their conversations punctuated by the city's mechanical heartbeat. The eldest among them bear intricate tattoos—charts of the waterways inked in deep blue, marking not just the permanent channels but the seasonal variations, the storm patterns, the subtle currents that newcomers never learn to see. When they die, their bodies are given to the tide at the convergence of the three rivers, and it's said their knowledge flows back into the waters to guide future generations.

The Mechanism Tenders form a separate caste entirely, descendants of the engineers who first brought steam and iron to tame these waters. They dress in leather aprons darkened by decades of oil and wear brass goggles that never seem to come off, the metal worn smooth by constant handling. Their workshops occupy the most stable platforms, filled with the patient click of precision tools and the hiss of small forges. They know every pump in the city by its particular rhythm, can diagnose a failing bearing by the change in vibration felt through the walkways beneath their feet.

These craftspeople have developed an almost mystical relationship with their machines. They'll spend hours listening to a steam engine, hands pressed to its housing, feeling for irregularities in its pulse. Some say they dream in the language of gears and pistons, that they can sense when a distant pump is struggling even while they sleep. Their children are apprenticed young, learning to oil the great mechanisms while the tide is out, to adjust pressure valves with movements so precise they seem choreographed.

Between the Tidekeepers and the Mechanism Tenders exists a third group—the Transitional folk, drawn here by the city's strange promise of constant change. They are artists who paint only on surfaces that will be submerged by the next tide, their works existing in brief cycles of creation and dissolution. They are merchants who specialize in goods that appear and disappear with tidal markets, their entire commerce built around impermanence. They are pilgrims seeking something unnamed in the rhythm of rise and fall, finding in the city's breathing space a meditation that solid ground cannot provide.

These newcomers often struggle at first, fighting the water's intrusion instead of accommodating it, trying to impose rigid schedules on a place that measures time in tidal cycles rather than hours. But those who stay begin to change, their movements becoming more fluid, their sense of urgency shifting to match the deeper rhythms. They learn to sleep in shifts, to eat when the tide allows rather than when habit demands, to find beauty in the temporary architecture of floating markets and bridges that appear for mere hours before vanishing again.

In the deepest parts of the city, where the oldest pumps still labor in chambers that flood completely at spring tides, there are whispers of others—the Deepkeepers, descendants of workers who tended the machinery generations ago and never surfaced. These may be only legend, stories told to explain the occasional glimpse of movement in flooded basements, the tools that appear mysteriously where they're needed most, the way certain ancient pumps continue running long after their mechanisms should have seized.

But whether real or myth, the idea of the Deepkeepers speaks to something essential about this place—that the boundary between human and machine, between inhabitant and environment, grows fluid here. The city's people don't simply live alongside the water and steam and iron; they become part of its circulation, their lives measured not in years but in the countless cycles of tide and pump-stroke, their stories written in rust-stains and the patient accumulation of barnacles on walls they've touched for generations.

The children of all these groups play together in the shallows during low tide, building elaborate sand cities that they know will be erased by evening, learning early that impermanence and beauty are not opposites but dance partners. They grow up understanding that home is not a fixed place but a pattern, not a structure but a rhythm—the eternal pulse of water rising and falling, steam condensing and dissipating, the great mechanical hearts of the city beating on through mist and time.
