# The Attention of the Wise

The first sign was subtle—a shift in the quality of light that filtered down through the perpetual mist. Where before the pale illumination had been diffuse and constant, now it seemed to gather and pool in certain places, as if drawn by invisible currents. Kerra the Deep-Reader noticed it first, her blind eyes turning toward shadows that had grown somehow deeper, more purposeful.

"Something watches," she murmured to her great-nephew as he guided her between the workshops. "Not with eyes like ours."

The watching presence—for it was a presence, though none could name what manner of being possessed such patient attention—had been drawn by the settlement's peculiar harmony. Here was something rare: humans who had learned to listen to stone, who worked not against the world's grain but with it. The quarrymen had achieved what so few mortals managed—a true partnership with the non-human world, built on generations of careful attention and humble craft.

For seven days, the presence simply observed. It noted how Tam's daughters counted their hammer-strokes to match the natural rhythm of water flowing over stone. It watched Mael the Dreamer sit in motionless communion with limestone that remembered ancient seas. It saw how every tool was cleaned and stored with the reverence due to instruments that bridged the gap between human will and mineral wisdom.

Most importantly, it saw their contentment. These people had found their place—not conquered it, not merely inhabited it, but found it in the truest sense. They belonged to this wet stone maze as surely as the limestone belonged to them.

On the eighth day, the presence acted.

It began with the water. The constant seepage that kept everything slick and alive suddenly ran clearer, more abundant. New springs opened in previously dry corners, each one emerging exactly where it would be most useful—by Hadric's basin-workshop to ease his careful curves, near the children's playing courts to create new pools for their games. But these were not random gifts. Each spring sang with a subtle music when water flowed over its lip, harmonizing with the settlement's natural acoustics in ways that made the daily percussion of work even more beautiful.

The stone itself grew more willing. Chisels bit cleaner, following fault lines with less resistance. New veins of colored limestone revealed themselves—deep blues that caught light like captured sky, creams veined with gold that seemed to glow from within. But again, these gifts came with perfect appropriateness. They appeared only where the quarrymen's skill had already prepared them to recognize such treasures, only when their current projects could benefit from new materials.

Some gifts were so subtle that only the most experienced workers noticed them. The acoustic properties of certain courts grew richer, creating pockets of perfect resonance where the sound of hammer on stone became almost musical. Tool steel stayed sharp longer, developing an edge that seemed to know exactly where to bite. Measuring rods made from the settlement's own stone began to sing faintly when held at true angles, providing a precision beyond human sight.

But the greatest gift was one of understanding.

Gradually, the quarrymen began to perceive what their benefactor already knew—that their work was not merely craft but a form of profound conversation. Each strike of chisel against stone was both question and answer, part of an ongoing dialogue between human consciousness and mineral patience that had been developing for generations. They began to sense the deep contentment of limestone that had found its purpose, the satisfaction of stone that had become exactly what it was meant to be.

Old Kerra understood first, as the wisest often do. "We are seen," she told the others as they gathered in the great spiral court one evening. "Seen and... appreciated. Not for what we produce, but for what we are. For how we listen."

The presence made no demand, required no worship or ceremony. It simply watched with the infinite patience of one who understood that true mastery comes only through decades of humble practice. It had seen countless human settlements rise and fall, had observed the endless struggle between will and world. But here was something different—a place where that struggle had transformed into collaboration.

As months passed, the quarrymen began to understand that their benefactor was not changing them but revealing them—showing them what they had already become through their own patient labor. The gifts continued, but always in service of their existing harmony rather than disruption of it. New workshops revealed themselves through lucky discoveries of natural chambers. Tools found their way to the hands that could best use them. Children born in the settlement showed an even deeper intuitive understanding of stone-lore.

The presence became part of the settlement's rhythm, as natural as mist and falling water. Sometimes the quarrymen felt its attention like warmth on their shoulders as they worked. Sometimes they woke from stone-dreams with new understanding singing in their minds. But always, the relationship remained one of mutual respect—a wise power recognizing and nurturing wisdom it had found already flowering in the patient hands of mortal craftspeople who had learned, over generations, to think like stone.

Years later, when traveling merchants spoke of the miraculous stonework that came from the Quarry Courts, of basins that seemed to sing and carvings that caught light like living things, the quarrymen would only smile and return to their work. They knew the true miracle was simpler and more profound than any magical transformation—it was the rare achievement of humans who had learned to belong exactly where they were, to work in perfect partnership with the world's deep patience.

The wise presence continued its watching, continued its subtle gifts, content to nurture this small pocket of harmony where mortal hands and ancient stone had found their perfect conversation.
