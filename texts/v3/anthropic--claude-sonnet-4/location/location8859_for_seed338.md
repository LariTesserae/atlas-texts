Deep beneath what was once surface, in chambers that exhale their first breaths of existence, a peculiar city takes form. The space feels both cavernous and intimate—vast enough to lose sight of far walls in the dim amber glow, yet so densely woven with growth and construction that every cubic meter pulses with purpose.

Here, in the perpetual coolness of the deep earth, an impossible garden-city crystallizes into being. The air carries the sharp mineral bite of fresh-carved stone mixed with the green exhalation of countless plants that have never known sun. Phosphorescent mosses trace the joints between newly-laid stones, their pale light supplementing the scattered oil lamps and ember-bowls that cast warm pools of illumination through the settling dust of construction.

The builders—though no animal walks these passages—are the plants themselves. Massive root systems, thick as human torsos, snake through the walls and form the city's living infrastructure. They pulse with slow hydraulic rhythms, drawing nutrients not from soil but from the mineral-rich stone itself, their chemistry adapted to extract life from granite and limestone. Broad-leafed vines cascade from carved balconies, their fronds so dense they form living curtains between the tightly-packed dwellings. Flowering trees with bark like polished metal grow directly from the bedrock, their branches intertwining to support platforms and walkways that seem to bloom from the living wood.

The settlement spreads through a network of natural caverns being actively reshaped. Walls rise where yesterday there were only raw cave surfaces—but these are not human walls. They grow, extruded by colonies of coral-like organisms that secrete a pearl-gray substance harder than concrete. The boundaries between plant, mineral, and architecture blur as root systems weave themselves into the growing walls, while crystalline formations sprout from the living timber like metallic flowers.

Tool marks score the newer surfaces—the patient work of simple implements wielded by unseen hands. Copper chisels and stone hammers lie scattered on work surfaces, still warm from recent use, though no workers are visible. The construction proceeds by some other logic, perhaps guided by the vegetative intelligence that suffuses every surface.

The air barely stirs, broken only by the soft percussion of condensation dripping from stone formations, the whisper of leaves adjusting their positions in the mineral-scented air, and the nearly inaudible groaning of growing wood under structural loads. Occasionally, a section of wall releases a soft sigh as it settles into its final form, or the scrape of stone against stone echoes from some distant chamber where the expansion continues.

Narrow streets wind between buildings that seem to lean inward with the weight of their plant-growth, creating a maze of intimate spaces beneath canopies of intertwined branches. Public squares open suddenly from cramped alleyways—circular spaces where the largest trees spread their crowns like living pavilions, their roots forming natural amphitheater seating carved from the bedrock itself.

Water, precious and scarce, is channeled through hairline cracks in the stone, collected in small basins carved into the living wood. The plants have learned to extract every drop from the humid air and the weeping stone, their leaves beaded with condensation that rolls down elaborate catchment systems formed by generations of directed growth.

This is a city in its first moments of true becoming—neither fully grown nor fully built, but something emerging from the marriage of cultivation and construction, guided by an intelligence that works through root and tool alike. The very walls continue to shift and strengthen as the settlement discovers what it will be, growing deeper into the earth's embrace with each quiet, productive hour.
