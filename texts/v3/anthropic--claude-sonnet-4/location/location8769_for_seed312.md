The Undergrowth sprawls beneath what was once the industrial heart of Millhaven, a sunken city that has become something between archaeology and living organism. The ground here lies twenty feet below the surface world, accessible through storm drains that weep constant condensation and collapsed subway grates that breathe cool, metallic air.

Water moves everywhere but never pools—a network of shallow channels carved through cracked concrete and rusted steel, fed by the intermittent weeping of pipes that snake through the ceiling like exposed arteries. The moisture beads on every surface: the corroded girders that still support phantom upper floors, the scattered patches of wire grass that push through foundation cracks, the hulking machinery that continues its inexplicable work decades after the power grid supposedly failed.

The vegetation grows wrong here. Sparse clusters of something like ferns sprout from steam vents, their fronds metallic blue-green and clicking softly in the perpetual breeze. Moss carpets sections of broken pavement in perfect geometric patterns—squares and hexagons that suggest deliberate cultivation by unknown gardeners. Between these patches stretch vast expanses of bare concrete and exposed earth, creating a checkerboard of growth and emptiness that extends beyond sight through the network of connected basements, sub-basements, and excavated caverns that honeycomb this buried district.

The temperature holds steady in that narrow band between uncomfortably cool and tolerably temperate—jacket weather, always jacket weather, with the dampness carrying a mineral tang that suggests deep earth and old metal. The air moves constantly through unseen passages, creating drafts that whistle through broken windows and gaps in masonry with sounds almost like conversation.

This is a place that has settled into its own strange equilibrium after catastrophe. The buildings—what remains of them—have achieved a kind of organic stability, their collapsed roofs and shattered walls finding new angles of repose that have held for decades. Twisted rebar blooms from concrete like cultivated vines. Pipes and cables snake across surfaces in patterns that follow no engineering logic but possess their own geometric beauty. The erosion here happened fast and brutal, then stopped, leaving everything locked in a state of photogenic ruin.

The sprawl extends for miles underground, a mirror city of broken foundations and half-buried infrastructure connected by service tunnels, maintenance corridors, and passages that might have been carved by water or intention or something between the two. From any vantage point, the eye traces corridors that branch and branch again into distance, punctuated by the vertical shafts of light that penetrate from the surface world above.

But the space breathes with emptiness as much as structure. The ruins are scattered like islands across vast underground plains where the foundations were torn away entirely, leaving only packed earth studded with utility covers and the occasional flight of stairs that leads nowhere. The density is all wrong for a city—too much open air, too much space between things, as if some force had stretched the urban fabric past its breaking point.

The infrastructure tells stories in multiple layers: the original city's bones, the industrial retrofitting that came later, and then stranger additions that defy easy categorization. Steam pipes that connect to nothing. Electrical conduits that pulse with light but power no visible systems. Conveyor belts that still move, carrying loads of unidentifiable materials between chambers where massive automated systems perform tasks that might be recycling, manufacturing, or something more ambiguous.

The machines never stop. Somewhere always, the deep thrumming of motors turning unknown wheels, the pneumatic hiss of hydraulic systems, the rhythmic clanging of metal on metal echoing through the caverns. The sounds layer and interfere until the entire Undergrowth becomes a percussion instrument played by invisible musicians. Some areas roar with industrial noise; others whisper with the sound of a thousand small mechanisms clicking in near-synchrony.

And through it all, threading between the machinery and the ruins and the sparse, metallic vegetation, move the creatures that have made this place their own. They are not quite rats, not quite birds, not quite anything that has a name in the surface world. Some are small and quick, chattering as they dart between shadows. Others are large enough to be glimpsed only in peripheral vision, their forms suggesting familiar animals rearranged by unknown principles. They move in vast numbers, following currents of behavior that sweep through the population like weather patterns. Sometimes they flood an area with frantic activity, building and destroying and rebuilding with materials they gather from the endless debris. Sometimes they vanish entirely, leaving only the echo of their presence and the structures they've abandoned.

The light comes from above but also from within—the amber glow of maintenance panels that never burned out, the blue flicker of electrical arcs that leap between exposed wires, the soft phosphorescence that seems to emanate from the walls themselves in certain chambers. It's enough to see by, usually, though the quality of light shifts constantly as clouds pass over the world above and the internal systems cycle through their mysterious rhythms.

But reality itself has grown unstable in the deeper regions. Corridors that lead east one day open onto different spaces the next. Rooms expand and contract based on no visible mechanism. The creatures sometimes move in patterns that suggest they exist partially outside the normal flow of time—gathering around things that haven't happened yet, avoiding spaces where yesterday's events still echo. The machinery occasionally produces items that couldn't have been manufactured from the materials fed into it, as if the industrial processes here operate according to laws that are almost but not quite the familiar ones.

The chaos builds and recedes in waves. Periods of relative stability give way to hours when the entire Undergrowth seems to reorganize itself, when the sound of reshuffling echoes from every direction and the creatures flee or gather in response to stimuli beyond human perception. The water channels change course. New passages open in solid walls. Machines that have been silent for months roar back to life, while others fall quiet without explanation.

It is a place caught between death and transformation, where the familiar infrastructure of the surface world has been broken down and rebuilt according to rules that shift like the weather, inhabited by creatures that embody the same fluid uncertainty, all of it humming with an energy that might be decay or evolution or both at once.
