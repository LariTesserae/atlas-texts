The Hollowed Quarter spreads before you like a living bowl carved from the earth itself, no larger than a cathedral's nave but dense as a jeweler's case. Here, in this sunken district of what was once a thriving settlement, the buildings have grown together into something between architecture and organism.

The structures rise only two or three stories, their walls built from a pale, chalky stone that weeps perpetually. Moisture seeps through the porous rock, leaving dark stains that bloom and fade in slow patterns, as if the buildings themselves are breathing through their skin. The narrow alleys between them collect this constant condensation into shallow streams that run along worn gutters, disappearing into drains that gurgle softly with an almost conversational murmur.

Every surface burgeons with growth. Thick curtains of moss cascade from the eaves, punctuated by clusters of luminescent fungi that cast a gentle, pearl-like glow into the perpetual twilight. Vines with leaves like cupped hands spiral up the walls, their stems so thick they've begun to blur the lines between window frames and doorways. In places, the vegetation has grown so dense that entire facades have vanished beneath green tapestries, leaving only the suggestion of human habitation underneath.

The air hangs cool and heavy, thick enough to taste—earthy and green, with an undertone of something mineral and ancient. Droplets form on every surface, rolling down walls, dripping from leaves, creating a constant, barely-audible percussion. The temperature never seems to shift; there is no wind here, no sun to speak of, only a diffuse brightness that filters down as if through deep water.

What makes this place truly strange is how the buildings seem to have melted together at their foundations. Where one structure should end and another begin, the walls flow into each other like cooled wax. Doorways open not onto streets but into the ground floors of neighboring buildings, creating a maze of interconnected chambers. Some passages lead through what might have been shops or homes; others tunnel directly through the living rock beneath, their walls lined with the same weeping stone.

The few creatures that inhabit this place move like whispers. Occasionally, a pale salamander no longer than your thumb will emerge from a crack in the wall, its skin so translucent you can see its organs pulsing beneath. Sometimes you glimpse something larger—a shadow that might be a cat or might be something else entirely—sliding between the moss curtains with liquid grace.

The silence here is not empty but full, pregnant with the sound of slow growth and patient decay. If you listen carefully, you can hear the buildings settling into themselves with tiny creaks and sighs, the whisper of leaves unfurling, the almost-music of water finding its way through stone. There are no voices, no footsteps, no signs of current human habitation—yet the paths are worn smooth, the gutters clear of debris, as if invisible caretakers maintain this place in a state of perfect, timeless equilibrium.

Nothing here seems to age or change. The moss hangs in precisely the same configurations day after day. The streams follow identical courses. Even the droplets of condensation seem to fall in the same rhythms, creating a world suspended in its own green dream, neither fully alive nor properly abandoned.
