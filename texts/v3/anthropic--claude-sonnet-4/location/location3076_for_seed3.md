# The Ascending Foundries of Keth Mirallos

High on the shoulder of a mountain that refuses to finish growing, the city of Keth Mirallos clings to slopes that shift imperceptibly beneath its foundations. This is a place caught between becoming and being—a settlement built on geology so young that the bedrock still dreams of what it might become. The mountain itself seems uncertain of its final height, adding inches of stone each decade through processes that make the earth whisper and sigh in the pre-dawn hours.

The air here is thin and cool, carrying the metallic taste of altitude and the constant, subtle dampness that seeps from the living rock. Water doesn't flow here so much as weep—condensation beads on every surface by nightfall, morning mists cling to the half-formed crags, and sudden brief showers appear from seemingly empty skies, drumming against the corrugated metal roofs before vanishing as quickly as they came. In the deeper hollows between the city's industrial terraces, small pools collect this intermittent moisture, their surfaces mirror-still until disturbed by the creatures that come to drink.

The vegetation defies the harsh altitude with an almost aggressive abundance. Hardy mountain shrubs have evolved into something more determined here—dense thickets of silver-green that seem to grow in fast-forward, their roots cracking fresh stone and their branches interweaving with the city's infrastructure in ways that blur the line between built and grown. Luminescent fungi colonize the damp spaces between buildings, casting a soft blue-green glow that supplements the inadequate streetlamps. Vines with leaves like burnished copper spiral up the factory smokestacks, and moss carpets every surface that stays still long enough.

The city itself sprawls across multiple levels carved into the mountainside, connected by a network of switchbacking roads, cable systems, and bridges that span the growing chasms where the mountain continues its restless expansion. The architecture is purely functional—prefabricated buildings and industrial structures that can be moved or rebuilt as the ground shifts beneath them. Steam rises from processing plants and foundries that work with metals drawn from the mountain's eager veins, where ore forms faster than it can be extracted.

But there's something wrong with the scale of things here. Buildings that should hold perhaps a dozen workers echo with the sounds of hundreds. Corridors stretch longer than they should, and residents speak of districts that exist only on certain days, of rooms that appear in familiar buildings without warning. The mountain's nascent geology seems to affect space itself—distances fold and expand unpredictably, and surveyors have given up trying to map the place accurately.

The industrial machinery runs constantly, its rhythms synchronized to something deeper than human schedules. The sound is a layered symphony of grinding, hammering, and the deep thrumming of engines that seem larger than the buildings that house them. Steam whistles punctuate the din at irregular intervals, and beneath it all is the mountain's own voice—a subsonic rumbling that residents claim helps them sleep but that visitors find deeply unsettling.

Animals thrive here in impossible numbers and varieties. Arctic birds nest alongside species that belong in temperate forests, while small mammals with too-bright eyes and uncanny intelligence move through the underbrush in organized groups. The workers tell stories of creatures that exist only at the edge of vision, and of familiar animals that occasionally cast shadows belonging to something else entirely. Mountain goats with metallic horns navigate the scaffolding as easily as natural rock faces, and there are persistent reports of a creature resembling a cross between a bear and a factory machine that tends to appear during equipment malfunctions.

In the perpetual dim light—filtered through industrial haze, elevation, and the strange atmospheric effects of the growing mountain—everything takes on a dreamlike quality. The line between shift work and sleep blurs as easily as the distinction between natural and artificial. Workers sometimes report completing jobs they don't remember starting, or finding their tools arranged in patterns that suggest purpose but resist interpretation.

The city grows as the mountain grows, adding new districts in response to geological changes that happen too quickly to be entirely natural. Construction crews work around the clock, not building so much as keeping pace with a landscape that won't stay still. Yet despite the constant noise and motion, time moves differently here—slowly, as if filtered through the patient processes of stone formation, even as everything around it transforms with unsettling speed.

This is a place where industry has made peace with impossibility, where workers punch time clocks that sometimes run backwards, and where the mountain's dreams of completion merge with humanity's dreams of permanence, creating something that is neither natural nor artificial, but persistently, stubbornly real.
