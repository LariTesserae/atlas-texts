The Obsidian Steppes stretch beyond sight, a vast tableland of volcanic glass worn smooth by eons of wind-carried grit. What were once jagged mountain peaks have been ground down to rolling swells of black stone, their surfaces polished to a dull mirror that reflects the pewter sky in fractured glimpses. The land rises in broad terraces, each level separated by cliffs of the same dark glass, creating a grand staircase that climbs toward a horizon lost in perpetual haze.

Scattered across these ancient plateaus stand the Listening Stones—towering spires of crystallized obsidian that ring with barely audible harmonics when the wind passes through their hollow cores. Each spire is unique, carved not by intention but by countless centuries of erosion into forms that suggest half-remembered purposes: doorways that open onto nothing, archways bridging empty air, spirals that twist skyward like frozen smoke. They emit a constant, almost subliminal humming—so low it's felt more than heard, a vibration that seems to emanate from the stone itself.

The air carries a metallic bite, sharp and thin at this altitude. Breath mists briefly before dispersing into the perpetual twilight that bathes this realm. The sun, when glimpsed through the ever-present atmospheric dust, appears as a dim copper disk, its light filtered and scattered until everything takes on the color of old pewter and tarnished silver.

Between the Listening Stones, the glass plains are broken by peculiar formations—geometric patterns etched into the obsidian surface, too regular to be natural yet showing no tool marks. These sigils stretch for hundreds of yards, their lines filled with a substance that might once have been metal but has long since transformed into something resembling mercury-colored sand. When the rare winds blow, this sand shifts within its channels, creating slow, hypnotic movements across the dark ground like liquid starlight.

Scattered throughout this vastness are the remnants of simple shelters—rough-hewn blocks of lesser stone stacked into low walls, their original purposes unclear. Most have partially collapsed, not from violence but from the patient pressure of time itself. Within and around these ruins lie artifacts of worked stone and primitive metal: bowl-shaped implements whose surfaces have been worn perfectly smooth, lengths of copper-green metal bent into spirals and crescents, and small spheres of unknown composition that feel warm to the touch despite the surrounding cold.

Life here exists at the very edge of possibility. Occasionally, creatures emerge from cracks in the obsidian—things that might be insects or might be tiny mechanisms, their carapaces the same dark glass as the landscape. They move with deliberate precision, following invisible paths across the stone, clicking softly as they walk. Their purpose, like everything else here, remains opaque.

The silence is profound but not complete. Beyond the subtle harmonics of the Listening Stones and the occasional click of the glass-bodied creatures, there are other sounds that seem to come from the stone itself: the distant groan of thermal expansion, the whisper of sand moving through ancient channels, and sometimes—though this may be imagination—what sounds almost like breathing, as if the entire plateau were a single, sleeping entity drawing breath across geological time.

At the highest reaches of the steppes, where the terraces give way to a final plateau, the Listening Stones grow taller and their songs more complex, creating harmonies that shift across hours rather than moments. Here, the geometric patterns in the obsidian become more intricate, forming mandalas and spirals that seem to pulse with their own inner logic. The air grows thinner still, and the horizon takes on an uncertain quality, as if distance itself becomes negotiable in this rarefied atmosphere.

This is a place where time moves differently, where the distinction between living and mineral blurs, and where the very stones dream slow dreams of ages yet to come.
