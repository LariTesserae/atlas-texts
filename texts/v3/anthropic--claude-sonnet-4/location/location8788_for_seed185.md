The Crystalline Hollows stretch beneath the world's skin like a vast honeycomb of amber-tinted chambers, each cavity worn smooth by eons of patient chemical weathering. Here, in this subterranean realm, reality grows thin at the edges.

The walls curve in impossible geometries—surfaces that seem flat until you realize you've been walking in a gentle spiral for hours, chambers that appear small until you notice the distant gleam of formations that should be within arm's reach. The stone itself is a warm, translucent mineral that glows faintly from within, casting everything in honey-colored light that shifts almost imperceptibly as you move, creating shadows that don't quite align with their sources.

Sparse clusters of thread-like organisms cascade from ceiling to floor in delicate curtains—not quite plant, not quite mineral, they pulse with a slow bioluminescence that follows no earthly rhythm. These ethereal growths cluster most densely around the scattered remnants of ancient settlements: crude stone platforms, circles of worked obsidian tools, and the occasional carved archway leading to chambers that exist only sometimes.

The air itself seems thick with presence. Creatures flow through this space in impossible abundance—translucent, jellyfish-like beings that phase in and out of the stone walls, leaving brief warm spots where they pass through solid rock. Chittering insectoid swarms move in rivers of coordinated motion, their shells clicking in soft percussion against the crystal surfaces. Above them, things that might once have been birds trace lazy spirals, their forms wavering between feather and flame.

The silence here is profound but pregnant—broken only by the occasional crystalline chime when temperature shifts cause the walls to expand, or the whisper-soft rustle of the thread-organisms swaying in unfelt currents. Sometimes, voices echo from empty chambers, speaking in languages that predate sound itself.

Time moves strangely in the Hollows. The creatures follow ancient migration patterns, flowing from chamber to chamber in cycles measured not in seasons but in geological ages. Stone formations that should take millennia to form appear overnight, while others fade like breath on glass. The primitive tools left by long-vanished inhabitants sometimes grow warm to the touch, as if their makers had just set them down moments ago—in some adjacent timeline where they never left at all.
