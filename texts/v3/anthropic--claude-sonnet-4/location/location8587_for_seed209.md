The ancient citadel of Korvash clings to the shoulders of a weathered peak, its stone foundations melding seamlessly into the mountain's own bones. Here, at the terminus of geological time, the rocks have surrendered their sharp edges to millennia of wind and the patient kiss of moisture that seeps from hidden springs and gathers in the perpetual mist that shrouds the heights.

The settlement spreads across a series of worn plateaus and gentle saddles, connected by broad stone causeways that follow the mountain's natural contours. These are not the dramatic spires of younger peaks, but the rounded, maternal swells of stone that have seen empires rise and fall in the valleys far below. The bedrock itself forms much of the architecture—great chambers hollowed from living stone, their walls polished smooth by countless generations of hands, their surfaces weeping condensation that collects in worn channels and feeds the settlement's cisterns.

Water moves through Korvash like a slow pulse. It emerges from cracks in the ancient granite, pools in carved basins, and disappears again into the mountain's depths. The air itself carries moisture—not the driving rain of storms, but the gentle, persistent dampness of high places where clouds come to rest. Icicles form and melt in daily cycles along the settlement's edges, their steady dripping marking time in a place where seasons blur into a continuous, cold present.

The absence of vegetation gives Korvash a mineral clarity. No roots crack these foundations, no leaves soften the stark geometry of stone meeting stone. Instead, the settlement's builders have worked with crusts of salt that bloom from certain walls, with veins of colored crystal that thread through the granite, with deposits of clay that seep from underground streams. These natural materials provide both decoration and function—the salt preserves food and melts walkway ice, the crystals catch and scatter the available light, the clay seals joints and forms vessels.

The population is dense but moves with deliberate purpose through the interconnected chambers and courtyards. Thousands live here, but they have learned the rhythms of a place where every resource must be shepherded carefully. Their tools are simple but refined by generations of use: bronze chisels worn smooth in the hand, stone mallets with perfect balance, wooden levers seasoned by the mountain's moisture. They work primarily in stone and metal, crafting the settlement's endless need for cisterns, channels, and chambers that channel both water and sound through the living architecture.

For Korvash is never quiet. The mountain itself provides a constant percussion—drips echoing in stone chambers, wind moaning through carved passages, the distant crack of ice expanding in crevices. To this natural symphony, the inhabitants add their own layers: the ring of hammers on stone, the scrape of tools, the murmur of voices that carries far in the thin, cold air. Children play games that involve sound and echo, learning to navigate by the acoustic properties of different spaces. Craftsmen time their work to the mountain's own rhythms, knowing that certain chambers amplify sound at different hours as temperature and humidity shift.

The fauna here has adapted to both the settlement and the stone. Hardy mountain birds nest in the eaves and feed on scraps from the communal kitchens. Small, thick-furred mammals tunnel through the spaces between the built and natural stone, creating a hidden network of passages. The people of Korvash have learned to read the behavior of these creatures—birds that roost early warn of weather changes, while the mammals' movements indicate the stability of different sections of the ancient rock.

Change comes slowly to this place. The mountain has been worn to its essential form, and the settlement has grown to match its patience. Repairs and improvements happen across decades, not seasons. A wall might be adjusted stone by stone over a generation. New chambers are planned by committees that expect to see the work completed by their grandchildren. Yet this glacial pace creates its own vitality—each modification is considered carefully, tested against time, integrated seamlessly into the greater whole.

As evening approaches, the mist thickens and the temperature drops. The settlement's inhabitants retreat to the inner chambers where the mountain's thermal mass provides warmth. Light comes from oil lamps and carefully tended fires, their flames steady in the still air of the deep chambers. The sounds of the day give way to the mountain's night music—the settling of stone, the whisper of air through distant passages, the soft drip of condensation feeding tomorrow's water supply. In this place beyond the reach of seasons, beyond the urgency of the lowlands, time moves like the gradual polish of stone, wearing everything slowly toward its most essential and enduring form.
