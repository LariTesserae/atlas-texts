The Drowned Reaches stretch across what was once a continental shelf, now claimed by the slow advance of ancient seas. Here, in the twilight depths sixty meters down, the skeletal remains of a mid-sized city emerge from sediment beds that have been settling for millennia. The water is neither ocean nor lake—something in between, brackish and dense, tinted the color of old copper by minerals leached from the ruins below.

The city's bones are vast and geometric: broad plazas of some ceramic material that neither corrodes nor grows anything, connected by channels that once were streets. Towers rise at regular intervals, their surfaces smooth as glass but made of something that bends light strangely—not quite transparent, not quite opaque. These structures seem to have been grown rather than built, their forms too organic for traditional construction yet too purposeful for nature. Some reach nearly to the surface, their tops crowned with sparse growths of something not quite kelp, not quite moss—pale, translucent fronds that wave in the sluggish currents.

The water itself moves with its own strange physics. Currents flow in impossible directions, sometimes upward through empty window frames, sometimes sideways along vertical walls. Objects suspended in the water—bits of that same ceramic, fragments of crystalline growths—hang motionless for hours before suddenly accelerating in perfect straight lines, only to stop again with no apparent cause. The temperature hovers just above freezing, cold enough that your breath would mist if there were air to breathe.

Sound travels strangely here. The constant groan and whisper of water moving through the ruins creates a low, thrumming chorus that seems to come from the structures themselves. Metal—or something like metal—expands and contracts with thermal cycles that follow no earthly rhythm, producing sharp cracks and resonant chimes that echo off the ceramic surfaces and multiply into complex harmonies. Occasionally, from the deepest parts of the ruins, comes a sound like singing, though no throat could make those tones.

The settlements scattered across this drowned landscape exist in the spaces between—floating platforms tethered to the tallest spires, their inhabitants moving through the water with technologies that shouldn't work: personal field generators that create pockets of breathable atmosphere, tools that manipulate water itself as if it were a solid material. The platforms are spare things, built only for necessity, connected by tubes of crystallized water that somehow maintains its rigid structure while remaining transparent.

Everything here exists in perfect stasis. The sediment stopped settling centuries ago. The currents follow their impossible paths without variation. The temperature never changes. Even the sparse vegetation grows in precisely the same configurations it has held for generations—neither advancing nor retreating, neither dying nor truly thriving. Time seems to have reached an equilibrium point and stopped, leaving this drowned realm suspended between what was and what might be, ancient beyond measure yet somehow still waiting.
