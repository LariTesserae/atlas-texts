The Conductor's Range stretches across a continent-sized plateau, a realm where the earth itself has become a vast resonating chamber. Here, at elevations that scrape the belly of low clouds, thousands of stone spires rise like organ pipes from the weathered basalt floor—some needle-thin and singing in frequencies that make your bones ache, others broad as city blocks and groaning with subsonic hymns that travel for hundreds of miles through the rock.

These formations are the product of eons. Wind and the sparse, intermittent rains have carved the volcanic plateau into an instrument of impossible scale. Each spire, each canyon, each perfectly-sculpted arch has found its voice through millennia of patient erosion. The stone here is dense with mineral veins—iron, copper, crystalline inclusions that catch what light filters through the perpetual mist and create harmonics within harmonics. The very density of the formations, packed together in forests of singing stone, creates acoustic chambers and echo corridors that amplify and blend the sounds into something that transcends mere noise.

The air moves differently here. Pressure systems born from the sheer scale of the range create their own weather—winds that spiral up from the plateau's edges, carrying moisture from distant seas to condense against the cool stone faces. Rain, when it comes, arrives as brief, violent percussion against the spires, each droplet adding its voice to the symphony before disappearing into hidden reservoirs deep within the porous rock. Streams appear and vanish mysteriously, emerging from cliff faces to cascade briefly down the stone before being swallowed again, their wet voices adding liquid tremolo to the deeper stone songs.

Scattered throughout this geological orchestra are the remnants of those who came before—not ruins, exactly, but modifications. Here, a spire has been carefully drilled with precise holes to change its pitch. There, a series of metal resonators, green with patina, have been mounted between stone pillars to create impossible chord progressions. Ancient cables, thick as tree trunks and humming with some internal current, snake between the formations. Some connect to weathered control stations built into cliff faces—rooms full of corroded dials and switches, their original purpose lost but their function clear: they were built to conduct this stone orchestra.

The few plants that survive here have adapted to the sound itself. Moss grows in perfect spirals around the base of the most active spires, its growth patterns following the acoustic pressure waves. Strange, fleshy fungi bloom from cracks in the stone, their surfaces rippling visibly with each note. Occasional stands of metallic grass—each blade thin as wire and resonating with its own frequency—create shimmering fields of overtones in the rare flat spaces between the spires.

Wildlife is sparse but specialized. Creatures here have either evolved extraordinary hearing or none at all. Deaf, albino cave-fish swim in the hidden reservoir systems, navigating by pressure and chemical traces. Above ground, peculiar birds with skull modifications that seem to serve as natural hearing protection make their nests in the quieter acoustic shadows. Most strange are the Listeners—tall, gaunt creatures that might once have been human, their ears grotesquely enlarged, their bodies swaying in perfect time to frequencies only they can perceive. They move through the spire forests like sleepwalkers, tending to the ancient machinery with a devotion that borders on worship.

The sound itself defies description. It is not chaotic noise but a composition of impossible complexity—themes that build over days, harmonies that require the entire continental range to complete, rhythms that pulse with the planet's own rotation. Sometimes the music grows so intense that loose stones dance on the ground, that clouds form and dissipate in time to the beat, that the aurora itself seems to flicker in sympathy with the deepest bass notes. The acoustic pressure can be felt in your chest, your skull, your bones—a physical presence that reshapes not just how you hear but how you think, how you move, how you understand the relationship between stone and sky and the vast spaces between sounds.

At the plateau's heart, where the greatest spires cluster most densely, the ancient control stations show signs of recent use. Someone—or something—still tends this instrument, still adjusts its voice to match some grand design. The cables carry power from hidden sources, the dials turn by unseen hands, and the symphony continues, vast and patient and utterly alien, a song that began before recorded history and shows no sign of ending.
