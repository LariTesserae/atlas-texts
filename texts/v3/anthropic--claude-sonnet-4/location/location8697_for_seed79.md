The Thermal Yards stretch across a narrow plateau, a compact industrial settlement built around the natural gift of hot springs that seep perpetually from fractured bedrock. Steam rises in lazy columns from a dozen points across this cramped quarter-mile expanse, the mineral-rich water collecting in carefully channeled concrete basins before flowing into the town's modest network of pipes and culverts.

The buildings cluster tight around these thermal sources—squat brick structures with tall metal chimneys, their walls streaked with rust-orange stains where iron-heavy condensation has run down for decades. Every surface drips. The corrugated metal roofs shed a constant patter of droplets back to the stone-paved streets below, creating an endless percussion that mingles with the deeper thrumming of steam engines and the rhythmic clank-hiss of pistons drawing power from the geothermal wells.

Between the tightly packed workshops and processing halls, sparse clumps of wire grass push through cracks in the stonework, their pale green blades beaded with moisture. Hardy succulents have colonized the warmer spots near steam vents, their thick leaves a dusty blue-green, growing in small congregations around the thermal sources but never spreading far into the cooler margins of the settlement.

The air moves with life despite the industrial density. Flocks of small, dark birds—heat-seeking swallows—dart between the steam plumes, catching insects drawn to the warm updrafts. Lizards bask on sun-heated metal surfaces, their scales glinting bronze and copper. In the deeper shadows beneath the buildings, where condensation pools in permanent puddles, colonies of pale frogs maintain their endless chorus, their voices weaving through the mechanical sounds like a biological counterpoint to the industrial rhythm.

The thermal yards exist in a kind of temporal suspension. The same steam has risen from the same vents for as long as anyone remembers. The same condensation follows the same paths down the same walls. The machinery, though loud and active, performs its eternal cycle without variation—grinding ore brought up from the lower valleys, processing mineral-rich sludges, turning the earth's heat into motion and the motion into modest prosperity.

At the edges of the settlement, where the platform drops away into misty ravines, you can see how young this landscape is. The bedrock shows fresh faces where it has cracked and settled, creating the fissures that allow the hot springs to surface. The stone is sharp-edged, not yet worn smooth by weather, and the thermal features themselves seem to shift slightly—new vents opening as old ones fade, the underground plumbing rearranging itself according to its own slow logic.

The whole place exists in a warm, humid envelope of its own making. Sound carries strangely in the thick air—the clatter of machinery seems to bounce between buildings and hang in the steam before dissipating. Voices become muffled, but metal strikes metal with crystalline clarity. The abundant moisture softens distances, making the already small settlement feel even more intimate and enclosed, a pocket of perpetual activity suspended above the wider world.
