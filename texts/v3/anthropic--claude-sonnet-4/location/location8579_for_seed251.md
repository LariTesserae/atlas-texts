The Canopy Cities stretch across mountain ranges that have forgotten their original names, vast enough that weather systems are born and die within their borders alone. Here, at altitudes where the air grows thin and cool, an ancient ecosystem has achieved something unprecedented: total integration between plant and civilization.

The trees—if they can still be called trees—rise in cathedral spirals three hundred meters high, their trunks hollow and chambered like the bones of prehistoric giants. These living towers house entire districts within their heartwood, rooms carved not by tools but coaxed into being through centuries of patient cultivation. The bark has grown thick and segmented into natural balconies, window frames, and doorways that pulse gently with the slow circulation of sap. Streets are massive branches, wide enough for ox-carts, their surfaces worn smooth by generations of foot traffic and woven with a carpet of cushioning moss that muffles all sound to whispers.

Between the tower-trees, suspension bridges of woven vines and living wood stretch across chasms of air, some spans reaching kilometers from one district to another. These bridges don't merely connect—they are themselves neighborhoods, thick with dwelling-pods that have grown like fruit from the bridge-branches. The constant gentle swaying has shaped a culture of fluid movement, where inhabitants flow through their city like sap through wood.

The moisture comes in delicate cycles. Each dawn, mist rises from the valleys far below, condensing on the billion leaves above until the entire canopy weeps. The water travels down through channels carved in bark, through networks of hollow roots that serve as both aqueducts and communication tubes—whispered messages travel through the root-telephone system, arriving as soft murmurs in chambers miles away. Rain falls only intermittently, but when it does, the city drinks it in with visible relief, leaves unfurling, bark darkening, and the whole vast organism humming almost inaudibly with contentment.

The fauna defies comprehension. Every surface crawls, flies, or hangs with life. Clouds of luminescent insects drift between the levels like living constellation maps. Tree-sloths the size of carriages move in geological time along the major branches, their fur gardens for entire ecosystems of smaller creatures. Serpentine beings—neither quite snake nor quite vine—thread through the hollow spaces in the wood, sometimes mechanical, sometimes organic, often both. Birds nest in such numbers that their combined presence creates its own weather: flocks that block out sections of sky, their collective wingbeats generating breezes that power the city's few remaining mechanical devices.

The machinery is old and integrated, grown into the wood itself. Steam engines carved from living heartwood burn no fuel but digest the city's organic waste, their brass fittings green with verdigris, their pistons moving with the patient rhythm of a sleeping giant's breath. Gear systems made of hardened root material turn elevator platforms between the levels, but they move so slowly that inhabitants often choose to climb instead. Pneumatic tube systems following the natural grain of the wood carry messages and small goods with soft sighs and whispers.

What makes this place strange—stranger than its mere scale or the fusion of forest and city—is the sense that it dreams. The wood remembers every conversation held within its chambers, every decision made in its councils. Sometimes the walls shift slightly in response to old arguments, rooms growing larger or smaller based on the happiness of their inhabitants. The city has opinions: it will not grow certain bridges, will not open certain passages, will encourage some construction and silently discourage others by making the wood brittle and uncooperative.

At night, the silence is profound but not empty. It's the quiet of something vast listening to itself, processing the day's input through networks of root and fungus and memory. The only sounds are the barely perceptible groans of settling wood, the distant whisper of sap moving through living pipes, and occasionally, from the deepest chambers, something that might be the city itself sighing in its sleep.

The inhabitants have long since stopped distinguishing between gardening and architecture, between biology and technology, between citizen and symbiont. They tend the city as it tends them, in cycles so slow and integrated that change appears almost static. A new district might take fifty years to mature. An old neighborhood might spend a decade considering whether to shift its orientation toward better light. The city ages not by decay but by deepening, becoming more itself with each passing season, more strange, more quietly alive.
