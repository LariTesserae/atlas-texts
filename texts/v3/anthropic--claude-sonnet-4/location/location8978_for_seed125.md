In a forgotten corner of the world, where ancient ice sheets once ground stone to powder, lies a hidden hollow no larger than a cathedral's nave. The depression sits cupped between low, weathered ridges that barely rise above the surrounding flatland, their surfaces still bearing the fresh scars of recent glacial retreat—parallel grooves and scattered boulders speaking of ice that withdrew perhaps only decades ago.

The air hangs bitter and still, carrying the metallic bite of perpetual near-frost. No snow falls here, and no streams flow, yet the basin thrums with impossible life. The ground itself seems to breathe—a thick carpet of cushion moss spreads wall to wall, punctuated by dense colonies of club moss that cluster like miniature forests. Lichens paint every exposed rock face in gradients of sage and copper, while hardy sedges form tight, bristling mounds wherever the moss thins.

This vegetation exists without water as we know it. The plants here have adapted to extract moisture from the very air, their tissues gorged with antifreeze compounds that glisten like dew in the perpetual twilight. They grow in impossible density, each claiming its tiny territory in a living mosaic so tight-woven that no bare earth shows through.

Scattered among this green abundance stand the remnants of some long-ago presence—weathered wooden posts, their tops carved with simple spirals, connected by lengths of rope gone silver with age. Stone rings mark cold hearths where fires once burned, their insides blackened but swept clean by wind. A single structure huddles against the north wall: a small shelter built from fitted stones and roofed with overlapping bark sheets held down by carefully placed rocks. Inside, wooden tools lie arranged on stone shelves—awls and scrapers, a bow drill, carved bowls darkened with use.

The light here never varies, a dim gray glow that seems to emanate from the sky itself rather than any visible source. It's bright enough to see by, too dark for warmth. Sounds come muffled and rare—the soft creak of moss expanding and contracting with the temperature's subtle shifts, the whisper of sedge fronds brushing against each other, an occasional tick of stone cooling or settling. No birds call, no insects hum, no small creatures rustle through the undergrowth.

Yet the hollow feels far from dead. The vegetation pulses with its own quiet energy, growing and dying and growing again in cycles measured not by seasons but by decades. Patches of moss slowly migrate across the basin floor, advancing and retreating like slow green tides. The lichen colonies creep up the rock faces grain by grain, their boundaries shifting imperceptibly over years.

This is a place where time moves differently—where the absence of familiar markers like flowing water or animal movement creates a pocket of suspended existence. The cold preserves everything: the ancient tools retain their edges, the rope holds its strength, the shelter stands firm against whatever rare winds find their way into this hidden space. It feels like a room sealed away from the world's haste, a place where someone once came to wait, to work with patient hands, to tend the strange garden that needs neither water nor warmth to flourish in its own impossible way.
