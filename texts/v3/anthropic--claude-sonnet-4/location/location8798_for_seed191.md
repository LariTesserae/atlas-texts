The Felting Grounds stretch across a shallow basin where ancient geothermal vents once steamed, now reduced to the faintest whisper of warmth seeping through cracked earth. What water existed here departed millennia ago, leaving behind a carpet of crystallized mineral wool—threads of gypsum and halite that grew fiber by fiber over geological ages, now matted into a dense, feltlike surface that extends to every horizon.

The terrain rolls in gentle, worn undulations, each rise and depression softened by this peculiar mineral textile. Underfoot, the felted ground yields slightly, like walking on a vast, ancient mattress. Between the fibers, microscopic cavities hold pockets of still air, giving the entire landscape an insulating quality that traps the earth's residual heat and muffles all sound to barely perceptible whispers.

Scattered across this expanse, sparse clusters of wire-grass push through the mineral felt—metallic, thread-thin stems that ring almost inaudibly in the slightest breeze, their sparse seed heads catching what little moisture drifts in from distant air currents. These grasses grow in careful, geometric patterns, as if following magnetic field lines or responding to some deep electromagnetic pulse from the earth's core.

The old thermal bore stations dot the landscape at regular intervals—squat concrete housings for long-defunct steam engines that once powered textile mills built to harvest this natural felt. Their smokestacks stand like weathered fingers, some topped with the skeletal remains of steam condensers, others crowned with the rusted housings of primitive dynamos. Thick braids of mineral wool have grown up around these structures over decades, slowly incorporating them into the landscape's textile grammar.

A dim, amber light suffuses everything, filtered through a high haze of mineral dust that never quite settles. The light seems to emanate as much from the ground as from the pale sky above, the crystalline fibers catching and holding luminescence like fiber optics made of salt. This creates an atmosphere of perpetual gentle twilight, neither day nor night, where shadows are soft suggestions rather than hard boundaries.

In this quietude, the rare fauna move like ghosts. Felt-moths with wings of translucent gypsum navigate by the faint electromagnetic signatures of the old machinery. Their larvae spin cocoons that add to the ground's textile density, their silk indistinguishable from the mineral fibers after a season's weathering. Occasionally, a thread-snake appears—a creature no thicker than heavy wire, evolved to slip between the felted fibers, its scales crystalline and perfectly transparent.

The only sounds are the almost-musical tensions of cooling metal in the old machinery and the whisper-soft displacement of air as creatures move through the dense ground cover. Sometimes, when atmospheric pressure shifts, the entire landscape seems to exhale—a barely audible sigh as trapped air moves through countless mineral cavities.

This is a world where industry became geology, where the earth learned to weave, where the boundary between manufactured and natural dissolved into something entirely its own. The felt continues to grow, fiber by mineral fiber, following its own patient logic, slowly erasing the last traces of human intention beneath its inexorable, gentle embrace.
