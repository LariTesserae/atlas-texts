The Hollow Peaks stretch endlessly upward through layers of frozen atmosphere, their crystalline spires reaching heights where normal matter begins to thin and strange physics take hold. These are not mountains in any terrestrial sense—they are vast, translucent towers of living mineral that sing.

The peaks themselves are hollow, their walls formed from a glass-like substance that pulses with slow, bioluminescent veins. Within these transparent chambers, immense forests hang suspended in the frigid air. The trees grow not from soil but from nothing—their roots threading through vacuum, drawing sustenance from the mineral song that resonates through the peak-walls. These are trees of impossible geometry: their trunks spiral in helixes that shouldn't support their weight, their branches fork into fractal infinities, their leaves shimmer with an oily iridescence that catches what little light filters down from the dim, perpetually overcast sky above.

The sound is overwhelming. Each hollow peak acts as a resonating chamber, and the entire range vibrates in harmonics that can be felt in the bones of the mountains themselves. The wind, thin and knife-cold, whistles through the peaks' hollow cores and creates haunting chord progressions that shift with the slow, decades-long weather patterns. The trees themselves contribute to this symphony—their crystalline sap crackles and pings as it freezes and thaws in microscopic cycles, their leaves rustle with a sound like breaking glass, their roots hum as they filter nutrients from the singing stone.

The scale defies comprehension. Individual peaks rise for hundreds of miles, their bases lost in perpetual mist, their crowns disappearing into the strange, dim radiance of the upper atmosphere. The range itself spans continents, creating its own weather systems in the spaces between peaks. Vast thermal currents spiral up through the hollow cores, carrying seeds and spores in slow, centuries-long migrations between the suspended forests.

There is no water here as we know it—instead, the trees have evolved to process the mineral songs directly, their modified root systems acting as crystalline receivers that transduce the mountain's harmonic vibrations into chemical energy. They excrete a kind of alkaline frost that accumulates in the hollow chambers, creating hanging gardens of salt-crystal formations that chime and tinkle in the eternal wind.

The forests themselves are empty of animal life, but they are not still. The trees move with glacial purpose, their branches slowly repositioning over decades to catch the optimal frequencies from the stone walls. Sometimes entire groves will shift and sway in unison, creating massive chord changes that boom across the landscape like the breathing of some vast, sleeping god. 

In the spaces between peaks, the air grows so thin it barely exists, yet somehow the sound carries—perhaps through the stone itself, perhaps through dimensions that don't quite align with normal space. The peaks rise so high that their upper reaches disappear into a realm where the usual rules bend: gravity weakens, time moves differently, and the boundary between the living forest and the singing stone begins to blur.

Weather here moves in vast, slow cycles. Every few decades, resonance storms sweep through the range—periods when the harmonic frequencies align and amplify until the very air becomes visible, shimmering with standing wave patterns. During these storms, the trees glow brighter, their growth accelerating in fast-forward bursts that can reshape entire forest chambers in a matter of days.

The peaks have stood this way for geological ages, their form settled into a strange equilibrium between the upward thrust of their growth and the endless, patient wearing of the wind. They are ancient beyond measure, yet eternally dynamic, their surfaces slowly flowing and reshaping as the mineral life within them follows rhythms too slow for any ordinary perception to detect.
