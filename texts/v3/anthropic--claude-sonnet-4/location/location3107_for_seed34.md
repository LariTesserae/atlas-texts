The Forgotten Stair-City climbs through perpetual mist, its weathered stone steps ascending into cloud-wrapped heights that stretch beyond any horizon you could name. This is architecture on the scale of geology—a vast urban form carved directly into and from the mountain itself, each terrace and plaza and dwelling hewn from the same grey-green stone that forms the peaks. The city rises through climate zones, from the moss-heavy lower districts where condensation drips constantly from carved overhangs, up through terraced gardens that catch the thin moisture of passing clouds.

Water is everywhere here, but never in abundance—it seeps from the stone in thin films, collects in shallow basins carved millennia ago, runs in hair-thin rivulets between the fitted blocks. The mist that shrouds the upper reaches condenses on every surface, beading on the smooth-worn balustrades and pooling in the cupped hands of weathered statues. You can taste it in the air, feel it settling on your skin like a cool blessing. The sound it makes is barely perceptible—the softest percussion of droplets finding stone, the whisper of moisture sliding down carved channels that route the city's lifeblood from level to level.

The vegetation clings to whatever purchase it can find in this vertical world. Thin grasses grow between paving stones, their roots following hairline cracks deeper into the mountain's bones. Moss paints the north-facing walls in gradients of jade and emerald, thickening where the mist lingers longest. Higher up, where the air grows thinner and the cold sharper, only the hardiest lichens survive, creating abstract murals across the city's crown. But even this sparse life creates density—every surface supports something, layer upon layer of micro-ecosystems painting the stone in subtle variations of green and grey.

The scale defies easy comprehension. You could spend years walking these stairs and never see the same district twice. Neighborhoods stack upon neighborhoods, connected by causeways that span impossible chasms, by tunnels that burrow through living rock, by bridges so ancient their builders' names have been worn away by wind. The city creates its own weather—clouds form in its deep courtyards, winds spiral up its grand staircases, temperatures shift between the sun-facing plazas and the shadow-pools of its deepest wells.

This is a place that has found its equilibrium over vast time. Every step is worn smooth by countless feet, every handrail polished by countless palms. The buildings settle deeper into their foundations with each passing century, becoming more themselves, more stone, more mountain. Earthquake and frost and storm have tested every joint, and what remains has earned its permanence. The city doesn't fight the mountain—it has become the mountain, learned to flex with it, to channel water as the stone would channel it, to shelter wind as stone would shelter it.

The silence here is profound but not empty. In the deepest courts, you hear only your own breathing and the almost-not-there trickle of hidden water. But listen longer and other sounds emerge: the soft settling of stone, the brush of wind through narrow passages, the distant echo of footsteps that might be your own returning from the labyrinth of stairways. Occasionally, very rarely, you might glimpse movement—a bird threading between the terraces, perhaps, or something pale and quick disappearing around a distant corner. The city feels watched, attended, though by what or whom remains a question that echoes in the mist.

The light here is complex, filtered through layers of vapor and reflected from countless stone surfaces. Even at midday, everything glows with a soft, diffused radiance that reveals detail slowly, like a photograph developing. Shadows are gentle things, edges blurred by the moisture in the air. As you climb higher, the light grows brighter but thinner, until in the uppermost reaches you move through a luminous cloud-world where the distinction between sky and stone becomes a matter of faith more than sight.

This is a city that remembers itself perfectly but reveals its secrets slowly. Each district you enter feels lived-in, purposeful, but empty of those who gave it purpose. The density of human intention saturates every carved doorframe and fitted stone, yet no smoke rises from the countless chimneys, no voices call from the deep windows. Only the mountain's own life continues—the slow seep of groundwater, the patient growth of moss, the endless conversation between stone and sky that this vertical city was built to join rather than interrupt.
