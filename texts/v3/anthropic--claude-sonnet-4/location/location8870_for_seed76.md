The Weeping Highlands stretch across a vast elevated plateau, where ancient volcanic domes rise like sleeping giants from a floor of perpetual mist. Here, at nearly ten thousand feet, the air itself weeps—not from rain, but from some deep atmospheric alchemy that draws moisture from the thin air and transforms it into an endless, gentle condensation that beads on every surface and drips with the persistence of geological time.

The darkness is not the darkness of night, but of perpetual twilight—a pearl-gray luminescence that seems to emanate from the mist itself, as if the water vapor has swallowed what little light penetrates this high realm and redistributes it as a soft, directionless glow. Shadows exist here only as suggestions, gentle gradations in the omnipresent dimness that never quite resolves into true black or brilliant white.

Across this strange plateau, sparse clusters of trees emerge from the mist like islands—not forests, but scattered groves of ancient, moisture-drunk giants. These trees, species found nowhere else, have evolved thick, spongy bark that channels the endless condensation down their trunks in elaborate spiral patterns, their wood so saturated it gleams like polished stone. Between these tree-islands stretch vast meadows of peculiar grasses that grow in perfect circles, each blade precisely distant from its neighbors, creating natural mandalas that extend for miles with mathematical precision.

The silence here is profound but not empty. It's broken only by the soft percussion of ten million droplets falling from leaf and stone, a sound like distant typing or the whisper of some vast, patient calculation. Occasionally, from somewhere in the mist, comes the low harmonic hum of wind moving through the perfectly-spaced grass stems, creating an organic pipe organ of such low frequency it's felt more than heard.

The plateau's edges are carved by eons of this gentle but relentless moisture into smooth, undulating canyons that curve like frozen rivers. These channels are lined with a peculiar mineral deposit—a translucent, glass-like substance that forms when the condensation, rich with dissolved atmospheric particles, crystallizes over geological time. The canyon walls gleam with these mineral veins, creating a network of light-conducting channels that carry the dim twilight deep into the earth.

At the plateau's heart lies its strangest feature: a vast depression where the mist pools so densely it becomes nearly liquid, creating what appears to be a lake but is actually a basin of suspended water vapor so thick that objects float upon its surface. Here, the few creatures that inhabit this realm—pale, translucent things that might once have been birds or fish—drift through the dense mist-water as if swimming through cloudy wine.

The temperature remains constant, a warmth that seems to radiate not from any sun but from the stones themselves, as if the ancient volcanic heart of the plateau still breathes its gentle heat upward through miles of rock. This warmth creates the endless cycle: stone heat draws moisture from the thin air, which condenses and falls, which feeds the strange circular grasses, which breathe out more moisture, which rises and condenses again—a perfect, unchanging system that has found its equilibrium and holds it with the patience of geological time.

Nothing here ever truly changes, yet nothing is ever quite the same. The droplets fall in patterns too complex to repeat, the mist shifts in ways too subtle to track, the grass circles expand at rates measured in decades. It is a landscape locked in the most gentle of motions, a place where change itself has learned to whisper.
