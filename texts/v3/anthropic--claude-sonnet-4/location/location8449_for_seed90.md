In the hollow of what was once a great crater—so ancient that geological ages have softened its rim into gentle ridges barely higher than hills—lies a circular grove perhaps two hundred paces across. The depression catches and holds the cool morning mists that roll in from distant highlands, creating a pocket of perpetual dampness in an otherwise dry region. Water seeps constantly through the porous stone floor, pooling in shallow depressions carved smooth by eons of the same patient drops.

The vegetation grows with startling abundance in this sheltered bowl. Massive ferns unfurl fronds the size of wagon wheels, their surfaces beaded with condensation that never quite evaporates in the filtered light. Ancient mosses carpet every surface in velvet cushions inches thick, and between them sprout clusters of peculiar flowering plants—pale, translucent blooms that seem to glow faintly in the perpetual twilight. Vines as thick as a person's waist spiral up the scattered standing stones, these remnants of the original crater's shattered floor now worn into smooth, organic shapes by countless seasons.

But it is not the plants that dominate this space.

They move in the spaces between—creatures that should not exist, yet clearly do. Translucent salamanders the length of forearms phase in and out of visible spectrum as they glide across the wet stone, sometimes solid enough to disturb the moss, sometimes mere suggestions of movement in the corner of the eye. Schools of what might once have been fish swim through the thick air itself, their fins rippling against an atmosphere grown dense and strange with age and moisture. They cast no shadows, make no sound as they navigate between the fern fronds.

Stranger still are the walking birds—if birds they are. They stand motionless for hours on legs like twisted driftwood, their bodies shifting slowly between forms: now feathered, now scaled, now something else entirely. When they do move, it is with a fluid grace that seems to bend the very air around them. Sometimes one will simply... fold... disappearing into a dimension that exists perpendicular to the usual three.

The silence here is profound, broken only by the soft drip of condensation and the occasional whisper of moisture beading and running down leaves. Even the strange creatures make no sound—the swimming things create no splash, the shape-shifting birds' feet fall without footsteps. Yet there is a constant sense of presence, of many eyes watching from many impossible angles.

Time moves differently in this basin. The light never quite changes, filtered through layers of mist and canopy into a perpetual green-grey dusk. Seasons pass, but they register only as subtle shifts in which creatures phase in and out of visibility, which flowers bloom in their spectral cycles. The water continues its patient work, wearing the ancient stone ever smoother, pooling and evaporating and pooling again in rhythms that predate memory.

At the center of the grove, a shallow pool reflects not the misty canopy above, but something else—depths that seem to extend far beyond what the basin could contain, showing glimpses of other versions of this same space, other iterations worn smooth by different kinds of time. Sometimes shadows move in those reflected depths, shadows of creatures that might exist here, or might once have existed, or might exist in the spaces between what is and what could be.

The boundaries of this place feel absolute yet permeable—as if stepping beyond the gentle rise of the ancient crater's rim would mean leaving not just a location, but an entire way of being. Here, in this pocket of impossible ecology, the normal rules have been worn away as surely as the stone, leaving something older and stranger in their place.
