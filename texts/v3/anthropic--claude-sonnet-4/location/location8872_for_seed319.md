The Singing Cistern rises from the mountain's shoulder like a crown of broken teeth—ancient stone ribs that once formed a perfect dome, now weathered into graceful arcs that frame the sky. Within this natural amphitheater, water has claimed dominion. The basin brims with thermal springs that bubble up through limestone fissures, creating a series of interconnected pools that cascade down terraced walls in endless, patient waterfalls.

The water itself defies easy description. It hangs thick in the air as perpetual mist, condensing on every surface until the boundary between liquid and vapor dissolves. Droplets cling to the sparse vegetation—twisted juniper and mountain sage that emerge from cracks like desperate fingers—each leaf beaded with moisture that never quite falls. The atmosphere breathes wetness; stepping into this place is like entering the lung of some vast, slumbering creature.

Sound dominates everything. The falling water creates not one voice but a chorus: the deep bass thrumming of the primary falls, the crystalline percussion of smaller cascades, the whispered sibilance of steam rising from heated pools. But stranger still are the harmonic overtones that emerge from the stone itself. The curved walls and fractured dome act as a massive resonating chamber, amplifying and distorting every sound until simple splashing becomes a symphony of echoes that seem to sing in languages older than words.

The density here is overwhelming—not of mass, but of presence. Every cubic meter of air carries moisture, mineral scent, the breath of heated stone, and threads of sound that weave between them. The mist itself seems populated, thick enough to part around your body as you move. In the pools, thermal currents create visible streams of warmer and cooler water, braiding together in slow, hypnotic spirals.

Life thrives in this saturated world, though sparsely. Schools of pale fish move through the pools like living mercury, their bodies adapted to the mineral-rich waters. They surface and dive in synchronized patterns that suggest intelligence, or perhaps they're simply riding currents invisible to human eyes. Salamanders the color of wet stone emerge from crevices at dusk, their skin so translucent you can see the slow pulse of their hearts. High in the remaining arches, swifts nest in the perpetual mist, their calls adding sharp counterpoints to the water's deeper song.

The ruins—for this place was once deliberately built—emerge and disappear like thoughts half-remembered. A section of fitted stone here, a carved channel there that guides water in patterns too precise for nature. These remnants suggest the cistern once served some greater purpose, but time and the relentless moisture have softened all human angles into curves that seem almost organic. Metal has long since dissolved into rust-stained streaks on the stone. Only the water's paths remain as testimony to ancient engineering, and even these have been claimed and reshaped by limestone's patient accumulation.

The light filters through mist and steam in strange ways, creating pockets of visibility that shift like rooms in a house of vapor. Sometimes the sun breaks through to illuminate a single pool in gold, while everything around it dissolves into grey obscurity. Other times, the light seems to emanate from the water itself—a phosphorescent glow that has no clear source but makes the entire cistern shimmer like the inside of an abalone shell.

Temperature varies by microclimates within this small cathedral space. The thermal springs create warm pockets where tropical orchids somehow survive at altitude, their roots drinking directly from the heated water. But move a few feet away, and mountain coolness reasserts itself, moderated only by the ever-present humidity. The result is a gentle, enveloping warmth that penetrates clothing and skin, as if the place is slowly transforming visitors into its own watery nature.

Time moves differently here. The water's endless cycling—spring to pool to mist to condensation to spring again—creates a rhythm that seems both eternal and immediate. Mineral deposits accumulate with geological patience, yet the fish move in patterns that shift by the moment. Standing still, you become part of this temporal layering, feeling decades compress into heartbeats while seconds stretch into geological ages.

In the deepest pool, where the primary spring emerges, something moves that might be imagination or might be the heart of this place made visible—a slow pulse of darker water that rises from depths no one has measured, carrying with it the memory of pressures and darkness and the mountain's dreaming core.
