**The Sunken Arbor City**

High in the Thousand Falls Range, where ancient volcanic peaks pierce perpetual cloud cover, lies the drowned city of Verdant Hollow. What was once a mountain settlement carved into terraced cliffsides now rests beneath forty feet of crystal-clear springwater, fed by countless cascades that thunder down the surrounding stone faces in an endless, deafening symphony.

The water here defies expectation—perfectly transparent yet dense with life, it moves with the sluggish weight of centuries. You can see all the way to the bottom, where stone foundations emerge from carpets of jade-green moss that sway like underwater prairies in the gentle, persistent current. The temperature holds steady at the cool kiss of mountain springs, never warming despite the filtered sunlight that penetrates the liquid column in cathedral shafts of amber and gold.

Every surface—walls, rooftops, cobblestones, the skeleton of an old market pavilion—has become substrate for an explosion of aquatic vegetation. Massive kelp-trees rise like living pillars, their broad fronds creating a secondary canopy just beneath the water's surface. Delicate filaments of waterweed trace the edges of submerged doorways in gossamer curtains that part and reform with hypnotic slowness. Thick mats of moss blur the hard edges of human construction until streets become soft green channels and buildings transform into coral-like structures more grown than built.

The original inhabitants adapted rather than fled. Their descendants move through this aquatic realm with practiced ease, using simple reed breathing apparatus and weighted stone tools to tend vast gardens of cultivated lake grass and harvest the fruits of enormous underwater orchards. Their dwellings occupy the higher floors of half-submerged buildings, connected by a network of pontoon bridges and rope walkways that sway gently with each ripple.

Fish move through the streets in schools that flash silver and crimson—some small as coins darting between the stems of water-flowers, others massive and slow as floating boulders, their passage stirring sediment clouds that drift for hours before settling. Freshwater rays glide over rooftops with otherworldly grace, while colonies of luminescent polyps cluster around the remains of iron lamp posts, their blue-green glow adding an ethereal undertone to the muted daylight.

The sound is overwhelming and constant—the crash and roar of dozens of waterfalls creating a white noise that drowns conversation and forces the inhabitants to communicate largely through gesture and touch. Yet within this acoustic storm, more subtle sounds emerge: the deep, resonant songs of the great fish echoing off stone walls, the musical ping of water-drops striking metal, the whispered susurrus of countless plants breathing in the current.

Time moves differently here. The waterfalls carve their channels deeper by mere inches each decade. The great moss-carpets extend their territory building by building, year by patient year. Buildings slowly settle and sink, their upper stories gradually joining the underwater realm while new construction rises on floating foundations above. The city is always the same, always slowly changing, caught in a dream of green water and eternal rain.

In the early morning, when mist rises from the water's surface and mingles with the spray of the falls, the boundary between liquid and air becomes gossamer-thin. The city appears suspended between worlds—neither fully terrestrial nor completely aquatic, but something altogether stranger: a place where stone learned to swim and humans learned to breathe water, where the patient intelligence of plants has transformed catastrophe into a new kind of paradise.
