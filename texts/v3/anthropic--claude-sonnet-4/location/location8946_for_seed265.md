The Hollow Basin stretches before you like a great cupped palm, its rim barely visible through the perpetual mist that clings to the air. This is a place caught between seasons—neither fully wet nor dry, but something restless in between. The basin floor, still soft and yielding from its recent emergence, bears the fresh scars of its violent birth. Jagged ridges of dark stone thrust upward at odd angles, their edges sharp enough to cut the low-hanging clouds that drift past like wandering spirits.

Water seeps and pools in the strangest places here. Not rivers or lakes, but sudden springs that bubble up through cracks in the young rock, only to vanish again into hidden channels. The air tastes of dampness without rain—condensation beads on every surface, dripping steadily from the sparse clusters of pale, skeletal trees that have managed to take root in the loose scree. These trees seem to grow only in small groves, their branches reaching toward each other across empty spaces like gnarled fingers trying to touch.

The temperature hovers in that uncomfortable zone where you're never quite warm enough, where the moisture seems to creep into your bones. Your breath mists faintly, and the damp chill makes every sound carry farther than it should. And there are so many sounds—this place pulses with a cacophony that seems impossible for such an empty-looking landscape.

The noise comes from the animals, and there are far more of them than the sparse vegetation should support. Flocks of small, dark birds wheel overhead in shifting formations, their cries echoing off the basin walls in layered waves. From hidden crevices comes the constant scrabble and chatter of unseen creatures—something between squirrels and ferrets, perhaps, that have learned to live in the vertical world of the new stone faces. Larger shapes move through the mist at the basin's edges, keeping to the shadows but announcing themselves with deep, resonant calls that roll across the hollow like distant thunder.

The strangest inhabitants are the colonies of pale, slug-like creatures that seem to farm the moisture itself. They cluster around the spontaneous springs and beneath the dripping trees, their bodies gleaming like wet pearls. When disturbed, they emit a high, sweet humming that harmonizes in complex patterns—beautiful but alien, like music from another world entirely.

Scattered throughout this active ecosystem are the remnants of some earlier presence. Not ruins, exactly, but artifacts: circles of standing stones no higher than your waist, their surfaces worn smooth by the constant condensation. Primitive tools lie abandoned in the gravel—bone needles, stone scrapers, wooden handles worn to silk by countless hands. These objects seem freshly dropped, as if their makers had simply vanished yesterday, yet they bear the patina of age.

The light here never quite commits to brightness or darkness. Even at what should be midday, everything exists in a perpetual twilight, filtered through layers of mist and low cloud. The sun, when visible at all, appears as a pale disk floating behind gauze. This dim illumination makes distances deceptive—what looks like a nearby boulder might be a distant cliff face, what seems like a small grove might shelter creatures the size of horses.

As you move through this landscape, the loose stone shifts and clatters beneath your feet, sending sharp echoes bouncing between the ridges. The very ground seems alive, settling and resettling as hidden waters carve new channels beneath the surface. Steam rises from unexpected vents, carrying scents of mineral-rich earth and something else—something organic and complex, like the breath of sleeping giants.

This is a place in transition, still finding its shape. The basin walls show fresh fracture lines where new sections might calve away at any moment. The springs appear and disappear according to no pattern you can discern. Even the animal sounds shift and change—sometimes the air fills with birdsong, sometimes with the percussion of hooves on stone, sometimes with calls you cannot identify or categorize.

In the deepest part of the basin, where the mist grows thick enough to taste, there stands a single structure: a tower built from the local stone, crude but purposeful, rising just high enough to pierce the lowest layer of cloud. No mortar binds its stones—they balance through careful placement and the patient work of hands that understood weight and pressure. Around its base, the bone tools lie thickest, and the pale creatures gather in greater numbers, their humming creating harmonics that seem to resonate with something deep beneath the earth.

The tower casts no shadow in the dim light, and when the wind is right, you can hear something moving within it—not the scurry of animals, but the deliberate sound of work being done, tools being used, a presence that maintains itself in this hollow between worlds.
