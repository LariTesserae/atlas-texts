The Throat of Echoes lies cupped in a high mountain cirque, a bowl of ancient stone worn smooth by countless millennia until its walls curve like the inside of a vast shell. Mist clings to everything here—not the heavy fog of lowlands, but a fine, persistent dampness that beads on every surface and pools in the countless shallow depressions that pit the cirque's floor like old scars.

The stone itself tells of unimaginable age. What were once jagged peaks have been ground down to gentle swells and hollows, their surfaces polished to a dull gleam by wind and water and time. The rock is a deep gray-green, shot through with veins of darker mineral that create sprawling patterns like frozen lightning. In the deepest hollows, where moisture gathers, the stone has taken on a blackish patina, smooth as river pebbles.

From every crack and depression springs an explosion of plant life that seems almost violent in its abundance. Thick mats of moss cascade over the stone lips like green waterfalls, their surfaces so saturated with moisture they squelch underfoot. Ferns unfurl from impossible crevices, their fronds creating dense curtains that rustle wetly in the cool air. Ancient lichens paint the rock faces in brilliant oranges and yellows, while delicate flowering vines thread through it all, their blooms pale as bone in the filtered light.

The vegetation grows in scattered colonies, each pocket of green separated from the next by spans of bare, gleaming stone. This creates an archipelago effect—islands of lush growth floating in seas of polished rock, connected by thin threads of hardy plants that trace the moisture channels carved into the stone over geological ages.

The air carries a bone-deep chill that seems to seep up from the stone itself. Even when thin shafts of sunlight pierce the perpetual haze, they provide little warmth, instead creating brief moments of golden illumination that make the moisture-heavy air shimmer before fading back to the prevailing dimness.

But it is the sound that dominates everything—a constant, overwhelming symphony that fills the cirque from rim to floor. Water is the primary voice: the steady drip-drip-drip of condensation falling from a thousand surfaces, the musical tinkle of tiny streams threading between the stones, the hollow plop of droplets hitting still pools. These sounds bounce endlessly off the curved stone walls, creating layers of echo that build upon themselves until the air itself seems to vibrate.

Woven through this aquatic chorus are the voices of countless creatures. Birds nest in the fern curtains—not the bright songbirds of lower elevations, but strange, pale things with calls like bells or breaking glass. Their cries ricochet off the stone, multiplying and distorting until a single bird's call becomes a crystalline cascade of sound. Insects drone in the humid air, their collective buzz adding a deep undertone that thrums in the bones.

Most unsettling are the other sounds—clicks and scrapes and soft splashes that might be animals moving unseen through the vegetation, or might be the stone itself shifting and settling. Sometimes there are sounds like whispers or sighs that could be wind through the ferns, or could be something else entirely. The acoustics of the place play tricks: sounds seem to come from everywhere and nowhere, creating phantom echoes of footsteps that follow no visible creature.

In the scattered pools, pale fish drift like ghosts, their movements creating ripples that catch and scatter the dim light. Translucent salamanders cling to wet stone, so still they seem carved until they suddenly flow to a new position with liquid grace. Small mammals—voles or shrews—scurry between the plant colonies, their passage marked more by sound than sight as they rustle through the dense growth.

The few tools left by rare human visitors are simple things—carved bone fish hooks caught on thorns, a wooden cup wedged in a rock cleft, a flint knife lost in the moss. These artifacts seem almost apologetic in their presence, quickly claimed by the moisture and the reaching plants. Metal would not last long here; the dampness sees to that.

Time moves differently in the Throat of Echoes. The light shifts imperceptibly through the day, filtered and diffused until noon and midnight seem only variations on the same gray theme. Seasons pass in subtle changes—slightly less mist in what might be summer, a thinner skin of ice on the pools during whatever passes for winter at this elevation. The stone remembers everything and reveals nothing, keeping its patience as it has for eons, slowly, slowly wearing away grain by grain.

The strange quality of the place reveals itself gradually. Sounds linger too long, as if the air itself were thicker here. Reflections in the still pools sometimes show movement where none should be. Plants grow in spirals that follow no earthly pattern, and some of the stone formations seem almost architectural despite being purely natural—arches and columns and steps that suggest purpose without revealing it.

This is a place that exists in the margins, where the familiar world grows thin and something else shows through. It is small enough to cross in an hour, but vast enough to lose yourself in forever, its boundaries defined not by distance but by the quality of sound and light and the weight of its ancient, patient presence.
