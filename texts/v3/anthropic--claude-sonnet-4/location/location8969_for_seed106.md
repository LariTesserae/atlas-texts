The Glass Terraces of Vethara rise in crystalline steps from the valley floor, each level a testament to volcanic fury frozen mid-birth. Here, where ancient eruptions once hurled molten silica skyward, the obsidian has cooled into perfect geometric planes that stretch like black mirrors across the highland plateau. The terraces climb in razor-sharp increments, their edges so pristine they might have been carved yesterday, though they have remained unchanged for millennia.

Water seeps everywhere through hairline fractures in the volcanic glass, emerging as countless tiny springs that feed shallow pools on each terrace level. The moisture never quite evaporates in this warm highland air—instead it hangs as a perpetual shimmer, condensing on the flawless surfaces and rolling in perfect spheres down the obsidian faces. The sound is barely perceptible: the softest whisper of water droplets meeting glass, like rain on windows in a distant room.

Between the terraces, settlement has taken root in the most peculiar way. Structures of fitted stone—not glass, but granite and basalt hauled from distant quarries—perch on the broader ledges. These buildings seem to grow from the terraces themselves, their foundations melding seamlessly with the volcanic platforms. Narrow bridges of worked stone arc between levels, and staircases carved directly into the cliff faces connect the various tiers. The architecture is dense where it exists, creating small neighborhoods that hover above the valley like hanging gardens, but vast spaces of empty terrace stretch between these clusters of habitation.

The air itself glows with refracted light. The obsidian surfaces catch and scatter the abundant sunshine, creating an environment that seems lit from within. Even shadows here are bright, filled with reflected radiance that bounces endlessly between the mirror-like faces of stone. The effect is hypnotic—a landscape that seems almost to breathe light.

Life finds few footholds in this realm of glass and stone. Occasionally, a bird crosses the sky far above, or small lizards dart between the joints of the stonework, their scales catching the omnipresent gleam. But for the most part, this is a place of mineral silence and human craft, where the sound of footsteps on obsidian carries far across the empty terraces, and voices echo strangely off surfaces too perfect to be natural.

The settlements cluster around the springs, where residents have built ingenious systems of channels and pools to collect the ever-seeping water. These communities exist in perpetual twilight-bright serenity, their daily rhythms as unchanging as the volcanic glass beneath their feet. Time here seems crystallized, held in the same eternal stasis as the moment when lava became mirror and the earth decided to build stairs toward heaven.
