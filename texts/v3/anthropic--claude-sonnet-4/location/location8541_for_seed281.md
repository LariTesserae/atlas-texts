The Moss Hollows stretch across a series of shallow depressions in the earth, each basin connected by meandering channels that carry intermittent streams during the cool season's fitful rains. From any ridge, you can see the pattern repeating—bowl after bowl of verdant green extending to the horizon, separated by low rises of exposed stone and sparse gravel where the plant-mass hasn't yet claimed dominion.

But to call it simply "moss" understates the reality. What fills these hollows is a living architecture of bryophytes, lichens, and cushion-plants that have grown so thick and so intertwined that they form a continuous substrate several feet deep. The surface undulates like a vast, breathing carpet, soft enough that your foot sinks slightly with each step, releasing tiny puffs of spores and the smell of perpetual dampness. Beneath this green skin, the plant-mass continues downward in layers—living on top, decomposing in the middle, and at the bottom, a rich black humus that never quite dries.

Water seeps everywhere but never pools for long. It beads on the surface tension of moss-heads in the morning, trickles through the plant-matrix during the day, and emerges as tiny springs along the edges of each hollow. The streams that connect basin to basin flow clear and cold, often disappearing entirely into the moss-banks, only to bubble up again dozens of meters away. When the intermittent rains arrive—sometimes daily, sometimes absent for weeks—they fall soft and steady, absorbed so quickly by the plant-mass that puddles barely have time to form.

The temperature hovers in that cool range where breath might mist on still mornings, where water feels refreshing rather than shocking. This coolness preserves moisture in the moss-matrix long after surface water has evaporated, creating a microclimate that feeds back into itself, encouraging ever-more-luxuriant growth.

These are young lands, geologically speaking. The bedrock that forms the ridges between hollows still shows fresh edges where it fractured, and the depressions themselves feel newly carved—perhaps by ice sheets that retreated only centuries ago, leaving behind these perfect receptacles for the plant colonization that followed. There's a sense that you're witnessing an early stage of succession, the moment when moss-pioneers are transforming bare mineral earth into something alive.

The scattered human presence feels almost accidental—a stone cairn on a ridge here, the remains of a simple shelter there, barely more than stacked rocks with gaps where wooden beams once lay. These structures seem abandoned not through catastrophe but through gentle irrelevance, gradually being embraced by creeping moss-fingers that blur the boundary between built and grown. No roads connect them, no paths remain visible for long in the soft plant-surface that heals itself over footprints within a season.

But while human artifacts are sparse and fading, the animal presence is overwhelming—not in any single dramatic manifestation, but in the constant, layered awareness of countless lives inhabiting the moss-world. The plant-mass pulses and shifts with movement: voles and shrews threading tunnels through the middle layers, creating highways invisible from above. Birds nest in the denser cushions, their calls echoing across the hollows in complex choruses that shift as flocks move between basins. Insects rise in clouds when disturbed, and the surface of the moss betrays the passage of larger mammals—deer or elk or stranger creatures—whose hoofprints create temporary dimples that slowly spring back to level.

The soundscape builds from this fauna: not the single voice of any dominant species, but a layered composition that varies by hour and weather. Dawn brings territorial songs and feeding calls that crescendo as the light strengthens, while dusk carries different voices—rustling movements through the moss-layer, the splash of animals drinking at the intermittent streams, the distant trumpet of something large moving between distant basins. During rain, the sound of water on moss creates a gentle percussion that seems to encourage more vocal activity rather than silence it.

Light in the Moss Hollows has a quality shaped by the landscape's unique geometry. The shallow basins collect and hold illumination like cupped palms, while the plant-surface reflects and scatters it in ways that bare earth never could. Even on overcast days, the moss seems to glow with captured light, creating an even, diffused radiance that eliminates harsh shadows. When direct sunlight does break through, it reveals the plant-mass in extraordinary detail—every tiny leaf and stem distinct, water droplets acting as prisms, the whole surface alive with subtle color variations from deep emerald to silver-green to the bronze of older growth.

The strangeness here lies not in any single impossible element, but in the way familiar components combine into something just slightly outside normal experience. The moss grows too thick, the water moves too purposefully through its underground channels, the animal movements create too-coherent patterns across multiple basins. Observers report moments when the entire landscape seems to inhale and exhale in slow unison, though whether this is real movement or an optical illusion created by wind-patterns across the plant-surface remains unclear.

Change happens on the timescale of seasons rather than moments. You might return to a familiar hollow after months away to find the moss-surface noticeably higher, a stream channel shifted by several meters, new varieties of cushion-plants establishing themselves in previously uniform areas. The scattered human ruins sink a little deeper into the plant-embrace each season, their angles softening, their purposes becoming more mysterious. Yet within these slow cycles, there are moments of quicker change—sudden flights of birds reshaping the soundscape, rain storms that temporarily triple the flow in the connecting streams, the emergence of new springs that shift local drainage patterns.

Standing in the center of any basin, surrounded by the green walls of living matter that rise toward the distant ridges, you become aware that this landscape operates by rules slightly different from the familiar world. Water flows upward through plant-stems as readily as it flows downward through gravity. Sound carries differently through the dense moss-layer, creating acoustic properties that seem to compress distance while amplifying detail. The boundary between individual and ecosystem blurs as your footsteps become part of the landscape's constant, gentle reshaping of itself, as your breath adds to the moisture that feeds the endless growth, as your presence joins the chorus of life that makes these hollows something more than simple geography.

The Moss Hollows extend beyond sight in all directions, each basin a variation on the theme, each ridge offering glimpses of more hollows beyond, creating a sense that this is not simply a place you've discovered but an entire world governed by the patient, overwhelming logic of plants claiming dominion over stone.
