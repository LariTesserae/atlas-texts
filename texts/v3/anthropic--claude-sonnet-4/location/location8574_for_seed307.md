The Shatter Fields stretch beyond every horizon, a maze of crystal spires and razor-thin ridges that catch the relentless white light and throw it back in ten thousand brilliant fragments. This is a landscape born yesterday in geological terms—perhaps a few centuries at most—when some tremendous force rent the earth apart along perfectly geometric lines, leaving behind corridors of black stone that run straight as arrows between towering walls of ice-pale crystal.

The passages wind for dozens of miles between these walls, some narrow enough to touch both sides with outstretched arms, others wide as city squares but still dwarfed by the crystalline ramparts that soar three, four, sometimes five hundred feet overhead. The crystal itself is not quite transparent, not quite white—it holds light like thick milk holds moonbeams, glowing from within with a cold radiance that makes shadows sharp as knife cuts on the dark stone floors.

Water lives everywhere here, but reluctantly. It weeps constantly from the crystal faces in beaded streams that never quite freeze despite the bitter air, collecting in long narrow pools that mirror the sky in broken strips. The stone channels route these tears along their length, creating a vast network of shallow waterways that gurgle and chime as they flow, the sound bouncing between the walls until it builds into a constant crystalline chorus that fills every corridor. Sometimes the water finds fractures in the crystal walls and disappears with liquid whispers, only to emerge again miles away as springs that well up through the stone floor.

The plant life clings to existence in scattered, determined patches. Cushions of silver-gray moss colonize the joints where crystal meets stone, drinking the perpetual mist. Threads of wire-thin vines trace the walls, following moisture lines with mathematical precision, their tiny leaves like scales of tarnished metal. In the wider passages, stands of something resembling bamboo grow in perfect geometric clusters—hollow stems that sing different notes as the wind moves through them, adding their hollow music to the water's chiming.

The wind here has character. It flows through this landscape like a living thing, following the straight corridors for miles before hitting intersections and dead ends, where it wheels and roars and builds pressures that make the crystal walls ring like enormous bells. Sometimes these wind-currents trap warm air from distant places, creating pockets where the temperature rises just enough for brief blooms of more complex life—tiny flowers that last a day, insects that live a week.

Sounds layer upon sounds in these channels. The water's constant liquid conversation underlies everything. The wind adds its voice—sometimes whispers, sometimes shouts, often the deep harmonic moaning of air forced through crystal flutes. When rare birds pass overhead, their cries echo back and forth until they become something else entirely, stretched and multiplied into unearthly harmonies. The few small creatures that live here—pale salamanders in the pools, insects that look carved from crystal themselves—add their tiny scratching and buzzing to the symphony.

The light comes from above but seems to originate everywhere at once, reflected and refracted by every surface until the air itself glows. There are no true shadows, only gradations of brilliance. The intensity shifts as clouds pass over—when they do, the entire landscape dims to pewter and silver, then blazes back to its normal painful clarity. During storms, the whole maze becomes a lightning gallery, electricity earthing itself through the crystal walls in displays that can be seen from orbit.

Water pools in the intersections where multiple corridors meet, forming perfect geometric shapes—triangles, pentagons, complex polygons that mirror the angular mathematics of the crystal formations above. These pools steam constantly despite the cold, their surfaces broken by the occasional leap of a pale fish or the emergence of something that might be a frog, though it moves with odd precision, as if following invisible geometric guidelines.

The Shatter Fields extend beyond measurement. Travelers report corridors that run straight for a hundred miles without deviation. The pattern seems to repeat infinitely—always the same basic elements of crystal wall and stone floor and running water, but arranged in ever-more-complex configurations. Some passages spiral upward in vast helices, others branch and merge like frozen rivers of light. There are chambers the size of cathedrals where dozens of corridors converge, their floors carpeted in the pale moss and filled with the sound of wind-chimes made from crystalline formations.

At the edges of perception, always, there's the sense of vast activity—geological processes still at work, the crystal still growing, new passages still forming with sounds like breaking glass that carries for miles. The water levels shift slightly but measurably with unknown tides. Sometimes whole sections of corridor will ring with subsonic harmonics for hours, as if the earth itself were tuning some enormous instrument.

This is a place where mathematics has become landscape, where the normally slow violence of geology has been compressed into human timescales and geometric perfection. It is beautiful and terrible and utterly without compromise—a maze that could swallow continents and still be extending its perfect, crystalline arms toward new horizons.
