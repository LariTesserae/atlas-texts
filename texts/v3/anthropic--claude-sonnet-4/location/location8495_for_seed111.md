The Weeping Chamber lies nestled in a fold of ancient basalt cliffs, a cathedral-sized hollow where reality has worn thin like old cloth. Here, the stone itself perspires—not metaphorically, but literally—beads of mineral-rich water seeping continuously through porous volcanic rock that has been weathered into impossible smoothness over eons. The walls curve inward like cupped palms, their dark surface polished to a dull sheen by countless millennia of this gentle, eternal weeping.

There is no green here, no hint that chlorophyll ever touched this place. Instead, the chamber pulses with stranger life—translucent, jellyfish-like organisms that drift through the humid air, their bodies catching what little light filters down from cracks above. They are not quite animal, not quite something else entirely, existing in that liminal space where familiar categories dissolve. Some cluster in hanging colonies that sway without wind, others pulse solo through the thick, warm atmosphere, their movements creating soft *plop* and *whisper* sounds as they brush against the weeping walls.

The temperature holds at the edge of comfort—warm enough that the constant moisture never quite condenses into proper droplets, instead hanging as a fine mist that makes every surface slick and every breath slightly labored. You taste minerals on your tongue: iron, salt, something faintly metallic and alive.

Sound dominates this space with surprising force. The weeping walls create a constant susurrus—a background whisper-hiss as water seeps through stone. But louder still are the *calls* of the floating organisms: hollow, resonant tones like someone blowing across bottle mouths, but in harmonies that shouldn't work, that make your inner ear ache with their almost-rightness. The acoustics of the chamber amplify everything, turning each droplet's fall into a tiny bell, each creature's movement into a percussion section.

The floor lies perhaps thirty feet below the highest point of the domed ceiling—intimate enough that you could shout to someone across the space, vast enough that sound travels strangely, seeming to come from everywhere at once. Underfoot, the basalt has been worn into smooth, interlocking pools, each one perfectly still despite the constant water input, as if the liquid here follows different rules about surface tension and flow.

In some pools, primitive tools lie abandoned—not metal, but carefully knapped obsidian and shaped bone, their edges still sharp despite obvious age. They seem to have been placed rather than dropped, arranged with a purpose that feels ritualistic, though no builder or user is evident. The tools' positions never change, even when the floating creatures drift directly through them, as if occupying slightly different layers of space.

Most unsettling is how the light behaves. What little illumination reaches the chamber—pale and indirect—seems to bend around the floating organisms, creating halos where there should be shadows, shadows where there should be light. Occasionally, for just a moment, you glimpse something that might be reflection in the still pools: not your face, but a landscape of impossible architecture, all flowing curves and surfaces that weep upward instead of down.

The chamber feels ancient beyond measure, locked in perfect equilibrium. The water that seeps through the walls has carved this exact configuration and stops, the floating creatures follow the same orbital patterns they have traced for millennia, the tools in the pools maintain their mysterious arrangement. Nothing grows, nothing decays, nothing changes—except for those moments when the light bends wrong, when the creatures' calls harmonize into frequencies that make reality feel thin, when you catch glimpses of that other place in the still water's surface, and wonder if you're looking through into somewhere else, or if somewhere else is looking back into you.
