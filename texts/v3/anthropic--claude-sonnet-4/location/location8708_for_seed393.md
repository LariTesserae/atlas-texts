The Forge Terraces stretch across what was once a broad valley floor, though no valley remains—only the vast stepped architecture of worked stone and fired clay that has wholly replaced the earth beneath. Level upon level of platforms rise in gentle gradations, each terrace perhaps fifty paces wide and twice as long, connected by ramps of sun-baked brick that wind between them in long, lazy spirals. The construction is ancient but sharp-edged still, as if the makers finished their work only decades ago, though clearly centuries have passed.

Each terrace holds its own character. Some are paved in fitted flagstones, their joints filled with a peculiar mortar that gleams faintly bronze in the warm light. Others are surfaced with ceramic tiles arranged in geometric patterns—hexagons, diamonds, interlocking spirals—fired in earth tones that shift from terracotta to ochre to deep amber as the eye moves across them. The craftsmanship is meticulous yet simple, achieved with basic tools and patient hands rather than complex machinery.

Scattered across these platforms, sparse but persistent, grow strange columnar plants. They rise like clusters of pale green candles, some as tall as a person, others barely knee-high. Their surfaces are ridged and waxy, designed to hoard moisture from the dry air. These plants appear only where the ancient builders left deliberate gaps in the stonework—small square apertures filled with a dark, mineral-rich soil that seems almost black against the warm earth tones of the surrounding construction. The plants never grow in clusters; each stands alone, separated from its nearest neighbor by dozens of paces of empty terrace.

The air itself feels constructed—too warm for the elevation, as if rising from hidden channels that conduct heat from some deeper source. It carries no scent of growing things, only the mineral tang of worked stone and the faint bite of metallic dust. When the wind does move across the terraces, it creates soft, hollow notes where it passes through the gaps in the stonework, like breath across the mouth of countless bottles. These tones layer and shift as the air moves, creating slow, patient harmonies that seem to emerge from the architecture itself.

The silence between these wind-songs is profound. No birds call, no insects hum, no small creatures rustle through the sparse vegetation. The only other sounds are the subtle ones: the tick and ping of stone expanding in the warmth, the whisper of dust settling, the distant grinding where the ancient masonry shifts by hairsbreadth increments as the seasons turn.

Above, the sky holds steady light—not harsh, but persistent, casting crisp shadows that reveal every joint in the stonework, every ridge on the columnar plants. The light seems to pool in the ceramic surfaces, gathering in the glazed tiles and radiating back with a gentle warmth that makes the air shimmer almost imperceptibly over the broadest terraces.

At the edges of vision, the construction continues—terrace after terrace extending toward distant boundaries that might be walls, or perhaps simply where the builders chose to stop. Nothing here suggests natural formation; every stone, every surface, every rise and depression bears the mark of deliberate placement. Yet for all its artifice, the place has found its own rhythm, its own way of being. The sparse plants have learned to thrive in their allocated spaces. The wind has discovered its songs. The stones have settled into their patient, gradual dance of expansion and contraction.

This is a place where makers worked with simple tools and infinite patience to transform an entire landscape into something between architecture and geology—not quite city, not quite sculpture, but a new category of constructed earth where the few living things that remain have adapted to dwelling within a wholly artificial world.
