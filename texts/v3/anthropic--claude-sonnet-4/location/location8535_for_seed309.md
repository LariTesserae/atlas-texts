Within the hollow of a single obsidian boulder, no larger than a freight container, lies a realm that defies its own boundaries. The interior walls curve in impossible geometries, their black volcanic glass surfaces worn smooth by some process that predates erosion—not weathering, but something more fundamental, as if the very concepts of roughness were slowly leaking away.

The space breathes with the soft whisper of machinery embedded in the glass itself. Hairline fractures trace delicate circuits through the obsidian, glowing faintly amber where microscopic filaments conduct current through pathways that seem grown rather than carved. These veins pulse in slow, patient rhythms—once every few minutes, a gentle surge of light travels the network, illuminating clusters of crystalline formations that sprout from the walls like geometric coral.

The air hangs cool and utterly dry, tasting of ozone and something metallic-sweet that might be the exhalation of the glass circuits themselves. No moisture exists here; instead, a fine particulate mist of pulverized minerals drifts in lazy spirals, each mote catching and refracting the amber circuit-light into prismatic fragments that dance across the curved walls.

The floor rises in concentric terraces of pure obsidian, each level perfectly smooth and separated by drops of exactly seven inches—a measurement that feels significant, though no architect walks this place. These terraces ascend toward the center, where the ceiling opens into a chimney that climbs impossibly high, its walls disappearing into darkness above. The perspective creates a dizzying sense of scale inversion: looking up feels like looking down into an infinite well.

Scattered across the terraces, mechanical forms rest in dormant arrangements. They resemble the offspring of clockwork and crystallography—brass gears fused with mineral growths, copper coils that have learned to spiral like ammonite shells, steam pistons crowned with perfect quartz points. Each mechanism is roughly the size of a house cat, and they number in the hundreds, arranged with the mathematical precision of a crystal lattice. They do not move, but they are not dead. Occasionally, one will emit a single clear note—a bell-tone, a whistle, a sound like metal cooling—and then return to its patient waiting.

The only fauna are the shadows themselves. In the amber circuit-light, shadows behave strangely here, pooling in corners where geometry suggests they shouldn't exist, or stretching across surfaces in directions that ignore the light source. Sometimes these shadows shift independently, flowing like dark honey from one mechanism to another, pausing to inspect or perhaps to commune with the dormant machines. They make no sound, but their presence creates the faintest disturbance in the mineral-mist, like the wake of invisible swimmers.

Time moves differently in the hollow. The light-pulses of the glass circuits mark moments, but their rhythm follows no earthly clock. Sometimes rapid sequences flash through the networks like thoughts racing, other times long minutes pass in amber twilight. The temperature never varies—always that perfect coolness that makes the skin aware of itself without discomfort.

This is a place of patient accumulation. Every surface bears the weight of countless small depositions: metallic dust settling in precise geometric patterns, mineral formations growing atom by atom along the circuit paths, the slow congregation of those strange mechanisms drawn by forces that operate on timescales measured in centuries. It is densely packed with presence and purpose, yet utterly still—a factory of dreams, perhaps, or the sleeping chamber of some vast distributed consciousness that experiences thought through the patient ticking of a thousand small machines.

The silence here is not empty but expectant, full of the potential energy of systems in equilibrium, waiting for some distant signal to wake them into motion that will transform this intimate cosmos in ways that cannot yet be imagined.
