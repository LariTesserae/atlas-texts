**The Rookery Gorge**

Between two knife-sharp ridges of black volcanic glass lies a narrow chasm no wider than a cathedral nave, its walls rising perhaps sixty feet on either side into the pewter sky. The rock faces are mirror-smooth obsidian, born from some recent volcanic upheaval that left them gleaming and unweathered, their edges still sharp enough to cut. But despite their newness, these walls weep constantly—not from any visible source above, but from within the stone itself, as if the mountain's grief condenses directly from its black heart.

The water seeps through invisible fissures, beading and streaming down the glassy surfaces in threads of silver, pooling in the narrow gravel floor of the gorge before vanishing again into hidden drainage beneath loose scree. The air itself seems saturated, heavy with moisture that makes each breath visible in small puffs, though the cold here is not the bitter cold of high peaks but rather the damp chill of a root cellar, held constant by the sheltering walls.

What sparse vegetation exists clings desperately to the minimal soil caught in horizontal cracks: pale lichens that look more gray than green, stunted cushions of arctic moss, and the occasional twisted dwarf willow no taller than a child's hand. These survivors huddle in their crevices like hermits in stone cells, drawing what life they can from the perpetual moisture and the dim light that filters down between the walls.

But the gorge thrums with a different kind of life entirely. Every ledge, every slight protrusion in the obsidian faces, hosts a chaos of nesting seabirds—though no sea is visible from this high mountain pass. Thousands upon thousands of murres, guillemots, and cormorants have claimed this sheltered cleft, their bodies packed so densely that the rock walls appear to move and breathe. They perch on impossibly narrow shelves, crammed wing-to-wing, their dark heads and white bellies creating a shifting mosaic against the black stone.

The sound is beyond overwhelming—a constant, crushing wall of cries, calls, and wing-beats that reverberates between the narrow walls until it becomes something almost solid, something you feel in your chest and bones as much as hear. Individual voices disappear into the collective roar: the harsh barking of seals that somehow live in the hidden pools, the shriek of wind-shredded calls, the thunder of ten thousand wings launching and landing in endless rotation. The acoustic chaos bounces from wall to wall, amplified and concentrated until conversation becomes impossible, thought becomes difficult.

Yet for all this frenetic activity, there is something timelessly static about the place. The birds follow patterns set in their bones; they fish the same invisible waters, return to the same precise ledges, call the same ancient calls. The weeping walls have found their rhythm of condensation. Even the scattered bones and guano on the gravel floor seem arranged in permanent configurations, as if this abundance of life and death and sound has reached some perfect, unchanging equilibrium.

The light that penetrates the gorge carries a quality of suspension—not quite dawn, not quite dusk, but something perpetual and in-between. It silveres the constant mist, catches the obsidian's gleam, and renders the massed birds into a living frieze that could belong to any century, any age. Time moves here only in the wheel of wings and the slow weep of stone—ancient rhythms in a landscape born yesterday.
