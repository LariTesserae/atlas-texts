The Hanging Gardens of Ashkenar rise from the valley floor in impossible defiance of gravity and geology, a vast terraced metropolis that climbs toward the clouds on pillars of living wood. What began as an engineering project has become something between city and organism, where the distinction between grown and built dissolves into irrelevance.

The foundations are massive baobab-like trees, their trunks hollow and chambered, housing the great steam engines that power the lifting mechanisms. These ancient giants—though "ancient" feels wrong for something so recently sprouted from engineered seeds—expand outward in concentric rings, each trunk wide enough to contain factories, workshops, and the humming dynamos that electrify the canopy above. Copper pipes spiral up their bark like metallic vines, carrying heated air and mechanical force to the platforms that bracket their enormous limbs.

Between the trunk-towers, suspension bridges of woven root-fiber stretch across dizzy gaps, their surfaces carpeted with moss that cushions footsteps and muffles the occasional conversations of workers. The air here is warm and thick with the exhalations of photosynthesis, tinged with the ozone scent of electrical systems and the sweet sap-smell of perpetual growth. Far below, the original valley floor has vanished beneath a canopy of smaller trees, their tops forming a green ceiling that few ever see.

Every surface that can support life does: the metal railings sprout bracket fungi that serve as impromptu shelves, while creeping vines thread through gear housings and transform mechanical joints into living hinges. The platform floors are not timber but compressed root-matter that continues to grow even as it bears weight, slowly reshaping walkways and occasionally opening new passages between levels. Street lamps are bioluminescent pods grafted onto the electrical grid, their soft blue-green glow supplementing the filtered sunlight that penetrates the endless canopy.

The inhabitants—a mixture of maintenance workers, botanical engineers, and the various small creatures that have made homes in this vertical jungle—move with the unhurried pace of gardeners tending a crop that measures its seasons in decades. Occasionally, a pulley system groans under the weight of materials being lifted to construct yet another platform, or the deep bass thrum of a steam engine reverberates through the trunk-halls, but these sounds are absorbed quickly by the omnipresent vegetation.

Birds nest in the junction boxes and weave their homes from discarded copper wire. Small primates have learned to operate some of the simpler mechanical switches, apparently for their own amusement. Insects pollinate both the flowering control panels and the more conventional blossoms that cascade from every vertical surface, creating an economy of mutual benefit that no one planned but everyone has learned to accommodate.

At the highest levels, where the platforms sway gently in the thermal updrafts, the view extends across an entire region of similar tree-cities, their peaks emerging like islands from a sea of green. The horizon shimmers with the heat of distant steam-works and the slow, almost imperceptible motion of megafauna—great creatures that browse the canopy tops and serve as living bridges between the various settlements.

This is a place caught between moments: no longer the wild valley it once was, not yet the fully realized vision its creators imagined. The trees continue their patient climb skyward, the machines continue their steady work, and somewhere in the balance between root and rivet, a new kind of landscape takes shape with each passing season.
