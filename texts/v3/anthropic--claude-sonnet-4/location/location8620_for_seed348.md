The Sunken Groves stretch across what was once a valley floor, now a vast depression where an ancient city has been slowly claimed by the patient advance of water and root. Here, in this bowl carved from limestone and shale, the boundary between settlement and wetland has dissolved into something entirely new.

Water seeps constantly through the porous bedrock, emerging in countless springs that bubble up between the foundations of long-abandoned buildings. The streets have become shallow channels, their cobblestones now slick with moss and algae, guiding streams that wind between structures in lazy meanders. Where grand plazas once spread, shallow pools reflect the pale sky, their surfaces broken by the gentle rise of gas bubbles from decomposing matter far below.

The cold here is not harsh but persistent—a bone-deep chill that keeps the air perpetually heavy with mist. Frost forms on the surfaces of buildings each dawn, only to melt by midday into trickling rivulets that feed the ever-present moisture. The temperature hovers just above freezing, cold enough to slow decay but warm enough to sustain the peculiar ecosystem that has taken hold.

Every surface blooms with life. Thick cushions of bryophytes—mosses, liverworts, and hornworts—carpet the walls of buildings in verdant tapestries. Where mortar has cracked, delicate ferns unfurl their fronds, and in the deeper shadows, fungi spread their pale networks across stone and timber alike. The vegetation grows not wildly but purposefully, following the architecture's lines, turning balconies into hanging gardens and filling window frames with living curtains of green.

This is a city in its infancy of transformation. The buildings stand intact but softened, their sharp corners rounded by decades of mineral deposits from the constantly weeping stones. Limestone walls show the white tracery of calcification where water has run in the same channels year after year, creating delicate formations that blur the line between natural cave decoration and human construction. The masonry is young in geological terms—perhaps a century or two of submersion—still recognizable but beginning its long journey toward dissolution.

The settlement spreads across several square miles of this sunken basin, dense with structures that rise from the water like an archipelago of inhabited islands. Narrow bridges, slick with moisture, connect building to building where the channels run too deep for wading. Every inch of space serves multiple purposes: rooftops support gardens of hardy succulents that drink the morning dew, while cellars have become root chambers where tubers grow in the perpetual dampness.

No metal tools glint here, no mechanisms turn. The inhabitants—for there are still inhabitants, though they move so quietly through their domain that they seem more like shadows—have adapted their lives to work with water and root, stone and fungus. They guide streams through carved channels, tend to the spread of beneficial molds, and harvest from the endless carpet of moss and lichen that provides both food and material.

Light filters down into this depression as if through water itself, diffused by the constant mist and broken by the canopy of low-growing trees that have claimed the higher ground at the settlement's edges. The illumination is soft, pearl-like, casting few harsh shadows but revealing every texture of the living walls, every droplet beaded on a leaf's edge.

The silence here is profound but not empty. It is filled with the whisper of water over stone, the soft patter of condensation falling from heights above, the nearly inaudible rustle of growing things expanding in their endless, patient reach. Occasionally, the creak of settling timber adds a note to this quiet symphony, or the gentle splash as someone navigates the shallow waterways.

Change moves through this place like the seasons themselves—measurable not in hours but in years. The moss advances across stone faces by fractions of inches each season. The mineral deposits grow their strange formations grain by microscopic grain. Buildings settle deeper into the waterlogged ground at rates that would require decades to notice, while new springs emerge and old channels slowly shift their courses.

The Sunken Groves exist in a state of perpetual becoming, neither fully wild nor entirely civilized, a testament to the strange beauty that emerges when human ambition learns to work within the patient rhythms of water and stone, cold air and growing things.
