In the highest reaches of an impossibly ancient mountain range, where peaks have been worn to gentle domes by eons of patient weather, there exists a hidden city built into the hollowed crown of what was once a mighty summit. The mountain's heart has been carved away by countless generations, leaving a circular basin perhaps two hundred paces across, its walls rising like the interior of a vast stone bowl toward a narrow opening that admits the sky.

The walls themselves tell the story of deep time—layer upon layer of sedimentary bands, each stripe a different hue of rust, ochre, and dove gray, polished smooth by the perpetual weeping of stone. Water seeps constantly through hairline fissures in the rock, creating vertical gardens of moss and lichen that grow in precise, dark green ribbons down the curved walls. The moisture never quite stops—not rain, but the mountain's own exhalation, a fine mist that rises from hidden springs and condenses on every surface, making the air thick and warm despite the extreme altitude.

The city itself defies logic. Structures have been carved directly from the living rock, following the natural grain of the stone so that it's impossible to tell where mountain ends and architecture begins. Narrow stairs spiral up the bowl's walls in dizzying helixes, connecting chambers and platforms that have been hollowed out like cells in a honeycomb. Bridges of shaped stone arch across the space at multiple levels, some so delicately carved they seem to float, their surfaces slick with the ever-present moisture.

But the city is not built for human scale. The doorways are too narrow, the ceilings too low, the stairs carved for feet that place themselves differently than ours. Bronze tools—hammers, chisels, strange curved implements whose purpose can only be guessed—lie scattered on work surfaces and embedded in the walls where they were abandoned. The metal has taken on a deep green patina, weeping its own copper tears down the stone.

The sound hits you first as you peer over the rim: a layered symphony of chittering, clicking, and humming that seems to come from everywhere at once. The city teems with life, but not human life. Thousands upon thousands of insects have claimed this place as their own—beetles the size of house cats with shells that gleam like polished obsidian, their legs clicking against stone as they navigate the carved walkways. Millipedes thick as a person's arm flow in undulating streams along the spiral paths, their bodies creating rhythmic percussion against the rock. Moths with wingspans broader than outstretched arms flutter between the levels, their wings catching what little light filters down from above and scattering it in prismatic fragments.

The insects have built their own structures within the stone ones—vast papery nests that bulge from windows and doorways, crystalline formations that catch and channel the seeping water, geometric arrangements of some unknown material that pulse with their own internal light. Their cities layer upon the carved city, organic curves meeting ancient angles in impossible harmony.

In the deepest parts of the bowl, where the last light barely reaches, pools of dark water reflect nothing. The surface tension breaks constantly as things move beneath—quick shadows that disturb the mirror without breaking through. The insects drink here in their thousands, creating a constant susurrus of tiny laps and sips that underscores the larger symphony above.

Occasionally, larger forms move through the insect masses—not insects themselves, but something else entirely. Pale, segmented creatures the length of a person but built like living ropes, they flow through the carved channels in the walls, appearing and disappearing through openings too small for their apparent mass. When they emerge, the insects part around them in precise patterns, as if following some incomprehensible choreography.

The air tastes of copper and time, warm and dense with moisture and the accumulated breath of countless tiny lives. Every surface vibrates with movement—not just the insects, but the slow shift of water finding new paths through stone, the patient expansion and contraction of metal and rock as temperatures change, the gradual transformation of the ancient city into something altogether new and strange.

Above it all, the narrow opening to the sky seems impossibly distant, a pale circle that admits only dim, indirect light. Weather passes across that opening—clouds, stars, the suggestion of a larger world—but down here in this hidden basin, time moves differently, measured not in days but in the slow accumulation of changes: new rust blooms on bronze, fresh channels worn by water, the gradual spread of the insects' own architectural ambitions across the surfaces their unknown predecessors carved so long ago.
