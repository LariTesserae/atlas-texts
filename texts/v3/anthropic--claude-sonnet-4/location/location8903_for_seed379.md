The Foundry Wastes stretch across a high plateau where the bones of an industrial civilization lie scattered like dice thrown by careless gods. Here, where the air shimmers with heat that never quite becomes unbearable, the remnants of great works rise from hardpan earth the color of old bronze.

Massive concrete pylons march across the tablelands in broken formations, their surfaces worn smooth by centuries of wind-carried grit. Between them, twisted rails of some extinct transport system curve through the emptiness, supported by rusted trestles that sing faintly when the thermal winds catch their hollow bones. The metal has taken on the patina of the place—not quite rust, not quite verdigris, but something between copper and ash that seems to pulse with its own inner heat.

The settlement that clings to this desolation defies easy comprehension. Structures of welded plate and riveted beam cluster around the still-functioning remnants of what might once have been refineries or power stations. Steam vents and cooling towers release their breath in rhythmic sighs—the only consistent sounds in this place of industrial whispers. The buildings seem to grow from one another organically, connected by networks of pipe and cable that snake overhead and underground with the logic of roots or veins.

Between the constructed spaces, the emptiness is profound. Kilometers of scarred earth stretch between each cluster of habitation, marked only by the geometric shadows of defunct machinery and the occasional glint of metal debris that catches the light. The ground itself tells stories—smooth basins where great machines once rested, linear scars where conveyors carved their paths, circular depressions that might have held storage tanks or processing vats.

Yet life persists here with startling abundance. Not plant life—nothing green has touched this place in living memory—but creatures that seem forged from the same metals and minerals as their habitat. Swarms of beetle-like entities with carapaces of tarnished brass move in complex patterns across the open spaces, their movements creating the only real activity visible from a distance. They cluster around heat sources, form spiraling columns that rise and dissolve without apparent purpose, and sometimes mass in numbers that darken entire sections of the plateau.

Larger forms lumber between the industrial remains—things that might be animals or might be escaped machines, their surfaces a patchwork of organic curves and mechanical angles. They move with deliberate purpose through territories marked by subtle chemical signals rather than visible boundaries. Some tend to the smaller brass creatures like shepherds; others seem to harvest something from the endless thermal vents that pock the landscape.

The sky above holds the memory of clouds without ever quite forming them. Heat distortion creates phantom structures that waver at the horizon—false cities, impossible towers, the ghosts of greater works that may never have existed. The light has weight here, pressing down through layers of industrial haze that filter it into something both harsh and diffuse.

At the heart of it all lies the great Converter, a structure so vast and complex that it seems less built than grown. Its cooling systems create their own weather patterns, drawing the sparse moisture from the air and releasing it as rhythmic exhalations of steam. The facility still functions, though for what purpose remains unclear—processing something from the earth, transforming it through arcane industrial processes, and releasing it again in forms that feed the strange ecology of metal and mineral that has claimed this place.

The silence between the mechanical sighs is not empty but expectant, as if the entire landscape holds its breath between one great pulse and the next. This is a world caught in the moment between collapse and transformation, where the detritus of one age becomes the foundation for something unprecedented and strange.
