The Sunken Valleys stretch across what was once highland country, now half-drowned beneath warm, mineral-rich waters that pool in every depression and flow in lazy currents between the hills. The original mountains still rise as wooded islands and peninsulas, their slopes disappearing into tea-colored depths where ancient root systems have adapted to perpetual submersion. Steam rises constantly from the surface in gentle veils, giving the air a thick, breathing quality that carries sound for miles.

Massive strangler vines cascade from the emergent trees down into the water, their aerial roots forming curtained grottos where the boundary between liquid and air becomes negotiable. Cypress-like giants stand knee-deep in the flood, their buttressed trunks swollen with adaptation, bark scarred smooth by decades of gentle wave action. Between them, floating meadows of water hyacinth and lotus drift in slow choreography, some large enough to support their own ecosystems of smaller trees and shrubs that have learned to live on living rafts.

The water itself teems with an overwhelming abundance of life. Schools of silver fish move in ribbons beneath the surface, their collective flash visible from the hilltops. Amphibians in countless varieties chorus from every reed bed and hollow stump—their calls layering into a constant symphony of trills, croaks, and bell-like tones that echo off the water and blend with the industrial drone that pulses from scattered installations.

Here and there, rusted platforms emerge from the flood on corroded stilts, remnants of some earlier attempt to work this drowned landscape. A few still function: pumping stations that churn and wheeze, their machinery green with patina, drawing up the rich bottom water to irrigulate higher terraces where cultivated groves cling to slopes. Steam-powered barges occasionally materialize through the mist, their paddle wheels churning brown foam, following channels marked by lines of floating buoys that bob like giant prayer beads across the valleys.

The light filters through layers of moisture and canopy in bands of amber and jade. Even at midday, the atmosphere holds shadows in solution—dim enough that the mechanical navigation lights on the old platforms burn constantly, their beams diffusing into soft halos that barely penetrate the verdant haze. Fireflies rise from the water plants at all hours, blinking in communication patterns that seem almost electric, as if responding to the pulse of distant generators.

Bird colonies nest in the upper reaches of the flooded trees, their populations dense beyond counting. They move in great clouds that darken portions of sky, their wingbeats adding percussion to the amphibian chorus and mechanical rhythms. Fish eagles and herons work the shallows in patient competition, while iridescent kingfishers flash like thrown jewels between the vine curtains.

The boundaries of this drowned world are hard to determine. Valley leads to valley through gaps between hills, each body of water connecting to the next in a network of channels and straits that extends beyond any horizon. The scale becomes apparent only from the highest remaining peaks, where one can see the pattern repeating—wooded islands, steaming waters, scattered mechanical installations, the whole system breathing with tidal rhythms and weather cycles that bring walls of warm rain to replenish what the sun slowly draws upward.

At night, the mechanical installations pulse with amber lights reflected endlessly in the still water, while bioluminescent organisms trace green fire through the depths below, creating a doubled constellation that makes it difficult to distinguish up from down in the gentle, eternal twilight of this half-submerged world.
