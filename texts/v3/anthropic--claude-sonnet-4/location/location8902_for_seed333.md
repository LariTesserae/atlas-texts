The Thundering Moors stretch endlessly beneath a dome of impossibly bright sky, where ancient plateaus dissolve into rolling vastness under the weight of geological epochs. Here, time has worn the world smooth—what were once sharp mountain ranges now rise as gentle swells barely a thousand feet above the surrounding expanse, their flanks carpeted in moss so thick it muffles footfalls like walking on centuries of accumulated silence.

But silence is the one thing this place lacks.

The sound begins before dawn and never truly ends—a deep, rhythmic percussion that seems to emerge from the earth itself. *Boom.* *Boom.* *Boom.* It pulses through the soles of your feet, rattles in your chest, fills the air with such presence that birds, when they appear at all, seem to time their calls to the gaps between beats. The source reveals itself slowly: scattered across the moors stand ancient stone formations, weathered into perfect hollow chambers by millions of years of wind and rain. Each one catches the constant cool breeze that flows across this elevated landscape and transforms it into a resonant drumbeat, deep as a cathedral organ's lowest note.

The wind never stops here. It carries moisture from distant seas, keeping the air thick with droplets that bead on every surface. The moss drinks constantly from this atmospheric well—emerald cushions of *Racomitrium* and silvery patches of *Leucobryum* that have grown so lush they form their own geography, creating hillocks and valleys independent of the rock beneath. In the depressions between stone drums, water pools in perfectly circular tarns, their surfaces dark as obsidian and so still they seem solid until a rare curlew alights to drink, sending concentric ripples across reflected sky.

The brightness here is startling—not harsh, but complete. Light seems to arrive from everywhere at once, scattered by the moisture-heavy air until shadows become suggestions rather than boundaries. Colors saturate differently in this luminous fog: the moss glows electric green, patches of bilberry shine wine-dark purple, and the weathered stone formations take on hues from pearl-gray to rust-orange to deep amber, depending on their mineral content and the angle of your gaze.

Scattered throughout this drumming landscape, the remnants of something older persist. Not quite ruins, not quite natural formations—carved stones that might once have been markers or altars, now worn smooth as river rocks. They appear without pattern, sometimes clustered near the largest stone drums, sometimes standing solitary on distant rises. Each one different in size and shape, unified only by an inexplicable rightness to their placement, as if they grew from the moors rather than being set there by hands.

The few creatures that inhabit this realm move like thoughts through a dreaming mind. A red deer materializes from behind a moss-covered boulder, stands motionless for long minutes listening to the stone drums, then fades back into the landscape as if it were never there. Mountain hares bound between the drumstones in erratic patterns, their white winter coats making them appear as spirits flickering through the perpetual mist. Ravens, when they come, arrive in ones and twos, never flocks—intelligent black shapes that seem to understand something about the rhythm of this place, often perching on the rim of a drum-stone just as it completes a particularly deep resonance.

The water cycle here follows its own ancient logic. Morning mist rises from the countless pools and streams, climbs perhaps fifty feet into the bright air, then condenses and falls again as fine droplets that never quite qualify as rain. This creates perpetual motion in the moss—you can watch it breathing, expanding as it absorbs moisture, contracting slightly as the mist lifts, then expanding again in an endless gentle pulse that somehow harmonizes with the deeper percussion of the drums.

As day progresses into evening, the light shifts but never truly dims—the saturated air holds illumination like a prism holds color, releasing it gradually so that sunset becomes an hour-long symphony of changing hues rather than a simple darkening. The stone drums seem to grow more resonant as temperature drops, their voices deepening, occasionally achieving harmonics that make the air itself seem to vibrate.

This is a landscape that exists in a state of eternal present tense—ancient beyond measure, yet constantly in motion. The drumming never stops, the mist never settles permanently, the water never finds final rest. It feels like walking through the circulatory system of some vast, slow organism, where every step, every breath, every heartbeat becomes part of a percussion section that has been playing the same cosmic rhythm since the mountains first began their patient surrender to time.
