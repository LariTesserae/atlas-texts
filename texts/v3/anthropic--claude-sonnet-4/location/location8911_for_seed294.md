The Hollows stretch across what was once a mountain range, now worn to bones of stone that pierce through a living mesh of metal and flesh. Millennia of erosion have carved the peaks into towering spires and impossible arches, their surfaces polished smooth as glass, yet the valleys between them writhe with industrial tumors—factories that have grown rather than been built, their smokestacks twisted like arthritic fingers, their walls pulsing with veins of copper and steel.

Mist rolls perpetually through these canyons, not quite rain but more than fog, carrying the taste of rust and ozone. It condenses on everything: the worn stone faces, the breathing machinery, the sparse clusters of silver-leafed trees that somehow root in pure granite. These trees grow in perfect mathematical spirals, their branches ending in crystalline pods that chime softly when the wind passes through them, adding delicate counterpoint to the deeper thrumming that emerges from the valley floors.

The cities here don't sprawl—they compress, layer upon layer pressed into the narrow spaces between the ancient stones. Buildings fold in on themselves like origami, their walls shifting between brick and bone, metal and membrane. Streets spiral upward in helical ramps that sometimes become elevator shafts, sometimes become arteries through which strange fluids pulse. The architecture breathes, literally—exhaling steam in slow, measured rhythms that echo through the stone canyons.

From the high places, you can see the pattern: thousands of these vertical settlements packed into every available crevice, connected by bridges that span the gaps like neural networks. The bridges themselves are alive, their surfaces rippling with bioluminescent threads that pulse in waves, carrying messages or nutrients or something else entirely. When the bridges pulse in unison, the sound builds into a roar that can be heard for kilometers—a mechanical heartbeat that sets the rhythm for everything below.

The wildlife has adapted in impossible ways. Flocks of metal birds with clockwork wings roost in the highest spires, their calls mixing the pure tones of struck chimes with the harsh shriek of grinding gears. In the misty middle distances, herds of something like deer move through the vertical cities, their hooves somehow finding purchase on walls and ceilings as easily as on floors. Their antlers grow in fractal patterns, branching into smaller and smaller iterations until the tips disappear into mathematical abstraction.

The sound never stops. The great machines in the valley depths churn and process materials that might be ore, might be organic matter, might be reality itself. Steam hisses from countless vents. The crystalline trees sing their eternal wind-chimes. Footsteps echo strangely in the folded architecture, sometimes arriving before the foot falls, sometimes lingering long after the walker has passed. Conversations drift on the perpetual mist, words spoken in languages that shift and evolve even as they're heard.

The light here exists in permanent twilight, filtered through layers of mist and the endless exhalations of the industrial-organic hybrid that fills every valley. Bioluminescent veins in the living buildings provide most of the illumination, pulsing in slow waves that make the entire landscape seem to breathe. Sometimes, when the mist clears for a moment, you catch glimpses of the ancient stone peaks catching starlight, but these moments are rare and brief.

Water here doesn't flow—it migrates. It collects in the breathing walls, travels through the living infrastructure, and emerges from unexpected places: a fountain that wells up from solid stone, a waterfall that flows upward before dissipating into mist, pools that form spontaneously in the air and drift like soap bubbles before bursting into brief rain. The residents have learned to read these water patterns, opening cisterns and vessels at precisely the right moments to catch what they need.

Time itself seems compressed in the Hollows. The ancient stones remember when they were mountains, and their memory bleeds into the present. The newer constructions—though "newer" might mean centuries old—experience moments that haven't happened yet. Workers sometimes arrive at their jobs before they've left home. Children age backwards for days at a time. The clocks in the spiraling cities all show different times, and somehow they're all correct.

This is a place where evolution never stopped, where the boundary between the made and the grown dissolved long ago. The Hollows continue to change, not gradually but in sudden convulsions that reshape entire districts overnight. Buildings migrate to new locations. Streets rearrange themselves. The metal birds sometimes become actual birds, and the deer occasionally remember they were once machines. In the deepest valleys, where the great processors work their unknowable transformations, new forms of life emerge daily—part mineral, part plant, part circuit, part dream.

The inhabitants move through this chaos with practiced ease, their routines adapted to a world in constant flux. They've learned to navigate by sound rather than sight, to predict the movements of their living buildings, to harvest water from the air and meaning from the temporal distortions. They are, perhaps, no longer entirely human—or perhaps they've become more human than ever, having learned to embrace change as the only constant in their impossibly compressed, eternally shifting world.
