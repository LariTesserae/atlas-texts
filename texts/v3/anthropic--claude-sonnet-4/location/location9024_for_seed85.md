The Floating Quarries of Keth-Moraan rise like a constellation of stone islands scattered across a shimmering void of superheated air. Each massive fragment—some the size of city blocks, others no larger than houses—hovers at different altitudes in the perpetual thermal columns that spiral up from the furnace-deep canyons below. The stone itself is young basalt and obsidian, still sharp-edged from recent volcanic births, gleaming black and deep red in the amber half-light that filters through the dusty atmosphere.

Between the floating platforms stretches only empty air thick with heat-shimmer and the constant drift of volcanic ash. The gaps yawn wide—sometimes a stone's throw, sometimes requiring the rope-and-pulley systems that the inhabitants have strung between the more stable chunks. These cables sway and sing in the thermal winds, creating a low, ever-present harmonic that mingles with the distant rumble of the deep furnaces and the occasional crack of cooling stone.

The settlement sprawls across perhaps two dozen of the larger fragments, connected by swaying bridges of woven metal wire and wooden planks. Simple stone buildings cluster near the centers of each platform, their walls built thick against the heat and fitted with narrow windows shuttered against the ash-laden air. The architecture is basic but ingenious—every structure designed to shift slightly as its foundation stone tilts and rotates in the air currents. Copper pipes snake between buildings, carrying precious condensed moisture from the rare cool-zones where thermal currents create brief pockets of lower temperature.

But it is the creatures that make this place truly alive. They pour from hidden crevices in the stone, emerge from the shimmering air itself, and cling to the undersides of the floating rocks in impossible profusion. Heat-dancers—translucent, jellyfish-like beings—pulse through the air between the platforms, their bodies catching and refracting the amber light into brief rainbows. Crystalline birds with wings of living glass ride the thermals, their calls like distant wind chimes. Most strange are the stone-swimmers: creatures that phase in and out of the basalt itself, leaving ripples in solid rock as they move, occasionally surfacing to bask in the heat-shimmer before diving back into their mineral ocean.

The very air here behaves strangely. Sometimes it thickens into visible currents that can support weight for brief moments. Other times it tears apart into void-pockets where sound dies and the amber light turns cold and blue. The thermal columns shift without warning, causing entire platforms to drift slowly into new configurations. What was a neighborhood connected by short bridges in the morning might be scattered across the sky by evening, requiring the inhabitants to constantly adapt their routes and routines.

In the workshops built into the platform edges, craftspeople work the still-soft stone with simple tools—copper chisels and wooden mallets, leather bellows to direct the natural heat. They mine the strange metals that sometimes crystallize in the thermal currents, forge them into tools and ornaments using furnace-vents that tap directly into the deep fires below. The work is interrupted frequently by the need to secure everything as platforms tilt, or by the sudden appearance of a heat-geyser that sends tools and workers scrambling for shelter.

As the day progresses toward its dim zenith, the activity becomes frenzied. The stone-swimmers grow more active, their phasing disturbing the structural integrity of platforms. The thermal columns strengthen and multiply, causing the entire archipelago to shift into rapid, chaotic motion. Some platforms spin like slow wheels; others bob and weave in complex patterns. The inhabitants move with practiced urgency, hauling in the longer bridges, battening down workshops, releasing tether-lines to let their fragment find its new place in the aerial dance.

The sound during these transitions builds from the usual quiet hum to a symphony of groaning stone, singing cables, and the alien calls of creatures stirred to excitement by the instability. And through it all, the craftspeople continue their work when possible, knowing that tomorrow the configuration will be entirely different, that the heat-streams will have carved new channels through the air, that some platforms may have drifted too far to recover while others, newly birthed from the deep fires, will have joined their precarious civilization in the sky.
