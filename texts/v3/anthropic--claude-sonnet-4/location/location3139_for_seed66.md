The Terrace Kingdoms stretch across the high plateau like a vast amphitheater carved by centuries of patient rain. What began as mining settlements has grown into a sprawling metropolis of stone and iron, built into the natural step-formations of weathered limestone that climb toward distant peaks shrouded in perpetual mist.

Water is everywhere, but never rushing. It seeps from the porous rock faces in steady streams, pools in the countless carved basins that serve as both cisterns and foundational elements of the architecture. The air hangs heavy with moisture—not oppressive, but present, like breathing inside a warm cave. Condensation beads constantly on the iron railings and copper gutters that snake between levels, creating a gentle, persistent dripping that forms the city's background percussion.

The terraces themselves are marvels of accumulated engineering, each level carved deeper and more deliberately over generations. What nature began with water and time, human hands have refined into a network of workshops, foundries, and residential quarters that climb the plateau face. Steam rises from a thousand chimneys, not black and choking, but white and constant—the exhalation of a city powered by the geothermal springs that bubble up between the limestone layers.

There are no trees here, no grass or flowering vines. The stone itself provides the only color beyond human construction: bands of rust-red iron oxide, streaks of green copper deposits, and the pale honey-gold of the limestone that forms the bones of everything. Where organic matter might have grown, instead there are vast colonies of creatures that have made this mineral world their own.

The fauna here defies easy categorization. In the perpetual dampness between stone layers, something like coral has learned to grow in air—branching, calcified structures that house thousands of small, chittering things with voices like dropped coins. They cascade down the terrace walls in living curtains, their constant movement creating shifting patterns of shadow and reflection. Larger creatures move through this abundance: six-legged things with shells like polished river stones that click and scrape as they navigate the stepped streets, and overhead, flocks of what might generously be called birds—if birds were made of translucent membrane stretched over frameworks of hollow bone, riding the thermals that rise constantly from the warm stone.

The sound of the place is layered like its architecture. The fundamental note is water—dripping, pooling, flowing through channels carved with mathematical precision. Above this, the industrial heartbeat: hammers on anvils, the steady chunk-chunk-chunk of steam-powered looms, the whistle and hiss of pressure valves. And woven through both, the living soundtrack of the creatures: clicks, chirps, the rustle of ten thousand small wings, and occasionally the deeper, resonant calls of the larger shell-backed things as they communicate across the terraced distances.

The light here is soft but sufficient, filtered through the ever-present moisture in the air. The sun, when it can be seen through the mist, appears as a gentle disk that casts diffused shadows. Most illumination comes from the warm glow of forge-fires and the copper-tinted lanterns that burn some locally refined oil, creating pools of amber radiance that reflect and multiply in the wet stone surfaces.

Everything here exists in a state of equilibrium achieved through centuries of gradual adjustment. The water flows have carved their channels and found their level. The industries have grown into their spaces like organisms finding their ecological niches. The strange fauna has adapted to live alongside human activity, neither fleeing nor competing, but weaving itself into the urban fabric as naturally as ivy might claim a wall—if ivy could sing and had shells and moved in coordinated murmurations between the steam-wreathed workshops.

Change happens here, but slowly, like the deepening of the water channels or the gradual darkening of copper pipes. Each generation adds another level, extends another terrace, refines another system, but always building on what came before, never disrupting the careful balance that makes this impossible city function in its mineral garden, surrounded by its chorus of adapted life, breathing in rhythm with the patient pulse of stone and steam and settled time.
