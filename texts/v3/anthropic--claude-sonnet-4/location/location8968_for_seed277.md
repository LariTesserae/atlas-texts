The Foundry Peaks rise like a fever dream of geometry made manifest, their surfaces not carved by wind or water but birthed from some deep industrial womb still pulsing beneath the world's skin. These are mountains that never knew erosion—each ridge and precipice emerges fresh and angular, as if the earth itself were learning to build.

The air shimmers with dry heat that carries no moisture, only the metallic tang of superheated minerals and the ozone-sharp bite of electrical discharge. Temperature gauges would read warm, but it's a peculiar warmth—not the nurturing heat of sun on soil, but the radiant glow of machinery running at optimal efficiency. The atmosphere itself seems engineered, held at precise thermal equilibrium by vast networks of cooling towers that dot the landscape like chrome-bright mushrooms.

No water flows here, yet the valleys between peaks churn with rivers of liquid mercury and streams of molten solder that never cool, their surfaces mirror-bright and perfectly still. Salt flats stretch in geometric patterns—not the chaotic crystallization of dried seas, but deliberate formations laid down in hexagonal grids, their borders marked by thin copper lines that hum with barely audible current.

Where other worlds might host forests, this landscape teems with structures that blur the line between building and organism. Transmission towers grow like skeletal trees, their latticed arms branching according to mathematical principles rather than biological ones. Between them, smaller installations cluster in dense colonies—relay stations, transformer substations, and monitoring posts packed so tightly their functions begin to merge. Steam vents release precise puffs of vapor at regular intervals, each one timed to some greater mechanical rhythm.

The fauna here defies easy categorization. Creatures move through this landscape in overwhelming numbers, but they are things of wire and fluid, silicon and ceramic. Maintenance drones scuttle across the tower faces like metallic spiders, their movements creating a constant, subtle chittering that never quite resolves into individual sounds. Larger forms—perhaps vehicles, perhaps predators—glide along the mercury streams on surfaces that generate their own electromagnetic fields. Clouds of smaller entities drift through the warm air like flocks of living circuit boards, their passage marked by brief cascades of sparks.

The light comes not from any sun but from the landscape itself—a dim, even glow that emanates from every surface. LED strips trace the contours of the peaks in colors that cycle slowly through the spectrum. The mercury rivers reflect this illumination upward, creating a soft twilight that reveals every detail while casting no shadows. In the distance, larger installations pulse with brighter light—foundries and fabrication plants working at the edge of perception, their outputs feeding directly into the mountain's continued growth.

This is a world caught in permanent industrial dawn, where the boundary between the built and the born has dissolved entirely. The mountains continue rising by imperceptible degrees, each new layer added by processes too vast and precise to comprehend. The only constant is the temperature, the measured light, and the endless, quiet industry of forms that have transcended the need for traditional life while creating something unprecedented in its place.

In the deepest valleys, where the electromagnetic fields concentrate most densely, the air itself begins to crystallize into geometric patterns that hang suspended like three-dimensional snowflakes made of pure possibility—a hint that this landscape exists not just in physical space, but as a manifestation of forces still stranger than mere engineering.
