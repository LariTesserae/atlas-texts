The mining settlement of Karasthen clings to a series of stepped terraces carved into the nearly vertical face of Mount Thessar, its foundations anchored by massive iron pitons driven deep into the rock wall. At this altitude—nearly three miles above the distant valley floor—the air carries a perpetual chill that seeps through wool and leather, while wisps of cloud drift between the settlement's levels like restless spirits.

The town exists in constant, controlled tumult. Steam-powered freight elevators clatter and groan as they haul ore cars up the cliff face, their brass mechanisms gleaming wetly in the dim light that filters through the persistent mountain mist. The sound of pickaxes rings from the mine shafts that honeycomb the mountain, punctuated by the distant boom of controlled explosions deep within the rock. Work never stops here—three shifts rotate through the darkness, their headlamps bobbing like fireflies through the settlement's narrow, zigzagging streets.

Water trickles constantly down the rock face in silver threads, collecting in carved gutters that channel it through the settlement before cascading over the edges in thin waterfalls that vanish into the mist below. The miners have learned to work with this perpetual dampness rather than fight it—canvas awnings stretch between buildings to catch the dripping condensation, and every surface is slick with moisture that never quite evaporates in the cool, thin air.

The buildings themselves are marvels of vertical engineering: narrow structures of local stone and imported timber, connected by suspended walkways and rope bridges that sway gently in the mountain winds. Smokestacks from the ore processing plants release steady plumes of white steam that merge with the natural clouds, while the rhythmic thrum of steam engines provides a mechanical heartbeat to the settlement.

Sparse mountain vegetation clings to any ledge or crevice that offers purchase—tough alpine grasses, stunted pines, and patches of lichen that paint the fresh-cut rock faces in muted greens and grays. The stone itself is sharp-edged and raw, showing clean fracture lines where it was recently blasted or carved. This is young rock, geologically speaking, still settling and shifting with audible groans that echo through the mine shafts.

Wildlife thrives in the settlement's margins. Mountain goats navigate impossible ledges with casual grace, their bells tinkling as they browse on scattered vegetation. Ravens nest in the cliff faces, riding the updrafts with raucous calls that carry for miles. Colonies of bats emerge from the mine shafts at dusk, filling the air with their chittering as they hunt insects drawn by the settlement's scattered gas lamps.

The whole place exists in a state of organized chaos—ore carts racing along narrow gauge tracks, workers shouting instructions over the noise of machinery, steam whistles marking shift changes, and the constant background percussion of industrial activity. Yet there's a rhythm to it all, a purposeful energy that speaks of a community that has learned to thrive in one of the most improbable places humans have ever chosen to call home.

At night, when the gas lamps flicker to life and reflect off the wet stone surfaces, Karasthen resembles a constellation that has fallen sideways against the mountain face—a glittering vertical city suspended between earth and sky, where the sound of hammers on metal mingles with the whisper of wind through stone.
