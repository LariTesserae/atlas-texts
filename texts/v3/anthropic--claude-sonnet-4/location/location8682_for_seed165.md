The Breath-Peaks rise like broken teeth from a world still learning to be solid. These are mountains so new they seem to pulse with their own birth-fever, their crystalline faces catching the brilliant light that pours down from three small suns wheeling overhead in restless arcs. The peaks themselves shift—not in any earthquake violence, but with the slow, deliberate breathing of stone finding its form. One moment a spire gleams translucent blue-white; the next it has grown another thirty feet skyward, or split into twin towers that weren't there before.

Water falls upward here as often as down. The peaks weep constantly—not from erosion, but from some deeper process where moisture condenses directly from the supercharged atmosphere, beading on razor-sharp crystal faces before streaming in impossible directions. Sometimes the water flows horizontally along cliff faces, defying gravity in silver ribbons that catch the tri-solar light and scatter it into prismatic cascades. The air itself is thick with vapor, though it never quite becomes mist—instead it hangs in the vast spaces between peaks like liquid light, so you can see for incredible distances even as every breath tastes of minerals and ozone.

In these enormous gaps between the mountain-spears, creatures move like living constellations. They are vast things, translucent and many-limbed, drifting on thermals that shouldn't exist but do—warm updrafts from valleys that themselves are changing shape as you watch. The creatures seem to feed on light itself, their bodies pulsing with absorbed brilliance before releasing it in soft chimes that echo strangely in the thin air. Schools of smaller beings follow in their wake: things like jellyfish made of structured wind, or origami birds that fold and unfold their geometric wings in patterns that make your eyes water.

The few structures here are clearly not meant for the peaks' current state. Scattered along ledges that jut and retreat without warning, you find small wayhouses built from some dark, non-reflective material that drinks light rather than casting it back. Their construction is crude but purposeful—hand-fitted stones held by wooden pegs, simple rope-and-pulley systems for hauling supplies, fire-circles ringed with rocks that bear the scars of many flames. But they are all empty now, their builders long departed or evolved beyond the need for shelter.

The wayhouses suggest that beings once traveled these peaks when they were more stable, perhaps following routes between the crystal formations that hummed with particular frequencies. Some of the empty buildings still contain tools: chisels for working crystal, shallow bowls that might have been used to catch the strange waters, lengths of cord woven from what looks like crystallized wind. But everything is covered in a fine dust that sparkles and moves on its own, rearranging itself into mandalas and spirals when you're not looking directly at it.

The silence between the mountains is profound but not absolute. The crystal peaks themselves whisper—frequencies just at the edge of hearing that resonate through bone and teeth. The floating creatures add their chimes, and occasionally the wayhouses creak as their foundations shift with the restless stone beneath. But these sounds seem to make the emptiness larger rather than filling it, emphasizing the vast hollowness between the peaks where ancient air moves in patterns older than weather.

As the three suns wheel through their complex dance, the light shifts in colors that have no earthly names. The peaks respond by changing not just their size and shape, but their very nature—sometimes becoming more transparent, sometimes taking on the quality of structured music made visible. And in those moments of transformation, shadows move in the deep valleys below: quick glimpses of something that might be the original builders, or their descendants, or something else entirely that has learned to live in the spaces between stone and light and the constant, gentle catastrophe of a world still discovering what it wants to become.
