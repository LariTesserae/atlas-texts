The Sunken Meadows stretch endlessly beneath the world's crust, a vast subterranean basin where crystalline formations in the ceiling capture and amplify the faintest surface light, casting everything in brilliant, dancing illumination. Water seeps constantly through limestone walls, creating perpetual curtains of droplets that catch the refracted light like suspended galaxies. The air itself seems thick with moisture, condensing into brief, glittering clouds that drift lazily across the cavern's breadth before dissolving again.

Across the uneven floor, patches of pale, hardy grasses grow in scattered clusters, their roots drinking from the countless shallow pools that dot the landscape. Between these verdant islands lie expanses of smooth stone, worn to silk by millennia of patient water flow. The grass itself has an otherworldly quality—translucent at the edges, nearly luminescent where the ceiling light touches it, rustling with a crystalline whisper when the rare, cool currents move through the space.

The temperature holds steady in the deep cold, breath visible in small puffs that dissipate quickly in the humid air. Stone formations rise here and there like sleeping giants: worn columns where ancient water carved perfect spirals, natural amphitheaters where the acoustic properties turn the gentlest sounds into resonant echoes. Every footstep seems to ring out across the meadows, every water drop creates expanding circles of sound that overlap and interfere, building into a constant, soft percussion that fills the space without overwhelming it.

Scattered throughout this underground expanse, the remnants of some long-forgotten presence persist: a circle of standing stones worn smooth as river rocks, their original purpose lost but their intentional arrangement still clear. Here, a series of shallow carved channels that once directed water flow, now barely visible beneath centuries of mineral deposits. There, what might once have been a simple shelter—a few worked stones arranged to redirect the endless seepage, creating a dry pocket in the perpetual dampness.

The tools that shaped these traces were simple things: stone hammers, bronze chisels green with age, wooden handles long since rotted away but leaving perfect impressions in the mineral buildup around them. Whoever worked here understood patience, carving their intentions into this place with methods as gradual and persistent as the water itself.

Animals are scarce but not absent. Occasionally, the pale flash of something moving between the grass clusters—perhaps descended from surface creatures that found their way down through sink holes generations ago, evolved now for this bright, wet darkness. Their calls, when they come, are hollow and echoing, adding unexpected notes to the meadows' constant water-song.

The light shifts slowly as surface conditions change far above, filtered through crystal formations that have grown into complex geometries over geological time. Sometimes the illumination takes on the quality of dawn, golden and warm despite the cold air. Other times it becomes silver-blue, like moonlight caught in ice. The grass responds to these changes with subtle shifts in color, and the water droplets become prisms that scatter tiny rainbows across the worn stone floors.

This is a place that exists in its own time, where change happens in measures of centuries rather than seasons, where the patient work of water and mineral creates beauty through processes so slow they become invisible to any single lifetime. The silence between the echoing drops is profound, a quiet that seems to extend beyond sound into something deeper—the hush of deep earth, the pause between one age and the next.
