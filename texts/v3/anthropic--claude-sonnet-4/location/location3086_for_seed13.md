The terraced city of Kethara clings to the steep flanks of a young mountain, its stone foundations carved directly into the sharp-edged granite that emerged from the earth only centuries ago. The settlement rises in careful steps, each level connected by narrow staircases hewn from the living rock, their edges still crisp and unmarked by the slow erosion that might, in distant ages, soften these precipitous walls.

Cool mist drifts through the city's levels like a slow river of air, pooling in the deeper courtyards before spilling over stone balustrades to the terraces below. The moisture comes not from any single source, but seems to breathe from the mountain itself—condensing on the cold granite at dawn, gathering in the carved channels that run along the edges of each platform, trickling into cisterns that never quite empty but never overflow. Intermittent rains sweep through the valley below, sending fingers of vapor up the mountainside that the city drinks in through its stone pores.

Between the inhabited terraces, lush gardens cascade in impossible abundance. Thick-stemmed vines with broad, waxy leaves spiral around the support pillars of bridges and walkways. Moss carpets every shaded corner in velvet green, while delicate ferns unfurl from cracks in the youngest stone. The vegetation grows with a patience that matches the mountain's stillness—each plant exactly where it was a decade ago, the same branch extending over the same balcony, the same flower blooming at the same stone corner, as if the entire ecosystem has found its perfect arrangement and sees no need to change.

The buildings themselves are marvels of simple engineering—stacked stone fitted without mortar, their walls thick enough to hold the cool mountain air inside even when the valley below grows warm. Wooden beams, shaped by adze and plane, support slate roofs that channel the occasional rainfall into those ever-present stone channels. The construction shows the careful work of generations, each stone placed by hand, each beam carved with simple tools that leave their honest marks in the wood grain.

Throughout this vertical city, life moves in quiet abundance. Mountain goats with silver-gray coats pick their way along the narrowest ledges, somehow always finding footing where none seems possible. Their soft hoofbeats on stone provide the only regular sound. Birds nest in the carved niches intended for them—small, dark swifts that emerge at precisely the same hour each evening, following the same aerial paths between the terraces. In the deeper pools, small fish circle in eternal, unchanging patterns, their scales catching what sunlight filters through the ever-present mist.

The unusual quality of this place lies not in any single strange feature, but in its profound stillness. Time seems to have paused here, not frozen but simply resting. The mist rises at dawn and settles at dusk with clockwork precision. The water level in every cistern remains constant. Even the people move through their daily routines with a measured quality, as if they too have found their perfect positions in this mountain tableau.

Sound here is swallowed by the stone and mist. Footsteps become whispers, voices carry only to their intended recipients, and the whole city maintains an atmosphere of perpetual, comfortable quiet. The silence feels not empty but full—pregnant with the potential for sound while perfectly content to remain hushed.

From any vantage point on the higher terraces, the view extends across a considerable valley where similar cities might dot other mountainsides, though they remain indistinct through the ever-present moisture in the air. This is a regional landscape, a network of such places connected by stone-paved roads that wind between the peaks, each settlement maintaining its own perfect equilibrium in the cool, misty heights.
