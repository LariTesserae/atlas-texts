The Copper Grove stands at the edge of a high plateau, where centuries of metallic dust have settled into the crevices of weathered stone. Here, in this cathedral-sized clearing bounded by walls of rust-red rock, something extraordinary has taken root.

The trees—if trees they can be called—rise in dense clusters, their trunks gleaming bronze and verdigris. These are not trees of wood and bark, but living metal, their surfaces worn smooth by countless seasons of gentle chemical weathering. Copper-bright branches spiral upward, supporting canopies of hammered leaves that chime softly when the rare breeze stirs them. Between the metallic trunks, crystalline ferns unfurl in impossible profusion, their fronds catching and refracting the warm afternoon light into prismatic cascades.

The air carries the faint ozone scent of electrolysis and the mineral tang of dissolved salts. What appears to be rich soil beneath reveals itself, upon closer inspection, to be layers of conductive sediment—centuries of accumulated metal filings and mineral precipitates that somehow nourish this peculiar ecosystem. No water flows here, yet the grove thrives on something else entirely: a slow circulation of metallic ions and electrical potential that moves through the ground like a patient bloodstream.

Ancient machinery sleeps beneath the grove's floor—not built structures, exactly, but the fossilized remains of some long-forgotten industrial process. Copper pipes emerge from the earth at odd angles, their surfaces green with patina, now serving as root systems for the stranger growths. Steam vents whisper between the metallic tree trunks, releasing not water vapor but thin streams of charged particles that spiral upward in barely visible helixes.

A few creatures have adapted to this place: beetles with shells of living copper that conduct the grove's subtle electrical fields, and rare serpentine forms that seem to be more cable than flesh, threading between the root-pipes with deliberate purpose. Their movements are slow, almost ritualistic, as if they too are part of some vast, patient circulation.

The light here has a peculiar quality—not quite natural, not quite artificial. It seems to emerge from the grove itself, a soft phosphorescence born from the slow oxidation of metal and the gradual discharge of accumulated potential. As day progresses toward evening, this internal glow grows more pronounced, turning the entire grove into a vast, breathing circuit board.

Time moves differently here. The grove has achieved its own equilibrium over generations of slow change—metal trees growing atom by atom, crystalline undergrowth extending in fractal patterns, the whole system balanced on the knife's edge between decay and renewal. The silence is profound but not empty; it hums with the subsonic frequency of vast, slow processes, like listening to the heartbeat of geology itself.
