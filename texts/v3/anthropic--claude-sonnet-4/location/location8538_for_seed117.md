The Singing Flats stretch beyond the horizon in all directions, a vast expanse of crystalline wetlands locked in perpetual morning. What appears at first glance to be water is something else entirely—sheets of liquid crystal that have pooled in the shallow basins between countless small hills, each no higher than a person's chest. The crystal-water holds the consistency of mercury but the clarity of mountain springs, never quite deep enough to drown in, never shallow enough to cross without wading.

These pools reflect and amplify the blazing white sky above, a dome of light so intense it seems sourceless, as if the air itself has learned to burn without heat. The brightness is clean and cold, casting no shadows—every surface receives equal illumination, creating a world without depth cues where distance becomes a matter of faith rather than sight.

The hills themselves are smooth and rounded, worn by eons into gentle swells covered in a thin carpet of silver-blue moss that grows in perfect geometric patterns—spirals, hexagons, branching fractals that repeat at every scale. No other vegetation breaks this sparse covering. No trees, no shrubs, nothing that reaches higher than ankle-deep. The moss pulses very slightly with bioluminescence even in the overwhelming daylight, a rhythm too slow to notice unless you watch for long minutes.

But it is the sound that defines this place. A deafening harmonic chorus rises from the crystal pools—not quite singing, not quite resonance, but something between music and physics. Each pool contributes its own note based on its size, depth, and the mineral content of its crystal-water. The combined effect is a wall of sound that penetrates bone and thought alike. The frequencies shift as wind moves across the surface of the pools, creating melodies that seem almost intentional, almost composed. Within this overwhelming symphony, smaller sounds hide: the subtle crack of crystal formations growing, the whisper of moss expanding along its mathematical patterns, the soft displacement of crystal-water as it moves according to currents no eye can detect.

Scattered throughout the flats, ancient structures emerge from the moss-covered hills—not built upon the land but grown from it, as if the earth itself had decided to experiment with architecture. These forms are organic and weathered, their original purpose long forgotten. They appear as smooth domes, twisted spires, and impossible arches, all the same silver-blue as the moss, all perfectly integrated into the landscape's geometry. Some contain hollows that catch and hold the crystal-water, creating new notes in the endless composition. Others seem purely sculptural, monuments to time's patient artistry.

The air itself participates in this strange equilibrium. Moisture condenses and evaporates in visible curtains, creating brief prismatic displays as water transforms to vapor and back again. The temperature hovers at that precise point where breath mists but skin doesn't shiver, where the crystal-water neither steams nor freezes but maintains its perfect liquid-solid ambiguity.

Nothing moves here but the wind, the water-vapor, and the subtle growth of moss along its predetermined patterns. No insects disturb the air, no fish swim in the crystal pools, no birds cross the burning sky. The landscape exists in a state of such perfect balance that it seems to have transcended the need for life as other places know it. Time passes, but nothing ages. The harmonic frequencies continue their eternal composition, each note as pure as it was a thousand years ago, as it will be a thousand years hence.

In this brightness without warmth, this music without musicians, this wetness without weather, the Singing Flats persist as a testament to what emerges when the world discovers its own voice and decides never to stop singing.
