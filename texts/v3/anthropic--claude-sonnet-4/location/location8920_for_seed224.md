# The Drowned Canopy

In the vast thermal layers of what was once atmosphere, a forest grows suspended. Here, three kilometers above the submerged earth, colossal trees stretch their root systems down through warm, mineral-rich water that fills the sky like a vertical ocean. Their trunks—some wide as city blocks—pierce upward through stratified zones of increasing darkness, their crowns lost in the crushing black depths above.

This is water with weight and history. Not the thin rain of old weather, but a dense, breathable medium that holds heat like a lover's embrace. Thermal vents, invisible but felt, send ribbons of warmth spiraling upward through the liquid atmosphere, carrying with them the chemical signatures of a world still cooling from its birth. The water itself glows faintly with bioluminescent microorganisms, providing the only illumination in this drowned sky—a constellation of living stars that pulse and dim with the forest's breathing.

The trees are new-made giants, their bark still soft and their massive branches growing visibly, hour by hour. Crystalline formations bloom from their surfaces like frozen waterfalls, mineral deposits left by the slow evaporation of mineral-heavy currents. These deposits crack and reform constantly, creating a quiet percussion that echoes through the water—the sound of geology learning to exist, of stone deciding its shape for the first time.

Between the titanic trunks stretch galleries of emptiness so vast that whole ecosystems drift in isolation. Here, in pockets of relative stillness, float gardens of impossible beauty: kelp-like vines that spiral in helical DNA patterns, their leaves broad as sails, filtering nutrients from the living water. Flowering air-plants have adapted to this liquid sky, their blooms opening only in the thermal currents, releasing clouds of luminescent pollen that drift like aurora through the dark water.

The fauna moves in vast migrations through these vertical territories. Schools of ray-like creatures, evolved from ancient flying things, soar between the tree trunks with wingspans that span city blocks. Their bodies are translucent, their movements generating barely perceptible pressure waves that set the hanging gardens swaying in long, hypnotic rhythms. Smaller creatures move in impossible numbers—streams of eel-like beings that flow between branches like rivers of living mercury, their passage marked only by the faintest whisper of displaced water.

Scattered through this aqueous forest are the remnants of the industrial age that preceded the Great Submersion: a factory smokestack that now serves as a hollow lighthouse, its interior colonized by tube-worms that glow like neon; the skeleton of a suspension bridge, its cables now thick with mineral growth, supporting nothing but providing highways for the creatures that have learned to navigate this vertical world. Steam-powered mechanisms, somehow still functional, pump and cycle in chambers carved into the largest tree trunks, their rhythmic beating like the heartbeat of some vast organism. These machines seem almost biological now, their metal surfaces encrusted with coral-like growths that have learned to process the mineral-rich water.

The darkness is profound but not complete. The bio-luminescence creates zones of soft radiance that fade into deeper shadow, while occasional flashes—the mating displays of deep-swimming creatures or the electrical discharge of some adapted machinery—illuminate vast distances for brief moments, revealing the true scope of this place. In those instants, one can see that this forest extends beyond any horizon, that its galleries and chambers stretch across continents, that what seems like a single ecosystem is actually a planetary biome, a new form of life that has claimed the sky as thoroughly as forests once claimed the land.

The water carries sound strangely here. The mechanical rhythms of the adapted industries create a bass note that travels for hundreds of kilometers, a subsonic pulse that coordinates the great migrations and perhaps helps the trees themselves communicate across vast distances. But mostly there is the profound quiet of deep water, broken only by the soft percussion of growing crystal, the whisper of creatures moving through liquid space, and the almost inaudible song of thermal currents carrying heat and life through this newborn world.

This is a place still deciding what it will become, where every day brings new formations, new adaptations, new impossibilities made manifest in the endless warm darkness of the drowned sky.
