The Thermal Plains stretch beyond every horizon, a vast shallow sea that spans continents under a blazing white sky. The water here runs warm as blood, never deeper than a few meters, heated from below by the planet's restless geological youth. Steam rises in towering columns that twist and merge into thunderheads, only to collapse back down in sheets of scalding rain that drum against the surface with the sound of ten thousand factories running at once.

This is a world still learning how to be a world. Beneath the warm, mineral-rich waters, the seafloor bubbles and shifts—not quite solid, not quite liquid. Volcanic vents breach the surface sporadically, spewing forth islands of pumice and obsidian that cool and crack in the superheated air, their edges already beginning to soften back into the restless earth. These temporary landmasses sink and rise like the breathing of some vast buried creature, their brief existence marked by the sparse, desperate vegetation that manages to take hold: salt-tolerant grasses that grow in impossible spirals, their roots seeking purchase in stone that may not exist tomorrow.

The water itself teems with mechanical detritus—not built by any intelligence, but extruded from the planet's core like a fever dream of industry. Pistons made of native copper pump rhythmically in underwater forests. Brass gears the size of wagon wheels turn slowly in the current, their teeth worn smooth by mineral-laden water. Steam engines built from volcanic glass and meteoric iron chug along predetermined paths across the seafloor, their purpose unknown, their fuel apparently inexhaustible. The sound they make—a constant, overlapping symphony of hissing, grinding, and metallic percussion—carries for hundreds of kilometers across the water's surface.

The light here has weight. The sun burns white-hot through an atmosphere thick with water vapor and volcanic gases, casting everything in a brilliant, diffused radiance that seems to come from all directions at once. Rainbows fragment and multiply in the endless mist, creating prismatic halos around the mechanical islands. The water itself glows faintly with bioluminescence from microscopic organisms that feed on the mineral soup, turning the entire sea into a vast, warm constellation.

Life, where it exists, is strange and sparse. Occasionally, something large moves beneath the surface—a shadow that might be biological or mechanical, its wake marked by a sudden silence in the industrial symphony. Schools of metal-scaled fish navigate between the grinding gears, their bodies adapted to temperatures that would kill most creatures. On the temporary islands, crystalline trees grow with impossible speed, their branches geometric rather than organic, shattering into musical fragments when the land beneath them shifts.

The weather here is violent and constant. Pressure differentials created by the planet's uneven heating spawn cyclones that can span entire hemispheres, their centers filled not with calm but with even more intense activity—waterspouts that carry industrial debris miles into the air before depositing it hundreds of kilometers away. The boundary between ocean and atmosphere blurs in these storms, creating vast volumes of space that are neither air nor water but something in between.

Time moves differently here. The landscape remakes itself daily, hourly, the mechanical systems below grinding out new configurations of reality with each cycle. What was east yesterday might be west tomorrow, what was deep might be shallow. The only constants are the warmth, the noise, the brilliant light refracting through endless veils of steam, and the sense that this whole world is still deciding what it wants to become.

In the distance, always just beyond the horizon, the sound of even greater mechanisms echoes across the water—engines the size of mountains, perhaps, or the planet's core itself, working with patient mechanical precision to birth something unprecedented from the marriage of geology and industry, water and fire, chaos and relentless, grinding order.
