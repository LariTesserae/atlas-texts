# The Stillwater Rookery

The place exists in a state of impossible suspension—a vast tidal plain that has been caught mid-flood for centuries, neither advancing nor retreating. Water stands knee-deep across miles of emerging mudflats, its surface mirror-still despite the overwhelming cacophony that rises from it. The water temperature hovers just above freezing, sending thin wisps of mist curling upward in the pale, unchanging light that filters through the perpetually overcast sky.

What first strikes you is the sound—a thunderous, layered symphony that never varies in intensity or rhythm. Millions upon millions of seabirds have made this their eternal roosting ground, their calls weaving together into a wall of noise so dense it becomes almost tactile. Gulls, terns, cormorants, herons, and dozens of species that seem to exist nowhere else in the world stand wing-to-wing on every available surface, their cries echoing and re-echoing until individual voices disappear into the collective roar.

The birds perch not just on natural formations, but on the remnants of what was once a sprawling coastal settlement. Wooden piers and boardwalks emerge from the still water at odd angles, their surfaces black with bird droppings and slick with condensation. Simple houses of weathered timber and thatch rise like islands throughout the flooded plain, their rooflines completely obscured by layers of nesting birds. Crude boats, little more than hollowed logs and woven-reed coracles, float motionless among the structures, each one serving as a platform for dozens of roosting fowl.

The vegetation is sparse but persistent—clumps of salt-tolerant grasses poke through the water in scattered tufts, their blade edges sharp enough to cut. Occasional stands of leafless, twisted trees emerge like gnarled fingers, their branches so heavy with birds that no bark is visible. Strange, rust-colored mosses cling to every wooden surface above the waterline, growing in perfect geometric patterns that hurt to look at directly.

But it is the impossible stillness beneath the noise that reveals the true nature of this place. The water never ripples, despite the constant movement of millions of wings above it. Dropped objects hang suspended just below the surface rather than sinking. The mist from the cool water rises in perfectly straight columns, defying any breeze. Time itself seems caught in amber here—the birds' positions shift constantly, yet somehow remain exactly the same. A cormorant that appears to be diving will hover at the same angle for hours, days, perhaps decades.

The settlement's inhabitants are long gone, but their tools remain scattered throughout the structures: stone knives dulled by centuries of disuse, bone fishhooks that never rust, leather nets that have become rigid as wood. Everything speaks of a people who lived simply from the water's bounty, until something—the flood, or perhaps something else entirely—transformed their home into this temporal prison.

In the distance, more of the same stretches to every horizon: an endless plain of standing water dotted with the dark silhouettes of more ruined settlements, all crowned with the same impossible masses of birds. The landscape holds itself in perfect, thunderous equilibrium, a place where the rules of physics have been gently but irrevocably broken, leaving behind a world caught forever between one moment and the next.

The light never changes, the water never moves, the sound never stops—and yet somehow, this frozen catastrophe has become its own kind of ecosystem, its own kind of home.
