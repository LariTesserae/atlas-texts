**The Sounding Bowl**

High in the volcanic highlands, tucked between two towering walls of black obsidian, lies a hidden amphitheater no larger than a village square. The rock here is so freshly formed that its edges still bite the air with geometric precision—sheets of volcanic glass that cleaved apart perhaps decades ago, leaving behind this secret pocket where the mountain's heart beats loudest.

Water seeps constantly from hairline fissures in the obsidian walls, each droplet catching the highland sun before joining the shallow pools that checker the bowl's floor. The pools are never deep—ankle-high at most—but they spread everywhere, separated by narrow causeways of dark volcanic sand and scattered pumice stones worn smooth by constant splashing. Steam rises steadily from the water's surface in the relentless heat, creating a thick, humid microclimate that clings to your skin and blurs the sharp edges of the surrounding cliffs.

The sound is overwhelming. Every droplet that falls from the weeping rock walls strikes water or stone with crystalline clarity, amplified and multiplied by the natural acoustic properties of the obsidian chamber. The walls act as a vast resonating instrument—each splash, each ripple, each bubble breaking the surface contributes to a constant symphony of liquid percussion that drowns out thought itself. The echo patterns are complex and layered: high silver notes from droplets hitting thin films of water, deeper hollow tones from larger falls striking deeper pools, and underneath it all, the constant whisper-hiss of steam rising.

Vegetation struggles in this harsh pocket. A few hardy moss species cling to the dampest cracks in the obsidian, appearing almost black-green against the volcanic glass. Scattered tufts of needle grass emerge from crevices where volcanic grit has accumulated, their roots somehow finding purchase in the minimal soil. Near the center of the bowl, where water collects most consistently, a single stunted tree—species indeterminate, perhaps a highland fig—extends gnarled branches no higher than a person's reach, its leaves thick and waxy to conserve what moisture the steam-laden air provides.

The fauna density is startling for such a small space. Clouds of tiny flies rise and settle continuously above the warm pools, their wings catching the light like animated motes. Jewel-bright beetles scurry across the wet sand between pools, and small frogs—no bigger than coins—populate every water pocket, their territorial calls adding to the acoustic chaos. Hummingbird-like creatures, their feathers iridescent blue-black like the obsidian itself, dart between the dripping walls, feeding on insects and the few hardy flowers that bloom in the rock crevices.

The temperature is crushing—perhaps what you'd expect at sea level in a desert, but here, trapped and concentrated by the volcanic rock and amplified by the humidity, it presses down like a weight. The air shimmers continuously above the water pools, and every breath feels thick, almost chewable.

The few signs of human presence are primitive and weathered: a circle of stacked pumice stones around one of the larger pools, perhaps used as a basin or ritual space; a series of shallow cup-marks carved into an accessible section of the obsidian wall, worn smooth by countless hands; scattered chipped obsidian flakes near the narrow entrance, suggesting someone once knapped tools here using the naturally occurring glass.

As the day progresses, the water levels and steam patterns shift subtly. The highland sun moves overhead, changing which cracks weep most freely. Afternoon shadows create brief respites of merely scorching heat rather than blazing. The acoustic properties change too—different water levels produce different resonances, so the bowl's voice deepens and brightens throughout the day like a massive, slow-breathing organ made of stone and steam and ceaseless, deafening song.
