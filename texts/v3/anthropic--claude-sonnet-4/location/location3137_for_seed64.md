Deep within the throat of an ancient volcanic caldera, where the mountain's heart once burned, lies a hidden cirque no larger than a cathedral's nave. The walls rise in perfect vertical sheets of black basalt, polished smooth by millennia of wind-carried grit, ascending so high they vanish into perpetual mist. No water has touched this place for geological ages—the air itself seems to drink moisture from your lungs.

Yet somehow, impossibly, life has found purchase here.

The floor is a living carpet of the most extraordinary vegetation: dense cushions of silver-green moss that seem to breathe with their own inner light, each pillow no bigger than your fist but packed so tightly there's no gap between them. From these emerge forests of crystalline ferns, their fronds sharp as shattered glass, refracting the dim light that filters down through the perpetual canopy of mist above. Towering over everything, pressed shoulder-to-shoulder in impossible density, stand columns of what might once have been trees—but these are things of mineral and metal now, their bark replaced by bands of copper and iron, their leaves delicate frameworks of tarnished silver that chime ceaselessly in the wind.

The temperature holds steady in that knife-edge zone between frost and warmth, where your breath barely mists but the metal trees ring with thermal stress, expanding and contracting in a rhythm that's remained unchanged for centuries. The sound is extraordinary—not quite music, not quite machinery. Each metallic leaf vibrates at its own frequency, and together they create cascading harmonies that echo off the basalt walls in waves of metallic song. Beneath this, the copper bark creaks and groans as it shifts, and deep below, something that might be roots or might be pipes hums with a low, industrial drone.

Scattered throughout this vertical garden are the remnants of some long-abandoned enterprise: brass gauges grown green with patina, their needles frozen at readings no one remembers how to interpret. Coils of corroded copper tubing snake between the mineral trees, occasionally erupting in small mushroom-clouds of harmless but acrid vapor. A single smokestack, no taller than a person, rises from the moss-carpet near the center, emitting nothing but adding its hollow whistle to the perpetual symphony when the wind finds its mouth just right.

There are no animals here, no insects, no birds—nothing that moves of its own accord except the endless dance of the metallic leaves and the slow, rhythmic breathing of the moss cushions. The absence feels deliberate rather than empty, as if life here has transcended the need for blood and heartbeat, finding sustenance instead in the slow circulation of minerals through root-networks that blur the line between organic and engineered.

Time moves strangely in this place. The light never changes—always that same pewter dimness filtering down through the mist. The moss breathes at exactly the same pace it has for decades. The metal trees sing their same eternal song. Even the vapor from the scattered apparatus rises and dissipates in precisely repeating patterns, as if the entire ecosystem has achieved some perfect, impossible equilibrium where entropy itself has been suspended.

Standing here, you become part of the stillness, part of the song, breathing the mineral-sweet air while the basalt walls hold their ancient vigil overhead, and the strange garden dreams its metallic dreams in the bottom of the world.
