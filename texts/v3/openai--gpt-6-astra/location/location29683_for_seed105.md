The valley is high enough for snow, but there is no air for snow to fall through.

Instead, pale crystals form in the water and drift sideways, gathering on the sheltered faces of stones. The water continues above them, above the ridge, above the last visible suggestion of a ridge. No surface can be seen. On clear days—clear meaning that the suspended mineral dust has settled—a faint grayness lies overhead. It gives the mountains their upper edges and nothing more.

The cold is almost still enough to become a material. It occupies the hollows, thickens between the stems of plants, and travels slowly downhill in transparent sheets. Where one of these sheets crosses warmer water, the landscape behind it bends. A distant terrace appears to lift from its foundations, then settles back as the cold front passes.

There is plenty of room here. Between one growth of vegetation and the next lie long reaches of bare shale, gravel fans, and sloping stone. The valley floor is broad, divided by low ridges into basins that cannot be called lakes, since everything is already submerged. Nevertheless, each basin holds different water. Some are clouded white. Some have a greenish darkness. One, beneath the eastern cliffs, is so clear that its floor seems nearer than it is; the broken stair descending into it has many more steps than can first be counted.

The plants favor the edges.

They grow thickly along cracks where slightly warmer water seeps from the mountain. Broad leaves overlap in dark, flexible shelves. Long stems carry whorls of narrow blades, each blade edged with a line of dull silver. Close to the ground there are plush mats the color of wet peat, spreading beneath the taller growth and climbing a little way up the rocks. Their oldest portions have hardened into a springy brown substance through which younger roots thread.

A single patch may be lush enough to conceal a house. Beyond it, fifty paces of naked stone.

Nothing grazes the leaves. Nothing nests among them. There are no small movements separate from the movement of the water: no sudden flight, no bodies slipping between stems. When a frond folds, the whole stand folds after it, and the reason is always a current, a change in temperature, or the slow adjustment of the plant itself.

The larger plants make their own dim light, though not continuously. A seam opens along the underside of a leaf and exposes a muted green, enough to illuminate the next leaf and the silt resting on it. A patch will brighten over several hours, then go dark for days. Across the valley these scattered awakenings reveal different pieces of terrain. Tonight a stone wall is visible. Tomorrow there may be only the roots growing through it, and farther off, a luminous stand marking a saddle between hills.

The mountains are old under this water. Their cliffs have lost the sharp rubble that once lay beneath them. Loose material has been sorted into bands: coarse chips beside the channels, fine sand in the lee of ridges, a soft flour in the deepest depressions. Exposed corners are rounded, but not erased. There are ledges broad enough to walk along, bowls deep enough to shelter an entire grove, and narrow clefts polished by generations of passing grit.

Much of that grit is still moving.

At intervals a current enters through the northern pass and crosses the valley at the height of the upper terraces. Its arrival can be heard before the plants lean. A low, rough sound develops in the rock, swells until the ledges tremble, and continues for hours. Sand strikes the cliff faces with a dry, close hiss, oddly distinct inside the greater noise.

The old structures were built below that current.

They are scattered rather than gathered into a settlement: a low chamber under an overhang, two walls beside a cleft, a roof of enormous flat stones supported on squat pillars. Most are made from fitted slabs, wedged with smaller stones. Some retain wooden braces, black and swollen but preserved by the cold. The wood was cut with broad, uneven blows.

Doorways face downhill. Roofs slope uphill. Each building has a narrow opening at its highest point, too small to enter, often surrounded by a crust of white mineral. Whatever once collected beneath those roofs was allowed to escape there.

One chamber still contains rows of shallow clay bowls. They are fixed to a shelf with twisted root cord, each tilted at a slightly different angle. Water passes through them in sequence. The first holds gravel, the next coarse sand, the last a stain like rust. Beneath the shelf, a stone pestle lies where it rolled against the wall. There are no ornaments and no writing, only repeated thumb marks around the rims.

Outside, the builders cut grooves into the slope to guide the warm seepage toward planted beds. The grooves still function. Roots have widened some and sealed others; low banks of vegetation now divert the flow across terraces that were never cultivated. Leaves accumulate in the abandoned channels, soften, and become dark sediment. New shoots rise through them. Where a seep fails, its garden thins over decades, leaving pale stems standing in the cold.

The strangest growth occupies the crest above the buildings.

From a distance it resembles a grove of slender trunks. Nearer, each trunk proves to be a root descending from somewhere overhead. They hang vertically through the darkness and enter the ridge in round, close-fitting holes. No branches are visible. No crowns. The roots are smooth, pale, and very slightly warm.

During the great currents they tighten.

The change is small but audible: a deep series of notes passing from one root to another, down the crest and out across the valley. Gravel shivers away from their bases. The fitted stones in the buildings answer with brief knocks. Long after the current has weakened, a vibration remains in the broad leaves and the soles of the terraces.

Above, there must be something that moves.

Below, the valley has accommodated it. Walls stand clear of the roots. Paths divide around them. At one place a stair has been rebuilt three times, its successive curves recording the slow sideways travel of a single pale column through the mountain.

Now mineral snow is collecting on those steps. A dark leaf brushes the lowest one, withdraws, and brushes it again. Far across the open basin, another garden begins to glow, revealing an intact roof beneath it and, behind that, the first few yards of a road climbing into black water.
