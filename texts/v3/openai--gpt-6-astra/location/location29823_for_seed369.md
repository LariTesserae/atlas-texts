The hollow lies just below the mountain’s highest shoulder, where warm air rising from the lowlands first brushes the stone. It is small enough to cross before a cloud finishes passing overhead. Beyond its lower lip there is nothing nearby for the eye to rest on: blue distances, a pale river far below, the dim backs of other mountains. But from inside the hollow, sitting low among the leaves, you see only a rim of weathered rock and the sky.

The ground is mostly bare, though the place is lush. Vegetation gathers in a few extravagant presences, leaving broad intervals between them. Three tree ferns stand at the uphill edge, each with a crown wide enough to shelter a whole patch of afternoon. A thicket of fleshy, jointed stems occupies one corner. Near the middle grows a single low tree whose branches descend, touch the earth, and rise again. Nothing crowds their outlines. Each has room to cast its complete shadow.

Between them, pale stone slopes in long, shallow curves. Its grain shows through the water covering it.

There has been rain, but perhaps not today. The hollow retains the consequences of rain so well that the event itself is difficult to place. Water shines in the seams. It beads beneath ledges that are dry on top. Put a hand against the rock and, after a moment, the heel of the palm is wet. Yet there is little mud. Most of the soil is held beneath roots, in dark beds thick with old frond stems and slowly collapsing leaves. Elsewhere the mountain presents its own skin: cream-colored, green in the hollows, warm wherever the sun has reached it.

At first the sound seems too large for the place.

It is a broad, continuous rushing, such as a river makes when heard through trees. There is no river here. Along the upper boundary, water seeps into a crack too narrow to admit a finger. Somewhere beneath the floor it drops through hollow stone. The cavity enlarges the falling water until the whole basin murmurs with it. Near the center, the sound has a low, rounded pressure. At the edges it breaks into separate splashes and small hard knocks. A person moving only a few paces can pass from apparent distance into apparent nearness without approaching anything visible.

Occasionally a single note comes through, clear and almost metallic. Then the broad rushing closes over it again.

The trees grow away from these notes. Their roots can be seen doing it. A root travels straight across exposed rock, reaches a place where the sound sharpens, and bends aside in a smooth elbow. Several roots have made the same accommodation, years or decades apart. Between them is an empty oval no wider than a sleeping body. The stone there looks ordinary. Rainwater lies across it without trembling.

The low tree has leaves as long as a forearm, thick, blunt-ended, and a little cupped. They are deep green above and silver underneath. In still weather they barely move, but a breeze climbing the mountain turns them one by one, so that brightness travels through the crown without the branches seeming to stir. The tree’s lowest limbs support beds of moss. Small ferns grow out of those beds, and still smaller plants grow in the lodged debris beneath the ferns. Its abundance is concentrated inward. A few steps from the outermost branch, the stone is empty again.

Warmth collects beneath this crown. It smells of wet bark, bruised leaf, and something faintly sweet fermenting in the roots. Out in the open, the air is lighter. There is enough altitude to make the sun feel close and the shadows unusually clean, but no bite of cold. Even the water is only cool. Clouds sometimes rise from below the rim and pass through the hollow waist-high, leaving the upper fronds in full sunlight.

When that happens, droplets gather on the silver undersides of the leaves before they gather anywhere else.

They grow slowly. Some remain suspended for much of an afternoon, broadening until they distort the stone beneath them. When they finally fall, they make a distinct tapping in the steady subterranean roar. Beneath each favored leaf-tip is a little pit, polished smooth and often holding a seed. The tree has been dropping water in nearly the same places for a very long time.

There are few animals. A dark snail occupies the underside of a loose stone, its shell stained the same cream as the rock. It may take several wet nights to reach the next patch of shade. Once, perhaps, a narrow bird lands on a dead fern stalk and holds its wings slightly away from its body. Its call is thin and difficult to hear against the water. After it leaves, the stalk continues to nod for longer than the visit lasted.

The hollow has no path, but it offers routes. Dry ridges join the rooted islands. A foot finds them easily. Here and there, flakes of stone have come away with edges keen enough to cut a stem, though most have lain so long that their exposed edges are soft and their buried sides dark. There is no sign that anything has been carried here or set in order. A fallen limb remains attached to the tree by a living strip of bark, making new leaves at its far end.

At the lower lip, all the water finally becomes visible. It gathers in a channel scarcely wider than a hand and slides toward the drop. After the noise beneath the ground, this little outflow is surprising. It passes over the edge without a splash. Leaning close, you can see it spread down the outside of the mountain as a trembling sheet, catching sunlight until it vanishes against the brightness below.

Toward evening the upper rock loses its warmth first. The leaves remain warm longer, and the soil beneath them longer still. The hollow begins to breathe moisture into the cooling air. Out beyond the rim, valleys disappear into blue shade; here, the last light stays on the undersides of the fronds.

The sound continues beneath everything. Nothing visible moves quickly enough to account for it. A leaf lowers by the weight of one more drop. A root presses into a seam. In the pale oval between the roots, shallow water reflects a small, unobstructed piece of sky.
