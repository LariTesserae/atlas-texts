Beneath the lowest step of the garden stair, where the ground has pulled away from the stone, there is a space about as large as two cupped hands. Most days its entrance is a black seam. This morning, a pale thread of light lies along it, bright enough to show through the wet earth lodged in the crack.

Inside, there is room for weather.

The roof is the underside of the step: coarse limestone, cool and beaded here and there with moisture. Below it, the ground has only begun to arrive. Little islands of chalky substance stand in a clear brightness, separated by intervals that look empty until something crosses them. Then a floor briefly becomes visible beneath the passing feet. It holds the creature’s weight, receives its shadow, and fades again.

The established islands have blunt edges. Nothing has been rounded by wear. Their grains fit loosely together, as though they have been set down and may still be rearranged. Along one margin, a row of crystals slowly tips upright. Along another, powder gathers into a shelf, hesitates, and spills upward to join the roof.

There is no dust in the light.

The light comes from the space left between things. Under a pebble it is almost white; in the open it has the faint yellow of a winter afternoon. Objects cast shadows toward whatever is nearest to becoming solid. These shadows are useful. Where several overlap, the ground tends to thicken. An insect resting for long enough can bring a small hill into existence beneath itself.

That is how most of the terrain has formed.

There are many animals here, though at first the place seems nearly vacant. They keep to different moments. A hollow may be empty while you watch it and crowded in the instant you turn your attention to the roof. Looking back, you find the evidence: a spray of pale eggs, six minute hoofprints, a long groove still closing in soft mineral.

Some creatures can be seen continuously. Flat, translucent animals cling beneath the limestone, their branching stomachs tinted green by things they have eaten. They move by releasing one edge of the body and letting it adhere a little farther on. Several carry young beneath them. The young are opaque, white as milk, and leave behind tiny patches of darkness which the adults return to lick away.

On the ground live jointed creatures with too many delicate legs to count. Each is no larger than a seed. They run in sudden, silent bursts between islands, stopping with the whole body raised. Their feet find purchase where the eye finds nothing. Occasionally one misses a step and falls a fraction of an inch, into a depth from which it takes a surprisingly long time to climb back.

Near the entrance, three blades of vegetation emerge from a damp crease. They are thick, nearly transparent, and tipped with closed brown mouths. No other green thing grows in the open. The blades lean away from the brightness and toward the stone. When a bead of condensation swells above them, they straighten.

Water arrives infrequently and changes everything.

A drop takes hours to assemble on the roof. While it gathers, animals collect beneath it at several distances, each kind apparently knowing how far the impact will travel. The smallest burrow into unfinished ground. The flat ceiling animals withdraw to the drier seams. A pale, bristled predator settles behind a ridge and folds its front limbs against its face.

The drop falls.

For a moment there is a round pool with a bottom much deeper than the chamber ought to allow. The limestone roof shines in it like a sky. Small swimmers appear at once, already turning, already pursuing one another through the cold water. Some are transparent except for a bright knot near the head. Others resemble scraps of skin drawn tightly around a flickering emptiness.

The pool lasts perhaps a minute. Its edges advance and retreat several times. At each retreat, a new bank remains: a lip of soft white earth, pierced with burrows that were occupied before there was a bank to burrow into.

The swimmers follow the sinking water. The last one goes down vertically, scarcely bending its body, through a wet spot no wider than its eye.

Afterward the air smells faintly of split stone and cucumber.

There are structures, scattered far enough apart that none can be seen from all the others. Beneath a roof blister stands a little arcade of empty snail shells. Their openings face a shallow basin. Something has joined them with dried secretion, making a passage just high enough for a soft-bodied inhabitant to drag itself through. It passes from arch to arch at irregular intervals, carrying a grain against its chest. The grains are placed in the basin and later removed. The basin is always nearly empty.

Farther in, a fence of upright root hairs encloses an area of bare brightness. Animals approach its edge, pause, and go around. Once, a long-legged hunter puts a forelimb between two hairs. The limb comes back wet. It spends a considerable time cleaning it.

The largest construction is a small earthen hood built against the step. Its maker is absent, or hidden within it. The hood has several entrances, most blocked with round white pellets. From the one open hole comes a current of air colder than the chamber. Nothing stirs in that current, yet the nearby soil develops fine parallel ridges, like sand beneath shallow running water.

The place is busy without being loud. There is the dry click of a loose grain finding its neighbours. A faint tack when a ceiling animal releases its grip. At long intervals, the roof gives a sound too soft to call a creak. Through it comes the distant pressure of someone using the stair.

That weight changes the chamber’s season.

The roof descends almost imperceptibly. The air brightens. Every exposed creature goes still except for the bristled predator, which springs across the basin and catches something that has not quite appeared. For a second the prey is visible between its limbs: a little folded animal, blue underneath, trailing drops of water. Then both are behind the new ridge.

Above, the foot lifts.

The chamber expands. Condensation vanishes from one part of the roof and begins gathering on another. The three transparent blades slowly turn. In the brief compression, a fresh patch of ground has become permanent, no bigger than a fingernail clipping. Its surface is smooth and cool, with a low rise at one end.

Already there are eggs on it.

Outside the seam, the garden remains grey, sparse, and ordinary. A few dead stems lean against the stair. Rain has darkened only the uppermost soil. Inside, under the stone, the new rise casts a sharp shadow into the brightness. Small animals come out to investigate its sheltered side, touching it with feet, mouths, and antennae. By the time the next drop falls, it will have paths.
