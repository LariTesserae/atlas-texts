The hollow lies only a little above the sea, behind a ridge of dark limestone. It is small enough that, from the notch where you enter, you can see almost all of it: the low roof of an abandoned bathhouse, several warm pools, and the enclosing rock, crowded with rounded shelves and shallow caves. There is no distant view. The ridge shuts out the coast, and the overhanging walls leave only a narrow, pale opening to the sky.

Everything is wet, though very little seems to be moving.

Water stands in the hollows of the stone and fills the seams between stones. It lies so close beneath the gravel that a footprint immediately darkens. Along the walls, a thin film spreads over black rock without gathering into distinct streams. You have to bring your face close to see it slipping over a grain, dividing around it, meeting again below. The air is warm enough that the damp does not chill your skin. It settles instead as a faint heaviness on your face and inside your sleeves.

There are no reeds at the pool edges, no moss on the steps, no roots working into the joints. The water is too mineral-rich, and the ground offers little that could be called soil. What looks from the entrance like a patch of pale turf resolves into needles of gypsum, matted together and glazed with moisture. Elsewhere, a dark skin of microbial growth lies beneath the water, so thin and uniform that it might be a property of the stone. Its edges are the only indication that anything lives there.

The largest pool occupies the center of the hollow. It is no wider than a modest courtyard, but its margins are intricate. Low mineral rims divide the shallows into a close-set arrangement of basins, some broad enough to sit in, others only large enough to hold a cupped hand. Water fills each to its lip. The rims are cream-colored on top and rust-brown below, with blunt, softened edges. Nothing is sharp except where a recent footstep has broken a crust.

Under the clear water, every surface is coated. A fallen pebble, a shard of pottery, the end of a wooden peg—all have the same dull, granular skin. Things remain recognizable here, but only just. The place gradually gives them a common finish.

There is scarcely any open, dry ground. Between the pools, ribs of limestone press close together, and the few places where one might stand are occupied by old paving slabs. The walls lean inward in heavy folds. Their lower portions have been eaten into pockets, then thickened again by deposits from the springs, until erosion and accretion are difficult to tell apart. A cavity has a lip; a projection has a hollow underneath. Every part of the rock seems fitted to another part, leaving narrow passages for water and very little room for anything else.

The bathhouse stands against the west wall, where the cliff provides most of its shelter. It is made of rough blocks laid without much mortar. Flat stones span its narrow doorway, and above them a few surviving roof slabs rest on short, blackened beams. The timber must have been carried here. There is nothing in the hollow that could have supplied it.

Inside are two stone benches and a sunken basin. The floor slopes toward a channel cut by hand, its uneven chisel marks still visible wherever the mineral coating is thin. A wooden sluice board remains in a groove at the basin’s outlet. It has swollen tight and become fixed there. Water passes beneath it in a shallow, steady sheet.

The builders did not try to straighten the place. One bench narrows where a shoulder of bedrock intrudes. The doorway is slightly off-center to avoid a seep. Outside, three steps descend into the central pool, but the lowest is a natural shelf with only its front edge dressed. The work has the economy of people carrying heavy tools down a difficult path: enough cutting to make a foothold, enough wall to stop a draft.

A shallow niche contains a broken clay cup. Beside the door, a flat stone has a smooth depression where something was ground or sharpened for a long time. There are no inscriptions. The traces of use are mostly at the height of hands and feet.

The light is dim even at midday. It enters from above, touches the upper wall, and reaches the pools after reflecting from wet stone. The water gives some of it back to the underside of the overhangs, where faint bands of brightness lie almost still. White deposits appear gray; the deeper rock is brown-black. In the bathhouse, you can distinguish the basin from the floor only after your eyes adjust.

There is no broad cloud of steam. The air and water are too nearly alike in temperature for that. Near one warm inlet, a faint haze softens the view of the far bank, but it stays low and transparent. The smell is limestone, soaked wood, and a little sulfur—stronger inside the roofed chamber, barely present by the entrance.

Sound gathers in small places. At the outlet beneath the bathhouse there is a soft, continuous friction, like cloth being drawn slowly over a rough edge. A seep in a wall pocket makes an occasional low tick. No echo follows it. The enclosing surfaces are too broken, the air too damp, and the distances too short. Even a footstep sounds close and contained.

You might find a small snail on the cooler underside of a paving stone near the entrance. Farther in, there is almost nothing for an animal to eat. No fish disturb the pools. No insects cloud the surface. A shed fragment of crab shell lies caught in the outer drainage notch, perhaps carried up from the shore by a bird, but there is no other sign of the visitor.

The hollow’s stillness depends on a great deal continuing exactly as it has. The springs supply the same quiet pressure. The shallow outlet takes away what arrives. Mineral grows on one face of a rim while water wears the other. Each basin remains full without spreading farther. The little channels have found their courses, and the rock around them has settled into shapes that accommodate the flow.

After a while, it becomes difficult to judge how long you have been looking. There are no branches to register a breeze, no passing shadows on open ground, no busy surface to follow. The few visible movements are so slight and so continuous that the eye stops separating them from the things themselves.

By the entrance, one paving slab stands a finger’s breadth above the damp. It is the only easy place to sit without getting wet. From there, the bathhouse looks less like a ruin than a portion of the hollow arranged for human bodies: a roof over warm water, a level place for bare feet, a bench within reach of a pool.

The broken cup is still in its niche. The sluice still holds. Along the lowest step, water keeps a dark line at precisely the same height.
