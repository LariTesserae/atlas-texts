The place occupies a shelf just below a mountain pass, no wider than a small village. From above, it looks like a dark green knot caught in the pale slope. Clouds snag there, thin out, gather again. Beyond its edges, the mountain is bare enough to show fresh fractures and the long blue shadows beneath slabs of snow. Inside, almost nothing mineral is visible.

The ground is wood.

Not a floor laid over earth, but a deep, interlocked growth of roots and buried stems, rising in some places to the height of a house. Moss packs the joints. Small stiff leaves emerge from cracks that would be mortar lines in another settlement. Even the steep outer wall is alive: roots descend in braided buttresses, thicken where they take weight, and spread their tips over the cold rock beneath. At the lower edge, some have not yet found purchase. They hang into the mist, pale and finely branched, with ice gathered on their ends.

There is little distance between structures. A passage may be only wide enough for a person to turn sideways, though no people live here and none appear to have made it. The walls on either side are grown from close-set trunks, flattened where they press together. Above them, branches join into low arches. Further up, a second layer of trunks stands on the first, rooted in pockets of accumulated leaves. The whole shelf is occupied vertically: chambers beneath chambers, narrow courts open to the sky, covered ways that pass under root masses and emerge onto higher terraces.

It has the compression of a town built within walls. Every sheltered surface supports something.

The tallest plants are barely twice a person’s height. Their crowns bend outward rather than up, burdened by frost and by the plants growing on them. Dark evergreen leaves lie in overlapping courses, turning runoff toward the edges of each crown. Beneath these roofs, smaller shrubs grow horizontally out of the walls. Their leaves are thick, curled almost shut, silver underneath. Where light reaches a seam, tiny red-stemmed plants crowd into it so tightly that their individual shapes disappear. They make a felt that can be peeled back by the weather but rarely torn free.

The cold is constant enough to become a property of the materials. Bark is hard and slick. Exposed moss gives under a foot with a faint crunch, then slowly rises. A leaf touched by bare skin feels less like foliage than a small piece of chilled leather.

Yet the place is not sealed in ice. Water remains close to the surface everywhere, moving through the living fabric.

It beads along the underside of arches. It darkens the walls of passages. It collects in the cups where roots divide and freezes first at the rim, leaving a black center that trembles when a drop falls. Some of these pools are no larger than a hand; one, in the central court, is several paces across. Its banks are steep cushions of moss, and its bottom cannot be seen. Thin roots cross just beneath the water, each carrying a row of tiny air bubbles.

Above the pool is the widest opening in the canopy. The sky there usually has the color of old snow. A little daylight descends through cloud and through the vapor rising from wet foliage, enough to show the inner walls but not enough to reach the passages beneath them. Those remain green-black even at noon.

Water follows several routes down through the settlement. Most are hidden, but they can be heard by leaning close to a wall: a restrained ticking, a brief trickle that stops, a deeper hollow drip. In one narrow lane, runoff has worn the moss away from the tops of three crossing roots. Their exposed wood is smooth and dark. The roots form steps, and water goes down them in a clear sheet scarcely thicker than a leaf.

Elsewhere, drainage has not yet settled into a channel. It spills through new gaps and leaves fresh white ice on anything below. These are the youngest parts of the place, where growth has pushed beyond the shelter of the older crowns. The stems there are straight and crowded, their bark unscarred, their angles abrupt. They make sharp little towers along the windward edge. Several have been split by freezing water. Through the splits, the inner wood is startlingly pale.

The entire mass began around a few low, spreading trees rooted in a fissure of the mountain shelf. Their branches touched the ground, rooted, and sent up further stems. Over many seasons, fallen limbs became supports for new growth, and the spaces between them narrowed. Now the oldest trees are difficult to locate. A trunk descends from a roof, turns along a wall, disappears beneath a mound of moss, and rises again on the other side of a passage. What looks like a row of separate buildings may belong to one plant.

There is no central plan, but there are repeated accommodations. The outer growth takes the wind. The inner growth keeps the damp. Long descending roots carry water past chambers that would otherwise fill. Where snow drops through the canopy, flexible shrubs bend beneath it, making temporary white ceilings. In spring, those ceilings sag and empty slowly into the floor.

Here, spring means a few hours of thaw on the brighter days.

During those hours, the place becomes quietly busy. Leaves uncurl by fractions. Droplets gather weight. The moss beside the central pool changes from almost black to a deep, luminous green. Under its surface, pale new shoots push sideways toward whatever opening the previous winter has left. Buds on the sheltered walls swell while the exposed branches remain glazed with frost.

Movement travels through the structure unevenly. A gust touches the upper crowns, and only after a pause does a lower arch shift, shedding a dust of ice into the lane beneath it. A laden branch settles against another with a soft wooden complaint. Somewhere out of sight, a brittle leaf breaks free and falls through several layers, making a series of small, separate sounds.

Most of the time, this is all that can be heard.

Animals are scarce. There are narrow tunnels in the moss, perhaps made by a vole, though many have frozen shut. Beside one of the pools lies a seed husk with its edges neatly opened. A small gray bird sometimes enters from the bare slope and works through the outer branches, inspecting bark without calling. Its presence makes the place briefly seem larger. It disappears behind a wall of leaves and reappears overhead, having found a route no upright visitor could follow.

In the deeper chambers, the floor is soft with old foliage. Decay proceeds slowly in the cold. Leaves retain their outlines long after their color has gone; a footstep exposes layers of brown, gray, and nearly white, all threaded with living rootlets. There is a faint smell of wet bark, and, where a stem has recently split, something sharp and resinous.

These enclosed spaces hold a slightly gentler air. They are not warm, but the wind cannot reach them. Hoarfrost grows around their entrances rather than within. Along the sheltered bark, minute green blades stand upright in rows, each with an unfrozen bead at its base.

Near the upper boundary, a passage rises until its roof becomes too low to follow. Through it, the pale mountain is visible: a strip of scree, a snowbank, a fractured outcrop still clean enough to show the color of its interior. The living mass ends abruptly there. Leaves press against stone. Root tips enter hairline cracks. A few young stems lean into open air, their buds small and tightly sealed.

That boundary changes a little each year. On one side, frost loosens a flake of rock. On the other, a root thickens in the space it leaves. A roof closes over a formerly open court; elsewhere, a snow-loaded crown collapses and daylight reaches a floor that has been dim for decades. New shoots gather in the light before the broken wood has dried.

At dusk, the higher terraces fade first into a single dark shape. Water continues moving beneath them. Along the exposed rim, the hanging roots acquire another thin layer of ice, while in the central court the last drops fall through the opening in the canopy. Each strikes the pool distinctly. Between them there is enough silence to hear the water rocking against its moss-grown banks.
