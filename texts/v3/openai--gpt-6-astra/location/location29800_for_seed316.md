There is no dry way into the basin. The firmer ground merely holds its water more quietly.

At the margin, your feet sink through a skin of pale grit into warm black silt. Farther out, the grit disappears. Water lies ankle-deep over a pavement of rounded stone, then knee-deep, then shallow again for distances too great to judge. Nothing rises high enough to give a useful view. What look like hills are often rainbanks resting on the ground. What look like distant shores are the backs of currents.

The basin occupies most of this side of the world.

Its oldest rocks have lost even the shapes by which rocks are usually known. No cliffs, no broken faces, no sharp exposures of strata. They lie in low, broad swells, polished not smooth but intimate: every hollow fitted to a pressure, every groove accommodating a flow that has occupied it for longer than the present sea has existed. A ridge a handspan high can divide waters that will not meet again for thousands of miles. A depression no deeper than a footprint may hold a different season.

The water is the color of wet tin where it reflects the sky. Beneath that it is brown, yellow, almost red, packed with flour-fine mineral matter. Lift a handful and it seems heavier than it should. Leave it in a bowl and it does not simply settle. Pale threads descend; dark threads climb. Little horizontal boundaries form between them, and storms begin along the boundaries.

The air is warm enough to soften anything that can soften. Salt grows damp. Fibers loosen. Skin never quite dries. There is a smell of clay opened after burial, with sweeter pockets where the sparse vegetation is rotting underwater. Rain comes frequently, but the sky seldom appears to be the source. You walk into curtains of falling water beneath an unbroken low ceiling of cloud; elsewhere rain rises a little way from the surface and hangs there, trembling, before losing its arrangement.

Light enters reluctantly. It spreads through the vapor without revealing where the sun is. At midday the whole basin has the subdued brightness of a room beside another room in which a lamp has been lit.

And the noise is immense.

At first you hear it as surf, although there is no coast nearby. Then individual sounds emerge: the grinding of submerged stone, the crack of mud layers splitting, a sustained rushing like water pouring down a stair too broad to cross. Beneath these is a bass vibration that reaches you through your legs. It disturbs the fluid in your ears. Your teeth touch differently while it passes.

There are no intervals of silence. When one sound stops, another is exposed beneath it.

Much of the noise comes from very small movements occurring everywhere at once. Grains slide against grains across a continent. Bubbles escape from a buried layer in fields hundreds of miles wide. Thin crusts lift, flex, and break under the pressure of water arriving from below. A single patch might make no more sound than a hand closing on wet sand. Here the number of patches is beyond anything a listener can separate.

For all this activity, the land is almost perfectly worn out. The violence does not build mountains or cut new valleys. There is too little relief left, too much weight evenly distributed. Instead, the surface rearranges itself sideways.

A sheet of water accelerates across another sheet moving in the opposite direction. Silt wells up in thick ropes, spreads into a fan, and is dragged away before the fan has finished forming. Low islands appear over the course of an hour, crowded with glistening pellets of clay, then unzip along their middles. The two halves drift apart while water flows into the fresh seam. A distant bank can approach without growing larger, arrive as a ripple against your shins, and continue behind you.

Navigation by landmarks is therefore a local custom, useful only for very short journeys.

There are plants, but not enough to soften the place. Most grow as dark, stiff bristles along seams where fresh water leaks through the mineral bed. Their roots are shallow and spread almost flat. They survive by yielding their entire hold at once: when the sediment moves, a whole patch comes loose and travels, still upright, until the roots catch again. You may see a thin line of reeds going steadily against the current, dragging its small allotment of soil.

Other plants resemble cracked leather laid over stone. They open pores during the brief cooler hours and close them when the warm rain returns. Around these mats the water clears for a finger’s breadth. Tiny pale bodies flicker there, feeding in that narrow margin. Almost all the visible life is concentrated in such places. A mile of seemingly empty water, then a crease in a rock holding more mouths than you could count.

Larger animals are uncommon.

Occasionally a long, low-backed creature crosses a shallows, with only the blunt plates of its spine showing. It moves so slowly that its wake overtakes it. When it stops, silt begins to settle on its back, and after a few minutes you cannot be sure where it was. The broad tracks remain longer than the animal does. Some tracks fill with clear water; some fill with water colder than anything nearby.

There are birds, or creatures that use the air as birds do, but they seldom fly high. Their narrow wings take advantage of the violent drafts running just above the surface. They vanish into gray distance without ever appearing to diminish. Once in a great while, one passes close enough for you to see a hanging throat and a small, sealed-looking eye. Its cry is swallowed before you hear it.

The basin is not empty between these encounters. There is scarcely room in it.

Every depth is occupied by a different movement. Every current carries sediment, heat, dissolved stone, fragments of older surfaces. Press a pole into the bottom and it may stop at once, or continue through layer after resistant layer, as though the ground consists of many floors pressed close together. Pull it out and water issues from the hole in successive colors.

Sometimes the layers fail to remain below one another.

A patch of shallows turns opaque and then becomes a stretch of dry mud under hard white light. You can see the cracks, the little beads of salt, the shadow of something outside the patch. The surrounding water piles against its edge without entering. After a few breaths the dry ground darkens, folds once, and is gone. Warm water closes over it with a noise like a heavy cloth being shaken.

These appearances have no reliable season. Some are no larger than a palm. Others occupy the horizon. During the largest, two weather systems can bear down on the same place from incompatible skies, and the water between them stands up in thick, wavering partitions.

The few structures stand on the broadest stone swells.

They are low enclosures made from fitted blocks, their edges so rounded that the joints are easier to feel than see. Some have doorways. Others have openings too narrow for a body, or chambers into which the water enters but cannot be heard. There is no metal, no glass, nothing that turns or lifts. Inside, the stone smells faintly dry.

Around one enclosure, a row of shallow cups has been worn into the pavement. Rain keeps them full. Each cup trembles at a different rate. In the surrounding roar, they appear to be listening.

No settlement remains beside them. No road can be followed from one to another. Yet the thresholds are polished, and now and then, after a surge has stripped away the silt, there are depressions approaching a doorway in pairs.

Toward evening, if it is evening, the vapor lowers until the visible world is reduced to a few yards of moving water. The warmth persists. You feel distant changes through the bottom: a tightening, a long shudder, then a pressure against one ankle as a new current arrives.

Something vast is being redistributed beyond sight.

The reeds lean. Water climbs the rounded stones. Out in the obscurity, an enormous area begins to rain, and the added sound is so great that for a moment it seems the basin has become quiet beneath it.

By morning the surface may have exchanged every grain you stood on. The low stones will still be there, fitted closely into their hollows. Their upper faces will glisten in the dimness, barely exposed, while the whole weight of the wet world passes over them again.
