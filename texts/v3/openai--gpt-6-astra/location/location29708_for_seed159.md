The plain is underground, but you can see across it.

Daylight comes through the salt overhead. In some places the ceiling is thick enough to turn the light honey-colored; elsewhere it is a thin white vault, crossed by the shadows of things moving on the surface. There are brighter seams where the roof has split. Beneath these, shafts of direct sun fall through dusty air and strike the ground with such ordinary clarity that you expect a sky when you look up.

The chamber extends for several miles. Its far side disappears behind a succession of low ridges, hanging mineral curtains, and a pale haze raised by animals. The roof rests on broad, irregular piers, some fused into walls, others standing alone with room for whole migrations to pass around them. Sound travels strangely between these supports. A little clicking nearby may vanish, while the collapse of a bank a mile away arrives with the intimacy of someone overturning a table.

It is warm. The warmth comes up through the soles of your feet.

Much of the floor is new.

Along the central trough, soft white material wells out of cracks and swells into blisters. The blisters harden on the outside, lift, split, and expose wet folds beneath. Some become low hummocks; some rise into ridges high enough to conceal a person. Their surfaces have the unhandled look of fresh plaster. No rain has pitted them, no long procession of feet has rounded their edges. Where they break, they break cleanly.

There are older islands of dark stone, but the pale ground presses around them and occasionally carries one a short distance sideways. You can see this happening at the edge of a pool: a black boulder shifts, the water trembles, and a narrow crescent of unclouded brine appears where its base used to be.

Water occupies the seams rather than the whole landscape. It beads on the ceiling, gathers beneath overhangs, and falls in brief, localized showers. A stretch of ground may remain dry for weeks while a few yards away a mineral curtain rains continuously into a shallow bowl. Warm streams emerge without warning from under rising plates. They run fast for an hour, whiten their banks, and stop. Their channels are left full of stranded animals and little hardening bubbles.

There is no grass, no moss, no root dangling from the roof. Even in the wettest places, nothing makes a leaf.

The animals supply nearly all the movement.

At first the distant slopes seem to have a restless grain, as if the whole chamber were being seen through hot air. Then individual bodies resolve: thousands of low, six-legged creatures crossing the white ground, each about the length of a forearm, with a blunt head and overlapping gray plates. They travel in crowded lanes, their feet making a dry sound like seeds shaken in a tray. Their mouths are underneath. As they walk, they rasp up the slick, cream-colored films that develop wherever fresh mineral meets seep water.

Those films are the pasture here. They grow fastest beside warm vents, smell faintly of struck matches, and look almost exactly like the stone they cover. A feeding lane is recognizable by its dullness. Ground that has not yet been grazed has a greasy shine.

Between the plated grazers run smaller animals, soft-bodied and quick, entering the gaps opened by each footfall. Some eat scraps. Some climb aboard and feed from the joints. Some remain motionless until a grazer steps over them, then fasten themselves underneath. A single overturned shell draws so many of these little bodies that within minutes it seems to be turning itself back over.

Larger animals follow the herds. They have narrow shoulders, long forelimbs, and broad, sensitive lips that they press against the ground. They listen this way. When they find a hollow beneath a fresh crust, they punch through with one forefoot and pull out whatever is sheltering there. The breaking makes a sharp report. Across the plain, these reports answer one another all day.

Above them, the air is occupied.

Small pale fliers roost in the ceiling seams by the tens of thousands. Their wings are nearly transparent, their bodies dark and heavy. They launch in sheets when a fissure widens or a shower begins, filling the light with a flicker so dense that the ground appears to pulse. They settle on warm backs, on mineral ledges, on the lips of pools. Sometimes a whole flock pours into a crack no wider than a doorway and does not emerge for hours.

Nothing here gets much uninterrupted rest. A ridge rises across an established route, and the herd divides around it. A ceiling shower wakes a dormant patch of feeding film, and three streams of animals turn toward the sound. A pool drains through its own bottom. Everything along its edge rushes inward after the exposed food, then outward again when the floor begins to sag.

The upheavals are usually small, but there are so many of them that the landscape never quite holds still. Watch any stretch of it long enough and something gives way, opens, fills, or is carried off.

In the wetter hollows live translucent, paddle-tailed animals with red organs visible through their sides. They crowd the water until it looks pink. When a pool shrinks, they climb out and travel overland, towing moisture in folded sacs beneath their throats. Their crossings leave broad, glistening tracks. Predators gather along these tracks, but so do smaller scavengers that drink from them. By the time the last stragglers pass, the first part of the trail is already dry.

There are a few human structures on the black-stone islands.

One is a shelter of ribs, hide, and flat mineral slabs, built low under a projecting ledge. Another is little more than a windbreak facing a warm fissure. Short ladders, cut from the jointed leg bones of something enormous, lean against raised storage niches. The vessels are fired clay or hollow shell. The tools are stone flakes mounted in bone, with handles polished dark by use.

The people who stay here do not build on the white ground. They do not leave a vessel where the ceiling is sweating. They keep their sleeping places above the height reached by the plated herds, which will climb over a motionless body with the same indifference they show a stone.

Near one shelter, a row of stakes marks a crossing. Each stake has a shell loosely hung from it. When the ground begins to move, the shells touch and chatter. The warning is modest, easily lost among the animals, so someone is usually watching them.

Routes are remembered by the old pillars, not by the ridges. A useful instruction might be to keep the forked black column on your left until you can see the sun through its lower opening. Yesterday’s easy slope may be a broken wall today. A stream may have changed direction twice since morning.

Yet there are quiet places. Behind the great supports, where the air barely circulates, the floor is cool enough to sit on. Dust collects there: shell powder, shed skins, fine white grains shaken loose from the roof. From these recesses you can watch the bright traffic without standing in it. The nearest animals pass with their heads down. Farther away, the herds gather into dark ribbons and vanish behind fresh ridges.

Then a deep knock comes from below.

For a moment, the feet stop. The fliers cling flat to the ceiling. Water in the hanging drops shivers.

Out in the central trough, a line opens across the plain. Warm vapor escapes, followed by a slow bulge of wet white stone. The two halves of a grazing herd draw apart. Small scavengers run along the edges, already searching the exposed underside of the crust. Before the new ridge has finished rising, pale animals are climbing it, and their feet leave the first marks on its surface.
