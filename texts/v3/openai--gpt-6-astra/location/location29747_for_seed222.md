The plateau is warm enough that the stone holds heat through the short night. By midmorning, a hand laid on a pale boulder feels the warmth coming out of it as well as the sunlight going in. The air is thin and extraordinarily clear. Across the basin, six or seven miles away, individual ledges show on the opposite escarpment, though the small animals moving along them are only flecks.

Nothing here rises to a sharp summit. The high country has had time to lose its points. Ridges end in rounded shoulders; gullies widen into shallow, comfortable-looking hollows. Even the cliffs are heavily worked, their faces divided into shelves and scoops, with heaps of old stone at their feet. The rock is cream-colored until it breaks. Inside, it is the pink of an unripe fruit.

There is a great deal of bare ground, but little of it is simple.

Up close, the basin floor is a mosaic of hard mineral crust, loose gravel, and plates of stone fitted together with dark seams. Some seams are dry. Others carry a line of moisture too narrow to reflect the sky. Kneeling beside one, you can see water advancing through it, hesitating at a grain of sand, dividing around it, joining again. These threads travel surprisingly far. They pass beneath boulders, reappear on the uphill side, and sometimes cross a sloping slab without descending.

Their routes are older than the current surface of the plateau. Where a slab has weathered away, a thread will briefly pass through open air.

Grass grows near the ordinary seepages: stiff, widely separated bunches, green only at the base. There are low shrubs with leaves turned edgewise to the sun, and cushion plants that resemble dropped pieces of gray upholstery. Most stand alone. Between one and the next lies enough empty ground to make each plant seem deliberately placed.

The damp seams support something less conspicuous and more productive. Amber films coat their undersides. At dawn the films swell into soft ridges, and by noon they have contracted to a varnish. Their grazers arrive before the contraction: translucent mites, six-legged animals no longer than a finger, beetles with white backs and dark bellies. In places the moving bodies entirely conceal the water. When a shadow crosses them, they separate into the nearest cracks, leaving the seam bare in a quick, soundless wave.

There are animals almost everywhere once the eye learns their sizes.

What seemed to be a row of pebbles beneath a shrub lifts into a row of heads. A loose scrap of pale bark on a boulder unfolds four legs and edges into shade. Flat-bodied grazers, each about the size of a hare, rest with their broad undersides pressed against the rock. They feed during the brief hours when the mineral films are soft. The rest of the day they conserve that meal, moving only when the shadow they occupy has become too small.

The larger animals travel. Slender, tawny herds follow long traverses across the basin, far enough apart that their paths are visible between them. Their feet have soft, spreading pads, and even a herd passing nearby makes little more noise than clothing brushing clothing. They browse the cushion plants without taking the growing centers. At certain slabs they stop to lick the damp underside, reaching with remarkably long, dark tongues.

Predators wait along the same routes. They are low and patient and almost the exact color of old stone. Occasionally the whole arrangement changes in seconds: a herd fans outward, one of the apparently empty hollows produces a running shape, dust rises, and then the basin is spacious again. The dust remains longer than the disturbance.

Overhead, broad-winged creatures work the thermals. Some are small enough to vanish against the brightness; others cast shadows large enough to cool a person from head to foot. They seldom flap. The warm plateau gives them height, and they spend it slowly, descending across the basin in long, tilted circuits. Now and then one opens a fan of fine filaments below its beak to strain airborne insects.

For all this movement, the place is quiet. There are no continuous insect calls. The shrubs are too low and too stiff to rustle much. Wind can pass overhead while the hollows remain still. Sounds arrive separately: a claw slipping on stone, a dry seedcase opening, three faint clicks from an animal under a ledge. At noon, you may hear your own swallowing.

The structures are easiest to overlook at a distance. There are perhaps a dozen in the basin, generally near the heads of gullies: small, pale buildings with rounded corners, each on a terrace cut so cleanly into the slope that it could be natural. No roads connect them. A few have low walls enclosing nothing but gravel. One has fallen partly open, and a shrub grows where its threshold used to be.

Inside the intact buildings, the light comes from the wrong direction.

Through an entrance facing the white afternoon sun, you can see blue twilight falling across an interior wall. The rooms are modest from outside. Within, their dimensions depend on where you stand. A doorway may be three paces away as you enter and remain three paces away through a long walk toward it. The floor is warm beside the entrance, cool farther in, and wet along a shallow channel that seems to run beneath the wall.

These are waterworks, still operating.

They admit air from places that are not adjacent to the plateau. One contains the pressure and cold of a deep ocean trench, held behind a surface as unremarkable as a sheet of window glass. Another opens, during certain hours, onto cloud. The difference between those places and this warm, thin air turns machinery too small to see. Moisture gathers on the interior surfaces and leaves through the old network of seams.

There are no turbines, no pipes crossing the land, no visible source of power. The buildings make a little clicking sound when their interiors change destination.

They do not supply much water. A building may spend an entire afternoon forming a single bead on the lip of its outlet. Then, near dusk, all the seams around it darken at once. Water enters a hundred small cracks; the amber films soften; animals emerge from beneath stones.

Whether this restraint was designed for the basin, or the basin has slowly arranged itself around the restraint, is no longer easy to determine.

Rain comes only occasionally. It is often possible to stand in sunlight and watch a shower occupy one end of the plateau, its falling water bright against the cliffs. The rain smells sharply of warm dust. It deepens the color of the stone without greatly changing the ground; most of the old channels are too broad for such brief flows, and a little water wanders along their bottoms among stranded gravel.

Afterward, the scattered buildings acquire dark hems. Small animals gather there, not to enter, but to drink from the outside corners.

Toward evening the heat begins to loosen its hold on the basin. Shadows reach across the open ground and finally join. The high fliers descend to cliff shelves crowded with silent bodies. Below them, the herds move toward the last illuminated slopes.

For a short while, the narrow water threads shine. You can trace them across the basin floor, past solitary shrubs and over the worn backs of stones, until they disappear into distance. Then the angle changes. What had looked like a connected system becomes bare ground again, with a few animals crossing it and the first cool air collecting in the hollows.
