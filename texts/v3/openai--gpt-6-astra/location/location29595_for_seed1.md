The ground is only a little higher than the sea, and in many places the difference has become a matter of timing. Water arrives before the tide does. It gathers in the shallow hollows, darkens the stone, lifts the thin plates of ice from underneath; an hour later, far out across the flats, the sea begins to rise.

There are no cliffs. Whatever once stood here has worn down into broad stone backs, low enough for a large animal to lie across two or three of them. Channels wind between them, branching and rejoining across a plain that takes most of a day to cross. Their bottoms are polished pale. Their edges have been rounded so thoroughly that water sometimes runs uphill along them, unable to find a definite place to fall.

Everything is wet. The black stone sweats through its crust of salt. Water beads on the undersides of ice shelves and hangs there in long, crowded rows. Mist deposits a brittle glaze on anything held still. Even the wind seems to carry weight: it presses cold droplets into the face, fills the mouth with a taste of iron, and leaves the teeth aching.

Above the flats, a dim gray sky is divided into strips by lower, darker clouds. There is usually enough light to see a long way, but not enough to distinguish movement from distance. What looks like a dark island may be a herd. What looks like a break in the weather may be something pale turning beneath the water.

The noise is continuous.

At first it resembles machinery heard through the walls of an enormous works: deep reciprocating knocks, a metallic rattle, the high note of something rotating under strain. The sound travels through the ground as readily as through the air. Set a hand on one of the stone backs and the palm receives several rhythms at once. Some repeat every few seconds. Others take so long to complete that they register as changes in pressure, a slow tightening and release in the wrist.

Nothing has been built here.

The largest animals account for much of the noise. They lie packed in the channels, shoulder against shoulder, with only their armored backs above water. Each is the length of a small boat, low and blunt at both ends, its skin divided into overlapping plates of dull iron. Openings along the flanks draw brine inward. Beneath the plates, muscular chambers compress it through membranes laid down in hundreds of folded layers.

The process releases heat, electricity, and a fine black sediment.

Their bodies shudder with each compression. Valves clap shut. Thin jets of warm water strike the underside of the ice. Across the plain, thousands of these animals work through the same cycle, though never quite together. A rhythm gathers, becomes almost regular, then breaks apart as a sleeper wakes, a channel drains, or a smaller creature obstructs an intake.

The warm outflow does not make the country warm. It buys each animal a few inches of liquid water. Beyond that, the cold takes everything back.

Around every outlet there is a dense fringe of lesser life. White jointed creatures hang by their forelimbs in the flow, combing black particles into their mouths. Flat transparent swimmers press against the larger animals’ bellies. Slender things with red internal threads enter the breathing openings and emerge farther down the body, swollen and slow. The animals tolerate most of them. Occasionally a plate lifts and snaps down, and a cloud of pale fragments joins the outflow.

There is no grass, no weed trailing in the channels, no lichen on the exposed stone. Color belongs to mineral deposits and living flesh: rust-orange seams, blue salt, the dull crimson visible inside a translucent throat. The black sediment feeds colonies too small to distinguish individually. They form greasy films where warm water meets cold, and the films feed almost everything else.

On a calm day, one can hear those films being grazed.

It is a close, busy scraping, like many knives being sharpened in the next room.

The animals on the exposed flats are smaller and much more mobile. They have six or eight feet, broad at the ends, and soft bodies tucked beneath shells polished by contact with their neighbors. They move in dense processions along the slight ridges between channels. There is so little unoccupied ground that the processions pass over resting animals, over eggs, over heaps of cast shells. Where two streams meet, the creatures climb until a temporary hill forms, then spill down its other side.

Their tracks fill instantly with water.

Some carry beads of light beneath their shells. These brighten when a discharge passes through the channels. For several seconds the whole plain can reveal its living traffic: lines of cold green moving around dark pools, green clustered beneath the lips of stone, green descending into cracks that appeared too narrow to admit anything.

Then the light goes out, leaving the sky to seem darker than before.

The electrical storms usually begin belowground. A low vibration rises through the flats. Animals in the channels stop pumping one after another, and the sudden loss of their noise makes the wind startlingly clear. Small creatures flatten themselves. The transparent swimmers turn edge-on.

Blue light then crosses the water in a branching sheet.

It runs around islands, enters mouths, leaps between exposed backs. For a moment, every wet surface has a bright border. The discharge can travel several miles without finding a dry place to end. When it is over, the channel animals resume their work, first a few scattered knocks, then the full overlapping thunder. Dead grazers drift into the intakes. Farther inland, egg masses loosen from the stones and begin to hatch.

The eggs need the shock. Some have waited through winters longer than their parents lived.

Age is difficult to judge here. The stone has the smoothness of something handled for an immeasurable time, yet the animals expose fresh surfaces constantly. They grind it in their feeding chambers, scrape it with their feet, dissolve it beneath their sleeping bodies. The debris washes into the lowest basins. There it settles, compacts, and occasionally rises again as stone already worn smooth.

A basin can fill overnight with a slab that contains the impressions of the animals resting on it.

Most impressions are accurate. A few show a limb folded the wrong way, or a shell broken along a line that has not yet cracked. The creatures seem sensitive to these discrepancies. A grazer finding its own outline will press down into it and remain still, sometimes until the next tide. Others hurry away, leaving the impression occupied by water.

The larger predators favor these basins.

They are difficult to see because their upper surfaces take on the color and shallow contours of the ground. A pool crossing one of them looks like any other pool. Small animals walk across. The predator waits until enough weight has gathered, then folds upward along three or four long seams.

The sound is heavy and intimate, like a wet coat being wrung out.

After feeding, it flattens again. The water redistributes. A few shells roll to the edge.

There are also hunters that travel beneath the solid rock. Their passage raises a smooth bulge no higher than a knee, moving at walking speed across the flats. The surface remains unbroken. Channels lift with it; water pours off both sides. Sometimes the bulge passes directly under a packed procession and several animals simply cease to be aboveground. Their companions close the gaps without changing pace.

This is one reason the broadest stretches of bare stone remain crowded. Solitude has a weight these hunters can detect.

Farther toward the sea, the flats become a confusion of low islands and tidal lanes. The horizon is occupied by pale, long-legged animals standing belly-deep in black water. Hundreds remain motionless together, their heads lowered beneath the surface. They feed on something brought in by the returning tide, something that produces no visible sediment and leaves a brief ringing in their bones.

When they lift their heads, the ringing carries across the country.

It is a clear sound above the grinding and hammering, almost painfully high. It continues after their mouths close. Sometimes it continues after the animals have moved away, marking the empty places where they stood.

In the dimmest weather, those ringing places are easier to navigate by than the islands.

There is no true silence even in the coldest part of the year. The sea thickens. Ice seals the shallower channels. Animals crowd into the remaining water until their armored backs form an almost continuous surface, wet and shifting, for miles. Frost grows between their plates. Their exhaust freezes into low white plumes that break off under the wind.

Beneath them the pumping continues, slower now, under greater pressure.

The light contracts to a few hours of gray. Out on the flats, a stone rises imperceptibly from its basin, carrying the shape of next season’s wounds. Small luminous bodies gather in its hollows. Water appears along its edge, though the tide is still falling.

Then, from somewhere under the crowded plain, comes a knock so deep that all the other sounds seem briefly to hang from it.

The animals pause.

A second knock answers, several miles away.
