The lane ends at a drainage canal, though you do not see the water at first. You see a railing leaning into fern fronds, a strip of duckweed, and the undersides of enormous leaves. Beyond them, a pump house occupies most of the far bank. Its windows are open. Its roof is almost indistinguishable from the trees behind it.

The whole district could be walked around in ten minutes, if there were a path all the way around. There is not. Behind the pump house, gardens, workshops, and narrow brick dwellings have filled the space between two bends of the canal. Passage is by covered steps, small bridges, and alleys that sometimes become drainage channels. At several doorways, a plank lies ready to be put down after rain.

Nothing stands much higher than the water. The lowest workshop floors are raised only a hand’s breadth above the canal’s usual level, and old flood marks darken the plaster behind shelves of tools. The land is flat enough that water needs instruction. Brick gutters lead it around corners; shallow concrete troughs carry it beneath houses; iron gates hold one reach of the canal slightly higher than another. The pump house supplies the final persuasion.

Its motor runs through the afternoon.

The sound is too large for the building: a deep, continuous laboring note, with a loose metal tremor above it. It passes through the footbridge into the soles of your shoes. Close to the open windows there is also the hiss of a belt and an occasional knocking that never quite becomes regular. Farther away, behind several walls and curtains of leaves, the higher sounds disappear, leaving a vibration that makes the place seem occupied even where no one is visible.

Vegetation has taken nearly every surface that is not used every day. It has not swallowed the district in one grand movement. It has accumulated in opportunities. A little earth lodged behind a downpipe became a fern bed. Fern roots retained more litter; now there is a small tree there, its trunk pressed against the wall and its roots descending on either side of the pipe. Roof gutters carry grasses so thick that the rain spills over their entire length. Vines lie along electrical conduits, kept back from the junction boxes but otherwise left alone.

Some of this growth is cultivated. Bitter gourds hang above a machinist’s yard. Tubs of taro stand in the shallow water behind a kitchen. Bananas screen an outdoor washstand, their older leaves split into long strips that brush the basin in a breeze. Other plants have simply become too established to remove without dismantling what supports them. One boundary wall survives as a row of brick islands held together by roots.

Underfoot, the paving is a narrow compromise. The centers of the stones are bare and dark from use; their edges disappear into moss. Where carts turn, the moss has been rubbed away in arcs. Where a door has remained shut for a season, fine plants have crossed the threshold.

The air is warm even in deep shade. After rain, the walls give off the smell of wet brick and lime, mixed with bruised leaves, canal mud, and the faint oily odor escaping the pump house. Clothes hung beneath the eaves dry slowly. A hand laid on a railing comes away damp. Water gathers on the outside of a cool supply pipe and drops into a shallow depression worn in the concrete below it.

Most of the district is softly lit, but not dark. Daylight enters through breaks in the canopy and through gaps between roofs, illuminating pale plaster, wet leaf surfaces, and the green canal. A corrugated awning throws a sharp-edged patch of shade across a doorway. Just beyond it, a sunlit banana leaf shows every parallel vein. When clouds pass, these distinctions flatten, and the buildings seem to move a little farther back into the plants.

There are workshops here, though none is large. Through one open door, a lathe stands beneath a fluorescent tube, its bed wiped clean while rust blooms on an unused cabinet beside it. Coils of hose hang from rafters. A stack of pump housings rests on timber blocks above the damp floor. Across the lane, a small welding shop has cleared its working space down to bare concrete. Ferns begin immediately beyond the cleared rectangle.

Maintenance leaves visible borders everywhere: a fresh band of pruning around the transformer, a scraped channel through the duckweed at the intake, white paint on the edge of a step. The district stays usable by these repeated, modest acts. A month without sweeping would change the alleys. A year without clearing the drains would change which rooms could still be occupied.

Yet the overall shape is old and settled. The canal bends have been armored with brick for so long that their corners are rounded. Steps sag toward their centers. Water follows the same stains down the same walls. The pump’s steady work does not produce much visible movement: a slight draw beneath the intake grille, a slow turning of floating leaves, a smooth patch downstream where the discharge enters below the surface.

Animals are scarce. A small lizard waits beside a warm motor casing. Occasionally a bird lands on a television aerial, looks down into the crowded greenery, and leaves. Even the canal offers little sign of life beyond a single disturbance under the duckweed, gone before its cause can be seen.

Toward evening, someone switches on a lamp above the footbridge. It lights the railing, three broad leaves, and a square of water between them. From a kitchen comes the clatter of a lid, briefly distinct above the machinery.

The pump continues. Along the back of the workshop, droplets enlarge on the fern tips. One falls; much later, another. Beneath the roofs and roots, through culverts whose entrances are almost hidden, the water keeps moving at the pace the small difference in levels allows.
