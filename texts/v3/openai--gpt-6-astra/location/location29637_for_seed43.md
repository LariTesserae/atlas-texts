The first thing you hear is the knocking of clay against clay. Not one knock, but several kinds: the hollow clop of water cups turning on a wheel, the sharper tapping of someone testing roof tiles, the irregular chatter of shutters lifted by warm air. Under these is a sound like grain being poured, which turns out to be birds moving in the walls.

The place lies below the surrounding streets, at the bottom of a broad, shallow well. It is perhaps forty paces across. You could call it a courtyard, except that almost all its open space has been occupied by something: a stair, a drying rack, a pigeon tower, a stack of perforated jars, a bridge between two doorways so close together that the bridge is only three planks long. Houses lean inward over workshops. Workshops extend beneath stairs. Along the remaining passages, a person carrying a basket has to turn sideways whenever someone comes the other way.

Everything underfoot, overhead, and within reach has been made.

The bottom is paved with thick ceramic slabs. The walls are brick faced with lime, patched with broken bowls and the curved shoulders of storage vessels. Even the massive pillars are not outcrops of buried stone but bundles of old kiln brick, their corners rounded by generations of hands and loads. Where a floor has worn through, another floor shows beneath it. Where that has broken, there are packed potsherds, then an older arch. No one here has seen bare earth.

The district above has risen around this court through repeated rebuilding. Its former ground-floor doors now open into upper galleries. A threshold with a groove worn by carts sits level with a third-storey window; below it, newer stairs descend past two generations of blocked entrances. The oldest stair has steps so deeply cupped that repairs have been laid inside them like teeth.

Yet it is not dark. Several roofs have been left open, and the shafts below them are flared and whitewashed. Light strikes one wall, crosses to another, and arrives in the lower rooms broad and pale. At midday, you can see the dust on a moth’s wings. A strip of direct sun travels slowly over the opposite gallery, brightening shutters, laundry, a sleeping dog. People move their trays of sliced fruit ahead of it. The pigeons move with it too.

There is warmth stored everywhere. It comes from the bodies packed into these rooms, from cooking fires, from a small communal kiln that is seldom entirely cold. At shoulder height the air is pleasantly warm; beside certain walls it presses against your face like breath. The potter has hung damp cloth over his doorway. The cloth dries from the edges inward, leaving a dark island that shrinks throughout the afternoon.

Water is present mostly in its traces.

There are black crescents beneath jar rims, pale mineral beards under spouts, a damp seam where two courses of brick meet. One passage smells faintly of wet rope. Another has been dry for weeks except for the exact place where a drinking cup is always set down.

At the lowest point, reached by five slippery steps, a covered basin holds the court’s reserve. It is not large enough to swim in. A child could wade across it, though no child is allowed to. Above it turns the source of the hollow clopping: a wooden wheel carrying little fired-clay cups on a cord. Someone pedals it for a few minutes at a time, raising water to a header jar. From there, narrow channels distribute a ration to kitchens, wash ledges, and the trough outside the kiln.

Every cup empties badly in its own particular way. One splashes. One gurgles. One, cracked and bound with twine, pours two thin streams. People upstairs can tell whether the wheel is being turned too quickly without looking.

The overflow feeds a gutter no wider than a hand. Along this gutter is almost all the greenery in the court: small ferns rooted in mortar pockets, a few wiry grasses, mint in a split jar. Higher up, a vine grows from a basket filled with sweepings and pigeon litter. Its branches have been trained along a cord, but there are long bare stretches between the leaves. A household keeps three of those leaves shaded with a scrap of cloth because a clutch of small, edible snails has settled beneath them.

Elsewhere, plants survive as isolated possessions. A thorny shrub stands in a pot beside a doorway, trimmed flat on the passage side by everyone brushing past. A fig grows in an old wash vat on an upper landing. Its roots have found a drain and split the lip of it. The family has repaired the drain around the roots twice.

There are far more animals than plants.

Pigeons occupy the purpose-built cavities high in the walls, where their soft grey bodies almost close the holes. Sparrows steal fibres from basket repairs. Small brown lizards wait beside the flour shelves, looking decorative until a beetle moves. Wasps shave exposed wood from the railings. At dusk, the bats emerge from behind the warm kiln flue and skim the light shafts, turning within spaces that seem too narrow for wings.

The rats keep to the outer passages until the grain sellers begin sweeping. Then they appear one by one at the edges of things. The court’s two cats do not pursue them immediately. They watch from different levels, tails hanging through the slats of drying racks. Sometimes a rat crosses safely beneath both. Sometimes the entire lower court hears a sudden violent scramble, a falling bowl, and a child shouting directions that the cats ignore.

Nothing here happens in isolation. Grain dust feeds beetles; beetles feed lizards; lizards lose their young to birds. Pigeon litter goes into the planting baskets. Broken baskets kindle the kiln. Ash scours the jars, and the rinsing water darkens the gutter where the ferns grow. Small losses keep other things alive, although the people losing grain or fruit rarely regard this generously.

By afternoon the noise thickens. The kiln mouth is opened, and workers pass warm vessels along a line. A woman pounds a wooden wedge into a swollen doorframe. The wheel starts again. Above it all, pigeons launch from one wall and settle on another, their wings making a sudden, papery roar that travels around the court before escaping upward.

The walls have been shaped by this life as thoroughly as by weather. Basket corners have polished the narrow turns. Smoke has found the same routes for so long that black fans spread above particular lintels. At the water steps, the rail is thin where hands close around it. Repairs follow these established paths: a curved patch where loads swing, a raised curb where wash water strays, a small tile roof over a ledge where birds will insist on nesting.

When rain comes, the upper streets send only a little of it down. Someone opens the catchment shutters. For a short while, water ticks through the channels overhead, rinsing dust from the white shafts and dripping from places that had seemed solid. Everyone brings out jars. The pigeons retreat deep into their holes. The court smells suddenly of warm brick and soaked fibre.

Afterward, the lower passages remain damp while the galleries dry. Beads gather beneath a cool cistern pipe and fall, widely spaced, into a broken saucer placed there years ago. Tiny flies assemble around it. A lizard comes down the wall headfirst.

By evening the strip of sun has climbed beyond the highest shutter. There is still enough light to work, reflected from the white rim above, but the rooms behind the galleries begin to glow with lamps. Someone covers the basin. Someone else gives the water wheel a final turn, too slowly to lift more than a cupful.

The knocking stops. In its absence you hear feet overhead, a broom against brick, the birds settling inside the walls. From the warm flue, the first bat drops into the court, opens its wings, and rises through the space left between all the things people have built.
