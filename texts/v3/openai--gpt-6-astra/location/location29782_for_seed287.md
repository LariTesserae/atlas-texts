The hollow lies just beneath the ground. Its ceiling is the underside of a road, or of something broad and level that once served as a road: slabs laid across a natural depression, their joints packed with roots. Through three of those joints, daylight enters in thin, dusty sheets. The light reaches almost to the floor at noon. Elsewhere the chamber is lit by a few red inspection lamps and the dull orange interiors of machines set into the walls.

You can cross the hollow in a few minutes, provided you know which surfaces will bear your weight. It takes longer to understand its shape. There are ledges behind the vegetation, recesses behind the ledges, a narrow gallery that returns you to the same place a little higher up. The floor slopes toward a dry central basin. Everything has acquired the same softened contours: natural stone, poured foundations, brickwork, the casings of buried pumps. Even the stair treads have shallow, irregular lips, as though generations of slow water have passed over them.

The heat is immediate and difficult to locate. It rises through the soles of your shoes, presses down from the slabs, comes sideways out of the walls. Some metal surfaces are too hot to touch. Others merely feel like skin with a fever. There is little moving air. The few hanging strips of leaf remain still even when the whole room seems to breathe.

Water arrives in small installments.

A bead forms beneath a pipe coupling, enlarges for most of an hour, and falls into a stone cup almost filled with black roots. Along one wall, a dark stain advances several inches during the night, then withdraws before midday. Rain above produces no rainfall here. Two or three days later, a seam in the ceiling becomes wet, and a row of plants under it begins to uncurl.

Much of the floor is dry, covered with fine pale grit that collects against stems and foundations. The vegetation is concentrated along precise, complicated routes. Roots follow mortar joints, pass through bolt holes, enter a machine at one broken gauge and emerge from another. A person could map the hidden moisture by mapping the plants, though the result would look less like a river system than the wiring diagram of an elaborate appliance.

There are glossy leaves as broad as shovel blades, clustered beneath the largest opening. Farther from the light grow plants with narrow, folded leaves, copper on one side and deep green on the other. They stand shoulder-high in dense beds of their own fallen husks. Low growth pads the masonry in overlapping scales. Here and there, a pale stalk rises from an apparently empty patch of grit, bearing a heavy flower that never quite opens.

Nothing visits these flowers. Their pollen accumulates in the folds and is eventually carried down the stalk by condensation. New shoots appear close to the old ones, sometimes so close that the parent stem must split to make room.

There are no wings in the light shafts, no small movements beneath the leaves. The empty inspection booth near the entrance has no nests in its rafters. Even its cobweb-like threads are roots, descending from a planter on the roof.

Yet the hollow is loud.

The deepest sound comes from a flywheel in a recess at the far end. It turns so slowly that you can watch it for several breaths without being certain it has moved. Its axle passes through the wall. Whatever drives it is elsewhere, under more ground. Each fraction of a turn sets a loose plate trembling, and the plate fills the chamber with a continuous, grinding note.

Above that are pipe sounds: a thick knocking that travels around the walls, a prolonged hiss from somewhere beneath the gallery, occasional metallic reports followed by a fine shower of mineral dust. The machinery works at an immense patience. One valve may spend an afternoon opening. The sound of its effort is enough to make conversation impractical.

The plants have grown into this noise. Large leaves face the vibrating plate rather than the daylight. Their ribs are unusually heavy, and where they touch, they transmit a faint shudder from plant to plant. In the darkest beds, leaves are anchored at both ends, grown across gaps like belts. Put a finger against one and you feel the machinery through it.

Near the basin stands a small brick building with a roof of curved sheet metal. It has two rooms, a ceramic fuse board, and a desk whose legs have sunk into the softened floor. Labels remain on the switches, but the lettering has lifted from them. Some of the black characters lie intact on the desk below, curled at the edges like tiny peelings.

A maintenance path circles the basin. Its handrail has been repaired repeatedly with materials that are now almost indistinguishable: iron rod, dark wood, a length of pipe, something dense and fibrous that continues to thicken. At one corner the rail enters a trunk. It comes out on the other side at exactly the proper height.

There is an established courtesy between the growing things and the mechanisms. Roots leave clear the sweep of the flywheel. A vine that crosses a valve stem has formed a loose spiral around it, allowing the stem to rise. Where a pressure gauge has fallen, the surrounding growth preserves a round opening of the same size. The hollow keeps room for functions long after their instruments are gone.

Its strangest feature is the basin.

From the gallery it appears empty: a shallow oval, perhaps six paces across, floored with dark ceramic tiles. On descending, you hear water close at hand. Not dripping, but the small, steady lap of a full vessel. The sound follows the rim. Kneel and the heat against your face lessens. Hold a hand over the tiles and moisture gathers on your palm.

There is a waterline halfway up the basin wall, fresh and dark. Leaves that hang below it are gently displaced, all in the same direction. Occasionally one turns over.

The basin has been like this long enough for its steps to wear, for a mineral crust to form around its overflow, for roots to descend to an exact depth and spread horizontally. A narrow brass ruler is fixed beside it. Someone continued marking the level for years after the water ceased to be visible.

By late afternoon the daylight has left the floor. The red lamps reveal small territories of brick and foliage. Heat gathers beneath the gallery, and the flywheel completes another imperceptible portion of its turn.

Then, somewhere inside the walls, a valve finishes opening.

The grinding note lowers. Moisture darkens the roots around the basin. Across the chamber, hundreds of folded leaves begin, very slowly, to spread.
