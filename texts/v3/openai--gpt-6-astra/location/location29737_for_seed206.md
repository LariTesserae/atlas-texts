The hollow is under a root, just above the level at which the ground becomes water.

You could cover its entrance with your palm. Inside, it widens to something like the space beneath a low table, though there is no dry floor on which to kneel. The floor is living tissue: pale, firm, slightly translucent, cupped between the root’s descending lobes. Water lies on it in a shallow sheet. Here and there the tissue rises through the water in smooth, flat islands, each with a little darker green at its center.

There is room between them. That is the first surprise. From outside, the vegetation seems to admit no interior at all—leaf against leaf, root against root, a warm coastal plain so completely occupied that even its mud is bound into mats of growing fibers. But here the plant has opened a chamber within itself. The ceiling is high enough for a small frog to jump without striking it. Three root-columns stand well apart. The space around them is clear.

Light comes through a split along one side. Ordinary daylight, softened by a single leaf: enough to see color, enough to distinguish the red joints of a creature standing on the farthest island. There are no luminous growths. The wet floor returns a quiet, pale reflection to the underside of the root.

The noise is extraordinary.

A heavy knocking comes from the left wall, too deep for the size of the place. Beneath it runs a continuous, rapid chatter. At intervals—not irregularly, but as part of the same repeating phrase—something releases pressure with a harsh wet sigh. The hollow sounds as though it has formed beside the working parts of a large engine.

The machinery is in the root.

Behind its thin inner skin, bundles of vessels contract around columns of sap. Thick valves close with the knocking sound. Smaller membranes flutter where the flow divides, making the chatter. Farther down, below the waterline, a ring of muscular vegetable tissue squeezes brackish water through a bed of tightly packed fibers. Salt is left on one side; warm, sweet fluid passes into the other. The rejected brine comes out beneath the entrance, a drop at a time.

The root belongs to an old creeping plant whose body extends beyond anything visible from here. This chamber is one of its pumping junctions. There must be others under the green plain, each keeping the same pressure, feeding the same immense, low system. No animal has assembled it. Nothing has been laid, bolted, or fitted. Nevertheless, there are intake passages, pressure reservoirs, filters, returns. Their operation has the settled insistence of continuous production.

If you rest a fingertip on the floor, the vibration reaches the bones of your hand.

Everything inside has been softened by long use. The root-columns have broad feet and rounded shoulders. Channels in the floor bend without abrupt turns. Even the split that admits daylight has thick, rolled edges, healed long ago around its own opening. Water has passed through here for so many seasons that the plant grows in the shape the water requires. New tissue replaces old tissue along precisely the same surfaces. The chamber maintains its worn appearance by continually renewing it.

There is almost no loose material. A fallen fragment of leaf would be caught in the shallow current and carried toward a dark seam at the rear. Small white mouths wait along that seam.

They belong to translucent grazing animals, each no longer than a grain of rice. Their bodies lie concealed inside the floor’s fine wrinkles; only the feeding ends are exposed. There are hundreds. They comb the water with fans so delicate that, until they move together, they look like scratches in the reflected light.

Most of the animals live this way, tucked into the place rather than filling it.

Flat snails adhere to the ceiling with their shells buried in a film of algae. Narrow worms occupy the wet collars around the columns. A crustacean with red knees stands on one island, turning a scrap of root skin between its front limbs. Under the next island, six more hang upside down, their antennae extended into the current. Minute flies gather on the dry lip of the entrance, leaving generous gaps between their bodies. Each has a particular patch of surface, and each returns to it.

A frog rests in a recess above the pump valves. Its back is the color of the root, including two pale spots that resemble old abrasions. Only its throat is conspicuous. When it calls, the chamber gives the sound a hard, metallic body. The call passes through the pump noise like a tool dropped onto a running machine.

There is food enough here that little needs to pursue anything far. The plant sheds a measured dust of cells. Thin films of algae grow where the light reaches. The water brings organisms from the flooded mat outside and takes away what remains of them. Predators wait at the passages. Grazers follow circuits shorter than a finger.

The few upright shoots are widely spaced, smooth green stalks with a single oval leaf apiece. Their roots merge invisibly with the floor. They turn their leaves toward the slit, and beneath each leaf is a small, warm volume of shade. Droplets gather at their tips, swell, and fall into depressions already shaped to receive them.

The air smells of split stems, wet bark, and the faint mineral sharpness of concentrated salt. It is warm without any shaft of hot sun. Heat seeps from the working root and leaves slowly through the entrance. Moisture beads on the ceiling but seldom falls from it; it travels along fine grooves to the columns, then descends their backs.

For all the activity, the place scarcely changes. The water holds the same depth. The islands keep their outlines. The frog can leave its recess, feed, and return without altering the impression that it has always been there. A membrane wears thin and is replaced from beneath. A grazer disappears, and another feeds in its crease.

Even a rainstorm outside reaches the hollow chiefly as a change in flavor. The outer mats swell and take up the excess. The pump knocks on. At low water, stored fluid returns through the same vessels. There is no flood mark on the walls.

Looking through the entrance, you see only a short reach of daylight, a wet green floor, one red-jointed animal standing alone. You have to lean close to discover how much is operating out of sight.

Then the sound fills your ear: the valves seating, the filters trembling, the frog’s single hard note. The root presses gently against the water it contains. Beyond it, the plain continues its low, uninterrupted growth.
