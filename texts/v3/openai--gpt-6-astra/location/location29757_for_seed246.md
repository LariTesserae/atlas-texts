The town occupies a shallow fold in the summit, small enough to cross before your hands go numb. Beyond its last enclosure, the ground continues almost level for a little way, then ends in sky. Clouds pass below that edge. In clear weather you can see other heights across the gulf, all worn down to the same blunt profile, as though an enormous weight once rested on them and was lifted very slowly.

Nothing here has a sharp summit. The stone is pale gray, fine-grained, and rounded even where it has recently broken. It comes apart along old curves. A piece loosened by frost will settle neatly into the hollow beneath it, rocking once or twice before becoming difficult to distinguish from the ground.

From the approach, the town looks inhabited. There are low walls, broad doorways, lanes that widen into courts. Some of the courts have raised central basins. A continuous parapet follows the exposed northern side, with openings spaced at the height of a standing person’s face. Yet there are no roofs, no timbers, no fallen lintels, and very little rubble. The walls rise directly from the bedrock. Their surfaces carry the same slow whorls as the plateau outside.

Only farther in do you notice that the doorways are still growing.

Growth is too slow to watch, but the evidence is everywhere: a soft white lip along an arch, a thumb-sized opening filmed over since the last freeze, dark old tissue enclosed within translucent new stone. The material is neither quite limestone nor shell. At the damp base of a wall it yields slightly under pressure, then hardens again. Tiny branching bodies live inside it, drawing minerals from the rock and laying them down at the surface. Across centuries they have made partitions, chambers, drainage channels. Where two colonies meet, they form a street.

There is no central square. There are five nearly identical courts, and no route that reaches all five without retracing part of the way.

The streets are astonishingly empty. Their floors are smooth, swept by wind, with only an occasional black pellet or a single loose blade of grass caught in a depression. Most walls stand well apart. Looking through one doorway, you see another beyond it, then open sky between two distant piers. Even in the thickest part of the town, the buildings seem to leave room for something much larger than themselves.

The cold has a dry edge until you step into shade. There, moisture gathers on every surface. It beads on the walls, hangs under the rounded sills, darkens the knees of your clothing if you kneel. A cloud passing through can wet the entire settlement without dropping rain. For a few minutes the lanes fill with white air; then the cloud moves on, leaving the place brilliantly outlined in droplets.

Water is scarce enough to have routes.

Each wall conducts it toward a particular hollow. The channels are shallow and clean, no wider than a finger, sometimes crossing a threshold and continuing up what appears to be a slight incline. The living skin pulls the water along. At a junction, two trickles pause in swollen beads before joining and slipping into a hole beneath the street.

Below ground, the town is much less empty.

You hear this before you find any opening. There is a deep, uneven rushing underfoot, interrupted by knocks so heavy they seem to come from somewhere inside the mountain. At the edge of one court, a slit between wall and pavement breathes cold air against your ankles. Looking down, you can make out a wet shaft and, far below, a trembling patch of green.

The gardens grow in these cavities.

In one sunken room, reachable by a gentle stone slope, vegetation covers the floor and climbs halfway up the walls. Low ferns overlap in thick blue-green layers. There are cushions of moss with red stems, glossy leaves no larger than fingernails, pale runners crossing from one patch of soil to another. Everything stays close to a surface. No stalk rises far enough to meet the wind.

Light falls into the room through three openings and shifts across it all day. Beneath each opening the plants are smaller, darker, almost purple; between them, in reflected light, the ferns spread broad and soft. Meltwater threads through the roots. Touching the moss releases the smell of wet bark, though there is no tree anywhere on the summit.

The abundance is confined to these shelters. You can stand ankle-deep in greenery, look through a low arch, and see a hundred paces of bare stone.

Sometimes the plants move before the air does. Ferns flatten, leaves turn their waxed undersides upward, moss cushions tighten. A moment later the underground rushing rises to a roar. Wind has entered the northern parapet and is forcing its way through the hollows below town. It comes out through doorways and pavement cracks in hard, local gusts, lifting droplets sideways, making the channels chatter. For several seconds you cannot hear your own footsteps.

Different courts sound different. One gives a continuous low bellow. Another rattles with a dry, rapid clicking, although nothing visible moves there. In the smallest court, the wind produces a clear note that persists after the gust has passed. It can be heard from anywhere in the settlement. When the weather turns, its pitch drops.

There are animals, but they spend little time in the open. A white, long-bodied creature sometimes crosses a lane with its belly nearly touching the stone. It stops at damp seams and presses its narrow mouth against them. Its coat is greenish underneath, stained by whatever it sleeps in. If startled, it slips into a gap that looks too shallow to hold it.

Once or twice a day, a dark bird comes up from below the summit. It flies straight into the wind, lands on a parapet opening, and waits for something to emerge. Small winged insects are occasionally blown from the underground gardens, stunned by cold. The bird takes them without leaving its perch.

Afternoon brings the brightest light. The walls lose their grayness and become faintly honey-colored. Their sealed layers show through: earlier doorways, older channels, the curve of a basin buried within a newer floor. The settlement has occupied this fold for so long that its own growth is now part of the geology. It has narrowed passages, abandoned courts, opened gardens beneath places that were once streets. Nothing has been demolished. An inconvenient wall is absorbed over a few thousand winters.

As the sun lowers, the damp seams freeze first. Fine white lines appear along the ground. Ice expands inside the channels and closes them in sequence, diverting the last liquid water toward the sheltered rooms. You can follow the change by sound: a little ticking nearby, an answering crack across the court, then the brief splash of water taking a new route under stone.

The deep roar continues. Above it, the green rooms become quiet.

By dusk the exposed town is dry again. Its pale walls hold the remaining light while the hollows turn blue. Under the pavement, water is still moving toward roots; soft new material is gathering along an unfinished doorway. Out beyond the parapet, the cloud layer rises until there is no visible drop, only a white plain surrounding this small arrangement of streets. The cold wind enters through the evenly spaced openings, and the whole place sounds.
