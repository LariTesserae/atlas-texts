The plain is only a little higher than its drains.

From the rim of a settling basin, you can see—or, in this light, gradually distinguish—a mile of low roofs, clay shoulders, retaining walls, and broad, shallow steps. Beyond them are more of the same, obscured by cold vapor. Nothing rises far. Even the largest halls have been sunk into the thickness of the ground, their roofs scarcely higher than a person’s waist. The horizon is a crowd of small obstructions.

Underfoot, the surface has the faint give of leather stretched over something hard. It is clay, recently beaten, still damp beneath its dusting of powdered brick. Heel marks remain for several minutes and then grow shallower as pressure from below pushes the material back into place. Along the paths, people have laid strips of fired tile to keep traffic from undoing the morning’s work. Some tiles bear fingerprints. Others carry the impressions of knees.

There is no soil beneath them. A broken edge reveals wickerwork made from rolled clay cords, packed with rubble, resting on slabs that rest on older vaults. Those vaults have floors of their own. Wells driven through them encounter chambers, plugs, buried stairs, and sometimes another well, set at a different angle. Nobody has brought up an unworked stone.

Most of the plain is dark. Light comes from thumb-sized oil bowls standing in wall niches, each flame protected by a hood with a narrow slit. They illuminate useful things: the lip of a trough, a change in floor level, a measuring notch. Between these marks, a traveler navigates by touch and sound. The long, low ridges visible across the district are rows of hollow structures, built so closely together that their walls nearly meet. Their gaps breathe cold air onto your ankles.

The sound is much larger than the light suggests.

Everywhere, mallets are striking. Wooden ones make a deep, hollow knock; fired-clay ones ring; stone-headed ones deliver a blunt report that travels through the soles of the feet. Crews work beyond walls, beneath floors, inside roof cavities. Sometimes a whole district beats in time for twenty or thirty strokes. Then one crew stops to move its scaffold, another begins somewhere underneath, and the rhythm comes apart.

Between blows there is scraping, the rattle of rubble descending a chute, the groan of a cord under strain. A little running water can be heard for a surprisingly long distance through an empty pipe.

Water is scarce enough to be accounted for, but common enough to make everything cold.

It collects under the lips of roof tiles and along the lower edges of lintels. Workers pass flat pottery spoons beneath these edges and empty the catch into stoppered jars. The broad drains usually hold nothing but a dull shine. At irregular intervals, a gate is lifted upstream and a shallow surge passes through, carrying clay shavings, brick dust, and pale fragments of mortar. People step onto thresholds to let it by. Children follow it with little wicker scoops, retrieving anything useful before the current reaches the settling basins.

Rain arrives from the roof of the sky.

That roof is seldom visible. On clear nights, when the vapor thins and the furnace vents glow, you can make out its enormous overlapping plates, each one supported by ribs disappearing into the dark. The undersides are studded with hanging droplets. A distant blow, or the settling of a support, can shake several acres of water loose at once. It falls loudly for half a minute, stops, and leaves the adjoining street perfectly dry.

The drops taste of chalk.

Above the inhabited level, ladders ascend into narrow shafts. Teams go up with baskets of patching clay and return with hardened flakes stripped from the sky’s inner surface. They say there are passages above it, and another roof above those. Their lamps are too small to establish much more.

Much of the landscape is new. Corners are sharp. Drainage channels still show the individual scoops that hollowed them out. The smooth slopes beside the basins have been drawn into place with boards; their parallel ridges have not yet softened. Where a passage has been abandoned, it does not immediately become a ruin. It remains cleanly unfinished: a wall ending at shoulder height, a stair whose final tread is still a basket of loose fill, a row of pillars with wet cloths wrapped around their bases.

Cloth, wood, leather, cord—all are old here, patched and apportioned. A mallet handle may have a dozen binding rings and no original grip left. The clay is more plentiful. It is reclaimed from sealed rooms below, where dampness gradually loosens the old work. Excavators kneel in those rooms and shave the walls into baskets. Above, other hands wet the shavings, tread them, roll them, and make the next stretch of plain.

The excavation and the building must keep pace. Too much removed below, and a neighborhood sags. Too much added above, and doors begin to pinch shut.

There are people whose entire occupation is listening at floors.

They lie with one cheek against the tiles while the crews fall quiet around them. After a while they mark a place with white slurry. Work resumes elsewhere. By the following day, a new buttress may occupy that spot, narrowing a kitchen or blocking half a street. Nobody takes the shortest route for granted.

Life gathers around the little warmth there is. Inside drain elbows, a translucent film feeds on oil washed from workshops. It wrinkles when disturbed, closing over grit with extraordinary slowness. Small, colorless animals graze it. They have flat bodies, many soft legs, and dark mouthparts that click against the pottery. You may walk for hours without seeing one; then a lifted cover exposes three pressed together on the damp underside, perfectly still in the lamplight.

The larger creature is known mostly by its work: a clean crescent bitten out of the grazers’ film, a thin drag mark across wet mortar, a pile of empty leg skins under a warm flue. Once in a great while it crosses an open path. It is scarcely longer than a forearm, low and jointed, carrying a loose armor of stolen tile chips. It pauses at each hammer blow, then hurries on during the brief silence.

Near the working edge, the plain becomes difficult to read. Floors have been spread without rooms above them. Doorframes stand in rows, braced with cord. Unfired pipes lie mouth to mouth across expanses of damp fill, waiting to be covered. Lamps hang from temporary stakes, showing a succession of small work sites rather than a single view.

Here the ground is warmest. Fresh batches of clay arrive in shallow baskets. Barefoot workers tread them into the spaces between ribs while others press grit into the surface. Beyond the last finished curb, a crew is making a basin. They have already formed its outflow, though there is no water yet and scarcely a bottom.

A woman checks the slope by pouring half a cup onto it.

Everyone nearby watches the dark patch advance. It hesitates at a thumbprint, gathers itself, and slips into the new channel. She scrapes a little clay from the place where it paused. The next basket is lowered. The mallets begin again.
