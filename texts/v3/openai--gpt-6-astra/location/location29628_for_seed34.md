The city begins before the road ends, though there is no longer much difference between them. Flat stones rise out of the wet ground in a line, acquire edges, become a causeway. On either side, shallow water lies among pale gravel. Farther on, the gravel resolves into fallen walls. There is no gate. You pass between two worn corners, and the open plateau becomes a street.

Everything is built low and close together.

The walls are limestone, mostly gray, with a yellowish grain where a surface has recently split. They stand shoulder-high in some quarters and several stories high in others. Houses share walls. Courts share drains. Stairways climb the outer faces of buildings to reach another street above, where the slope has carried the next neighborhood over the roofs of the first. Most roofs are gone, but enough stone vaults remain to make dark rooms beneath the open rooms. Water collects on their floors. Looking down through a doorway, you see a small, complete reflection of the sky.

From within the streets, the place feels crowded. There is always another opening, another partition behind the first, a passage too narrow to see into until you stand beside it. From the ridges, it becomes almost incomprehensibly extensive.

The plateau continues north in broad, shallow folds. Built terraces follow those folds to the edge of sight. Beyond them, under a separate bank of cloud, another pale district occupies the shoulder of a distant rise. Beyond that is a darker band which might be a cliff or rain. The city does not cover the whole country. There are miles of bare pavement rock, wet meadows, wide depressions filled with water. But construction returns on every usable shelf, as though settlement had spread through the highlands by finding the same few conditions again and again: sound ground, a little shelter, a place for water to leave.

There are no towers to gather the eye. Its enormity is cumulative.

The air is cool enough that shaded stone feels cold through clothing. In sunlight, a wall can warm your back while the wind chills your face. Clouds travel quickly, dragging their shadows over several neighborhoods at once. Rain can be seen falling far away in distinct curtains, one crossing a ridge, another dissolving before it reaches the ground. Here, for the moment, the light is clear. Every wet edge shines. The flooded courts hold a brighter blue than the sky itself.

Water is almost everywhere, but seldom deep.

It beads along the undersides of lintels. It darkens the lower courses of walls. In long passages it runs so thinly over the paving that the moving surface is visible only where it bends around a grain of sand. Some streets have become streams, though they still look like streets: steps descending beneath clear water, thresholds cutting across the current, a central gutter carrying the faster thread.

Elsewhere the drains still work. Their openings are simple gaps under the masonry, their channels laid with a patience that has outlasted whatever required them. A flooded square may empty through one dark slit no wider than a hand. Below it, three terraces down, water comes quietly out of a wall and spreads over a fan of polished pebbles.

No mechanism moves it. The place depends on weight, slope, and the permeability of stone.

The plateau has been wearing down for a very long time, but it is not collapsing in any general or hurried way. Corners have rounded. Exposed steps have become shallow saddles. At the foot of a wall, the fallen stones are settled into one another and stained to the same color as the foundation. Some collapses are old enough to have redirected whole sequences of pools and drains. The water has accepted these changes. It no longer seeks the routes shown by the original streets.

At the margins, it is difficult to say where the buildings end. Limestone breaks naturally into beds and blocks, and the builders used those beds as floors, foundations, and sometimes entire sides of rooms. A row of houses may terminate against a low cliff whose horizontal seams continue almost perfectly into the masonry. Farther along, a natural fissure serves as an alley. Its walls have never been cut, but feet once polished the narrow floor.

There is little green.

A thin skin of algae colors the submerged steps. Moss grows where seepage keeps one side of a stone wet, leaving the other bare. On the exposed terraces, short sedges occupy cracks filled with black soil. Their leaves lean together in the wind, showing a dull silver underside, then lift again. They do not spread far. Soil is scarce, and much of what gathers during dry weather is carried down into the courts by the next rain.

The sheltered hollows are different. There, between retaining walls, small marshes have formed. Rushes stand in tawny clumps around open water. Old stems remain upright among the new, so that a patch only a few yards across looks dense enough to hide something large. Usually it hides nothing. A beetle climbs a stem. A little movement troubles the water at the roots.

Once in a long while, a bird crosses a street ahead of you. It is small and brown, briefly distinct against the pale stone, then gone into a crack beneath a fallen slab. Much later, from another quarter, comes a single thin call. Distance takes it before you can locate its source.

Most of the time you hear your own passage.

Water sounds only where you stop to listen: a drop striking a pool inside a roofed chamber; a faint, irregular trickle under paving; the soft release of bubbles from mud. The wind is nearly silent over the bare walls. It becomes audible in the rush beds, where it makes a dry brushing sound despite all the wetness.

Yet nothing is quite still. Cloud shadow advances across the courts. The edge of a pool creeps over a pale line in the stone. A grain loosened by last night’s cold rolls down a slope and stops against another grain. In a wall exposed to the sun, moisture retreats inward, leaving a light patch that slowly enlarges. Beneath the same wall, water continues to rise through the mortarless joints.

By afternoon, the distant rain arrives. First the far neighborhoods disappear; then the ridge above you loses its outline. Drops stipple the courtyard pools. Their separate reflections break into a common gray, and the streets begin to carry more water.

You can shelter beneath a surviving vault. The room smells of mineral damp and a little old silt. Through its doorway, the rain falls almost vertically into a narrow court. Across the court is another doorway, and through that, another pale opening farther away.

As the rain strengthens, a shallow groove beside your foot fills and begins to flow. It passes under the threshold without entering the room. Whoever laid the floor understood this weather.

When the shower moves on, sunlight reaches the upper wall first. Then it descends, revealing each uneven course. Out beyond the doorway, water is still arriving from higher terraces. The rain has ended here, but its passage through the city has only begun.
