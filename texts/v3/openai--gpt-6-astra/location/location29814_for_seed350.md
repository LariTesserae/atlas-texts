The water is warm enough that the metal sweats underwater.

Clear beads gather on the submerged girders, hold their roundness against the current, and rise only when they have grown as large as fists. They pass through the darker water with a faint blue sheen. Above them, somewhere beyond the vegetation, they strike the underside of the air. You can hear each impact through the railings: a heavy knock, almost lost in the continuous roar.

The basin extends for several miles between low banks of newly formed black stone. Nothing here has had time to become rounded. The shelves have sharp lips; the fissures meet at clean angles; even the mud breaks into little plates with cutting edges. Warm mineral water surges from cracks that were not present an hour ago. Along the eastern margin, a ridge is still assembling itself, lifting in short sections beneath the flood. Each section rises furred with pale roots. The plants seem to reach the stone before the stone has finished arriving.

There is very little dry ground. What looks like an island from a distance may be a mat of leaves, a factory roof, or a patch of water standing higher than the water around it. The true banks are scarcely a body’s height above the flood and are repeatedly overtopped. Beyond them lie more drowned flats, obscured by rain.

The rain does not fall everywhere in the same direction.

Across the center of the basin, it drives nearly horizontally through the submerged forest, threading the slow water with faster water. Beneath the western bank, it falls upward in a broad, trembling curtain. Elsewhere it behaves conventionally enough to make those regions more disturbing: drops descending through a few feet of humid air, striking a surface, vanishing into depth. There are places where you can follow a single drop all the way down and watch it strike a second surface far below the first.

Light enters reluctantly. The sky is a low, greenish gray, and the water takes most of it within the first few yards. Under the leaves, visibility comes from industrial lamps: amber bars behind thick glass, occasional white searchlights mounted on swiveling brackets. Their beams are crowded with torn foliage, silt, bubbles, and thin mineral flakes that turn edge-on and disappear. A lamp may illuminate the same drifting leaf several times as the current returns it by different routes.

The vegetation is immense without being tall. It spreads, folds, overlaps. Broad translucent leaves lie stacked like sheets of wet glass; ropes of jointed stems pass through them and descend into the mud. Some plants carry their roots in loose bundles above their crowns. Others grow along the boundaries between currents, holding to a difference in pressure as firmly as ivy holds to masonry.

There are entire groves made of one repeatedly divided stem. Their newest growth is almost white. Older portions deepen through bottle green to a purple so dark that the lamps reveal only a slick highlight on each ridge. When a surge comes through, the groves compress into a solid-looking mass, then open again, revealing narrow passages, the corners of concrete foundations, another moving layer of leaves.

Nothing eats them.

No fish scatter from the light. No insects collect above the flood. Nothing calls from the banks or makes a small, separate movement in the undergrowth. Broken leaves soften where they lodge, their margins turning to clear gel. The gel strings between the stems and is drawn out by the current until it becomes another kind of water. Growth is everywhere; grazing is absent. The basin is packed with living surfaces that have never been bitten.

The machinery stands at long intervals, far enough apart that each installation has its own clearing and its own weather.

One is a low pump house on six concrete legs, its windows below the usual waterline. Another is a lattice tower carrying three massive flywheels, only one of which is visible at any given time. Near the center of the basin, a row of transformer housings sits on a platform just above the flood. Rain hisses on their fins. Thick cables descend from them into the plants, reappear a quarter mile away, and enter a brick building whose roof is slowly flexing.

There are no roads between these structures. Maintenance rails run along the bottom, interrupted by fresh rock. Catwalks end at upright sheets of rushing water. Here and there, a yellow handwheel protrudes from a tangle of stems, clean on one side and plated with mineral crust on the other.

Everything is operating.

The sound reaches you first through your feet and teeth: the low, relentless load of engines working against resistance. Above that comes the hammering of valves, the metallic shriek of stressed bearings, the concussion of water meeting water at incompatible speeds. The leaves add a vast leathery clapping. Sheets of them strike one another under pressure with enough force to send pale bruises spreading through their tissues.

There is no quiet interval in which to distinguish the sources. A valve closes nearby, and the answer comes from the far bank before the local impact arrives. A turbine accelerates; for several seconds, its rising note issues from every hollow stem in the grove. Loose bolts turn in their sockets. Mud lifts from the floor in little trembling columns.

Close to the pump house, the roar has worn patterns into the vegetation. Leaves grow with regularly spaced holes. Stems branch at the nodes of standing vibrations. A patch of fine, red growth outlines a sound that has remained in the same place for months.

The pumps do not appear to lower the flood.

Their intake mouths are broad enough to admit whole thickets, yet the plants rooted in front of them lean away rather than inward. Through inspection glass, you can see impellers revolving in a dense green darkness. At the discharge outlets, fresh shallows spread across the basin floor. Mud, angular stones, rooted shoots: all emerge together, moving outward in a low front. The machinery is making more bottom.

Where this new ground meets an older patch, the water becomes violently unsettled. Depths disagree. A shelf that should lie three feet beneath the surface opens abruptly onto a dim gulf, while a deep channel can flatten into mud beneath a passing raft of leaves. The vegetation reveals these changes a moment ahead of everything else. Roots tighten. Broad leaves turn edgewise. Then the basin folds, and a whole grove drops out of sight without leaving a whirlpool.

Sometimes it returns nearby.

On the south side, three pump-house stairways stand in a row. Their chipped paint and bent lowest steps are identical, but only the middle stairway reaches the building. The others continue down through dense stems. Their handrails remain warm. Small bubbles issue from the bolt holes at the same instant on all three.

Toward evening, the work intensifies. More lamps come on beneath the flood, marking distances the daylight never revealed. What seemed a continuous bed of vegetation separates into groves and channels, low terraces and long troughs of rushing black water. Far out, an amber rectangle tips slowly as its supporting structure settles into newly manufactured ground. The light stays on.

A pressure front travels across the basin. You can watch its progress in the leaves: first a darkening, then a flattened band, then an eruption of pale undersides. It reaches the nearest tower with a blow that rings through every exposed surface. Warm water climbs the legs and hangs there in thick sleeves.

For a moment the central channel clears. Beneath it are fresh concrete footings, bright anchor bolts, cables still tightly coiled in their shipping bands. Plants are already unfolding between them. Farther down, through another wavering layer of water, a lamp swings over a second channel.

Then the foliage closes, the engines take a heavier load, and the low banks begin to move apart.
