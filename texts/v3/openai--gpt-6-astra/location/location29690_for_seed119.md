The place is about thirty paces across, though the noise makes it seem much larger.

It is a shallow court of fired clay, enclosed by a round wall and covered by a low, vaulted roof. A little light enters through the gap between wall and roof: a continuous gray slit, scarcely wider than a hand. At midday this draws a pale line around the room. Below it, the walls disappear gradually into damp darkness.

There is almost nothing in the middle. A basin, long and low, lies slightly off-center. Beside it stands a single square pier that does not reach the ceiling. The rest is floor, a great deal of bare floor for so small a place, sloping so gently toward the basin that the slope becomes apparent only when water moves.

Everything has been made.

The floor is brick, the basin is brick, the wall is brick beneath an uneven coat of lime. The roof consists of clay ribs joined with clay pegs, each rib bearing the thumbprints of the person who pressed it into its mold. Even the rounded stones set along the basin’s lip are not stones. They are lumps of kiln-fired earth, shaped in the hand and pierced before firing. Water has worn some of the holes into slots.

Where the floor has broken, there is another floor beneath it. Beneath that, seen through a deeper fracture near the wall, are hollow jars laid sideways in pale mortar. There is no visible soil anywhere. A root entering a crack finds a joint, then a cavity, then another joint.

The court sits low in whatever surrounds it. Water pushes inward through its drains.

It does not arrive as a flood. First, the dark mouths in the floor begin to tick. A bubble swells in one, holds a tiny crooked image of the light slit, and bursts. Then the water comes in flat, clear tongues, spreading over the bricks. Each tongue follows an old path. It separates at the same imperfections, joins the same shallow hollows, and gathers along the basin’s edge before spilling in.

The worn surfaces have made this choreography very exact. A ridge no higher than a fingernail keeps one stretch of floor dry for half an hour longer than the rest. A depression left by a lost brick becomes a pool deep enough to hide a fish. On the square pier, generations of wetting have drawn dark horizontal lines, but the highest line is old and powdery. The water has kept to its lower habits for a long time.

It is cool enough that a hand laid on the wall soon aches. The air smells of wet pottery, old lime, and the faint mineral sweetness released when rain first touches a roof tile. Here that smell never quite passes. Moisture beads on the underside of the vault. The largest drops hang from the clay pegs, trembling in the draft, until some vibration shakes them loose.

There are many vibrations.

A booming comes through the floor at irregular intervals. It has the deep, loose sound of someone striking the mouth of an enormous empty jar. After each boom, smaller sounds travel around the wall: a knock, a rattle, a hollow gulp. The court is built over chambers that fill and empty through narrow passages. Air is driven from one into another. Somewhere beneath the basin, a trapped pocket gives a prolonged, almost comic groan.

Nothing with gears or teeth drives this. There are channels, jars, weighted clay flaps, and water pressing from outside. Some of the flaps still open. Others have worn away, leaving their hinges to clack against the seats.

Over this comes the noise of the animals.

At first there seem to be none.

Then a ripple goes the wrong way across the basin, and the water turns briefly silver. Small fish have crowded beneath its overhanging rim, too numerous to distinguish while they remain still. They come out together when a drop strikes the surface. Their mouths open upward. A moment later they are gone again, leaving the center empty.

The walls hold the rest.

Crabs occupy the hollow bricks behind the lime skin. Their entrances are small and dark, placed at no regular height. During the rise of water they retreat inward, pushing against one another with a dry ceramic clatter. During the fall they emerge, first a claw, then two stalked eyes, then the whole pale body. Many carry pieces of fired clay over their backs. The pieces are carefully chosen: curved fragments light enough to drag, broad enough to shelter beneath. Some have been carried so long that the animals’ feet have polished tracks along their undersides.

Tiny snails graze the damp wall in files. There is little visible food, only a gray-green film that makes the lime slippery. Their passage leaves cleaner lines, and those lines are slowly erased by new growth. Near the light slit, three tufts of stiff, colorless grass hang inward from a mortar seam. Their roots spread through the wall rather than downward. When condensation runs along them, the grass tips drip into the same three spots on the floor.

Winged insects hatch from the basin’s shallow end. They gather in the narrow light until the slit looks fuzzy, then scatter when a long-legged hunting creature comes out of the roof. These hunters are almost transparent. Suspended against the clay, their bodies are invisible except for the dark contents of their stomachs.

The loudest inhabitants live inside the square pier.

Its sides are pierced with holes, most of them stopped with thin membranes of dried secretion. Behind these, small amphibious animals call to one another. Each animal is scarcely the length of a finger. The pier enlarges their voices. A single call emerges as a wooden bellow; several together produce a harsh, rolling chorus that can be felt through the soles of the feet.

They answer the chambers under the floor. When the water booms, the pier replies. Then the crabs begin to move, and falling droplets stipple the basin, and the whole court is loud enough to make speech useless.

Between these episodes there are pauses so complete that one hears a snail’s shell touch the wall.

The animals know the workings of the place. The fish leave the drain mouths before the water reverses. The crabs withdraw from certain bricks before those bricks cough air. The hunters fold themselves into the roof when the deep chamber begins to groan.

One rule is less easily understood: nothing living crosses the floor while the square pier is silent. Even an insect fallen onto a dry brick waits there, moving its antennae. When the first call comes, it runs. A crab caught halfway back to the wall stops with one leg raised and holds that posture through the entire quiet interval.

The water observes no such restriction. It continues slipping over the floor, carrying shed skins and scraps of lime toward the basin.

Eventually the drains take back what they brought. Small whirlpools appear. The fish retreat beneath the rim. Water thins to a trembling skin over the bricks, then divides into threads. In the hollows it remains, reflecting the roof in patches too dark to show its shape.

For a little while, the age of the court becomes especially visible. Every worn edge shines. The old hand-shaped surfaces emerge from the wetness: a palm flattened into a brick, the drag of a cord across soft clay, a peg pushed in slightly crooked and left that way. None of these marks is sharp. Even the mistakes have become part of the drainage.

Then the roof begins to drip more heavily. Somewhere below, a jar fills to its neck. Air presses through a submerged opening, and a single large bubble rises into the basin.

The pier answers before it bursts.
