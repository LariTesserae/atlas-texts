The ground is pale green and gives slightly beneath a foot, although it has carried freight for longer than the present sky has existed.

At first it looks like a plain of tightly laid boards. The grain runs east to west. Every few hundred paces, a seam crosses it, packed with white fibers so fine they disappear when viewed directly. But the boards widen during the morning. Their edges darken, their seams admit a little light, and somewhere far below, a new layer of flooring unfolds. The whole plain is a living deck. Its underside has never been seen.

There are no hills worth naming. There are rises where old conduits have thickened, shallow depressions over exhausted growth chambers, long gentle slopes engineered to deliver fallen material to the reclamation mouths. From an elevated loading platform, you can see hundreds of kilometers across a surface whose irregularities remain mostly below the height of a person. Distance gathers in horizontal bands: ivory, celadon, a dark green almost black, then the brilliant white of the cultivation districts.

Everything visible has been planted to specification.

The roads are stems laid sideways and persuaded to join. The walls are leaves that have forgotten how to stop thickening. A warehouse consists of one immense folded organ, its roof held taut by branching ribs, its interior partitions budding off wherever storage demand becomes persistent enough. Even the fixtures are alive. Hinges heal around their pins. Signs grow their lettering in a slower, darker tissue. Beneath the walkways, cables carry a faint red charge through strands of transparent root.

There is no soil. When a section of pavement wears through, it exposes older pavement, compressed and cross-grained, and below that an earlier arrangement of roads.

The lowest surviving construction records refer to foundations. They do not say what the foundations rest on.

***

It is cool here, despite the light.

Brightness comes from broad ceiling-leaves suspended so high that they appear to form a continuous sky. Their undersides shine with a steady mineral whiteness. There is no visible sun. Occasionally a maintenance opening dilates overhead, revealing another luminous layer beyond, crossed by the silhouettes of roots as thick as cities.

Shadows are green and shallow. Beneath a canopy, you can still read the smallest print on a delivery label. Inside a sealed room, the walls gradually brighten until the room reaches the prescribed working level.

The air smells of split stalks, chalk, and the inside of an unused cupboard. It draws moisture out of exposed skin. There are no pools, no gutters, no dark stains beneath leaking joints. Nothing drips. The few molecules of water admitted through incoming freight are collected by sensitive white growths around the loading apertures. These become swollen and translucent, then are cut away and carried to restricted chambers.

The vegetation has other arrangements.

Its flexible tissues contain dry chains of salts that loosen under electrical charge and lock again when the charge passes. Its sap moves as a pale powder, lifted grain by grain along the inner surfaces of vessels. Cut a branch and the wound emits a brief, delicate plume. Then its edges draw together with the sound of paper being folded.

Much of the landscape is occupied by the business of making more landscape.

There are seed mills whose grinding surfaces are enlarged molars of lignin. There are kilometer-long grafting beds under adjustable light, and extrusion groves where hollow stems emerge continuously from the ground, pass through sizing rings, and turn toward the districts that require them. Broad leaves travel overhead on living belts. By the time they reach their destinations, some have become insulation, some partition walls, some the thin translucent wrappers used to keep young junctions free of dust.

The factories are difficult to distinguish from the vegetation around them. A stand of silver reeds may be a switching station. A field of low, round leaves may be assembling pressure vessels underneath itself. You recognize an engine by the regular spacing of its growth scars.

Almost none of this makes a sound.

The mills turn slowly under enormous pressure. Their bearings are soft, replaceable tissue. The belts have no joints to knock. When a load is transferred, the receiving structure grows up to meet it, taking its weight before the carrier lets go.

Everywhere, work proceeds with the discretion of something happening in the next room.

***

The place is old enough to have developed wastefulness.

At the edges of active districts, escaped architecture fills every available gap. Stair plants climb walls without leading anywhere. Packing vines produce rigid, empty cases that split and scatter smaller cases. Safety rails branch into thickets so dense that a person could lean against them anywhere and be held.

Here the green deepens. Narrow leaves overlap in stacks, trapping white light between them. Roots cross the roads in flattened bundles, each bundle polished by passing traffic. Fallen structural fronds lie curled beside reclamation slots too small to receive them. Some have taken root and begun building modest enclosures of their own.

Erosion happens through use and patient shedding. Wind lifts the dead outer cells from exposed surfaces. Cargo rubs smooth tracks into the decking. At old intersections, corners have become rounded bowls of grain, every annual layer visible. The worn material travels close to the ground as a fine cream-colored dust. It collects behind buttresses, in abandoned service channels, against the lee side of labels.

During the shedding season, whole regions disappear beneath this dry weather. The overhead light turns the airborne fibers dazzling white. A distant building remains visible as a green shadow while its nearer loading bays vanish completely.

The dust storms are quiet. You hear a little brushing at your ankles. When they pass, fresh buds protrude through the drifts.

There are animals, though many workers spend their lives without seeing one.

Small gray creatures inhabit the insulation around the oldest power trunks. They have six narrow feet and mouths suited to shaving dead fiber. Their bodies carry a faint static glow. At intervals they emerge, cross a bright surface with agonizing caution, and disappear beneath a seam.

An entire freight route may be paused for one of these crossings. The automatic warning leaves open along the track; the carriers wait with their loads suspended; a tiny animal negotiates the exposed distance.

Then the leaves close, and production resumes.

***

Travel becomes uncertain beyond the maintained districts.

The plain was built in sections, and some sections were grown before the present rules of distance were standardized. Along their boundaries, roads have acquired peculiar tolerances. A straight corridor can take three days to cross in one direction and an hour in the other. Survey roots mark the discrepancies with rows of black nodules. Most travelers know to keep those rows on their left.

Older freight sometimes arrives before it was dispatched. Receiving departments stack it beneath dormant leaves until its paperwork catches up.

There are districts visible across the plain that cannot be approached at all. Their towers remain the same size through days of walking. The surrounding landscape changes; the towers do not. Their output still enters the distribution network: bundles of pale struts, folded roof membranes, replacement light. They are evidently operating.

At certain seams, gravity runs through the decking like a service current. A loose tool will slide gently toward a junction, pause, then stand upright on its handle. The nearby vegetation grows around these faults with practiced ease. Leaves form horizontal shelves where weight turns sideways. Freight lines curve into what appears to be open air and continue underneath themselves.

Repairs are made by grafting.

For a small discrepancy, a crew plants a ring of corrective tissue around the affected place. For a serious one, entire neighborhoods are allowed to thicken until the incompatible geometry has been buried. Those low rises visible across the plain are not all conduits.

Some contain rooms whose doors opened into the wrong year. Some contain loading yards where every object acquired the same shadow. The growth over them is healthy, dense, and closely inspected.

***

Late in the work cycle, the ceiling-leaves rotate by a few degrees. Light remains abundant, but its direction changes. Grain that was invisible all day stands out across the roads. The worn tracks shine. Far away, the extrusion groves bend together as a new order passes through their roots.

A cool current moves across the plain.

For several minutes, millions of narrow ventilation leaves lift along the edges of buildings. Stored heat leaves the production chambers in broad, transparent columns. Loose fibers rise, hang in the brilliance, and settle somewhere else. There is a faint ticking as charged salt moves through the walls.

Then the current passes.

On an unused platform, beneath a sign whose letters have almost grown shut, a new doorway begins to form. First a crease appears in the green wall. The crease lengthens. Fibers separate along a clean vertical line, exposing a space already lit inside.

No one has ordered a room here for centuries.

Still, a narrow strip of flooring extends across the threshold. It waits a little while, then widens.
