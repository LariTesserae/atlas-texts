The village occupies a fold in the mountain no wider than a few shouted conversations. From the opposite slope it looks like a darker patch of forest, with smoke caught in it. Only when a gust turns the leaves does the settlement show itself: a row of shutters, wet steps, the pale underside of a footbridge, someone carrying a basket through what seemed to be the crown of a tree.

There is very little open ground. What passes for the square is a platform around three old trees, their roots holding the uphill edge and their trunks passing through the roof of the grain shelter. Ferns grow between the floor poles. Along the downhill side, stalls lean out above a ravine, supported by timber braces so thickly clothed in moss that their joints have disappeared. Water drops from the moss onto everything below.

The place is high enough that clouds regularly enter at street level. On clear mornings there is a view beyond the roofs: another wooded ridge, a pale river far down, distant slopes cut into narrow fields. Usually the view ends at the next house. Cool mist presses through bamboo screens and gathers on the hairs of hanging roots. Sleeves stay damp. A hand laid on a railing comes away cold.

Rain has stopped, but the village has not finished raining.

The upper leaves shed onto the lower leaves; those tip their loads into bromeliad cups and the crowded forks of branches. Roof gutters spill into hollow bamboo pipes. The pipes knock and gurgle, feeding washing troughs, small household cisterns, and a channel beneath the main stair. Every few breaths a much larger drop falls from somewhere overhead and strikes a roof with the distinct sound of a thrown pebble.

Under all this growth is an old, rounded shoulder of rock. It can be seen in only a few places: polished dark where people step across the stream, green-black behind the washing trough, pale on the underside of an overhang where the rain cannot reach. Generations of runoff have worn the same grooves deeper without greatly changing their courses. The houses occupy the ribs between them. No one builds in a groove, however dry it appears. Newcomers learn this from the gaps in the village, the narrow, apparently useful spaces left empty between crowded houses.

Most construction here is a matter of fastening one thing to another.

Bamboo is split with broad knives and tied with cane. Roofs are layered in shingles overlaid with thatch, then repaired wherever a drip becomes intolerable. A living trunk can be a corner post, provided there is room for it to thicken. Fig roots have been guided across the narrowest part of the ravine and bound around a timber frame; the frame has almost rotted away, but the crossing is stronger now than when it was made. Its handrail is smooth where palms polish it and rough where new rootlets keep trying to descend.

The village has several such crossings, all within sight of one another. One carries people. Another carries water. A third, little more than two poles and a hanging mesh of vines, belongs in practice to animals, though children use it when no one is watching.

The animals make the settlement seem larger than it is.

Swallows work the air beneath the platforms, passing so close that their wings make small, dry sounds in the wet atmosphere. Starlings pack the fruiting trees and argue from the roof ridges. There are nests under steps, behind shutters, inside the unused ends of water pipes. A pair of birds has built above the knife sharpener’s bench, and he has hung a strip of bark beneath them to protect his work.

In the ravine, frogs call from ledges scarcely big enough to hold them. Their notes overlap with the stream until it becomes difficult to decide which sounds are voices and which are water striking hollow wood. Beetles crowd the lanterns after dark. Moths settle under the eaves in such numbers that a brushed beam releases a soft, fluttering dust. Snails travel the doorframes, leaving clean tracks through the green film.

There are domestic animals too, though they have not been given much room. Chickens occupy the steps and object to being displaced. Two goats are tethered beneath the upper houses, where they can reach bundles of cut foliage but not the bridge lashings. Small pigs nose through a fenced bed of wet leaves beside the communal latrine. Rats pass behind the basket stalls with the brisk familiarity of people on errands.

At midday the square becomes almost impassable.

Baskets of fern shoots, beans, sour fruit, and roots arrive from terraces hidden farther up the slope. Someone brings fish wrapped in large leaves, carried all morning from the valley. Firewood comes in on backs, each bundle protected by a little roof of its own. People turn sideways to pass one another. A porter pauses beneath a low branch while a woman pulls a hanging pot out of his way. Children chase a hen through the legs of a bench, setting off three dogs, which set off the birds above them.

The stream is loud enough that prices must be repeated.

Hammering comes from a shelter beside the bridge: a short rhythm, a pause, then another as an iron edge is beaten straight. Elsewhere a wooden mallet drives a new peg into a stair. Someone shakes a mat over the ravine. Someone calls down for a bucket and receives an answer from above. The houses are so closely layered that voices often arrive from an unexpected direction.

Then the wind enters.

It comes down the fold of the mountain and pushes the treetops toward the ravine. For a moment every wet surface brightens. Leaves show their pale backs; ropes tighten; loose shutters slap. Water held for hours in the canopy comes down in a brief, heavy shower. The market contracts beneath its awnings. A basket rolls. The birds lift together from one tree and land in another, producing an enormous commotion over a distance of twenty paces.

Within minutes the stream rises against the lowest step.

People keep talking as they move things out of reach. A boy lifts the slatted cover from a drain with a hooked stick. An older woman catches the mat of leaves released beneath it before they can block the next opening. Two stallholders unfasten the low bench nearest the water and stand it on end. They have done this often enough to make room for one another without asking.

The rain strengthens. Across the square, roofs and foliage disappear behind descending water. The bamboo aqueduct spills at every joint. Somewhere a branch breaks, followed by a heavy sliding rustle and a splash below the houses. Everyone listens briefly for a second sound. When none comes, the hammering resumes.

By late afternoon the steps are emerging again. Fresh twigs lie wedged against the bridge, and a little bank of silt has formed behind a root in the square. Chickens begin scratching it before the puddle has drained. A man checks the lashings of the water crossing while his daughter holds his ankles from the platform.

Light returns through a break in the cloud, ordinary pale daylight, enough to show the distant ridge between two roofs. Steam rises from a cooking pot. Leaves drip into leaves. High in the village’s central tree, a bird pulls a loose fiber from an old binding and carries it under the eaves.
