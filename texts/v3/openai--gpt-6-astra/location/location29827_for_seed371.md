The hollow is just below the ridge, high enough that the clouds passing through it have already shed most of their rain. It occupies a bite in the mountainside no wider than a small church. Above it, the ridge rises in a steep, broken lip; below, the ground falls away so abruptly that there is no visible foreground beyond the last shrubs. In the dark, that edge has to be learned by the way the wind behaves. It comes up from underneath.

Almost everything that can hold a root is occupied.

Rhododendrons stand shoulder to shoulder along the sheltered wall, their trunks twisted low and their crowns driven flat toward the drop. Dwarf bamboo fills the intervals. Beneath both, ferns have crowded into cracks barely wider than their stems, and moss covers the humps of older ground in thick, dark cushions. There is very little room between the vegetation and the rock. A branch displaced by a gust catches three others; a shudder travels across the hollow before any individual plant can spring back.

The rock is pale, though little of that paleness is visible tonight. A recent slip has opened the uphill wall and left a raw face among the shrubs. Its edges are still sharp. Thin plates project from it, separated along the grain, and at its foot lie fragments with clean corners and a dusting of crushed mineral. Nothing has had time to stain them. Roots hang out of the fracture in dense, torn bundles, some still bearing clots of soil.

Part of the fallen ground landed intact. A slab of turf, large enough to stand on, rests at an angle against a boulder, carrying its ferns and young shrubs with it. The plants continue to grow sideways from their displaced patch of mountain. Their newest shoots have begun turning upward.

There is no stream. Water exists here in small, reluctant quantities: a bead beneath a leaf tip, a black seam under a stone, moisture pressed from moss when a branch strikes it. The cloud leaves a fine deposit on exposed surfaces and moves on. Under the shrubs, the soil is often dry enough to crumble. The richness of the vegetation depends on how little escapes the hollow—dead leaves held in root forks, mist caught on thousands of small surfaces, shade lasting through most of the day. Even now, when the air is only a few degrees above freezing, the deepest litter has the soft smell of decomposition.

The darkness is nearly complete. A low ceiling of cloud hides the stars, and the mountain blocks the distant valley lights. Occasionally the cloud thins enough for the broken rim to separate from the sky. Then the hollow acquires a few dimensions: the pale cut in the slope, the packed black crowns of the shrubs, a fern frond briefly silvered as it turns. A moment later these distinctions close again.

Sound gives a much larger account of the place.

Wind strikes the ridge with a sustained, coarse roar, but inside the hollow it arrives in blows. Bamboo canes knock together. Rhododendron leaves, thick and stiff, make a rattling unlike the softer rush of ordinary woodland. Loose grit runs down the fresh scar. Sometimes a stone drops through the roots, taps twice against the wall, and vanishes over the edge. Its last sound comes from surprisingly far below.

Under these close noises is a deeper, regular vibration.

Far down the mountain, beyond sight, a crushing plant works through the night. Its engines and conveyors cannot be seen from here, and there is no road or cable crossing the hollow. Nevertheless, the low machinery noise reaches the slope when the wind turns. It arrives stripped of its higher notes: a heavy, continuous rumble, briefly interrupted by the impact of a load entering the crusher. For several seconds the hollow seems to have a mechanical floor. Then a gust carries the sound away, leaving only the vegetation thrashing against itself.

There are no calls, no wingbeats, no movement independent of the weather. A cluster of leaves jerks upright and seems briefly purposeful, but it is only a bent stem coming free. Whatever animals use these elevations are absent or hidden tonight. The exposed litter has no fresh tracks. The new stone holds none at all.

The air changes direction without warning. A current descending from the ridge meets another rising along the cliff, and the shrubs are pressed one way, released, then struck from the other side. The sheltered pockets are small and temporary. Behind a boulder, fern tips remain still while the bamboo immediately above them bows almost to the ground. A handspan farther out, the cold would be difficult to bear.

The slip has altered more than the shape of the wall. It has opened a passage for wind that previously passed overhead. Plants grown tall in shelter now take its full force. Their roots still hold in deep old soil, but the soil itself ends at the fracture. Each hard gust flexes the hanging mats. Fine particles sift out beneath them.

Near the lower edge, a small rhododendron has fallen forward without coming loose. Its crown hangs over the drop, leaves turned inside out by the updraft. The exposed roots tighten and slacken. One root, stretched across a sharp stone, has begun to split.

For a while it holds.

Cloud pours across the ridge again, catching in the branches as droplets too small to hear. The crusher starts another load below. The bamboo rattles, the broken root gives a little farther, and a handful of dark soil falls out of the hollow into the unseen height beneath it.
