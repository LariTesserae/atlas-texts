The shelf is no larger than a village square, and it has not finished arriving.

At its outer edge, the ground thins into a lip of translucent stone. Beyond that lip there is cloud, then a long interval in which falling things become invisible, then the dark backs of mountains. Sometimes a gap opens far below and reveals a river bright enough to seem closer than it is. The air up here ought to be cold. Instead, warmth rises through the soles of your feet. Kneel, and the stone feels like a wall with a furnace on the other side.

Most of the shelf is bare: broad, softly bulging plates of gray mineral, separated by seams the color of raw mussel flesh. Nothing has been rounded by weather. The low knobs, the shallow hollows, the little overhang above the western pool—all are their original shapes. Even the gravel is not broken rock. Each piece has grown separately, and a few still stand on fine stalks that will eventually withdraw into the ground.

Water is everywhere without occupying very much space. It beads on the undersides of ledges. It trembles in hairline cracks. At intervals the whole surface darkens as moisture comes up through it, enough to wet a footprint but not enough to wash one away. There are three pools, none deeper than a knee. Their beds are smooth and unmarked, interrupted here and there by pale mineral buds.

The largest pool lies near the center. Its water tilts slightly toward the east, independent of the slope beneath it.

That is the first thing you notice that cannot be explained by the mountain.

The second is the rain. It falls from a low ceiling of cloud, but slows as it approaches the shelf. Drops hang briefly at face height, elongating, gathering tiny reflections of the ground. Then they descend the remaining distance with an audible tick. During a shower, thousands of these little impacts merge into a hard, rattling applause. The ground steams afterward, although the water itself is warm.

There are four structures here.

One is a shelter with a narrow doorway and a roof like a folded dark leaf. Inside are a bench, six empty hooks, and a square depression in the floor that stays perfectly dry. A coat hangs from one hook only when seen from outside. Step through the doorway and the hook is bare.

Two other structures are slender frames standing over unfinished patches of terrain. They have no visible footings. Their lower ends stop a finger’s width above the stone, and in that gap grains of mineral rise continuously, arranging themselves into little terraces before becoming solid. The frames make minute adjustments all day: a corner contracts, an upright leans, a crosspiece divides into two parallel lines. They work without moving parts you can follow.

The fourth structure is almost beneath notice—a white post at the edge, about waist-high, with a handle worn smooth by use. Pulling it does not open anything. It makes the entire shelf heavier for several minutes. The cloud presses flat beneath the perimeter, the pools become level, and every animal goes quiet.

No one has pulled it recently. Small winged creatures have built a nest inside the handle.

They are the most numerous animals, though at first they look like flakes shaken loose from the cloud. Each has four translucent wings and a thick amber body, with jointed legs folded beneath it. They fly in loose columns over the wet seams. Every few moments a column collapses: the creatures drop together, touch their mouths to the damp stone, and rise again. Their wings produce a shrill electrical whine that shifts in pitch as they turn.

Something hunts them from the pools.

You see an eye first, positioned where there seemed to be a bubble. Then a second eye opens farther away, and the clear shape connecting them becomes apparent. The animal is flat, nearly as wide as a dinner plate, with a fringe of swimming limbs and a mouth underneath. It waits just below the surface until a column of insects descends. Then the whole pool seems to jump. Water and predator rise together in a low dome, the mouth closes in the air, and everything falls back with a heavy slap.

There are more of these hunters than the pools appear able to hold. They overlap, transparent body over transparent body, their eyes moving independently through the stacked water. At dusk, when light comes up from the mineral seams, their organs show as soft violet knots.

Plants have found very little purchase. A patch of black reeds grows beside the shelter, no more than thirty stems, all bent away from the doorway. There are several thumb-sized cushions of waxy green growth on the western lip. Under one overhang, roots hang in the air without any visible plant above them. They comb moisture from the passing cloud. Tiny red animals travel along these roots, stopping to prise open droplets with their forelegs.

Most of the feeding happens elsewhere. A cream-colored film develops wherever fresh stone meets air. You can watch it appear: first a dullness, then a faint greasy sheen, then the arrival of grazers.

The grazers come out of the warm seams.

They have low, six-legged bodies covered in overlapping ceramic plates. The largest is the length of your hand. They scrape with paired teeth, leaving clean tracks that refill within an hour. Their shells clack against one another when they crowd a productive patch, though they seldom remain crowded for long. New feeding ground is always opening somewhere. A tremor passes through the shelf; a seam widens; a square yard of pale, wet rock lifts into view, and the animals begin to move toward it.

This makes the place seem busier than its emptiness should allow. There are long stretches where nothing stands higher than your ankle, but nearly every surface is being crossed, tasted, warmed, or quietly displaced.

The loudest sound comes from below.

Every twenty or thirty minutes, a deep hollow report rolls through the stone. You feel it in your jaw before you hear it. The pools flatten. The reeds snap upright. Loose droplets leap from ledges and hang for a moment in the dim air.

Afterward, the shelf has grown.

Usually the change is slight. A crack has gained a new margin. A shallow bowl is now deep enough to collect water. Once, a whole rounded lobe rises at the northern edge, glossy and faintly pink, pushing the cloud aside. For a few minutes you can see into it: dense threads crossing, bright specks traveling along them, darker material settling around a central void. Then opacity spreads inward from the surface. The lobe becomes stone. Water condenses on it. Before it has cooled, three of the plated grazers are climbing its side.

The frames respond with a low chord. One shortens by half its height.

Light never arrives directly. The sun is somewhere above the cloud roof, reduced to a broad, sourceless pallor. Near midday, wet surfaces gleam and the mountains below become briefly distinct. Later the sky darkens, and light starts showing through the newest ground. Thin seams shine amber. A pool acquires a green rim. Beneath the shelter, something pulses slowly enough that you may not notice it until you have watched through an entire breath.

The animals use these lights. The winged columns gather over amber fissures. The transparent hunters move toward green. Small, blunt-headed creatures emerge only beside the slow pulses, pushing themselves along on muscular forefins. They carry pebbles in their mouths and set them down in groups of three. By morning the pebbles have softened together into low porous ridges, with eggs tucked into the holes.

Around those ridges, the ground stops growing.

There are other arrests: the dry square inside the shelter, the patch beneath the white post, a narrow strip where someone once laid seven dark tiles. Everywhere else, elevation and distance remain provisional. A pool that was three strides from the doorway may be four strides away tomorrow, with no crack or disturbance between. The roots under the overhang lengthen to keep touching the same band of cloud.

Toward evening a stronger wind arrives. It drives warm mist across the shelf in low, fast sheets. The reeds beat against the shelter wall. Wings scream above the pools; grazers rattle over the fresh ground; the unseen hollow below sounds again, louder this time.

At the edge, a bead of water gathers on a new point of stone. It swells, catches the faint green light, and drops.

Behind it, another point begins to form.
