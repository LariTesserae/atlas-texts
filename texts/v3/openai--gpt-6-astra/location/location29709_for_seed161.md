The settlement occupies a hollow in the summit, no larger than a market square. From within it, almost nothing of the surrounding mountains is visible. There are walls, leaves, low doorways, the undersides of balconies; then, far overhead, a narrow strip of violet sky. Only at the western entrance does the ground fall away enough to reveal where the place actually stands: above successive ranges of bare mountains, above the weather moving through their valleys, at a height where a visitor has to stop halfway through a sentence to breathe.

The entrance has no gate. A waist-high stone, polished by hands, marks the last safe place to lean.

Beyond it, the old path descends across the mountain’s shoulder. There are no sharp peaks nearby. Everything sharp has long since broken off. The summit is a broad, exhausted swelling of stone, its exposed surfaces worn into shallow dishes and long, low ribs. Pebbles lie fitted into hollows almost exactly their own size. The rock has weathered past the picturesque stage: no extravagant spires, no precarious arches, only massive forms with very little left to surrender.

The hollow is warmer than it should be.

Heat rises through a seam beneath the houses, faint enough that nothing steams or smokes, steady enough that the lowest rooms remain comfortable through the bitter nights. People sit on the floor to mend baskets. Lizards flatten themselves along the bases of walls. In certain houses, sleeping platforms have been raised a hand’s breadth above the paving because direct contact becomes unpleasant after several hours.

Outside the hollow, there is scarcely a rooted thing. Inside, vegetation crowds every space left between the buildings.

Most of it is succulent: thick leaves in dusty green, branching stems with the texture of old cork, dense cushions of little overlapping scales. Some plants hang from wall pockets in heavy ropes. Others rise from jars with swollen trunks and crowns of narrow leaves that brush the ceilings. A pale climbing plant passes through three households, its main stem disappearing into masonry beside a communal oven. Nobody alive planted it. Each household tends the portion within reach.

There are flowers, though they are easy to miss in the dimness: small cream stars against the stone, dark red cups under thorny branches. A few larger blooms open only after the sky above the roofs has lost its color. Their scent collects in the lanes, sweet and faintly animal.

The greenery looks lavish until one looks at the soil. There is almost none. Roots enter cracks packed with sifted grit, powdered droppings, and the black crumbs of generations of discarded leaves. Every planting pocket has a rim to prevent its contents from spilling. Every rim has been repaired.

No water is exposed anywhere.

There is no well mouth, no trough, no sound of pouring. Moisture arrives in miserly ways: frost gathered from the outer stones before sunrise, occasional snow carried down from shaded clefts, a little seepage intercepted deep in the warm fissure. Covered vessels pass from hand to hand. Plants receive the last cloudy spoonful from washing a bowl. Their abundance is the result of long keeping, not plenty.

The houses are made from the mountain’s own fragments. Old blocks, worn round before they were ever built into anything, sit in courses of smaller packing stones. Their faces are greasy with touch. Here and there a doorway has been cut through a wall that was once the back of another house, so that the village seems to have grown inward as much as upward.

There are perhaps thirty households. They occupy so little ground that a person grinding meal can exchange news with someone weaving on the balcony opposite without either raising their voice—although both usually do.

For such a small place, it is loud.

Sparrows quarrel inside the roof thatch. Doves crowd the warmer ledges and clap their wings when someone opens a shutter. Insects rasp behind the plaster, especially near the heated floor seams. Somewhere a hand mill keeps up its gritty turn, interrupted by the knock of grain being shaken into the hole. Wooden sandals sound sharply on stone. A child calls from a roof and receives three answers from three different levels.

The sound has nowhere to go. It strikes the enclosing rock and returns softened but still crowded, so that even an empty passage carries the impression of company.

The animals know routes unavailable to people. Small brown rodents travel beneath the grain platforms; narrow snakes follow them into the walls. Beetles emerge from cracks around the ovens to gather spilled flour. At dusk, bats slip from the upper masonry one by one, dropping almost to head height before finding the opening above the roofs. Their departure is the quickest thing that happens here.

Most movement is slower. A shaded cloth lifts and settles. A woman shifts a clay jar along a shelf by the width of her thumb, bringing one branch into the light. Someone works a chipped iron edge against a whetstone. Smoke from a little dung fire spreads beneath a balcony until a change in the air draws it upward.

Light reaches the bottom of the hollow for only a short part of the day. The houses have narrow upper stories, but even so they overlap the sun’s path; woven awnings and fleshy leaves close much of what remains. Down in the lanes, color is muted. Red cloth looks brown. Skin catches a dull glow from doorways. The brightest objects are the pale upper leaves, visible against the opening to the sky.

People sometimes climb to the rim to dry grain or mend the outer windbreaks. They return with narrowed eyes and cold fingers. Below, the warm paving receives their feet.

Nothing here is untouched, and little is new. A stair tread has been turned over after wearing hollow, and its second face is now hollow too. Doorposts have shortened as their rotten ends were trimmed away. Baskets become screens, screens become kindling, ashes become part of the soil held behind a broken bowl.

Near the entrance, beside the polished leaning stone, a shrub grows from a crack no wider than a finger. Its branches have been trained flat against the wall so they do not obstruct the passage. The oldest branch is dead, but nobody has cut it away: it supports a shelf where the doves nest.

From that spot, one can hear the whole settlement behind one’s shoulder—the grinding, the birds, a tool being dropped, someone laughing—and look out over miles of empty stone.
