The district occupies a shallow depression, no wider than a few streets, with a retaining wall around it and a pale strip of sky above. You can see almost the whole place from the stairs: a pumping house, two long machine sheds, a square chimney, and the open ground between them. Nothing is tall enough to conceal the opposite wall. There are no distant suburbs. Beyond the coping lies flat, bare country, the same gray material as the yard but without paving joints.

The stairs are worn deepest near their edges. Their middle portions still bear a roughness that might once have kept boots from slipping. Below, the pavement has settled into broad, gentle hollows. It is not broken. Centuries of weight and vibration have given it the shape of something held in a palm.

Water occupies only the lowest seams. It makes dark threads between blocks, collects under valve housings, hangs in clear beads beneath the iron handrail. Most surfaces are dry. The damp has a precise boundary: a pipe may be black and wet along its underside while its upper half carries pale dust. Nothing drips often enough to establish a rhythm. The drops hang swollen, trembling in the noise.

The noise is already immense at the top of the stairs.

It has no obvious loudest source. There is the bass pressure of machinery, a hard rushing sound like gravel moving through a chute, and, above both, countless thin notes too closely packed to separate. The handrail sings against your fingers. In the open yard the sound seems to come up through the soles of your shoes; beside a building it presses outward from the masonry. Closing your ears removes very little of it.

At first there appear to be almost no animals.

A gray creature rests beside the pumping-house door. Another lies halfway under a freight scale. They are low and jointed, about the length of a boot, with rounded backs that resemble cast-iron inspection covers. Their legs fold completely beneath them. Neither acknowledges your arrival. Farther off, a white, many-legged animal occupies the dry center of an old basin. There is a considerable stretch of empty pavement around each.

Then you look into a ventilation grille.

The darkness behind it has eyes all the way down.

Not pairs of eyes in a crowd, but eyes arranged at intervals through a dense, interlocking depth of bodies. Some are polished black. Some are milky and lidless. Between them, plates of skin slide over other plates, feelers pass through sockets in neighboring animals, and pale throats vibrate with the high component of the district’s roar. There is no vacant space into which any one creature could withdraw. Each fits the creatures around it as closely as stones fit in a wall.

Every grille is like this.

The machine sheds have windows of thick wired glass, opaque not with dirt but with bodies pressed against their inner faces. Along the eaves, the shapes you took for brackets are gripping animals. The grooves in the brickwork are lined with smaller ones, their backs flush with the mortar. A drain cover contains such a fine mesh of translucent legs that the metal underneath can scarcely be seen.

The open ground remains almost empty. The abundance is inward.

There is no leaf, no root, no tuft of grass where the steps meet the wall. Green appears only as a stain on copper. Even the puddles are clear, their bottoms clean enough to show individual grains of pale grit. Whatever feeds this multitude arrives by pipe.

You can follow the main supply around the district: a thick conduit mounted at waist height on ceramic saddles, entering each building before emerging on the other side. Its joints have been polished by maintenance. At one inspection port, cloudy fluid passes behind a small circle of glass. Suspended in it are bright flakes that turn edge-on and vanish, then flash again.

The animals nearest the port have mouthparts the color of those flakes.

There must be furnaces somewhere, but this is the cool end of the works. Waste heat has been taken away before the fluid reaches the yard. The pumping-house walls feel like stone in deep shade. A metal bench beside the door is cold through clothing, and the air smells of wet iron, chalk, and the faintly sweet interior of a shell.

Inside the sheds, the distinction between machinery and occupancy becomes difficult.

Large flywheels turn behind ranks of upright housings. Each housing bears a number, an oil cup, and a cluster of breathing apertures. The drive belts are ordinary belts, stitched and darkened with use. What receives their motion is less ordinary. Through gaps in the guards you can see muscular folds tightening around shafts, hard teeth engaging gears, rows of minute animals passing a load from one to another without changing their positions.

The factories do not keep animals. The animals are carrying out an industrial process, and the buildings provide the spacing, lubrication, and restraint it requires.

No shift ends.

The wheels turn, yet the white mark on one rim always appears at the same height. A hanging chain trembles without swinging farther to either side. Behind the glass, the bright flakes continue to tumble through the supply fluid, but you cannot follow a particular flake out of sight. Look away and look back: the same three flashes occupy the same triangle.

Movement is plentiful here. Alteration is absent.

The worn stairs will not wear further. The rust beneath a bolt has reached the exact breadth it will always have. A bead of water under the handrail strains toward falling, its narrow neck lengthening and thickening with the vibration, but it retains itself. The district is not preserved against its work. It has settled into the work so completely that nothing remains for the work to change.

Even the animals possess this finished quality. None looks young. None looks old. Their shells are scuffed at every point of contact and immaculate where nothing touches them.

Near the center of the yard stands a public fountain, a low octagonal construction with four iron spouts. Only one carries water. Its stream is narrow and unbroken, entering a small hole in the stone before it can form a pool. Around the unused spouts sit hundreds of tiny white animals, too small to notice from the stairs. They face the water with their legs tucked under them. Their open mouths contribute a clear, steady whistle to the general thunder.

There is room beside the fountain to stand.

From there, the district is all visible again: the empty paving, the closed sheds, the little lengths of pipe crossing bare walls. Ordinary daylight lies evenly over it. No smoke darkens the square chimney. The sky offers no sign of approaching weather.

Under that modest arrangement of roofs and drains, behind every accessible surface, the animals continue in their uncountable numbers. You feel their labor in the stone beneath you. Nothing comes out into the yard except the few creatures already resting there, and the noise, which fills all the space they have left.
