The chamber lies just beneath the desert pavement. Above it are three meters of salt, a layer of black shale, and the foundations of a building whose purpose has been forgotten. You enter through a square opening behind the building’s only standing wall. The ladder is warm all the way down.

At the bottom, the space widens to the size of a small railway hall. Its ceiling is low around the edges and rises in the middle, where a slab of shale has bent upward without breaking. Everything is crowded. Mineral blades descend between thick columns; shelves overlap shelves; narrow passages turn sideways through clusters of crystals the color of smoked glass. There is scarcely anywhere to stand without touching something.

The light comes from little orange bulbs enclosed in wire cages. Most are buried deep inside the formations. Their wires enter the stone at one point and emerge several meters away, as though the minerals grew around an installation that had never been switched off. Each bulb illuminates its own cramped volume: a red seam, a hooked claw, the underside of a pale shelf. Between these pockets, distances are difficult to judge.

The chamber is very loud.

A low mechanical note fills the floor and enters through the soles of your feet. Above it are a metallic shrilling, a dense abrasive hiss, and something like thousands of small teeth being drawn across combs. There are no intervals. Your ears keep expecting a hammer to fall, a belt to slip, a mechanism to complete its cycle. Nothing does. After a while, you hear your own breathing as an irregularity in an otherwise seamless material.

Near the ladder stands the only substantial machine: a cast-iron compressor on four concrete feet. Its flywheel is sharply visible. So are the individual teeth of its drive gear. There is no blur, no visible movement, yet heat rises from the cylinder head, and the oil in the inspection window holds a deep, steady dimple.

Someone has removed the pressure gauge and screwed a brass plug into its socket. The plug is polished from handling.

Beyond the compressor, the ground consists of angular chips that have never settled comfortably against one another. Every fracture looks recent. Edges fine enough to split a fingernail remain intact even beneath the ladder, where boots must have landed for decades. The salt has no rounded corners. Shale flakes project from the walls with the fragile crispness of newly broken pottery.

Nothing here acquires a patina.

An old boot print beside the concrete footing is perfectly legible, down to a cut across one heel. Beside it, a dropped washer balances on its rim. Dust does not gather in either.

The animals are difficult to distinguish from the crowding minerals until you find an eye.

There are many eyes.

Small flattened creatures occupy the seams between salt plates, their translucent bodies pressed so thin that the orange light passes through them. Their jaws are open against the stone. Farther in, jointed animals hang in ranks beneath the ceiling, each attached by two hind feet, each holding its forelimbs slightly apart. They have black, lacquered shells and soft amber throats. Every throat bears the same shallow indentation.

The shrilling comes from them.

They make it without a visible tremor. If you hold a scrap of paper near one, the paper vibrates violently, though the animal’s throat remains as still as the mineral behind it.

On the floor are heavier creatures, the size and approximate shape of folded coats. Their backs carry overlapping ceramic plates. Short legs fill the shadows underneath. Some have broad grinding mouths pressed against veins of dark ore; others hold smaller animals between fine, comb-like mouthparts. None withdraws when approached. None blinks.

The chamber contains an abundance of feeding, but no meal is finished.

In a recess behind the compressor, a pale animal is halfway out of its old shell. Its new limbs are soft and faintly luminous. There is a pencil mark on the wall beside it, then another lower down, accompanied by dates thirty-one years apart. Both marks indicate the same position.

Vegetation survives in three or four places where the electrical conduits enter the floor. It grows as sparse bundles of copper-colored needles, stiff and dry, with tiny dark cups at their tips. The roots spread over the insulation rather than into the rock. A broken conduit exposes a green conductor, and the nearest plant has threaded one root neatly into its stranded core.

There is no smell of leaves. The air smells of hot porcelain, powdered salt, and the inside of an old electric motor.

Water has left almost no evidence here. No drip marks cross the ceiling. No stains descend the walls. A shallow metal dish rests beneath a valve on the compressor, but its interior is white and absolutely dry. Whatever once passed through the machine did not need to condense.

The remaining human works are modest: two steps cut into a difficult ledge, a short handrail, ceramic terminals screwed to a board. There is a little shelter made from corrugated sheet in one corner, too low to stand in. Inside are a stool and a ledger swollen with mineral grit. Its pages record temperatures, all nearly identical. Later entries omit the numbers and use ditto marks. The final page is filled with them.

From the shelter you can see the whole central chamber, though never all its inhabitants. Each surface conceals another occupied surface. Pale limbs fill cracks behind the bulbs. Armored backs lie under the iron pipes. Far overhead, in the lifted fold of shale, something broad has spread itself against the ceiling, its ribs fitting the rock’s shallow corrugations. Its mouth surrounds the end of an exhaust duct.

The machine supplies heat. The rooted needles take current. The hanging animals sound their unbroken note. Along the ore seams, mouths remain engaged with the mineral. Everything appears to be receiving exactly what it requires.

You are the only thing that seems poorly adjusted: shifting your weight, swallowing, wanting the noise to stop or change.

When you leave, the ladder knocks against its brackets. The sound is startlingly distinct.

Below, the little chamber continues at its single temperature, with its fresh-cut edges and its crowded, unfinished appetites. The pale animal stays halfway into its new body. The washer stands on edge. Around the compressor’s motionless wheel, the air is warm enough to shimmer.
