The city occupies a high basin, above the cloud layer but beneath another weather.

From its western rim you can see most of it: a shallow bowl of dark stone, perhaps twenty kilometers across, divided into enormous empty courts. Low walls mark the divisions. Here and there a stairwell descends into the ground, or a square chimney rises no higher than a person. There are no towers, no wires, no visible traffic. At first the place looks unfinished, an industrial foundation abandoned before the buildings went up.

Then you notice the wear on the stairs.

Each flight is hollowed deeply in the middle. The thresholds have been replaced several times, different generations of stone fitted inside one another. Drain covers are polished around their edges. This is an old city. Most of its construction is beneath your feet, and most of what lies beneath your feet is also empty.

The heat comes through your soles.

It is greatest in the open courts, where the paving is almost too hot to touch. The air above them moves in thick, slow folds. Yet everything is wet. Water stands in the seams between slabs, slips down the stairwells, beads along the underside of railings. On the basin’s southern wall, whole faces of rock weep continuously without any visible spring. Their surfaces are rounded and finely fluted, the result of water taking nearly the same paths for an immense length of time.

There is little loose rubble. Whatever could readily fall has fallen; whatever could dissolve has gone into the drains. The surviving stone has the settled shape of knuckles.

Above the basin hangs a low, opaque canopy, pale underneath and black where its folds overlap. It does not move with the wind. It is not quite cloud. From time to time a fine luminous seam opens in it, illuminating a strip of pavement with the weak copper light of a furnace seen through dirty glass. The seam closes. Your eyes readjust.

There is enough light to see across a court, but not enough to make out the far side of the basin clearly. Distances lose contrast rather than detail: a doorway remains a doorway until it becomes indistinguishable from the wall around it.

Nothing grows in the joints.

There are no roots lifting the paving, no reeds around the cistern mouths. A few drains carry a translucent skin that wrinkles when water passes beneath it. If disturbed, it slowly draws itself back across the opening. It might be a colony of simple organisms or merely something precipitated from the water. In the municipal galleries there are old enamel notices requiring that this skin be skimmed off before it reaches a marked thickness. Long-handled copper rakes still hang beside some of the sumps.

The city’s work continues below.

A descending passage takes you into a hall large enough to contain several streets. The ceiling is supported by piers so far apart that each seems isolated in its own portion of darkness. Between them run open channels, maintenance walkways, and broad, dry floors sloping almost imperceptibly toward circular grates. There are pipes of riveted iron, glazed ceramic, and a gray metal that sweats even where the air seems dry. Some pipes enter the rock without valves or joints. Others have elaborate bypasses, little staircases, inspection windows clouded from within.

Every few minutes a mechanism turns somewhere far away.

You hear the beginning of its effort: a low strain, one heavy contact, a diminishing sequence of clicks. Water rises in a nearby channel by the width of a finger. A float moves. Nothing else appears to happen.

It takes time here to recognize activity. A basin empties over an hour. A hot wall darkens as moisture works through it. A pressure door eases outward against its counterweight, stays open for half a minute, and closes with scarcely a sound. The machinery is enormous, but it does not hurry. Much of it is driven by the water it is arranging.

There are addresses on the piers.

Some have been repainted so often that their letters stand proud of the surface. Beside them are evidence of ordinary occupation: a tiled washing recess, a cupboard with two hooks, a counter worn low in one place by resting elbows. Narrow doors lead into rooms built along the hall’s outer wall. The rooms have beds of porous stone and vents at floor level. Shallow shelves hold cups turned upside down against the damp.

Between one cluster of rooms and the next lies a walk of several hundred meters.

A city of absences, but not a ruined one. The railings are sound. Many of the valves have fresh grease. At one junction a lamp burns behind an amber shield, and beneath it a maintenance ledger lies open under a glass weight. The most recent entry is still wet.

Outside, the basin changes slowly with the pressure below it. Water appears in a court that was dry that morning, first as dark seams, then as a continuous sheet. It never grows deep. At a certain level the surface begins to draw itself toward one corner, although that corner is visibly uphill.

This is what the surveyors’ marks are for.

Little brass arrows have been embedded in the paving, indicating the direction water will travel rather than the direction of descent. In some places the two agree. Elsewhere a gutter climbs a wall, crosses a lintel, and pours into a receiving pipe above a doorway. The oldest stone channels follow these routes without explanation. Newer ironwork has been made to accommodate them.

There is a point near the center of the basin where water stands vertically in a narrow slot between two walls. It trembles there, clear and hot, three meters high. A ladder gives access to its upper edge. Someone has built a small shelter beside it, with a stool and a ceramic cup.

The rare animals gather where the water cools.

You may find one under a stair: a flat, pale creature with six short limbs and a head almost indistinguishable from its body. It rests with its belly against a damp patch, absorbing what it needs. When your shadow falls across it, it lifts itself onto the tips of its feet and waits. Its skin bears the same shallow parallel grooves as the runoff stone.

Occasionally there is a shell in a drain basket, thin as a fingernail and much larger than the animal that seems to have left it. No one appears to remove these. Water passes through their broken chambers with a faint, reedy note.

That is often the loudest sound aboveground.

Later, the overhead canopy lowers. The upper rim disappears first, then the farther chimneys. Hot droplets settle on your face without falling visibly through the air. Metal acquires a milky film. In the empty courts, the brass arrows vanish beneath a fresh sheen of water.

Below, the mechanisms begin their longer cycle. A vibration comes through the paving, gentle enough that you feel it most clearly in your teeth. Doors open deep in the rock. The gutters fill.

For a while the entire city seems occupied by the passage of water: water entering rooms set aside for it, waiting behind gates, crossing vast halls, climbing toward reservoirs whose dark undersides you mistook for ceilings.

Then the pressure falls. The trembling stops.

Across the court, a single doorway releases a breath of cooler air. There is a dry footprint on its threshold, already filling with moisture.
