The upland begins above the haze, on a broad shelf of pale mineral that extends for miles without quite becoming a plain. Its surface rises in shallow, square-edged swells. Farther away, these lift into terraces, then into the unfinished shoulders of mountains. The light is ordinary daylight: a warm white sun, blue distance, shadows with distinct edges. You can see a very long way.

Nothing has rounded the ground. Every fracture retains its original lip. Where a slab has lifted, its underside is exposed as a clean band of lavender-gray crystal, sometimes taller than a tree. There is no rubble beneath it. The smaller fragments remain fitted to the larger ones, separated by narrow black spaces, as though the land has opened its joints without yet deciding how far to move.

It is warm underfoot. Warm, too, in the shade.

Across most of the shelf there is only stone and air. A dark seam travels toward the horizon, branches once, and disappears behind a rise. Several miles to the east stands a grove. Another occupies the rim of a distant hollow. Between them, the bare country is so extensive that their abundance seems improbable: dense, generous crowns suspended in an almost vacant landscape.

Up close, the first grove has the richness of an orchard left entirely to itself.

Its plants grow from the dark seam. Their lower parts are thick, polished columns, green-black where shaded and bronze where the sun strikes them. Above these, broad leaves overlap in tiers. Some are the size of hands; others could cover a doorway. The newest are pale chartreuse and thin enough to show the lattice of conductive fibers inside. Older leaves are deep green, with copper margins and undersides the color of tarnished silver.

There is no soil around the stems. Their roots enter the stone through fine, precisely fitted apertures. A few roots travel along the surface first, flattened into ribbons, making right-angle turns at crystal boundaries. Where several plants share a fissure, their root systems join into a single dark strip.

The ground beneath them is clean. Nothing has fallen.

These plants contain no sap. Split tissue would show a flexible mineral mesh threaded with a transparent, oily compound, but there are no split leaves here, no broken branches. Their chemistry carries charge and rearranges carbon without water. The green is genuine, the growth is genuine, and yet the grove has something of the scale and concentrated purpose of heavy industry. Its trunks stand over deposits deep in the plateau. Its roots reach the contacts between those deposits. Each crown spreads a large working surface into the light.

In the shade, you can make out bands of tiny plates along the stems. They resemble overlapping bark until a change in angle reveals their exact spacing. There are hundreds on each trunk. At every junction, a bead of dull red mineral holds the same faint heat.

Nothing was assembled here. There are no foundations, fastenings, roads, or discarded parts. The apparent machinery belongs to the organism, and the organism belongs to the seam.

A low tone is audible inside the grove. It is quieter than distant ventilation, almost too low to hear as a sound. You notice it in the bones of the face when you stand between two mature trunks. A few paces out onto the open rock, it is gone.

Beyond the grove lies a wide depression. Its bottom is flat and ivory-colored, unmarked by channels. Angular shelves project from its sides at different heights. On one shelf grows a second kind of vegetation: soft-looking, densely packed fronds, dark blue-green, with every tip turned slightly toward the sun. From a distance they suggest a field of ferns. Close to them, the illusion changes. Their edges are smooth and substantial, like very thin ceramic. They hold their positions in the still air.

The hollow has never held a lake. Its shape comes from material rising around an area that did not rise with it. That distinction is visible everywhere here. The landscape has been lifted, parted, expanded, and exposed, but scarcely worn. Even the finest textures are textures of formation: parallel striations where a crystal lengthened, little hexagonal pits where a gas escaped, broad satin faces that were once pressed against other faces.

At the western edge of the depression, a ridge stops halfway through emerging. One end is still a low crease. The other stands high enough to cast a long wedge of shade. Between them, successive folds show the whole process plainly.

It has held that shape for longer than a weathered mountain elsewhere might survive.

Here, emergence has reached an equilibrium. Pressure from below meets the weight above at exactly the point that leaves the folds open. The electrical difference across the buried seams persists. The groves draw on it without exhausting it, converting the same gentle supply of light and mineral energy into the maintenance of their enormous leaves. Their pale tips remain pale. Their mature crowns neither thicken nor thin.

This is a place in which beginning can be a lasting condition.

There are no insects among the fronds, no birds above the hollows, no marks on the ground except those made by the ground itself. The air carries a faint hot-metal smell near the roots. Elsewhere it has almost no scent. Even beneath the broadest canopy, where the foliage closes overhead and the light turns green, there is no dampness, no softness underfoot, no hidden rustling.

From a higher terrace, the groves resolve into small, widely separated patches. Their crowns occupy the points where dark lines intersect, each lush island surrounded by miles of naked mineral. Beyond them are more shelves, more incomplete ridges, and pale summits whose sharp faces catch the afternoon light.

The country looks newly exposed. Nothing seems missing from it.

In the nearest grove, a single leaf projects beyond the others, brilliantly lit along its copper edge. Beneath it, the shadow falls on an unblemished slab. Both have been there a very long time.
