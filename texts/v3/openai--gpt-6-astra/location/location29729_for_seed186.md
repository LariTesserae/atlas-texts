The plateau lies so high that most weather happens below it.

Along its southern edge, cloud moves through the lower valleys with the slow, continuous folding of something being kneaded. Lightning occasionally reveals the undersides of the cliffs. From above, it makes no sound. You see a white branching deep in the cloud, a momentary depth, and then the edge returns to darkness.

There is very little skyglow here. The stars are sharp but do not illuminate the ground. What light the plateau has comes from beneath its vegetation: a dull green in split roots, a blue point at the tip of a frozen leaf, the brief copper glow of an animal opening its mouth.

At first it seems to be an empty expanse of black stone.

Then your eyes adjust, and almost everything you had taken for stone turns out to be growing.

The vegetation stays low. It spreads in thick, overlapping mats, following the contours so closely that it preserves the look of the terrain beneath it. There are leaf fields soft as brushed fur, stands of rigid black needles no higher than an ankle, and broad, fleshy growths folded into the hollows of weathered slabs. Their upper surfaces are dry. A hand pressed between them would find cold moisture and densely interwoven stems, some fine as hair, some as thick as a wrist.

Nothing rises very far into the wind. A plant that lifts a branch above the general surface loses it before the branch can harden. The few tall growths occupy cracks, where they descend rather than ascend: long, richly divided fronds hanging into sheltered fissures, their tips disappearing into a green-black depth.

The land is lush in this downward direction.

Between the ridges are basins several miles across, shallow and open, with barely a boulder to interrupt their sweep. The ridges themselves have broad, worn backs. Frost has been working at them for a very long time, loosening a grain here, opening a seam there, but the loose material seldom travels far. Roots catch it. Mats grow across it. A broken slab becomes a slightly higher part of the same continuous living surface.

There are no paths, walls, cairns, or clearings. From any height, the plateau looks unvisited.

Yet, beneath your feet, it is full of machinery.

The first sign is a regularity that weather should not possess. A patch of leaves closes in six successive strips. A pause follows. Farther away, another patch closes in exactly the same order. Somewhere under the roots, a hard object engages another with a tiny click.

The sound carries through the ground rather than the air.

Down in the fissures grow mineral organs: wheels of dark cartilage edged with iron, nested cylinders, chambers lined with a flexible glass. Some belong to plants. Others are occupied by small pale animals that graze their inner surfaces and keep the passages clear. Root pressure drives them. Temperature differences drive them. The electrical charge accumulating on the exposed needle fields is carried downward through wet tissue and stored in thick, buried knots.

These organs pump warmth from deep cracks, compress thin air, separate trace metals from groundwater. Across a basin, thousands of them operate together. Whole hillsides switch from gathering charge to spending it.

You can watch the change move through the vegetation, though there is little to hear: a darkening band, a slight inclination of leaves, frost loosening from one slope while it thickens on the next.

No single organism contains the whole mechanism. Roots graft to roots. Animals carry replacement tissues between fissures. A pump that dies becomes a passage through which another species feeds its own tubes. Some assemblies have been growing in the same place longer than the cliffs have had their present faces.

There is remarkably little water.

The ground feels damp only in protected places, beneath a curled leaf or on the lee side of a stone. Most of the plateau’s moisture arrives when cloud rises against the escarpment. Fine ice drifts over the lip and catches in the needle fields. The plants comb it from the air. For a few hours the ridges acquire pale borders, visible even in the dark.

Then the needles fold, taking their frost underground.

At intervals, the buried systems release a little heat. Narrow pockets thaw. Water beads along root channels, runs for a few yards, and vanishes into another bed of absorbent tissue. The largest pools are scarcely wider than an outstretched body. They occupy old hollows in the rock, black and unexpectedly deep, surrounded by such dense growth that their banks overhang.

Their surfaces sometimes sink all at once.

A pool will lower by a finger’s width without a ripple as something below draws from it. Several minutes later, another pool on a different part of the slope rises. The relationship is precise but difficult to follow. Some hollows fill only when an animal steps into them; others close under a skin of transparent growth at the approach of a storm.

Life is abundant, but it seldom presents a whole body to view.

The leaf mats lift over moving backs. Six or eight eyes pass beneath a translucent frond. White jointed creatures stand inside the root chambers, turning slowly as they clean the glass. In sheltered cracks, small grazers hang by their feet and crop the downward-growing forests. Their fur grows around trapped pockets of air, making each animal look larger and less substantial than it is.

The predators are flatter.

They wait spread beneath the vegetation, matching its warmth, with limbs extended along the channels their prey uses. When one strikes, a few square feet of greenery tighten, settle, and become still. Nearby animals continue feeding. Alarm travels as a chemical change through the roots; it may take several minutes to reach them.

Above this dense, low world there is almost nothing.

Occasionally a great glider crosses a basin. Its wings are too broad for the body you glimpse between them, and it flies so slowly that it seems suspended. It searches for the faint heat leaking from active pumping beds. When it finds one, it descends, places a long feeding tube through the mat, and stands with its wings still open while warm fluid passes into it.

Several smaller creatures emerge to feed on the droplets left around the puncture. By the time the glider lifts away, the hole has begun to seal.

The cold makes exposed movement costly. Most activity takes place inside something else: under leaves, within roots, in the hollow of an animal’s insulating coat. Even the wingbeats are nearly inaudible in the thin air. A sound close by can be startlingly intimate—the brittle touch of claws on ice, the faint suction of a feeding tube being withdrawn—while an entire slope changes state in silence.

During the deepest part of the long night, the plateau draws down its stored charge.

Blue lights retreat into the ground. The small grazers crowd into warm fissures, and the leaf fields pull tight against the stone. Far below, cloud continues to turn through the valleys.

Then, in one basin, a line of frost breaks.

It breaks without a crack, softening along a buried channel. A little vapor rises and immediately freezes into glittering grains. Nearby, the folded plants open one after another. Animals begin to move beneath them.

The awakening travels slowly uphill. From a distant ridge it would look like a broad patch of darkness becoming slightly darker, spreading across an otherwise motionless country.

By the time it reaches the crest, the first basin is already resting again.
