Nine paces across, perhaps twelve along the diagonal: a small court caught between the backs of three buildings, high enough on the mountain that clouds sometimes pass beneath its drain.

No street opens into it. Access is through a maintenance door, flush with the wall and the same pale gray as everything around it. From the rooms above, it would look like an interruption in the building plan—a square of exposed mountain that the city had elected not to cover. There is no sign, no bench, no reason to come here unless something needs attention.

The mountain occupies the north corner. Its rock is blue-black, freshly cut, with pale seams that end abruptly at the faces left by excavation. The edges have not yet softened. Thin splinters lie where the stone meets the paving, each with a bright fracture surface and, underneath, a damp patch exactly its own shape. A few pieces have split again since falling. At this elevation, a little water is enough to continue the work of the cutters.

Most of the court is empty. There are two low access housings, a narrow strip of metal grating, and a circular depression no wider than a dinner plate. Everything else is paving, wet rock, or air.

Yet the place is rarely still.

Water emerges from the mountain along a nearly horizontal seam. Not a spring, not quite seepage: a line of droplets continually assembling, swelling, and letting go. They fall only a hand’s breadth onto a stone ledge, so there is almost no sound. Below the ledge, water spreads into a transparent skin. Here and there it freezes from the edges inward, leaving small dark windows through which the remaining liquid can be seen moving.

The buildings keep their own surfaces a fraction warmer. Their walls wear condensation instead of frost, thousands of tiny beads held apart by a finish too smooth to feel with a fingertip. At intervals, a vertical band turns dull, and all the water on it runs downward at once. The band clears. Beads begin forming again.

This happens on different walls at different times.

The paving receives the runoff without becoming slippery. Its shallow grooves change slowly, bringing themselves into alignment with each new trickle. Watch one tile for several minutes and a channel that had angled toward the rock will straighten toward the drain. Nothing lifts visibly; no mechanism clicks. The surface simply acquires a slightly different fall. Dirt collects along the abandoned courses, making faint brown lines like marks left by a receding puddle.

There is very little dirt.

No moss thickens the joints. No green stains the damp stone. The grit consists of mineral dust, fragments of sealant, a few pale fibers shed by work clothes. Even the sheltered corner behind the larger access housing is bare. It contains an old screw, three grains of black glass, and a curl of ice that survives through the brightest part of the day.

Above, the gap between the buildings is narrow but not dark. High-altitude daylight enters cleanly, revealing every scratch. For a short time around noon, direct sun reaches the court and lays a sharp white rectangle across the paving. The wet places shine. The dry ones look almost chalky. The rock remains dark enough to seem like a hole cut through the surrounding construction.

Beyond the opening, only pieces of the city are visible: the underside of a bridge, four windows, a rail along the edge of a roof. Once in a while a transit cabin passes across the gap. Seen from below, it is a long matte shape with no wheels, no cable, and no perceptible wake. Its shadow crosses the rock faster than the eye expects. By the time one looks up, it has gone.

The city’s occupied spaces are elsewhere, on the outward faces of the buildings, where the view opens over the valleys. This court sits behind their insulation, their water recovery systems, their lift shafts. It is close to thousands of people without offering much evidence of them. Occasionally there is the pressure change of a distant door closing. Once, a muted sequence of footsteps travels through the grating and stops.

Wind rarely reaches the paving directly. It skims the upper opening, stripping loose frost from the roof rails. The crystals descend in brief, bright showers. Some melt against the warm wall. Others land on the rock and remain distinct, six-sided and delicate, until the next wash of water folds them into the ice.

When a cloud rises around the buildings, the court loses its upper boundaries. The bridge disappears first, then the windows. Droplets gather on exposed corners. The atmosphere has the mineral smell of cold stone wetted in a sealed room, though the room has no roof. Nothing is distant enough to become picturesque. Everything is within reach: the wet wall, the sharp rock, the narrow black slots of the drain.

Under the lip of one access housing, a spider has made a loose web. It is the only web in the court. Most days its owner remains concealed beside a warm joint in the casing. There is little for it to catch except the occasional tiny fly brought out through the maintenance door. Several threads carry drops much larger than the spider’s body. During the wall’s drainage cycle, they tremble but hold.

The systems alter their behavior as the cold deepens. Runoff comes in shorter pulses. The drain’s rim warms before water reaches it, clearing a dark circle through the frost. Beneath the grating, recovered water travels inward through pipes whose surfaces stay dry. A soft tick comes from the exposed mountain. A splinter has loosened, or ice has found another fraction of space inside a crack.

By late afternoon, the sun has left, but reflected light still fills the court. The pale walls make the fresh ice visible well into evening. Water continues to move under it. One narrow route closes; another opens along the stone.

By morning, a new flake of rock will lie on the paving. The channels will bend around it until someone comes through the gray door and sweeps it away.
