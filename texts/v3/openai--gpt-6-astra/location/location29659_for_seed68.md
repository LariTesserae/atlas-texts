The place is no larger than a few city blocks, enclosed by the blind backs of workshops. From the street, you enter through a passage beneath a machine shop, following two rails set into the paving. The passage is dark enough that its far end looks white. Then you come out into clear daylight, cool air, and a noise so broad and steady that for a moment it seems to belong to the sky.

Three ventilator towers stand in the open ground. They are widely spaced, pale green, and not particularly tall: brick plinths, riveted housings, cowls turned away from the prevailing wind. Their fans revolve with a heavy, measured beat. Smaller sounds collect inside it—the tick of cooling metal, a loose grille, the occasional shriek of a belt in the buildings around the perimeter. You can hear birds, but only when they are close.

There is a great deal of unoccupied space.

A loading platform faces an empty apron of concrete. Beyond it lies a stretch of pale gravel, then a low, newly broken ridge, then another paved strip with nothing on it except a handcart. The buildings are urban in their insistence: party walls, numbered doors, cables drawn in straight lines, windows placed for work rather than views. Yet their backs have withdrawn from one another as though leaving room for something that has not arrived.

The greenery occupies distinct territories. One flight of exterior stairs is almost entirely lost beneath a climbing vine; the next wall is bare. A bed of enormous rhubarb-like leaves fills the space between two foundation piers, dark and glossy beneath their chalky bloom. Ferns grow shoulder-high in a disused inspection trench. Between these luxuriant patches are many yards of clean, dry ground. From above, the plants would look like separate green furnishings in a large, sparsely arranged room.

Water comes mostly from the buildings.

The workshops discharge warm air through buried pipes, and the pipes pass beneath the cooler yard. Moisture gathers on their inner walls and reaches the surface through little ceramic outlets. Each outlet serves a patch of growth. Some give no more than a drop every few seconds. Some remain dry all afternoon and begin to weep at dusk. There is one shallow basin beside the western tower, scarcely wider than a wash tub, where the overflow has worn a dark mark down the concrete.

Away from these places, the earth is powdery. Rain falls occasionally, enough to bring out the smell of brick and make the rails shine, but there are no puddles that survive long. The leaves do not depend on rain. Their roots follow the pipework, making a second, less orderly system beneath the first.

The yard stands only a little above the surrounding streets. Nevertheless, the ground at its center is rising.

It does so slowly enough that no one watches for movement. A surveyor comes twice a year and takes measurements from brass studs in the walls. Every few winters, workers lift and reset a length of track. The western drain has been cut lower three times. Across the center of the yard, the fresh ridge stands about knee-high, composed of close-grained grey material that resembles stone until you knock two pieces together. Then it rings like thick crockery.

Its broken faces are sharp. There is no soil in the cracks yet, no softened edge, no old lichen. Thin flakes lie where they have sprung free, showing darker grey underneath. In bright weather the ridge has the unfinished look of something removed too soon from a mold.

No one agrees on what lies beneath it. The oldest workshop records call the area a settling yard, and the name persists despite the measurements. Deliveries are still addressed to the Lower Settling Yard. The man who resets the track keeps a pile of the ringing fragments beside his shed, sorted by thickness, because they make excellent packing pieces.

Life uses the new ground without waiting for an explanation.

Sparrows dust themselves along its dry foot. Beetles shelter under the flakes. There are sleek brown rats around the warm foundations, too well fed to be particularly furtive, and small lizards on a south-facing sheet of corrugated metal that catches an hour of sun after lunch. Moths crowd the shaded mouths of ducts. Where the condensation runs, snails gather in such numbers that the maintenance workers carry a soft brush for clearing the valve handles.

The birds have learned the ventilation schedule. When a fan slows for inspection, starlings descend onto its grille to pick at the insects caught there. They arrive before the blades have quite stopped. When the motor starts again, the whole flock lifts together, makes one tight circuit of the yard, and settles on a cable.

Most motion here is small and repeated. A leaf shakes continuously in an exhaust current. A strip of canvas above a loading door lifts, hangs, and falls. The same handcart moves from one patch of shade to another over the course of a day. People cross the open space at an ordinary pace, wearing thick work clothes, carrying a bearing or a lunch tin. They speak close to one another’s ears.

The light is surprisingly generous. None of the surrounding buildings is high enough to close out the sky, and the pale ground throws daylight under the plants. You can see the red veins inside a young leaf, the copper sheen on a fly, the individual grains of mortar around an old bolt. Even beneath the loading platform there is a diluted brightness. The air is cool rather than cold, with a faint mineral taste near the rising ground and the warmer smell of oil beside the doors.

Late in the afternoon, the western wall catches the sun. Its vine casts an intricate shadow across two sealed windows. Condensation begins to bead along the lip of a pipe, and a robin takes up a position beneath it, waiting for the first drops to disturb the little creatures in the mulch.

The factories do not shut down all at once. One belt falls silent, then another. With each subtraction, sounds that have been present all day become briefly audible: claws on sheet metal, leaves rubbing together, water striking the hollow of a broken tile.

The three big fans continue.

Under their steady beating, the ridge keeps its place between the tracks. Its newest crack is clean enough to look wet, though it is dry to the touch. At the bottom of that crack, beyond the reach of the afternoon light, a pale root has found its way in.
