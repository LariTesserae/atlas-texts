The hollow lies beneath the lip of an old retaining wall, where the ground has subsided by less than the height of a person. From outside, there is little to distinguish it from the other dark gaps between the foundation stones. A slab leans across the entrance. Wind has packed its upper edge with dead needles and pale grit, leaving just enough room underneath to enter on hands and knees.

Inside, the floor drops another half meter. There is room to stand, though not everywhere.

The cold is immediate and still. It does not move across the skin like outdoor cold; it waits in the stone, in the soil, in the metal lip of the entrance. Anything brought here begins to surrender its warmth. Breath briefly gives the darkness a texture, then settles as frost on the nearest leaves.

There are leaves everywhere.

They crowd the low ceiling and occupy shelves in the walls, overlapping in thick, uneven layers. Some are narrow and folded lengthwise, like straps stored badly. Others are broad enough to cover a hand, with rolled margins and a dense felt on their undersides. Along the floor, small plants form cushions so closely knitted that the boundary between one and another has disappeared. Older stems lie underneath them, flattened but not quite dead. Roots descend through this bedding in pale cables.

There is almost no light by which to see any of it. During the brighter hours outside, a thin gray wedge reaches beneath the entrance slab and stops at the first mound of vegetation. Here the leaves look blue-black. Deeper in, color is something the place possesses without displaying.

A lamp reveals muted greens, burgundy stems, the silver nap of new growth. It also reveals how small the hollow is: five paces at its longest, perhaps three across, with an alcove behind a projecting stone. Without the lamp, it feels less measurable. A shoulder touches foliage before a foot finds the wall. There is always another layer between the hand and whatever supports it.

The plants are not reaching toward the entrance. Their leaves face into the room, toward one another, or down. Light has ceased to be an instruction.

Beneath the central bed is a piece of machinery no larger than a paving tile. Most of it is buried. One corner shows through the roots: matte ceramic, rounded with age, crossed by three shallow grooves. Fine conductors spread from it into the soil. They resemble root hairs until they pass straight through a pebble.

The system feeds the plants through their fungal partners, delivering small, steady quantities of stored chemical energy. Its deeper workings are inaccessible here. There is no motor housing, no cable leading out, no surface that becomes warm. The exposed corner remains at precisely the temperature of the surrounding bed. Whatever pays for this little winter garden does so elsewhere.

Its presence is most evident in the regularity of things. The innermost leaves do not freeze even when the entrance stones are white with rime. Growth slows, but never stops. A broken stem seals itself over several days with a clear, hard cap. The oldest roots have engulfed the conductors without disrupting them.

Nothing looks newly made.

The hollow has been occupied long enough for its construction and its erosion to become difficult to separate. A ledge might have been cut into the wall, or might simply be where a softer layer wore away. Water has smoothed the sill below the entrance into a shallow saddle. The ceiling is a single rough stone except where flakes have detached, leaving lighter scars. Each fallen flake now lies buried beneath leaf litter, and the plants above it make a slightly higher mound.

There are three other artifacts.

A narrow frame is fixed inside the entrance, visible only along one side where the vegetation has not covered it. It keeps the outside weather from entering too quickly. Snow can collect against it without blowing through; air passes with almost no detectable movement. The frame has no door.

In the alcove stands a shallow tray of cloudy glass. Its corners have been rounded by mineral deposits, and a root has crossed the rim. Most of the time it is empty. After a thaw outside, a little water gathers at one end, darkening the dust into a crescent.

The last object is a marker pressed into the soil beside the central bed. It carries a row of small indentations. Frost fills them. There may once have been a readable face beneath the crust, but the marker has tilted backward into the foliage, and the leaves have grown around its edges.

Water enters reluctantly. Rain must soak through the earth above the wall, find a seam, and follow it down. Sometimes the ceiling remains dry for weeks. Sometimes a single bead forms at the same low point for an entire afternoon, growing until its weight overcomes the stone’s hold.

When it falls, the sound is surprisingly large.

It strikes a stiff leaf, slips from its tip, and disappears into the bed. The leaf continues to move after the sound has ended. Nearby stems touch and separate.

Most moisture comes by less noticeable routes. Frost accumulates inside the entrance. A shift in the outside air loosens it, and the crystals shrink back into the stone. Farther in, the soil holds dampness below a dry, fibrous crust. The plants shed little. Dead leaves curl inward and remain caught among the living ones until fungi soften their attachments. Eventually they sink.

The smell is faint because of the cold: stone dust, bitter greenery, an occasional trace of wet bark. Close to the floor there is the richer smell of old soil, but it does not spread through the chamber. It stays where it is made.

There are no insects in the leaf folds, no slugs beneath the tray. Nothing has chewed the margins of the broad leaves. Fine debris rests undisturbed on horizontal stems. A loose strand of fungal growth can bridge two stones for years without being broken by anything passing through.

Decay still happens. It proceeds from within, through whitening veins and softened joints, through the gradual collapse of a stem that remains upright long after it has died. The garden recovers its materials patiently. Even the barest patch of soil contains layers of previous growth.

At long intervals, a root displaces a grain of grit. Somewhere beneath the foliage, it clicks against another grain.

Outside, sounds lose their detail before reaching the hollow. Footsteps become a soft pressure through the wall. Rain is a distant brushing. Wind can be inferred from the changing shape of the gray light beneath the slab, but seldom heard directly. The machinery contributes nothing audible. Only when the surrounding silence has lasted a long time does a tiny contraction in the glass tray seem loud enough to turn toward.

The seasons are narrow here. Winter thickens the frost at the entrance. Spring brings two or three days of dripping. In summer the outermost plants uncurl an extra length of leaf, gaining perhaps a finger’s width before the cold persuades them to stop. Farther inside, these events arrive as minor adjustments in the soil.

The retaining wall continues to settle. Its stones press a little farther into their beds. The entrance slab has lowered enough that an old scrape on its underside no longer aligns with the metal frame.

Below it, the garden keeps its small clearance.

A new leaf has begun to open in the alcove, just above the empty tray. It is tightly folded, almost rigid, and bears a few crystals deposited by the last visitor’s breath. Over the next several weeks it will widen into the space left by an older leaf. For now, the two rest against each other in the dark.
