Near the summit, on the underside of a stone that has not finished becoming a stone, there is a hollow about the size of two cupped hands.

It opens sideways into the mountain’s heat. You would have to lie flat on the high ledge and lower your face past its edge to see inside. Sunlight enters through a slit at the back, although from outside there is no slit, only a thickness of pale mineral. The light is ordinary afternoon light: bright enough to distinguish olive from green, soft enough to leave a shadow beneath a leaf. Its arrival is one of the few things here that does not announce how unlikely it is.

The hollow has a floor of new ceramic.

Not pottery, not anything fired by a maker. This material rises directly out of the mountain, already hot, already smooth, its little expanses divided by fine seams. Some patches are ivory; others have the bluish cast of steel seen through milk. Along their edges the substance is still deciding how thick to be. A lip lifts imperceptibly from the floor. Over many days it develops a second surface underneath and becomes a shelf.

Nothing has yet worn down. There is no sand, no grit, no accumulation of broken things. Every sharp edge is an original edge. Even the hollow itself has not been excavated: the mountain grew around an absence and left it alone.

Three plants occupy it.

They are far enough apart that each seems to have a climate of its own. Between them lie broad, empty reaches of porcelain floor, crossed only by shadows and a faint metallic discoloration.

The nearest plant has thick, folded leaves of a deep bottle green. It grows sideways from the wall, crowding itself into a sumptuous rosette, leaf over leaf, their undersides tinted copper. The folds look capable of holding water, but there has never been water here. What gathers in them is heat. The innermost leaves are visibly warmer than the outer ones: the air above them has a delicate, persistent misalignment, as if the leaf tips have been printed twice.

Its roots do not branch. They enter the wall as separate, straight filaments and end at different temperatures.

The second plant is low and sprawling, with round leaves on long red stems. It occupies very little of the hollow’s floor but gives an impression of abundance, of having more leaves than it can reasonably support. Some stand upright; some lie on their sides; a few seem to rest against surfaces that have not appeared yet. These last leaves hold quite still. In time, a mineral ridge will grow beneath them and make their posture understandable.

The third grows at the back, in the bright slit. Its foliage is almost black. You cannot see where its stems meet.

All three are lush in the way a furnace can be lush: full of active interiors, layered exchanges, a richness sustained by difficult conditions. Their leaves are flexible, cool at the margins, and hot enough at the central veins to discolor the stone beneath them. Instead of drinking, they establish differences. Warmth passes inward through one tissue and outward through another. Salts separate. Carbon settles. A new cell is made.

The whole hollow smells faintly of hot iron and a green stalk snapped between fingernails.

Under the ceramic floor, the mountain is working.

There are no machines in it. No buried installation, no ancient mechanism left running. Nevertheless, its geology has reached the condition of industry. Crystal beds compress one another in long, regular strokes. Conductive seams carry current from hot depths toward the cooler face of the cliff. Inclusions turn within sockets that grow at exactly the rate necessary to admit their turning.

A full revolution may take a season.

Occasionally a narrow band of the floor becomes magnetic. The red stems incline toward it, then slowly recover. Nothing clatters. Nothing races. Production here is measured in a few millimeters of fresh mineral, a new leaflet, one additional degree of separation between two adjacent stones.

The sound is mostly the sound of your own position on the ledge: cloth against rock, breath made noticeable by altitude. Below that, if you remain still, there is a low electrical note. It seems to come from the roots rather than the mountain. Once in a long while it stops, and the silence has the peculiar release of a workshop after its power has been cut.

Then a tiny click comes from somewhere beneath the black plant.

There are no insects among the leaves. No eggs glued to their undersides, no feeding scars, no silk. The plants have no reason to disguise their vulnerable parts. Tender new growth hangs openly into the warm air, pale and intricately folded. Nothing arrives to eat it.

Their abundance is not a competition. Each plant has a separate connection to the heat below. Between their root territories, the bare floor remains bare.

At the back of the hollow, distance behaves less reliably.

A fallen leaf—if it is fallen, rather than waiting to grow a stem—lies in the light. From the opening it appears no larger than a fingernail. Lean closer and it becomes smaller. Withdraw, and its veins resolve into branching valleys, with dark vegetation following the branches. You cannot hold both scales in attention at once. The little leaf and the country of the leaf replace each other without any sensation of change.

This is how the hollow acquires depth. It does not become larger. It gives certain small things more room inside themselves.

The high mountain outside continues into thin, brilliant air. Its summit is still accumulating above you, rising with a slowness that makes the word *rising* seem unnecessarily dramatic. Fresh stone appears along a crest no weather has rounded. Far below, slopes spread away in immaculate planes.

Inside, the rosette has begun a new leaf.

At first it is only a warmer line between two existing leaves. By evening it has a green edge. The rest will take much longer. The plant holds its older foliage apart to accommodate it, opening a space before there is anything substantial to put there.

The mineral beneath it does the same.

For a while, in the quiet heat, you can see the shape of what is coming without being able to say whether it will be leaf, ledge, or another small recess in which something can grow.
