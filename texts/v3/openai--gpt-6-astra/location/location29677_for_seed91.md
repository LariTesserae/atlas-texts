The ground lies below its own former surface.

You can see the old level along the edges of the basin: a dark, almost horizontal line beneath the snow, interrupted by hanging roots and pale grass stems. Below it, the peat has been cut into broad terraces. They descend only a few metres, but from the bottom the surrounding country disappears. There is no distant road, no line of farms, only the low rim and the winter sky resting on it. The excavation extends far enough that its opposite edge loses definition in the dimness.

Almost everything underfoot was grown here. The black floor, the brown walls, the soft shoulders of the uncut banks are compressed sedges, mosses, roots, and wood. In a fresh face you can pick out the flattened stems. Some retain their pale colour. Others come apart as dark fibres when touched. There is very little stone. Even where the surface looks like bare earth, a boot heel exposes the felted structure of plants.

The basin is mostly empty, yet vegetation occupies it completely. It has become the substance through which the emptiness was cut.

Above the workings, the bog still carries its low, close cover: sphagnum hummocks, cotton-grass, cranberry runners, dwarf birch bent close to the ground. In winter these make a rough crust, green and rust-red wherever the wind has stripped away the thin snow. Nothing grows high enough to offer shelter. There are long views across the uncut shelves, with a few black spruce standing so far apart that each has its own patch of horizon.

Down in the cut, the air is colder. It collects in the trenches and stays there through the brief daylight. Frost whitens the upper edges of peat blocks while their sheltered sides remain nearly black. The newest cuts are startlingly square. A shovel mark can remain legible for weeks. Where a bank has broken, it has broken in slabs, leaving sharp corners and hanging mats of roots rather than a rounded slope.

The place has not yet had enough thawing seasons to soften.

There is water, but little of it is available to the eye. Most is locked in the ground. The drainage channels contain narrow strips of cloudy ice, sometimes with a black thread moving beneath them. At a culvert mouth, water wells up through an unfrozen hole, spreads a few centimetres, and disappears under the ice again. A rim of white crystals grows around the opening. Nearby grass stems are thickened into fragile glass rods.

The channels lead toward a pumping station set against the lowest bank.

It is a small building for the noise it makes.

Corrugated metal, a concrete plinth, two exhaust stacks, a door with a scraped yellow handle: that is almost all there is to see. A pipe leaves its wall and climbs the cut on timber supports. Behind the building, a generator runs continuously. The pumps add a deeper, uneven pounding, and the ventilation fans tear through both sounds with a hard rushing roar.

Close to the station, speech is useless. You see a person’s mouth move inside a fur-edged hood and wait for the gesture that follows. The handrail trembles under a mitten. A loose sheet of metal on the intake shelter rattles so rapidly that its separate impacts become a single harsh note.

There are quieter parts of the basin, but not a silent one. Out along the terraces the machinery loses its sharpness and becomes a heavy, continuous presence. Small sounds vanish into it. A boot can break through a skin of ice without producing anything you can hear.

Beyond the station stand a fuel tank, a low maintenance shed, and a conveyor on thin steel legs. The belt is stopped. Frost has gathered along its rubber edges. Farther away, an excavator sits with its bucket lowered into a mound of frozen peat, its cab dark and its tracks half buried by windblown snow. These structures are scattered across an area much larger than they seem to require. Between them lie hundreds of metres of flat cut ground, interrupted by drains and long stacks of drying blocks.

The stacks have weathered grey on top. Their sides are still brown-black, fibrous, close-grained. Some have small plants growing out of them: a tuft of grass, a moss cushion, a low birch seedling established before the cold. They are fuel stores slowly becoming ground again.

Light comes from a low gap beneath the clouds. It reaches the upper bog first, touching the sedges with a muted copper colour, while the floor remains blue-grey. The pumping station has three floodlights. One points at the door, one at the pipe junctions, and one shines across a patch of empty ground. In that last beam, fine ice particles travel almost horizontally. Beyond it, the darkness seems thicker than it is.

Nothing moves quickly except the machinery hidden inside its housings.

The exhaust rises, bends, and spreads over the bank. A little heat escaping from the building keeps a strip of peat soft beside the wall. Moss there is dark green and wet to the touch. Condensation drips from the underside of a vent, forming a small mound of ice that has to be broken away periodically with a bar. The broken pieces lie beside it, clear at the centre and dirty at their edges.

Animal life is easy to overlook. A hare has crossed one of the uncut shelves, leaving tracks between birch stems. There are vole tunnels under the grass at the rim. Once, a raven lands on the conveyor and works at something caught in a joint. Its beak opens, but whatever sound it makes is lost.

By the end of the short afternoon, almost nothing has visibly changed. The excavator is still. The stacks have not shifted. The water in the drain has advanced beneath its ice by an amount too small to judge.

Yet the pumps have been working the whole time. Water is being lifted out of the lowest peat and carried over the rim. Air enters the drained fibres. The exposed surface dries by degrees, shrinks, settles. Along one fresh wall, a crack has opened behind a hanging mat of sedge roots.

For now it is no wider than a finger. Snow has begun to collect in it.
