The plain is still drying.

At noon it looks pale enough to be salt, but underfoot it is red-brown clay dusted with lime. The uppermost skin has hardened into thin plates. Some hold a person’s weight; others break with a small, dry click and expose darker material underneath. Press a palm against that darkness and it comes away warm and slightly damp. By late afternoon the mark will have hardened. There are footprints here that have become part of the ground without ever filling with dust.

Nothing rises very high. Broad banks swell a few feet above the flats, run toward the horizon, and disappear into the trembling air. Shallow basins lie between them. Some are empty; some contain a wash of cloudy water no deeper than a finger. Far away, a darker band might be a higher bank, rain falling, or simply the edge of a region that has not yet dried.

All of it was laid down.

This becomes apparent slowly. The banks are too steady in their width. Where the ground has split, the broken faces show layers of pressed earth, thin beds of sand, another layer of earth. Here and there, a seam of overlapping bricks runs through the surface, each brick large and irregular, its edges rounded by hands rather than wear. Other stretches were made as continuous floors, kneaded and smoothed while wet. Along their margins are the shallow marks of fingers.

There are no buildings standing on this construction. The construction is the country: its slopes, its drainage, its dry distances, the beds of its future streams. Even the bottom of a puddle is a finished surface. Where a deeper fissure allows a view below it, there is more laid material, extending down into the shade.

No work is being done by anyone you can see. There are no implements, no piles awaiting use, no abandoned shelters. Much of the ground nevertheless has the freshness of something completed only days ago. A bank face bears a clean thumb-sized ridge where wet clay was pushed upward. A basin has never held enough water to stain its sides. Across an enormous flat, the last smoothing strokes remain visible in low evening light.

The heat changes how this place can be crossed. Early in the morning the broad, level surfaces are firm and almost cool. By midday they give heat back through the soles of the feet. The darker beds become soft without becoming wet. Their surface yields slowly, and standing still is worse than walking. Along some seams, trapped moisture swells the clay into blisters. These are only inches high, scattered hundreds of yards apart. When one subsides, it leaves a little crescent of expelled water.

Most of the water is concealed.

It lies between layers, beneath the lime skin, in the deepest parts of the low banks. Occasionally it finds a seam and darkens it for miles. There is no visible flow at first, just a line becoming brown. Then beads gather. They join, spill a few inches, soak away, and appear again farther downslope. A stream here may take the better part of a day to establish itself, connecting many small wet places before any water reaches its destination.

The first channels are exceptionally clean. They have not had time to collect gravel or fallen leaves. Water slides over smooth clay and carries a faint red cloud ahead of it. In shallower sections it spreads into a reflective film; elsewhere it threads through the joints between bricks. A drop of only an inch can make the loudest sound in a whole basin.

There is little wind at ground level. The banks are low, but their reach is so great that they divide the moving air into broad, slow currents. Heat rises over the driest expanses. Above the damp regions, clouds accumulate through the afternoon, small at first, then substantial enough to throw cool shadows across several basins at once.

Rain may be visible for hours before it arrives. It makes a gray veil whose lower edge blurs into the plain. Sometimes the veil thins to nothing overhead, leaving only the smell of water in hot air. Sometimes large drops strike the clay far apart, each making its own dark circle. A true shower briefly covers the ground in stippling. The newest smoothing marks disappear. The water stands bright and shallow, and the banks release a close, earthy smell.

Afterward, the whole region is busy drying again.

Edges curl. Soft shoulders settle by fractions of an inch. A seam that was closed in the morning opens wide enough to admit light. Fine sand, washed out of an upper layer, collects in pale fans at the foot of a bank. These are the first deposits in places that have never had a history of runoff. There are no worn stones, no old gullies, no rounded debris. The land has barely begun to modify the shape it was given.

Vegetation follows the damp seams, though never continuously. You might walk half a day beside one before finding a plant.

The commonest growth is a low, fleshy herb with gray-green leaves held close to the ground. Its roots enter the joints where clay meets sand. Around a well-established specimen, the surface is slightly raised and finely cracked. A little way off there may be three more, or none for miles. Their shade is scarcely larger than a hand, but beneath it the earth stays darker into the afternoon.

Rushes occupy a few of the deeper receiving basins. They stand in loose groups rather than beds, each stem sharply distinct against the pale floor. Their lower portions are coated white where water has risen and evaporated. When a basin dries, the rushes remain, green at the joints, waiting over buried moisture.

Elsewhere there is only a green stain. It appears after rain on the sheltered sides of banks and fades as the surface heats. Close up, it has the faintly rough texture of a living crust. It holds a little dust, softens the impact of the next shower, and begins to change the soil immediately beneath it. These patches are small, but there are enough of them to alter the color of a distant slope.

No insects rise from the water. No birds mark the distances. Nothing moves among the rushes except the stems themselves.

The quiet makes ordinary events surprisingly distinct: a crust separating from the clay beneath it; several grains falling into a crack; a short patter of rain reaching one edge of a basin while the other remains in sun. Occasionally a larger section settles with a soft, heavy thud. Then there may be hours in which the only audible sound is your own passage.

At sunset, the low relief becomes visible all at once. Banks that seemed almost level throw long shadows. Smoothing strokes cross the flats in broad overlapping arcs. The plain resolves into adjoining surfaces, some pink, some ocher, some nearly white, each drying at a different rate. It extends beyond sight in every direction, and the small differences repeat without becoming quite regular.

Night releases the heat slowly. Moisture gathers first on the lime, then along the cooler edges of cracks. By dawn, narrow lines of condensation have traced portions of the buried construction. They shine for a short while before the sun reaches them.

In one basin, yesterday’s rain is still travelling toward the lowest corner. It advances across the floor in a thin, uneven front, stopping at each slight ridge, deepening behind it, then spilling over. Ahead of the water the clay is pale and unmarked. Behind it, the first sediment has begun to settle.
