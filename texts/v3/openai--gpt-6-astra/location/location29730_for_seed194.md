The city occupies the hollow beneath a seed coat. From outside, it would fit comfortably under a thumbnail. There is no opening large enough to admit a grain of sand.

Within, the distances are considerable.

A low plain runs from one curved wall to the other, interrupted by shallow rises where the floor has folded over itself. All of it is vegetable: the plain, the enclosing shell, the fine ribs overhead. Even the loose grit consists of dead cells rubbed into hard, smooth particles. There has never been stone here. The things that serve as stone are the oldest parts of the plant, compressed through so many dormant seasons that they break with a mineral click.

It is cold enough that a newly exposed surface stays brittle. There is no frost. Nothing drips, condenses, or leaves a damp mark. The channels inside the walls carry a pale wax that advances by increments too small to see, transporting dissolved salts from exhausted tissue into living edges. In the deeper cold it stops altogether. The city can wait through such stoppages. Its longest streets were laid out during one.

Darkness is complete. It has no weather and no gradations. Height must be found by climbing; distance, by waiting for an echo. The shapes of the city are available to touch, though there is nothing here now that moves about touching them.

Most of the hollow is empty.

The buildings cling to its lower surface in a thin, continuous settlement: close-packed foundations, joined walls, courts connected by narrow passages. They scarcely rise above the plain. At their tallest they are no higher than three thicknesses of seed coat, and the great unused volume above them has outlasted everything built beneath it. The city has districts, retaining walls, public stairs, raised crossings. It has almost no skyline.

Every surface is living or capable of returning to life. The smooth roofs are leaves thickened until their two faces have fused. Doorposts are short roots trained upright. Fibers cross the passages at regular intervals, holding opposite walls in tension. Here and there a root has grown through an old joint and been split lengthwise, its halves bent aside to preserve the doorway. The wound has healed around the bend.

Construction was done by levering, scraping, knotting, and waiting. The tools survive in recesses beside the walls: splinters with their ends worn flat, hooked thorns, loops of braided fiber too stiff now to untie. No material was brought in. A builder loosened a layer of floor, raised it, braced it, and let it decide whether to become a wall.

Some walls refused. Their remains are low ridges across rooms whose entrances still exist.

There are no builders left. Nor are there nests, shed skins, burrows, or the small accumulations that animals leave behind. Whoever arranged these streets may have been a temporary form the plant once took. There are suggestions of this in certain houses: five or six rootlets gathered around a tool, their tips hardened into grips; a hollow in the floor shaped to receive an upright body, with feeding fibers radiating beneath it. But the fibers have withdrawn, and the grips have opened. It would be possible to mistake all of it for unfinished growth.

The place is loud.

A deep, almost continuous note fills the empty space above the roofs. It comes from the seed coat, which is under tension from forces outside. Whatever contains the seed presses on it unevenly. The shell gives a little, catches, and holds. Its ribs carry the strain across the hollow, where it becomes a sound broad enough to seem like a condition of the air.

Closer to the floor, there are sharper noises. A drawn fiber slips against its anchor. Two dry leaves separate along an old seam. A roof releases a fraction of its stored twist with a report that travels down every street. Afterward, tiny particles come ticking down.

These events are rare, but their echoes are long. A single crack passes through the courts, returns from the upper shell, enters the mouths of abandoned conduits, and emerges altered. In some districts it becomes a rough bellow. In others it is divided into quick, diminishing knocks. By the time it has faded, another part of the city has usually shifted.

The oldest buildings have been worn low by this trembling. For ages, a ledge has touched a neighboring ledge, withdrawn, and touched it again. Their edges are rounded now. Thresholds have become shallow saddles. The high steps of the central terraces have settled into a slope so gentle that only the parallel bands of harder tissue show where the risers were.

There is no buried younger city beneath the old one. The same settlement has been adjusted, pared back, and grown over until its original arrangement persists mostly as differences in texture. A plaza can be recognized by the fineness of its floor. A heavily used passage has a polished strip down its center, although nothing has passed along it for an interval the plant no longer records.

At the lowest point lies a wide basin, scarcely deeper than a scraped mark. Several streets descend to it. Their gutters are dry and have always been dry; they were made to collect the slow migration of dust.

This is the richest ground in the hollow.

Here the living layer has enough discarded material to thicken. It forms a soft, close nap, too short to sway. Each filament ends in a flattened plate that catches falling fragments and folds around them. Over many cycles, the fragments are reduced and drawn downward. Nothing hurries to claim a newly fallen particle. A chip may lie among the plates through a hundred episodes of thunder before one edge is finally held.

The basin feeds the city through roots laid beneath the streets. Some districts receive almost nothing. Their walls remain intact, but their surfaces have lost the faint yielding quality of living tissue. Other districts are slowly making repairs: a lintel thickens from below; a cracked court closes along one side; the floor of an unused room rises by a cell’s breadth.

Growth is permitted only where something else has come apart. The shell admits no new substance. Every repair has a corresponding loss somewhere in the dark.

Near one wall, a row of houses is being quietly consumed to maintain a bridge.

The bridge crosses a fissure narrow enough to step over, if there were feet here. Its deck is a single trained leaf, supported by two cords descending from the shell. Under the pressure of the hollow’s great note, the cords vibrate. The leaf answers at a higher pitch. It is the clearest sound in the city, a hard singing that can be felt through the floors far from the crossing.

The houses beside it have sunk almost flush with the street. Their doorways remain as little depressions. Eventually the doors will vanish, then the street between them, while the bridge continues to hold over a gap that erosion is slowly closing.

Beyond that district, the floor meets the seed coat in a long, sheltered crease. There are no buildings in it. A single sheet of living tissue lines the fold, smooth and very thin. This is the youngest place in the hollow. It has been extending toward the city for longer than the city’s tools have lain unused.

Its advancing edge is blunt from age. It moves only when the wax softens, taking possession of one loose particle, then another.

Above it, an enormous volume of dark air shakes with the noise of things staying in place.
