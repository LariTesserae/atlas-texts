The stairs go down only a little way before the heat begins to rise around your legs. By the last landing it has become the temperature of the whole place: the handrail, the wall, the air held inside your clothes. There is no cool underground smell. The smell is of warm stone, leaf wax, and water left overnight in an iron vessel.

Below is a chamber about the size of a village square, though its boundaries are difficult to see at once. Four square pillars stand well apart beneath a low, black ceiling. Between them lies a mostly bare floor, interrupted by islands of vegetation. The plants are thick where they grow. Beyond their edges, there may be ten paces of empty ground without so much as a fallen leaf.

It takes time for the chamber to emerge from the dark.

First there are the leaves nearest the stairs, catching a little light from above. Then a rail embedded in the floor, its upper edge faintly silver. Farther away, a rectangular instrument face glows a very low red. Eventually you can distinguish three narrow shafts in the ceiling. They admit daylight, but not views of the sky. Each has a bend in it, and whatever light reaches the bottom has already touched several surfaces.

Beneath those shafts, the vegetation rises almost to the roof.

The largest plants have jointed trunks and broad, drooping leaves, some longer than an arm. Their upper leaves are held nearly vertical, turned toward the pale rectangles overhead. Below, smaller leaves spread horizontally to catch what the others miss. There are glossy, tightly furled shoots; mats of soft growth hugging the bases of the trunks; vines that climb a few feet and then hang down again in loops too heavy to support themselves.

Nothing has been eaten. The leaf edges are complete. No holes, no bitten stems, no little trails through the softer growth. Old leaves split under their own weight and remain suspended for a long time, slowly becoming brown ribbons. Where they finally fall, they collapse into a black, close-textured material that does not rustle underfoot.

The roots stay within remarkably definite borders. A dense bed may end against a shallow groove in the floor, with clean stone immediately beyond it. In other places, they follow a buried pipe, making a long green island scarcely wider than a bench. Much of the chamber’s life seems to depend on differences too small to feel with a hand.

The floor is new in places.

Not newly laid: newly exposed. Thin slabs have lifted and cracked, showing pale mineral surfaces beneath the old dark skin. Their edges are sharp enough to cut a sole. Some stand tilted against one another; others have only begun to separate, with a straight black opening beneath a raised lip. There is almost no grit around them. Whatever is pushing upward has not yet been reduced to rubble.

You can see that the upheaval is recent because several roots are suspended over it. They still follow the former level of the floor, crossing a gap before entering the ground again. One small plant has been lifted intact on a plate of stone, its root mat hanging over the edge like a piece of badly fitted upholstery.

A little water collects in the lowest cracks. It is clear until you look along it, when a rusty film becomes visible. The pools are narrow and deep rather than broad. Elsewhere, the floor is dry enough to preserve a shoeprint in the fine dust beside a pillar.

Water arrives from above, but seldom as rain.

The ceiling carries a loose network of pipes. Most are thick, insulated, and painted a colour that has disappeared beneath heat-darkened dust. A few thinner lines are bare. On certain mornings these bead with condensation. The beads enlarge, join, and travel toward the lowest elbow. Under each such elbow there is a patch of growth, or a shallow collecting pan, or both.

The old arrangement was evidently more precise. Little metal gutters once carried the drips from pipe to pipe, then down chains into stone basins. Several gutters have twisted out of position. One chain ends two handspans above its basin, and the drops now land on a root that has grown across the opening.

That root is smooth and swollen. Water travels along it before sinking out of sight.

There are structures among the plants, but only a few. A glazed booth occupies one corner. Its door stands open, and the glass has become cloudy from the bottom upward. Inside are a chair, a shelf, and a row of switches with ceramic collars. A vine has entered through the floor, climbed one leg of the chair, and stopped. Its leaves all face the open doorway.

Near the opposite wall stands a cylindrical machine on a low concrete plinth. It has a flywheel, a belt guard, and two heavy pipes disappearing underground. Nothing on it visibly moves. Nevertheless, the side of its casing is clean in one broad oval, as though something inside keeps that particular patch too warm for dust to settle.

If you rest your palm there, the heat is almost painful.

The red instrument belongs to this machine. It has no numbers, only a vertical line and a luminous mark close to the bottom. Watching it for a minute reveals nothing. Watching it for an hour might reveal that the mark has risen.

There is no engine noise. The chamber has the quiet of equipment that has not quite stopped working, but whose remaining work is too slow to hear.

Occasionally a drop strikes a collecting pan. It makes a small, rounded sound, surprisingly distinct. Once in a long while there is a faint click from the pipes, or a dry adjustment in the floor. These sounds have short echoes. The leaves absorb them, and the low ceiling returns them without grandeur.

No wings move in the light shafts. No insects gather around the red gauge. There are no nests in the cable trays or tracks in the dust beyond those left by visitors. Even the shallow water is still. The chamber’s decay proceeds without scavengers: fallen growth softens in place, darkens, and becomes the damp footing for another stem.

Near the centre is a low masonry curb enclosing what must once have been a service pit. It now holds the densest growth in the room. The plants there are shorter, with thick leaves arranged in overlapping fans. Their undersides are pale, almost the colour of unglazed porcelain.

Over many hours, the fans change position.

They do not turn toward the shafts. They turn toward the warmer sections of floor. At the edge of the pit, leaves lie almost flat against the stone; nearer the machine, they stand upright, exposing their pale undersides to the casing. When the instrument’s mark rises, these plants slowly open.

The others open later.

This is easiest to notice from the stair landing, if you leave and return. A passage that seemed broad in the morning may have narrowed by evening. A dark space under a clump becomes a wall of leaves. Nothing has travelled any appreciable distance, but the available room has changed.

The chamber is not abandoned in the ordinary sense. Someone still replaces the collecting pans. There are bright bolts in one old bracket and a length of newer cable following the wall. Beside the stairs hangs a brush with a long wooden handle, used to clear the gutters without standing beneath them. Its bristles are stained green.

But there is no sign of an attempt to restore the original floor or cut back the plants. Where the rail vanishes under a raised slab, it has been left there. Where roots surround a valve, a small iron extension has been fitted so the valve can still be turned.

Maintenance here has become a matter of keeping access to a few necessary points.

Late in the day, the last borrowed daylight withdraws from the shafts. The high leaves lose their separate shapes. For a while the pale undersides near the machine remain visible, then those disappear too. The red mark on the gauge is all that is left to establish distance.

The heat continues without the light. Somewhere above the ceiling, a pipe begins to cool. A bead forms on its underside and hangs there, gathering enough water to fall.
