From the upper service road, the district looks like a green valley under low cloud. Its slopes are long and stepped, its hollows dark, its far side almost lost in the damp. Only after looking for a while do you notice that every contour has an edge.

The ridges are factory roofs. The ravines are gaps between retaining walls. What looks like a stream descending through trees is a sequence of concrete spillways, each feeding a settling basin on the roof below. Even the road beneath your feet is a deck: through its drainage slots you can see another road, twelve meters down, with wet leaves plastered against the lane markings.

There is no exposed ground anywhere in sight.

The district occupies a high saddle between two mountains, but it has been built over so completely that the original shape survives chiefly in the gradients. Freight routes still bend around a buried spur. Water still gathers above an old depression, although it now gathers in a tank. The lowest buildings have become foundations for the next generation of buildings, and some of those have become foundations in turn. Across several kilometers, roofs join to platforms, platforms to bridges, bridges to the backs of warehouses. There are places where a tree grows six stories above a loading floor and its roots descend through three different owners’ property.

The trees are ordinary ones suited to the altitude: alder, birch, rowan, a few spruce with their upper branches combed thin by wind. They stand in long troughs of manufactured soil. Fallen leaves are gathered, shredded, composted under warm floors, and brought back uphill. The troughs have been replenished for so many years that they carry a deep, dark rooting layer, but beneath it are brick aggregate, filter cloth, and a waterproof membrane. A fallen tree sometimes lifts this whole arrangement like the corner of a carpet.

Between the trees, vegetation fills everything it can hold. Ferns crowd the mouths of redundant ducts. Currant bushes lean through safety rails. Moss thickens on the shaded faces of concrete stairs until the treads look upholstered, except for a narrow strip kept clear by boots. On the old processing sheds, sedum and coarse grass have been overtaken by willow scrub. The willows are cut back every few years; their pale stumps disappear beneath new shoots before the end of the next summer.

Nothing has much room, but everything has height. Vines hang down walls occupied by pipes. The pipes pass through shrubbery rooted in ledges. Beneath the ledges, stacked windows glow faintly behind condensation. You can look across a broad prospect and find no unoccupied surface, no clean interval between one use and another.

The light is thin and silver-gray. Cloud catches on the upper structures and drifts through the open floors of unfinished buildings. At noon, lamps remain on inside the potting halls and electrical rooms. Their reflections lie in the wet paving, greenish under old glazing and amber where newer fixtures have been installed. There are few hard shadows. Distance loses contrast before it loses detail: a gantry on the opposite slope remains perfectly legible after the building beneath it has become a gray patch.

It is cool enough that a hand left on a railing begins to ache. Water beads on cable jackets and the underside of handrails. The rain has stopped, or has thinned beyond the point where it feels like rain, but everything continues to drain. A roof empties from one corner in a patient string. The padded bark of an alder releases drops when a branch moves. Along the retaining walls, seepage follows joints that have been wet for decades, leaving white mineral fans beneath the bolts.

Water is common here without being abundant. The channels are narrow, the basins shallow, and most of the broad surfaces remain walkable. Every roof is a catchment. Every planted terrace has an overflow. In dry weeks the upper gardens receive reclaimed water through buried hoses; in wet weeks their valves close and the whole district slowly hands its surplus downhill.

The old spillways have rounded lips. Their concrete has worn into shapes that resemble stone, although rust lines and exposed reinforcement keep revealing its origin. At some corners, successive repairs form scalloped layers: dark original material, pale patch, darker sealant, a final collar of stainless steel. People have stopped trying to make the repairs disappear. They work around them, and the accumulated mends have become the district’s most reliable landmarks.

Below all this growth, the factories are running.

You see the activity much more readily than you hear it. Through strips of glass, pallets advance, pause, split into different lines. A bridge conveyor carries trays of seedlings toward an upper nursery. Farther off, a bank of ventilation shutters opens all at once, exposing black rectangles, then closes in an uneven ripple. An overhead crane moves a cylindrical tank across a gap between roofs. Its load is large enough to hide a building as it passes, yet from the road it makes no distinct sound.

The loud work is enclosed in heavy, earth-loaded structures. Motors sit on isolation mounts. Freight vehicles move electrically and slowly through the inhabited levels. Their tires hiss on the wet decks; at a blind junction, a soft warning note repeats until the crossing clears.

Most of the time you hear a drip striking sheet metal, the faint rub of branches against mesh, your own clothes when you turn.

Yet the view scarcely holds still.

Cloud opens a pale corridor down the valley, then shuts it. Greenhouse screens draw across whole slopes of glass. A lift rises from behind a stand of birches, carrying a maintenance crew and a folded length of ducting. Water, released somewhere out of sight, abruptly fills a dry channel beside the road; brown leaves lift from the bottom and race under a grate. On the opposite terrace, irrigation booms begin to travel through the nursery rows. Closer by, workers unclip a wind screen, and the bushes behind it spring outward into the space it occupied.

The district is always making room by moving something.

Growing racks are shifted to follow available light. Temporary bridges are lifted into place for repairs. Roof sections slide back when humidity rises, then close against another bank of cloud. A loading court that was empty a minute ago fills with shallow carts; when you look again, the carts have gone and a tracked machine is washing the floor. The paths remain fixed, but their margins change from hour to hour.

There are rules for walking here, most of them learned from water and machinery. Stay inside the white line even when plants have grown across it. Do not stand beneath a striped gutter: it may discharge without warning. A flashing lamp beside a bridge means the bridge itself is about to move, not merely that a vehicle is coming. In the older quarters, where signs have faded, polished handrails and the paths through the moss make these distinctions clear.

Animals are surprisingly scarce. Fine mesh closes the ventilation openings. Doors descend promptly. There are insects in the sheltered flowers, though fewer than the amount of greenery seems to promise, and occasionally a small bird works along a gutter. Its movements draw the eye because they are so slight and unregulated. It can stay where it likes. Nothing needs its patch of railing until it chooses to leave.

Toward evening, the cloud settles lower. The far slope disappears floor by floor. Nearby glass becomes opaque with reflected lamps, and the planted roofs turn into dark masses with individual leaves shining at their edges.

On the upper service road, a row of freight wagons passes behind the trees. Their lights move steadily through the stems. Then a curtain of mist crosses the deck and hides them, leaving only the wet rails, the overflow channel, and a fern trembling where warm exhaust rises through the paving.
