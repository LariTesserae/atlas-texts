The water is only knee-deep over the old road, but the road is a long way above the clouds.

You can make out its surface when the wind briefly drops: two pale wheel tracks beneath the wavering brown, running between beds of submerged grass. Then a gust crosses the lake, the grass lies flat, and the tracks disappear under overlapping waves. Farther out, their course is marked by a line of iron posts. Some still carry ceramic insulators. Waterbirds perch on them with their feathers blown backward.

This is the top of an enormous plateau, flooded from one shallow basin to the next. Its height is most apparent at the margins. There, beyond the last sedge islands, water pours through breaches in the rim and vanishes into cloud. Occasionally the cloud opens. You see the waterfalls falling past a wall of dark rock, then a second wall much farther down, then green foothills so distant that their forests have no visible texture. Thunder comes up from below.

Across the plateau itself, the country is broad and worn. The hills have long, low backs. Their stone shows through in rounded patches, smooth enough that rain spreads over it in sheets rather than gathering immediately into streams. There are few sharp summits. Everything exposed has been weathered, polished, split by frost, and weathered again; even the boulders look half dissolved. The mountain’s enormous height belongs to its foundation. Up here, on its ancient surface, the land has almost finished becoming level.

Water finds what little slope remains.

It slides beneath peat banks, fills hollows, spills over stone lips no higher than a doorstep. Channels divide around mats of roots and come together a hundred yards farther on. What looks like a single lake is a connected system of lakes, flooded meadows, drowned riverbeds, and moving shelves of vegetation. Some channels are deep enough to turn black. Others carry a bright, trembling skin of water over gravel, every pebble visible.

The rain has been falling for days. It is not always heavy, but it is seldom absent. Fine rain hangs almost motionless in the lee of the hills; over open water it travels sideways in pale bands. Hard showers arrive with enough force to flatten the lake’s small waves into a boiling, stippled surface. Between them, there is ordinary gray daylight, sufficient to show the rust on a distant shed or the yellow eye of a bird riding close to shore. When sunlight breaks through, whole miles of wet country shine at once.

Vegetation occupies nearly every shallow place. Sedges grow in thick, flexible ranks, their leaves dark green at the base and straw-colored where the cold has bitten them. Moss swells over the low islands. Water buttercups trail through the clearer channels, flowering just above the surface. Beneath them, dense beds of pondweed turn slowly in the current. In sheltered coves the plants almost touch across the water, leaving narrow passages roofed with leaning stems.

There are shrubs, too: low willows pressed into the lee of stones, their lower branches submerged and their upper leaves silvered by rain. A few patches of stunted woodland survive in protected folds near the plateau’s edge. From a distance they resemble dark stains. Close to them, the air smells of wet bark, leaf mold, and the cold mineral breath of running water.

The ground under the plants is uncertain. A green island may be anchored to rock, or it may be a raft of peat held in place by roots. Some rise and fall a little when a wave passes underneath. Along their torn edges, black fibers hang into the water. Flood currents have pulled entire pieces loose; they drift slowly across the open basins carrying flowers, beetles, and sometimes a nest whose occupants continue to defend it.

Birds are everywhere. Small ones skim between the sedge beds, briefly lost against the dark water. Ducks shelter behind the peat islands. Long-legged waders stand on drowned hummocks, taking quick steps when a wave overtops their feet. Above them, broad-winged scavengers work the updrafts at the escarpment, scarcely moving their wings.

In calmer water you can see fish nosing among the roots. The flooded meadows have opened a great temporary feeding ground: larvae shaken from stems, worms driven out of saturated soil, small crustaceans carried from basin to basin. Life crowds into the sheltered inches. Under an overhanging bank, the surface may be almost still and crowded with insects while, a few feet away, a current strong enough to take a person off their feet drags whole clumps of vegetation downstream.

The noise makes these differences difficult to judge.

Wind rattles the stiff sedge leaves. Water slaps the hollow undersides of peat shelves. Every narrow outlet has its own continuous roar. From the plateau’s edge comes the heavier sound of falling water, sometimes muffled by cloud, sometimes suddenly clear. Bird calls cut through in sharp fragments. Loose metal knocks somewhere in the rain.

There are only a few buildings: low pumping sheds on concrete plinths, a maintenance house with boarded lower windows, an abandoned cable station at a crossing too deep to ford. Their roofs have been weighted with stone. Rust spreads beneath the hinges and bolts, but the buildings are still recognizably useful things, put here to move water, measure it, or keep a route open.

One pump is running.

Its exhaust pipe trembles beside a shed surrounded on three sides by the flood. The engine’s steady pounding carries through the waterlogged ground, felt through the soles before it can be distinguished from the weather. A discharge pipe empties into a channel already running bank-full. Nearby, a staff gauge stands in the lake with most of its numbered marks submerged. Someone has tied a strip of orange cloth above the last visible figure.

A narrow metal footbridge reaches the shed. Grass and torn roots have collected against its upstream railing. Each new mass of debris bends the current sideways; each shift sends water over another patch of bank. The workers have left a rake hooked to the handrail, a coil of cable under the eaves, a pair of gloves turned palm-up on a windowsill.

Beyond this little machinery, the flooded country continues for miles.

The weather moves visibly across it. A gust darkens one basin, crosses a low divide, and lifts spray from the next. Rain erases the far hills, then gives them back. Outlets deepen as their soft banks collapse. New shallows form where the debris catches. The stone barely changes, but everything resting on it is in motion.

By evening, the old road carries a current. Water runs along the wheel tracks toward a culvert hidden beneath the sedges. A bird leaves one of the iron posts as it begins to lean. The pump keeps its rhythm. At the plateau’s rim, the clouds draw apart once more, revealing a tremendous fall of water, white all the way down to where distance and rain conceal it.
