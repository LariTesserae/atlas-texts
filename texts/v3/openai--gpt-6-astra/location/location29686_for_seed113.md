The district lies one level below the old ground, in a hollow no wider than a public square. From above, it would look like a block removed from a city: four straight sides, a few stairheads, a dark opening among pale roofs. Down here, the opening is mostly hidden by the overhanging floors. Daylight arrives as a gray stripe high on one wall, moving so slowly that its passage is easier to notice in the plants than on the stone.

It is warm. The warmth comes through the soles of your shoes and collects beneath the galleries, where condensation hangs in rows along the undersides of handrails. Every few seconds a drop grows too heavy and falls. There are so many places for this to happen that the rain never quite stops, though the sky above may be clear.

Most of the hollow is empty.

Its architecture has been pressed to the edges: six levels of shallow rooms, balconies, conduits, doorways, all built into the retaining walls. The rooms are narrow and numerous. Some have shopfronts; others have the small vestibules and paired windows of dwellings. A little freight platform waits outside a loading door. Higher up, a bridge crosses from one wall to another without a support beneath it. There is enough construction here for a neighborhood, but it surrounds a volume occupied only by wet air.

The floor is a shallow basin, almost flat, with the faintest rise toward the center. Water covers much of it to the depth of a fingernail. Under that film, the paving is dark green and soft-looking, although it rings when struck. Its joints meet at precise angles. Nothing has settled crookedly yet. The steps descending into the basin still have edges sharp enough to catch a sleeve, and the pale mineral crusts beneath the drains are only beginning to take shape: short teeth, delicate shelves, little white lips that break under the weight of their own drops.

At the foot of the eastern wall, the ground has split.

The opening is narrow enough to step across, but it exposes several layers of the district’s foundations. There is a black membrane, then a translucent material full of straight silver threads, then a fresh-looking seam of clay. Water wells out of the clay without bubbling. It brings a faint smell of iron and something warmer, like a flowerpot lifted from a sunny sill.

This is where most of the vegetation grows.

Broad leaves stand in the runoff, their stems crowded into a strip scarcely two paces wide. Some leaves are almost black; some have copper undersides that show when the ventilation turns them. Fine roots spread over the foundation membrane and disappear into its joins. Moss thickens on the lowest stair, making it look less like a step than a green object laid across the water. Farther up the wall, plants occupy the old planter channels outside the dwellings, spilling down in lush, isolated curtains.

Between these places there is bare architecture. A whole balcony may hold one trailing stem. Across three floors of windows, a single fern opens from a defective seal.

The plants turn toward the walls rather than the sky. After dusk, the reason becomes visible. A weak violet light shines through the building material, especially around roots. It is too faint to light the walkways, but it gives the leaves enough to live on. The illumination shifts as the roots grow. Small bright regions appear behind a new shoot; old ones fade after a leaf falls.

The district was built to accommodate this. Inside the walls, water and heat move through channels finer than veins. A dry planting pocket receives moisture before its soil has visibly changed color. A damaged conduit closes around its own leak, leaving a bead of clear material on the surface. Much of this work happens silently, at a scale too small to see.

The larger systems are loud.

Every few minutes, a deep rushing sound passes behind the western wall. It builds until the railings vibrate, then breaks into a hollow, uneven knocking. Water is being lifted somewhere out of sight. You feel the machinery most strongly in your chest, though there is no machine room and no rotating equipment visible through the service openings. Just dark shafts, smooth corners, and water traveling up an exposed face in a sheet.

For a little while after each pumping cycle, all the drains speak at once.

Then the hollow returns to dripping, leaf rustle, and the continuous hiss of water sliding beneath the lowest gallery. There are no insects around the wet lights. No bird has nested in the open rooms. Fallen leaves remain where they land until softened by fungi, or until a flushing current carries them toward the collection slots. The richest smell comes from those slots: leaf pulp, warm silt, the clean mineral scent of newly wetted stone.

Once in an afternoon, sometimes twice, the basin empties.

The water film begins to move, revealing slopes too slight for the eye to detect. Separate streams draw together. The reflection of the upper bridge stretches and breaks. Within minutes the floor is exposed, shining with residual moisture, and its central rise can be seen clearly: a low oval, perhaps the foundation of a fountain that has not yet been installed.

A dull light passes beneath the paving.

It travels from joint to joint, pausing at certain intersections. Where it pauses, the floor lifts by a fraction. The district is still adjusting its drainage. At the eastern split, a fresh sliver of foundation extends into the clay, glossy and pale before the water darkens it. A stair tread lengthens almost imperceptibly over the course of a week. The freight platform has recently acquired a curb.

There is no sign that anyone is directing these changes. Nor does the place seem abandoned in the usual way. Nothing has been boarded up. The doors fit their frames. A bench beneath the lowest overhang remains dry and pleasantly warm, with room for four people to sit facing the basin.

When the water returns, it comes first around the bench’s feet. Then it spreads across the square, carrying a scrap of copper-colored leaf. The pumps fall quiet. High overhead, the stripe of daylight reaches the hanging plants, and for a few minutes their wet edges shine against the dark galleries.

By then the new foundation seam has disappeared beneath the shallow water. Only a small cloud of clay remains to show where the work has been done.
