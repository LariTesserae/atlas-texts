The valley hangs above the weather.

From its southern rim you can look down through breaks in the cloud and see mountain ranges: whole systems of peaks, their snowfields small and dull with distance. Thunderstorms open beneath them. Occasionally lightning illuminates the underside of a ridge, and for a moment the world below resembles something held up to a lamp. Here, much higher, the stones are too hot to sit on at noon.

The valley is broad enough to contain several lakes, a forest, and a succession of green terraces that diminish toward a copper-colored escarpment. Its floor slopes so gently that water takes weeks to travel from one lake to the next. Channels wander through sedges and groves of low, heavy trees. Their banks are neither building nor collapsing very quickly. Roots hold the dark soil in thick stitches; mineral deposits seal the exposed rock. Even the bare gullies have softened into durable shapes, with rounded lips and shallow steps on which water rests.

There is no snow. White patches on the heights are colonies of wax-leaved plants, or salt left where a warm spring has changed its course.

The heat rises from below and arrives from above. Sunlight is ordinary sunlight, yellow-white, hard on the exposed slopes. The ground contributes a more intimate warmth. Kneel beside a pool and it comes through your knees. Put a hand beneath an overhang and the stone feels like the wall of a furnace long after the fire has gone out. At night the valley cools just enough for moisture to gather on every surface. By morning the trees are dripping into already saturated earth.

At this altitude there should be almost nothing to breathe.

Instead the air is thick, hot, and faintly metallic. It has weight without much movement. A leaf released from the hand falls normally, but a rising plume of steam flattens at the height of a tall tree and spreads into a low white roof. Larger clouds form above the forests and remain there for days, gradually raining themselves back into the same catchments.

Along the western rim, slender structures maintain this impossible pocket of atmosphere.

There are seven visible from the central lake, though the farthest might be two aligned. Each stands on a bare stone platform just outside the vegetation. They are taller than houses, narrower than chimneys, and made from a dark material that reflects neither the sky nor the person approaching it. Their upper ends divide into fine, crooked branches. Nothing turns. There are no vents or wires. Once in a while, usually in the hottest part of the afternoon, a narrow band of shadow travels upward along one of them, against the light.

The nearest trees lean away from these structures. Moss grows up to their bases and stops along a line too exact to be natural. Yet insects land on them freely, and one of the eastern towers supports a sprawling nest built from pale reeds. The birds have polished their perches with generations of feet.

Whatever the towers were built to sustain, the valley has occupied it thoroughly.

The forest stands mostly in the shallow water between the lakes. Its trees have broad buttressed trunks and crowns that begin close to the ground. Their leaves are long, leathery, and dark green above, rust-red underneath. In the still air the red seldom shows, except when an animal moves through the canopy or a branch bends under accumulated water. Then a whole patch of forest turns briefly the color of exposed flesh.

Smaller plants grow in the joints of the larger ones. Ferns fill the forks. Rooted vines descend into the channels, carrying curtains of pale flowers just above the surface. Some flowers open underwater and trap bright bubbles in their petals. Little fish enter these bubbles with their mouths, withdrawing with a minute clicking sound.

There is very little unoccupied ground, but the growth is not impenetrable everywhere. Broad-backed grazers keep passages open through the reeds. Their routes form a network of submerged lanes, worn down to smooth stone. Seen from the high terraces, these lanes divide the marsh into irregular green islands. At water level they are warm corridors smelling of crushed stems and animal skin.

The grazers are roughly the size of cattle, with six short legs and a high seam down the back where mats of vegetation take hold. They stand motionless for so long that birds mistake the smaller ones for banks. When they move, they do so deliberately, testing the bottom with a front foot before committing their weight. Their heads remain below water. A pair of breathing tubes opens behind the shoulders, closes, opens again.

They make a deep, hollow booming noise that travels through the pools. Near one of the larger herds, the sound can be felt in the teeth.

Other calls occupy the air above it. Hard insect rattles come from the sunlit reeds. Small green animals, almost invisible against the leaves, strike inflated throat sacs with their forefeet. There is a persistent, three-note birdcall from the forest margin, answered farther off, then farther again. At midday the sounds overlap into a loud, uneven fabric. Nothing seems hurried by it. A lizard on a black rock can spend an hour lowering its eyelid while the marsh shouts around it.

The loudest water is not in the valley but at its edge.

Several channels reach the rim and spill over. They should become immense waterfalls, dropping through miles of cold, empty air. Instead, a few yards beyond the rock, the water slows. It gathers into hanging lengths, thick as tree trunks, with rounded ends. Droplets separate reluctantly and remain nearby. Sunlight passes through these suspended falls and lays shifting bands on the cliff.

From the viewing ledge, you can hear the water continuing to pour into them: a close, forceful roar with no distant crash beneath it.

Over months, each pendant length swells and bends inward. Eventually its tip touches a lower shelf, where the water resumes an ordinary downhill course through a chain of small basins. Old mineral ribs mark where previous falls have rested. Some are dry and covered in shrubs. Others have become tunnels through which warm water moves out of sight.

This is how the valley keeps most of what it receives.

Beyond those shelves, the cliff falls cleanly away. Plants stop. A stone thrown outward drops, then seems to hesitate at a distance difficult to judge, then disappears so quickly that the eye cannot follow. The air at the lip has a sharp, unpleasant dryness. Flying animals avoid it. Even the large gliding birds turn back while their shadows are still on safe ground.

There are a few buildings.

One sits on a low island in the central lake, reachable by a causeway submerged ankle-deep. Its roof is a single pale slab, slightly bowed, with shallow dishes worn into its upper surface. Rain collects there. Birds bathe in it. Inside, the building is cool enough that sweat stops running, though there is no discernible source of cooling.

The rooms are empty except for benches joined seamlessly to the walls. In one room, a rectangular opening looks out onto the valley from a much higher vantage than the building occupies. Through it, you can see the roof on which the birds are bathing. Clouds passing over the island appear in the opening several breaths before their shadows arrive outside.

The glass, if it is glass, is scratched near the floor. Something has used that corner as a den.

Farther north are pieces of a road. Its surface emerges from the turf for a hundred yards, disappears beneath a grove, returns on the opposite bank of a pool. Roots do not crack it. Instead they spread flat across it, thickening sideways. Here and there, the road is still dry after rain. Warm droplets roll to its edges and wait there in shining rows until enough have joined to fall into the soil.

No settlement surrounds these remnants. There are no broad clearings, no fields, no regular noise of work. The structures stand far apart, absorbed into the routes of animals and the habits of water.

In the late afternoon, the escarpment casts a long shadow over the eastern terraces. The upper slopes remain copper-bright while the marsh turns blue-green. Grazers climb out of the deeper pools and settle on the warm mud. Their breathing tubes release small flags of vapor. Birds work patiently along their backs, pulling insects from the rooted mats.

Below the rim, a storm advances across a distant mountain range. Its upper cloud rises almost to the valley’s lowest shelves. For a time, lightning flickers inside it at eye level.

Up here, rain begins without wind. Large drops fall widely spaced from a cloud that has been forming over the forest since morning. They strike leaves, lake water, the pale roof of the island building. The smell of hot stone lifts from the paths.

By darkness, the rain has become steady. The lakes rise by less than the width of a reed stem. Water enters the hanging falls and waits there, held against the vast drop, while the seven dark towers stand along the rim with rain running up their sides.
