The plateau is high enough that the horizon falls below your feet. Beyond its southern lip, the air is the color of heated tin; lower ranges appear through it as faint parallel ridges, each one flatter and less substantial than the last. Nothing moves down there that you can resolve. Up here, the light is plain and merciless. It shows every grain.

The ground consists of pale ceramic shale, broken into plates and worn almost smooth. The plates overlap along low terraces that might take an hour to cross. Between them lie shallow basins of iron beads, each bead polished on one side, matte on the other. A steady wind rolls them a fraction of a turn, stops, tries again. Over centuries they have cut tracks in the basin floors. Some tracks are deep enough to catch a boot.

There is almost no loose sand. Anything fine enough to lift has long since gone over the edge.

Heat comes from both directions. The sun presses on your shoulders, and the stone warms the soles of your feet through whatever separates you from it. In places, the shale has lifted into broad, low blisters. Their undersides glow a faint red in daylight. They do not burst. They rise and settle so slowly that the people who measure such things—if there are people anywhere in this world—would need to leave and return to be certain of the movement.

The sound makes certainty difficult.

It is a continuous heavy rattling, with a bass note beneath it that you feel in your teeth. Sometimes a higher whine gathers far to the east and travels across the plateau, passing through your body before fading in the west. The effect is of immense machinery running in chambers underfoot: loaded shafts, reciprocating masses, fans turning against resistance. Yet the broken edges of the plateau reveal solid rock. There are no rooms, no ducts, no buried installations.

There are animals.

The first one you notice stands six kilometers away, on a rise so gentle that you had mistaken it for part of the animal. It has six legs, widely spaced, and a body like a dark horizontal slab. Its upper surface carries ranks of thin silver vanes. In the hard light they alternate between brilliance and blackness.

For a long time it does nothing.

Then one leg releases its hold on the ground.

The noise is tremendous: a succession of metallic knocks, each one answered by the landscape. The foot rises perhaps a meter. Powder falls from its underside in a straight, narrow curtain. By the time it settles again, you have ceased to think of the motion as a step and begun to experience it as weather.

Others become visible once you know the shape. One lies along a far escarpment with its legs folded beneath it. Two stand on opposite sides of a basin, connected by nothing you can see, although their vanes tilt in exactly opposing increments. Another is so distant that only its waste heat distinguishes it from the horizon.

They are few, widely separated. There may be ten within sight.

Their young are everywhere.

Look closely at the ceramic ground and its pale stippling resolves into backs: creatures smaller than sesame seeds, embedded flush with the stone, exposing only a hard dorsal plate. Each has bored a precise socket for itself. Around every socket is a ring of black abrasion. They remain there for decades, rasping minerals out of the sides and passing the residue upward through a pore.

What seemed a clean, empty surface is occupied almost without interruption.

The rattling comes partly from them. A million tiny cutters engaging, disengaging, engaging again. They do not eat one another. Their disputes concern gradients: a favorable difference in temperature between one end of a body and the other, a narrow seam of ore, an orientation that lets waste heat escape. A change of a few degrees can set a whole acre grinding louder.

In the shade of a raised plate, the sockets are empty. The shells have opened and their occupants crawled away. You can follow the evacuation in a fine combing of the dust, a migration perhaps three fingers wide, extending for hundreds of meters. At its leading edge, the migrants lie packed together, waiting for the sun to move.

There are no leaves, no stems, no place where the eye can rest on green. A rusty film survives around certain vents, thickening only where a trace of vapor condenses before dawn. By noon it has drawn itself beneath the mineral crust. Its annual expansion could be covered by a fingernail. The animals scrape past it without feeding.

They live on the passage of heat through matter. Their bodies contain ceramic sleeves, copper-colored muscle, soft seals folded deep where the light cannot reach. The largest have rotating organs. Through openings under their flanks, you can see the slow turn of concentric rings, wet-looking with a dark lubricant that gives off no vapor even in this heat. Electrical discharge moves between the vanes on their backs in thick blue threads.

The whole plateau smells faintly of hot bearings.

At first the machinery seems an analogy. After several hours, that distinction becomes less useful. A joint tightens to accommodate wear. A damaged vane drops free, and beneath it a smaller replacement has already unfolded. The fallen part lies where it lands, sterile and gleaming for a season, before the socket animals begin to perforate it. There are old vanes scattered at great intervals across the plateau, reduced to delicate mineral lace.

No one has built anything here. Manufacture is something the fauna do inside themselves.

Farther inland, the terraces bend around an enormous depression. Its floor is empty except for three standing animals and their shadows.

There are too many shadows.

Some lie directly beneath their owners. Others extend in directions that have nothing to do with the sun. A few cross the basin without any visible body attached. They move at the same patient rate as the animals, stopping for hours with one leg raised.

A shadow reaches the basin wall and continues up it. Where it passes, the stone becomes momentarily transparent. Inside the cliff are more animals, standing upright in clear air, their feet planted on a surface that intersects the visible world at an angle. Their plateau extends through yours. Its daylight is warmer, almost orange.

Then the shadow passes, and the cliff is stone again.

These crossings account for much of the noise. They also explain why an apparently vacant stretch of ground may be impossible to enter. You walk toward it and find yourself climbing, although the surface remains level. The effort increases. Your knees lift higher. After twenty steps you are breathing hard in the thin, hot air, and the place you meant to reach is still precisely as far away.

Something is standing there.

You must wait for it to finish standing there, which may take longer than you have.

Across the region, invisible herds occupy slopes that only occasionally coincide with light. Their bodies pass through the same volume without touching, but their heat leaks across. This produces the shimmering columns seen at noon, each rooted in bare ground. Their calls arrive together. A low mechanical bellow swells from an empty basin; another answers from above it; a third seems to come from the interior of your own chest.

The visible ten are a small sample of an immense congregation.

Evening takes a long time. The sun lowers, the iron beads cast little shadows, and the plateau begins surrendering the heat it has stored. The socket animals quieten in patches. Their grinding gives way to the slower knocks of the adults, which spread their vanes to expose a second surface, dark and finely ribbed.

For a while, the other plateaus become easier to see.

They hang through this one at shallow angles, broad weathered planes carrying their own escarpments, their own basins, their widely spaced animals. One passes overhead so low that you can make out the worn undersides of its shale plates. Another descends through the southern horizon. Distance opens in several directions at once.

There is no sudden transformation. A ridge acquires a second edge. A foot settles where there was only air. The loudest sound gradually becomes the nearest.

As the heat drains out of the surface, one of the great animals turns toward you. The movement takes most of the remaining daylight. Under the front of its body, recessed between cooling fins, a cluster of small apertures opens.

It is looking across the plateau.

Perhaps at you. Perhaps through you, toward a favorable seam of stone on a slope you will never see. Its apertures adjust one by one. Behind you, far beneath the ground, something answers with three measured knocks.

The animal waits until the last vibration has gone, then begins to lift another foot.
