The basin occupies the broken summit of a mountain. It is no wider than a small village, and from its rim the slopes fall away into cloud. Yet the water above it is deep enough to extinguish daylight.

It does not spill over.

At the edge, the water stands upright: a dark, trembling wall through which the neighboring peaks can sometimes be seen, stretched thin or doubled. Clouds drag against its outer face and come away in shreds. Inside, a pebble knocked loose from the summit falls toward the basin floor. Outside, the same pebble would fall for miles. Along the rim there is a narrow place where it cannot decide. Small stones turn there for days, striking one another with the hurried clatter of teeth.

The sound is enormous. It has no quiet intervals, only changes in pressure and character. Deep water thunders overhead. Cracks in the mountain whistle. Somewhere beyond the visible ceiling, whole currents collide with reports like cliffs coming down, followed by a grinding that seems to move through the bones of the summit rather than through the water. Near the bottom, the roar becomes a continuous battering. Even a hand laid flat against stone feels it: countless impacts, too close together to distinguish.

The basin itself is young. Its black walls have split along clean planes, leaving corners sharp enough to shave translucent curls from whatever brushes past. There is little sediment. The hollows contain pale stone flour, but each surge lifts it before it can settle into anything like a bed. Long fractures show fresh pink interiors. Some are so recent that the two faces still fit each other perfectly, though several feet of water now force them apart.

Heat comes through these openings.

The lower water is warm, almost bodily, with hotter ribbons that wander across the floor and bend upward. Their passage makes the dim world buckle. A ledge appears to fold; an overhang draws away and then returns. Most of these distortions are heat. A few remain after the hot water has passed.

There are six or seven substantial rocks on the basin floor. It is difficult to keep the count. They stand far apart, each with a wide vacancy around it, as though their arrival displaced more than water. One resembles a square pillar broken at waist height. Another is a black slab balanced on a corner, rocking slowly despite the violence around it. Beneath that slab lies a patch of perfect stillness. Fine white grains hang there in an orderly cloud, neither rising nor sinking. The edge of the patch cuts straight across the current. Outside it, grit races past fast enough to score the rock.

There are traces of shelter.

Three low walls occupy a shelf above the deepest hollow, made from flat stones fitted without mortar. They enclose spaces scarcely large enough for a curled body. Their roofs are gone, if they ever had roofs. Nothing resembling a path connects them, and the stones show no chisel marks. They might have been stacked by hands, or sorted into place by a current whose habits have since changed.

The tallest wall has an opening at its base. Water rushes toward it from both sides. Neither stream comes through.

At intervals the opening gives out a heavy, low note, barely distinguishable within the general uproar. Then the fitted stones draw fractionally closer together. The gaps shrink. A little burst of white powder escapes from the joints. Whatever holds the wall together is still tightening it.

Vegetation survives only in the lee of such things. Behind one wall, a few brown straps grow from a seam, thick and smooth, with no branching. They lie flattened most of the time. When the current reverses they rise together, revealing pale attachment discs pressed into the stone. Each strap ends in a blunt, swollen tip. New growth is almost colorless.

Elsewhere, green exists as a stain in protected cracks. It never spreads across an exposed face. The water scours too hard, and the light is too uncertain.

Above, what should be the surface is seldom visible. There is instead a dim, mobile brightness at an impossible height. Occasionally it descends in a broad sag until it hangs just above the summit rocks, bringing with it the blurred impression of a cloudy sky. Then it withdraws so rapidly that the whole basin lurches upward after it. Loose stones leap from their hollows. The brown straps pull taut. From the mountain’s outer face, visible through the upright water, a brief avalanche rises rather than falls.

For several breaths the water is full of stone.

Then weight returns.

These changes leave the floor newly arranged. A block tips onto a different face; a fresh splinter lands upright in a crack. The few old surfaces are easy to recognize by their dullness, but they grow rarer. The place is continually exposing its inside.

There is little animal life. Once in a long while, a pale, narrow creature passes close to the bottom, keeping all its fins folded against its body. It does not swim so much as choose a current and become rigid within it. Only at the last instant before striking a wall does it flex, slipping sideways into another flow. Its eyes are dark and crowded near the top of its head. It carries scars along both flanks.

Smaller things may live among the standing stones. There are pinholes in the pale seams and, sometimes, fresh pellets caught behind a ridge. Nothing ventures far enough out to be seen. Food arrives violently: a torn insect wing, a knot of roots, once a whole branch with warm rain still trapped beneath its bark. Such debris circles the basin until it is driven into a fissure or thrown out through the vertical boundary. Little stays whole.

The water itself seems to arrive from several places.

One current tastes of salt and carries a faint blue light. Another brings the mineral warmth of deep springs. A third is cold, brown, and crowded with flecks of bark, though there is no forest on the mountain. They twist through one another without always mixing. Where two incompatible streams touch, a thin skin forms between them, silver in the gloom. It swells into a hollow shape, holds for a moment, then bursts with a concussion that strikes every wall at once.

Some of these skins harden before they break. Their fragments fall slowly: curved, milky plates, too brittle to survive a second collision. A few collect inside the roofless shelters. Against the black stone they look like pieces of enormous eggs.

Near the outer rim, the footing narrows to a ridge with water on one side and open altitude on the other. Looking out, one sees cloud, distant slopes, perhaps a strip of evening far below. Looking inward, there is only the little flooded summit, its scattered blocks, its three shelters, its handful of battered plants. It should be possible to see all of it at once.

But the deepest hollow is usually farther away than the basin is wide.

It shifts during the heaviest surges, receding between two nearby rocks until those rocks frame a darkness large enough to contain another mountain. Warm water pours out of it. The roar acquires a slower rhythm there, a vast dragging pulse beneath the crashes and whistles. Objects carried toward the hollow become difficult to follow. Their movement remains rapid, but their arrival takes longer and longer.

Eventually the current turns. The hollow comes close again. It is only a shallow depression, bare and angular, with a thumb-sized pebble trembling at its center.

For a few moments the summit can be understood: fresh rock, warm water, sparse growth gripping the cracks. The walls stand where they stood. A pale animal waits behind a stone.

Then the dim ceiling bows downward, every fracture begins to whistle, and the pebble lifts.
