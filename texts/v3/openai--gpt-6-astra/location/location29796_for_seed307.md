Near the summit, where the mountain is still too new to have scree beneath its cliffs, a flake of black rock has lifted without breaking away. Behind it is a wet space no wider than a cupped hand.

The opening faces the morning. For an hour, ordinary sunlight enters sideways, showing the amber depths of droplets, the white grains in the rock, the tiny shadows beneath moving feet. Beyond the opening there are other summits and, far below, a cloud layer so level it looks solid. Nothing grows on the exposed stone. The wind up there can strip warmth from flesh almost immediately.

Inside the flake, water is nearly boiling.

It comes out of the mountain along a seam fine as a hair. There is no spring basin, only a succession of bulges: water swelling against the hot mineral, holding, becoming too heavy, slipping down to the next irregularity. The rock is so fresh that some of its edges still part a droplet cleanly. Elsewhere a skin of pale salt has already rounded them. The landscape is being made and softened at once, though the making is ahead.

Under the largest bead is a hollow with a floor of translucent grit. Several ledges overhang it. A splinter leaning across the hollow divides the light, and behind that splinter the space goes farther inward than the thickness of the mountain’s outer flake should allow.

This is where most of the animals are.

At first they resemble imperfections in the water. Look longer and the clear grains have legs; the legs have passengers; the passengers are opening and shutting fans. Pale bodies slide through the narrow interval between water and stone. Segmented things the color of diluted egg yolk brace themselves against the current with their mouths. Soft, many-footed grazers crowd the mineral crust, scraping something too thin to see.

There are no leaves, no roots, no green even in the cooler corners. A greasy discoloration around the hottest seep is the pasture. The grazers remove it in overlapping arcs, exposing fresh black crescents that slowly turn bronze again.

Everything has something living on it.

The larger animals are scarcely longer than a fingernail clipping, but their backs carry dense populations. Some passengers feed on secretions. Others pick eggs from the joints. A few hold their bodies out into the water and catch what passes. When a host turns over, its inhabitants shift together from the drowned side to the exposed side, a pale crowd flowing over a hill.

Almost none of this makes a sound.

Occasionally a claw releases stone with a tick. A bubble breaks with the smallest possible wet click. The constant water does not fall far enough to splash. It slides, adheres, stretches, gathers. Even the predators work quietly, pressing prey against a mineral edge until movement ceases.

The hollow has several kinds of height.

There is the height outside, measurable in the blue distance beneath the summit. There is the height from the grit floor to the underside of the flake, crossed by animals with hooked feet. And there is a third height that appears when the water becomes sufficiently hot.

Then a creature climbing inward begins to diminish.

It does not recede behind anything. Its feet remain visible against the same patch of stone, yet each step makes it smaller. Soon it has the proportions of an animal seen across a ravine. Behind it, within a darkness that a fingertip could cover, other slopes become apparent: wet shelves, narrow precipices, crowded pale migrations ascending toward a point much higher than the mountain.

The first time this happens, the hollow seems to open.

After watching several cycles, it becomes clear that it was open already. The cooling water had merely prevented anything from getting very far.

Heat arrives in pulses. The seam darkens; fresh water presses through; the innermost grit lifts and settles. Animals waiting beside the seam begin to move before any visible change. Some hurry outward. Others turn toward the deep height and climb. The smallest are carried away by the expanding wetness, revolving slowly with their legs folded close.

There is a low ledge where the animals leave their young.

It is crowded with transparent capsules, some attached directly to the stone, others clinging to abandoned skins. Each contains a jointed shape wrapped around a bright bead. The bead shrinks as the animal develops. Near hatching, almost nothing remains except an unusually brilliant point between the folded limbs.

Adults visit these capsules with flakes of mineral held in their mouths. They use the flakes to lever open clogged channels and guide cooler water over the ledge. A tool may be used once and dropped, then taken up by another animal approaching from the opposite direction. Nothing is fitted together. Nothing persists as a structure. The little slivers circulate among mouths, currents, and cracks.

Along the ledge’s outer edge, a predator waits upside down.

Its body is broad and flat, nearly invisible against wet rock. Around its rim are dozens of fine legs, each ending in a dark hook. Its mouth lies in the center of its back. When prey moves across it, the whole animal cups gently upward. Water closes over the struggling shape. A moment later the predator is flat again.

But the opening in its back does not always close at once. Sometimes it shows daylight: a tiny, sharp view of a white cloud far beneath the summit. Smaller animals gather around the opening and drink. One occasionally steps through. The predator neither turns nor seems to notice.

By midday the sun has passed the entrance. Light remains plentiful but indirect, reflected up from the clouds. The black stone takes on a blue sheen. Condensation thickens on the cooler outer lip, and the exposed edge becomes the busiest part of the place.

Animals arrive from inside bearing heat in their bodies. They crowd into the hanging drops until the drops are cloudy with them. Their limbs move slowly now. Some shed a transparent skin. Some divide along seams that were not there a moment before. Others unfold wings too soft for the wind outside and use them to paddle.

Eventually a drop falls.

For an instant there is a clear gap beneath the lip. Then the remaining water advances, and the crowd closes around it.

Far below, the falling drop should freeze.

Within the hollow, a new bead swells from the hottest seam. In its center, a small tangle of animals uncurls. Their bodies are rimmed with frost. The frost vanishes before they reach the surface, and they scatter across the fresh stone, joining the feeding, the climbing, the patient work at the nursery ledge.

Outside, the summit casts a long shadow over the clouds. Inside, another mineral edge cracks under the heat. The sound is faint but unusually sharp. Every animal touching that piece of rock stops.

Water enters the crack.

A few of the grazers turn toward it.
