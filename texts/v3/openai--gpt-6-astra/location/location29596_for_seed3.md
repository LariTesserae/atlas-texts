The city stands above the weather, but not above its light. Clouds rise around it in long, stationary walls, their upper surfaces brighter than the streets. Between them are glimpses of lower mountain ranges, blue and very small. Nothing in those glimpses moves fast enough to be seen.

Up here, the ground is still being made.

It arrives beneath the city as pale stone, almost white, with a fine grain like broken porcelain. There are no rounded boulders. Even the smallest loose fragments have sharp edges, and the cliffs descend in clean planes that have not yet acquired the stains of running water. In some districts the rock is translucent for its first few years. Looking down through a pavement crack, you can see the dark foundations of buildings descending into it, along with trapped bubbles, old drill bits, and roots growing toward things that have not become opaque.

The stone does not rise evenly. A broad shelf may gain the thickness of a fingernail in a month, while a neighboring ridge takes thirty years to lift itself clear of a railway. This is slow enough for ordinary life and fast enough to require an entire municipal department.

There are survey marks on every substantial wall.

The city extends across five high shelves and several pieces of terrain that have not yet joined them. Its density is mostly in the buildings themselves: enormous residential blocks, turbine halls, warehouses with four levels of loading platforms. Between these are distances one would expect to find outside a city. A tram leaves a crowded station and crosses half a mile of bare mineral ground before reaching the next building. Streetlights continue through these intervals, though many illuminate nothing but their own concrete bases and the small animals sheltering behind them.

From a distance the settlement resembles a collection of industrial objects set out to cool.

Closer in, there is considerable intricacy. Walkways hang beneath other walkways. Freight elevators travel up the outside of apartment houses. Cable bundles sag across lanes too narrow for vehicles, and hot pipes enter walls through collars packed with dark felt. The oldest buildings have several ground floors, each with a blocked doorway and a different date cut into its lintel. The newest have foundations that can be unfastened.

On the northern shelf, an unfinished avenue ends against a smooth stone face. The tram rails turn upward with it. They were laid level, and remain level according to every instrument the city possesses. Drivers stop at the last shelter anyway.

The air is cool enough that metal handrails hurt bare fingers. It is seldom severely cold. Somewhere beneath the fresh rock, heat is being produced in quantities that cannot be predicted from depth. Cellars sometimes become warm rooms; warm rooms sometimes become cellars again. The heating authority keeps a network of movable exchangers, sending crews out in small yellow locomotives to connect whatever portion of the ground is giving heat that decade.

Above all this hangs a dim, persistent illumination. The sun is generally hidden behind the cloud walls or behind a taller elevation whose location is disputed. At noon there is enough light to distinguish the green of leaves from the black of wet machinery. By late afternoon both become textures. Windows, signal lamps, and the red seams of foundry doors take over the task of describing distance.

Water is scarce in the open and plentiful in particular corners.

A stairwell may be wet for its entire height while the square outside remains dry for weeks. Condensation gathers on certain varieties of new stone, forming beads that roll uphill until they encounter a crack. Rain comes sideways from the cloud walls, briefly and without a change in the sky. People stop beneath awnings, wait through the soft ticking on sheet metal, and emerge before the pavement has darkened all the way across.

Every roof is a collector. Gutters feed tanks, tanks feed public laundries, and the laundries release their cooled water into narrow planted channels running between the buildings. These channels are among the most crowded places in the city.

There are ferns with thick, blunt fronds, low shrubs wearing a gloss of condensation, and cushions of tiny leaves so closely packed that fallen screws remain on their surface. Vines grow along warm return pipes and spread across walls in heavy panels. Their stems are pale under the foliage. Where a pipe has been disconnected, the leaves gradually turn silver and remain attached for several seasons.

Away from these watered corridors, vegetation occurs in islands: a lush hollow behind a switching station, a thicket beneath a leaking reservoir, a single deep-green tree in an acre of unfinished stone. The islands are separated by such wide stretches of bareness that each has its own visible weather. Mist rests in one. Another shakes with birds. A third is perfectly still, though its leaves are all turned in the same direction.

There are animals everywhere, but seldom distributed evenly enough to make the landscape feel full.

The bare squares belong to long-legged grazers with gray coats and narrow heads. They cross them in groups of six or seven, hooves making small, distinct clicks. They eat the mineral film that appears on new rock before plants can establish themselves. In the evening they lick the survey marks, preferring blue paint. Their young sleep in the sheltered angles of loading ramps.

Birds occupy the city in much greater numbers. They nest in the warm backs of transformers, in disused ventilation shafts, and along cliff ledges scarcely wider than their feet. When a factory changes shifts, the whistle lifts thousands from one district while birds in the next remain settled. For a few moments a whole building seems to shed its surface.

There are also small, heavy-bodied creatures in the water channels, with smooth backs and hands that close around stems. They are heard more often than seen: a splash beneath the ferns, the knocking of a shell against a culvert. Maintenance workers leave escape boards in every drained basin. Without them, the creatures gather at the lowest point and make a noise like a roomful of people moving chairs.

At night, moths collect beneath the streetlamps. Some have wings larger than an open hand. They move reluctantly, walking up the poles rather than flying, and their bodies carry a fine pale dust from the cliffs. Children learn not to brush it into their eyes. It continues to crystallize when wet.

The factories are loud, though nothing in them appears hurried. Presses descend once every several minutes. Conveyor chains drag with a steady, spacious clatter. A hammer on the eastern shelf can be heard across the whole basin, followed much later by an answering impact from somewhere below.

Not every answer is an echo.

The acoustic surveys have identified places where a sound returns before it is made. These areas are fenced with ordinary wire and marked on workers’ maps. Near one of them, beside a public reservoir, you can hear a door close and then watch someone approach it carrying groceries. The interval is usually a few seconds. On damp evenings it can be longer.

People avoid speaking names there.

The industrial works produce cables, bearings, pump housings, structural brackets, and the wide articulated shoes fitted beneath heavy buildings. Much of the city’s labor goes into allowing the city to remain where it is. Teams check the tension of bridges. Crews loosen bolts around courtyards. Whole apartment blocks are raised a fraction on hydraulic jacks while the ground beneath them is shaved flat.

The removed stone is stacked in yards. It cannot immediately be used for construction. Some pieces grow back toward the shape they had underground, slowly pressing apart their storage racks. Others lose weight without becoming smaller. Mature material is identified by leaving a plumb line beside it through the winter. If the line still hangs toward the ground in spring, the stone is released for sale.

Beyond the last occupied shelf, the terrain becomes less certain.

There are ridges with no opposite slope, thin from one direction and broad enough to support a meadow from another. Paths across them are maintained by walking, not by grading: a route neglected for too long may shorten, or acquire an extra turn around a rock that was previously elsewhere. The postal service is responsible for several remote footpaths because its carriers are the only people who use them regularly.

Farther out stand unfinished peaks. Their upper portions are soft-edged, not with mist but with an absence of detail. Snow cannot settle there yet. Birds approaching them veer aside without alarm, as if avoiding a familiar window.

These peaks darken very gradually. Over a lifetime, one may acquire a definite shoulder. A notch appears. A face becomes capable of holding a shadow. Then the first lichen establishes itself in a crease, and that portion of the mountain is considered dependable.

There are small houses near this frontier, connected to the city by power lines so long that their sag disappears into cloud. Around each house is a tank, a garden, a shed full of adjustable tools. The residents listen through pipes driven into the ground. Sometimes they hear cracking. Sometimes water. Sometimes the unmistakable passage of a freight train in a place where no tunnel has been cut.

Toward evening, the city’s separate shelves become harder to distinguish. Lit tramcars move across the emptiness between buildings, their windows briefly revealing passengers in coats, a basket on someone’s knees, a child asleep against the glass. Under the tracks, grazers settle beside the warm supports. Birds pack themselves into the eaves. The watered gardens release a faint green smell into the cold.

A press comes down in a distant hall.

For a while afterward there is the machinery, the dripping gutters, the restless life beneath the leaves. Then, from deep in the newest cliff, comes the sound of the press coming down again.

On a wall beside a closed bakery, a survey needle trembles. It takes most of the night to move one mark.
