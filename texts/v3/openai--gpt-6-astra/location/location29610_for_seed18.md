The garden is the inside of a hollow rivet.

It hangs beneath the lip of an elevated machine, so high that weather usually passes below it. Through its lower opening you can see the backs of clouds, their folds lit an ordinary afternoon white. Sometimes they separate and reveal another layer of distance, blue and without detail. There is no visible ground. The rivet’s function, and the shape of the structure it fastens, cannot be understood from here. You have only its interior: a chamber wide enough for a modest chapel, rounded at the top, open at the bottom, with a narrow maintenance ledge winding twice around the wall.

Almost all of it is empty.

There is no central platform, no trunk, no suspended apparatus to give the eye a resting place. A loose leaf can travel a long way before touching anything. The ledge occupies so little of the chamber that from certain angles it disappears behind its own rail. Across the gulf, vegetation lies close to the metal in a deep green lining, following seams and rivet heads, leaving the volume between the walls entirely clear.

The plants are luxuriant where they are allowed to grow. Small, thick leaves overlap until you cannot see their stems. Ferns spread sideways from the inspection slots. Pale root mats fill the recessed channels once intended to carry cable, and hanging stems descend from them in close, orderly fringes, their tips all cut off at roughly the same height. Nothing grows out toward the middle. The empty air there has a faint shimmer.

The chamber is warm. Putting a hand against the wall is like touching a pipe some distance from a boiler: no immediate sting, but after a few moments the heat becomes uncomfortable. Behind the greenery, the metal continues upward into an inaccessible network of working machinery. Its heat arrives evenly, without regard for sunrise or season. Only the lower rail cools at night.

What little water there is comes through the wall.

At irregular intervals, a patch of steel darkens. Droplets appear around a bolt or along the folded edge of a plate. They remain there for hours, swelling. Roots have found every dependable leak, and their fine white ends lie in crescents just beneath the places where water gathers. A few droplets escape into shallow channels worn along the ledge. These feed three little basins, each no larger than a cupped hand, before disappearing into fibrous growth.

There is no soil. Under the leaves are layers of root, discarded insect casing, rust scale, and the black felt that once insulated the chamber. In exposed places the felt has the texture of old bread. Plants enter it through holes punched to a standard spacing. The oldest roots have enlarged those holes, splitting the material without pulling it free.

Everything underfoot was made.

The ledge is cast iron surfaced with a gritty ceramic coating. Most of that coating has worn away, leaving islands beneath the rail posts. Its inner edge has become round and polished, not from feet alone but from decades of water, vibration, and the passage of small animals. The rail bends inward where its attachments have slowly yielded. No edge is quite straight. Even the stamped numbers on the wall have softened, their lower halves obscured by mineral deposits.

The place is old enough that its damage has acquired a dependable shape. There is a particular depression where a foot fits. A particular joint leaks. One plate expands every afternoon and closes a hairline gap that opens again after dark. Moss occupies the gap but never crosses it.

The sound is overwhelming.

It comes through the metal rather than through the opening: a continuous, heavy concussion, too closely repeated to hear as separate blows. Beneath it is a lower note that presses against the chest. Every railing carries it. Every leaf trembles, though almost too finely to see. There is no quiet corner. Covering your ears changes the noise from something around you to something inside your jaw.

At first it seems impossible that anything could live here. Then you notice that most of the life is already touching the wall.

Insects move through the leaves on broad, adhesive feet. Tiny lizards lie flattened along the warm seams, their throats pulsing against the metal. There are snails with low, heavily ridged shells and pale beetles gathered beneath the basin rims. Nothing calls loudly enough to be heard. Courtship, warning, and territorial dispute pass through stems and plates as patterns of tapping. A lizard lifts one forefoot, waits, and sets it down three times. Several inches away, under a leaf, another answers.

The birds are harder to see.

They nest behind the loose upper flashing, in spaces no human hand can reach. Their nests are shallow pads of clipped fern fiber. Their feathers are the color of the insulation. Occasionally one crosses the chamber, making a fast, deliberate descent before catching the air along the opposite wall. They never hover in the center.

A feather dropped there does not immediately fall.

It shakes open, turns edgewise, and rests in the air. Then it moves upward a little, sideways a little, as if feeling for a stair in darkness. The chamber’s roar holds certain light things at certain heights. You can discover these levels by watching pollen collect in faint horizontal veils. Most days there are two. In hotter weather a third appears near the top, and the birds begin building beneath it.

The plants know these levels too. Their hanging tips stop just above the lowest one. New growth sometimes ventures farther, trembles for a day, and breaks cleanly off. The fragments remain suspended until they dry. Later they drift to the wall, where insects take them apart.

This is why the middle stays empty. It is not unused, exactly. It is occupied by a force that provides no foothold.

Light enters through the bottom opening and a row of narrow vents near the crown. It is sufficient, undramatic light. The leaves nearest the opening have pale undersides that turn outward over the rail. Higher up, the ferns are darker and broader, catching what is reflected from the curved wall. In the middle of the day, the empty chamber is brighter than its planted edges.

Rain is rare at this height. When a storm rises far enough to reach the garden, the opening becomes gray and droplets strike upward against the ledge. The birds retreat. The roots darken. Water runs down surfaces that have been dry for months, finds the same old grooves, and vanishes through the bottom. For several days afterward the chamber smells of wet iron and crushed leaves.

Otherwise, change is slow. A fern uncurls beside a number no longer legible. A root thickens around a washer. The deepest basin gains another thin rim of pale deposit. Somewhere far above, engines continue at a pace too regular to count.

Standing on the ledge, you feel almost no wind. The railing is warm beneath your palms. Across the open space, one small animal moves out from under a leaf and settles against the wall, and the leaf returns slowly to its former position. Below, a cloud crosses the opening without touching it.
