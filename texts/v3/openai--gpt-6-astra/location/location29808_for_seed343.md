By noon the ground is warm enough to lie on, though the clouds are far below.

They collect along the southern edge of the plateau, hiding whatever lies beneath it. Sometimes a rent opens and shows a slope descending through several climates: first the pale apron of lichens, then dark forest, then distance too blue to read. Nothing visible establishes a bottom. Up here the horizon is almost level. The mountains have had time to lose their peaks.

What remains is a country of long, shallow swells, broad basins, and passes that scarcely rise above the surrounding land. Crossing one takes half a day. From a distance the surface looks upholstered in faded green velvet, interrupted by ocher patches and the silver of turned leaves. There are no forests to give the distances a familiar measure. A moving speck may be an animal nearby or an entire herd on the next rise.

Almost everything underfoot is alive.

The dominant plant grows sideways, making leaves so small and closely fitted that they resemble scales. Its stems fork, rejoin, and root into older stems. Beneath them is a thickness of living wood, old fiber, trapped dust, and slowly digested ancestors. In places this covering is deeper than a person is tall. Elsewhere a heel presses it against stone. The difference is difficult to see, and experienced walkers test unfamiliar ground with a long forked stick.

The stone itself appears only at wind-scoured lips and in old drainage cuts. It is smooth, brown, and without much fracture. A handful taken from a streambed could be mistaken for something shaped by use. Even the cliffs have rounded shoulders; their height survives, but their sharpness does not.

The plants have inherited those worn forms and softened them further. They bridge fissures. They roof narrow gullies. They climb over boulders until the boulders become nothing more than firmer places in the floor. Some of the broad green flats are actually stretched across hollows, their load carried by interlaced woody runners. The oldest crossings have paths worn into them by generations of hoof and foot.

Under these roofs there is open air.

A torn edge reveals galleries with floors of powdered fiber and ceilings bristling with roots. Some are only high enough for a crawling animal; others admit a person standing upright. Their air smells of warm cork, dung, and the faint sourness of germinating seed. Light comes through little holes overhead. At midday each hole draws a bright shaft through the drifting dust.

This is where much of the landscape’s enormous noise is made.

The first impression is of running machinery, although there is no machinery here: a continuous metallic rasp, rising into shrieks, falling into a coarse rattling that can be felt through the soles. Winged insects cling beneath the living roof and call into its hollow chambers. Different colonies occupy different lengths of gallery. One produces a low, pulsing note; another answers with a much higher whine. The sound travels through the rootwork as well as the air.

Above ground, it becomes difficult to tell where any particular noise begins. Speech is useful only at close range. People on opposite sides of a hollow get each other’s attention by stamping.

The plateau looks empty until something disturbs it.

Then a patch of green releases thousands of tiny pale bodies. They lift together, turn once in the sun, and settle a few yards away, leaving the air full of powder from their wings. A dark seam along a ridge resolves into lizards warming flank to flank. What seemed a dust-colored stain begins moving uphill: beetles, each carrying a fragment of shed leaf. There are grazing larvae beneath the surface scales, mites on the larvae, hunting insects in the seams, birds following the hunters.

Most of this life fits into the thickness of the ground. Above it stretches an extravagant volume of vacant sky.

The larger animals are sparse but never absent for long. Long-legged grazers travel in loose files, putting their narrow feet into old depressions in the mat. They feed without stopping, plucking shoots from either side of the route. Small birds ride between their shoulders, then drop off to work the freshly exposed stems. At the edge of a herd, low-bodied predators wait with their ears flattened against the insect roar. They watch feet. A grazer that begins favoring one leg soon finds itself being followed.

There is little standing water. Rain comes in brief, widely spaced showers, often while the opposite side of the sky remains clear. The surface darkens, exhales a hot vegetable smell, and drinks almost everything. For an hour afterward, shallow bowls hold water the color of weak tea. Animals gather before these disappear.

The more dependable moisture arrives at night. Low leaves cool under the stars and gather dew. Their overlapping edges lead it inward toward the stems, and the mat retains it well below the reach of the daytime wind. In deep hollows, roots remain damp even after weeks without rain. A few seepages emerge beneath the plateau’s outer lip and fall into the clouds in threads too fine to reach the ground intact.

The people who cross this country build near those seepages, but not directly beside them. Too many animals need the same water.

Their houses are low, widely separated, and difficult to distinguish from the terrain. Living runners are lifted over bent wooden ribs and allowed to knit together again. The result is a green-backed shelter with a dark entrance and a little collar of woven reed to keep the threshold open. Inside, the floor is raised on bundles of dry stalks. Scrapers, digging wedges, water gourds, and lengths of braided fiber hang from pegs driven into the supporting frame.

Keeping a house requires cutting. Left unattended, its walls slowly thicken inward. The doorway narrows. Fine roots descend toward cooking pots and sleeping mats. An abandoned dwelling does not fall down so much as become unavailable.

A few still have their doorways maintained by animals.

Paths are marked with pale stones, since anything made of wood eventually roots, rots, or disappears under growth. At dangerous crossings the stones lie in pairs. Elsewhere they stand singly on little shaved patches of ground. Some are so worn that they must have marked routes before the nearest houses were made, but the routes remain useful: along the firm backs of ridges, around the breathing roofs of hollows, toward the few places where water can be drawn without digging.

Late in the afternoon, the wind strengthens. The small leaves turn their pale undersides outward, and whole hills change color in a slow wave. Insects lower themselves into shelter. Birds skim just above the surface, feeding rapidly before the temperature falls.

For a moment the roar diminishes enough to reveal other sounds: a grazer tearing a mouthful free, water ticking from a root into a stone basin, the creak of a loaded crossing.

Then the galleries begin their evening calls. The notes gather beneath the ground, pass from hollow to hollow, and rise across the open plateau while the last sunlight remains on its immense, almost level rim.
