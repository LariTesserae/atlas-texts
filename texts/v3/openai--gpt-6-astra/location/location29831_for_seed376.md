From the ridge, the country looks like a succession of pale green valleys filled almost to their brims. Darker ribs separate them. Beyond those are more valleys, rising toward a white horizon where low cloud drags across the uplands. There is enough light to see a long way. The sun, when it comes through, is small and hard, and brings out a silver nap on every exposed surface.

Nothing visible is stone.

The ribs are roots, broad as mountain shoulders, worn smooth along their crests. What seems at first to be scree is a slope of overlapping bark scales. The pale ground between them is a close weave of leaves, most no larger than a fingernail, fitted so tightly that there are few shadows between one plant and the next. Older leaves remain underneath, flattened into dark, flexible layers. Below those are stems, old roots, living roots, and compressed vegetable matter whose lower limit has never been exposed.

The land is high enough that the cold seems to come from the sky and the ground equally. In sheltered places, frost survives through the middle of the day. It outlines the mouths of pores, collects beneath curled leaf edges, and turns broken fibers into little white brushes. There are no tall trees. Height here belongs to the whole growth, not to individuals. A valley floor may be deeper than a forest is tall, but its uppermost leaves barely rise above your ankle.

For a while, standing still, you might mistake this for a sleeping country.

Then a dark line opens across the nearest slope.

It runs downhill faster than you could follow it, forks, and disappears beneath a ridge. On one side of it, the leaves lift their silver undersides to the light. On the other, they flatten. A low swell moves through the valley floor, carrying no water that you can see. It reaches a mound, divides around it, and closes behind. The mound begins to sink.

All this happens with scarcely a sound. A faint rubbing, perhaps, like a palm passed over heavy cloth.

The valleys are always redistributing themselves. Beneath the leaf surface, long muscular roots contract in sequence, moving sap and fine pulp from one basin to another. Hollow stems pressurize, empty, and pressurize again. Whole slopes are supplied through bundles of pale vessels thicker than a person, each vessel divided by regularly spaced valves. Where the ground has split, you can see them working: a slow tightening followed by a brief passage of something dark.

The scale of it suggests pumping works spread across a province. There are parallel runs, reserve channels, swollen storage chambers, places where dozens of conduits gather and separate again. But none of it has been installed. The connections grow toward pressure. Failed channels seal themselves and become reinforcement. New ones open through last year’s dead tissue.

There is no engine note. The machinery is wet, flexible, and buried deep.

Water is scarce on the surface. It arrives mostly as brief showers swept sideways across the ridges, or as condensation when cloud settles into a basin. The leaves bead it carefully. For a few minutes after rain, every depression has a bright point in it; then the points dwindle as the water is taken below. Even the steepest slopes produce little runoff.

Here and there a channel retains an open ribbon of water. Its banks are brown and polished, rounded by a history of small floods. Fine root hairs extend into the current and retract when it freezes. In the deeper pockets, the water is almost black, with a clear skin of ice suspended above it after the level falls. These little reservoirs are the stillest-looking parts of the landscape, although something is always drawing them down from underneath.

The ridges themselves are old. Their broad outlines persist while their substance is continually replaced. Wind has lowered them, frost has split them, and generations of growth have closed the splits without quite erasing them. Long shallow grooves follow the prevailing weather. The outer bark is gray, soft at the edges, and scored with the same patient wear found on an old doorstep, though no foot has made it.

In places the living cover wears through faster than it heals. There, a dry brown basin opens among the greens, its floor a felt of shredded fibers. The cold wind picks up the loose material and carries it a short distance. It does not rattle or scrape. It travels in low, soundless streamers and catches in the damp mouths of new leaves.

Much of the country is being dismantled in this way while the rest is building thickness.

By afternoon, the valley below the ridge has changed markedly. The sinking mound is gone. A broad pleat has risen where there was level ground, and along its top a seam of yellow tissue shines in the ordinary daylight. The seam closes while you watch. Farther away, a patch the size of a meadow turns dark, not gradually but in a series of quick rectangular advances, each bounded by the underground channels feeding it. Its leaves have folded inward against a coming freeze.

Other patches remain open. Still others flush an almost imperceptible red.

From above, these changes make moving patterns across several miles: closures, releases, pulses traveling against the wind. No single wave governs them. One basin swells while its neighbor drains. A ridge sheds a skirt of bark. A chain of shallow hollows puckers shut and forces a new hollow open uphill. The surface is crowded with events, but they rarely break into noise. Even a collapse is caught by what lies beneath it.

Animals are difficult to find. Once, along the margin of a frost hollow, a small white creature emerges from between two root plates. It has a narrow head and dark feet. It stands with its body low, watching the leaves ahead, then crosses in a few quick movements. It chooses the gray patches that have already stiffened in the cold. At the first ripple of green, it stops.

There are no calls to answer it.

Toward evening, the light becomes clearer under the cloud. Every fold has a sharp shadow now, and the distances become easier to judge. What seemed a nearby bank is a ridge several miles away. Between here and there, an entire basin is lifting, slowly enough that you notice it only by the changing line of the horizon behind it.

The ground beneath your feet remains firm. It is a weathered root crest, one of the oldest shapes in the region, and its shallow grooves hold their frost. On either side, leaves close over newly opened cracks. Moisture gathers at their tips. Below, beyond sight, the valves continue their work.

The silence lets you hear a single drop fall into a hollow.

Before another follows, the hollow has narrowed.
