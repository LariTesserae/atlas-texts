Below the last occupied street, the city has an unfinished square.

It is about the size of a small market court, enclosed by retaining walls and roofed with the undersides of roads. Square columns divide it into bays. There are doorways, a stair, the beginnings of a raised pavement, but the center is still excavation: dark clay pressed flat by machinery, interrupted by shallow water. The tracks of the last excavator remain sharp beneath the water. Nothing has had time to wear them away.

The heat comes down through the ceiling and sideways through the pipes. Two district steam mains cross the chamber, their insulation darkened at the joints. Even at night, when the road above loses some of its heat, the air here stays close to forty degrees. A handrail feels as though someone has just let go of it. Water on the floor is warm enough that stepping into it brings no immediate relief.

There is no single large leak. Moisture arrives everywhere in small quantities. It beads along the cold-water main, runs from tie holes in the concrete, and seeps through the exposed clay at the foot of the western wall. Each source has made its own little territory. Beneath one joint lies a round black patch. Beneath another, a fan of pale grit. Along the western wall, several inches of standing water extend into doorways that were meant to become storerooms.

The square was built to service a neighborhood overhead. Around its edges are meter cupboards, cable chambers, a transformer behind mesh, and three large pumps on concrete plinths. Only one pump is connected. The others still wear portions of their shipping wrap, which has become opaque with dust and condensation. Conduit ends hang above places where lamps should be. Numbers painted on the columns are easier to see than the columns themselves.

There are just four working lights.

They leave most of the chamber unlit, but not entirely invisible. A green indicator reflects in the water. A strip of light comes through a road drain and lies across the clay, changing color when a vehicle stops above it. Near the stair, daylight falls through a ventilation grille onto a narrow ledge. The ledge supports two grass clumps and a seedling with long, widely spaced leaves. Its roots have found the damp seam behind a mortar fillet. Fine silt accumulates there whenever the street is washed.

Elsewhere, the growth is lower and less distinct: a slick around a drip, a green stain under the grille, a little fur on a piece of timber left against a wall. Most surfaces remain bare.

The animals make up for it.

At first they are easy to mistake for defects in the construction. A rough edge below a pipe is a row of cockroaches, antennae reaching into the open. What looks like dust around the lamps is a moving cloud of flies. The water has a faint granular motion that persists after the pump stops; on looking closer, almost every square inch contains larvae hanging just beneath the surface.

There are different kinds of water here, and each holds a different crowd. In the shallow, still patches, mosquito larvae bend and straighten. In the darker channels near the drains, pale, soft-bodied things withdraw into silt. Where a trickle drops from the wall, small flies gather on the wet concrete, packed close enough to give it a velvety texture. Their empty cases collect along the waterline in narrow brown rafts.

The abundance stays mostly at the edges. Between a column and a pump there may be twenty feet of empty floor, yet the base of that same column is crowded with life. Every bracket makes shade, every loose cover makes shelter. A person standing still can hear insects striking the lamp cages overhead.

Rats travel along the raised pavement. They seldom cross the center. The unfinished curb gives them a continuous route from the stair to the drain chambers, and repeated passage has polished a narrow line through the construction dust. In one corner, behind a stack of unused cable trays, they have pulled insulation into a nest. There is often a brief quarrel there, a scraping and a hard little cry, then quiet.

The pump is louder than all of them, but it works slowly. As the water rises, a float lifts inside a slotted tube. Eventually there is a clack from the control box. The motor takes hold with a deep vibration, and the nearest puddles acquire rings. For several minutes the chamber seems to be standing inside an idling machine.

Water retreats by fractions of an inch. Wet clay emerges. The larvae concentrate in hollows. Cockroaches move down to newly exposed debris.

Then the float falls, the motor stops, and dripping becomes audible again.

This cycle governs the place more reliably than day and night. A maintenance worker comes down occasionally, checks the cabinet, clears the intake, and leaves a fresh shoeprint beside the old ones. Traffic passes overhead in muffled pairs of impacts. Sometimes someone tips a bucket into the street drain, and a brief, dirty waterfall interrupts the steady seepage.

The unfinished ground is beginning to acquire features of its own. A channel scarcely wider than a finger has formed between two excavator tracks. At a leaking joint, a white mineral lip projects from the concrete, still thin enough to break under a thumbnail. Rust from an exposed reinforcing bar has colored a small cone of sand orange. The cone grows grain by grain as water drips through a hole in the deck.

On the dry side of a column, the builders’ pencil marks survive. A level line, an arrow, a measurement. Below them, the first tide marks are appearing.

The chamber still smells of new concrete when the pumps uncover enough floor. Then the water rises again, bringing back the heavier smell of warm drains, wet insulation, and silt. Under the lamps the flies settle, lift, and settle. Along the western wall, a bead of water swells in a hole left by a steel tie, drops onto the clay, and begins the next small change.
