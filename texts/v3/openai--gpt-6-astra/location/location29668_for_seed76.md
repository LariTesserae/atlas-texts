The first impression is of distance lit too clearly.

There is no haze to soften the far escarpments. Their pale rims lie one behind another under a dark blue sky, each apparently close enough to reach before the light changes. Yet a shadow moving along the nearest rim may take half a day to reveal itself as a weather front. Beyond it are further uplands, and beyond those a white curvature that might be another range or the edge of the world. Nothing in the foreground provides a reliable measure. A tuft of foliage on an opposite slope could be knee-high or a forest.

This is the high country of a dry planet: not a chain of peaks, but an elevated continent whose surface has had time to lose its sharpness. Mountains have worn into broad shoulders. Ravines open gradually, with sloping mouths and floors rubbed smooth by traveling grit. The hardest rock persists as low ribs, so widely spaced that the land between them seems almost level until sunlight turns sideways and reveals the long descent.

Most of the ground is the color of unglazed porcelain. There are stretches of lavender shale, beds of iron-dark gravel, and pale green seams exposed in the sides of shallow depressions. Wind has taken away whatever was easily carried. What remains fits together closely: plates of stone, coarse sand settled in their joints, occasional rounded blocks resting in hollows they have occupied for ages. Even where the air is moving fast, little dust rises.

Between these expanses grow the gardens.

They are far apart, separated by hours or days of bare terrain, but within their boundaries almost every available surface is alive. They occupy the lee of ridges, the shaded folds of escarpments, and wide, shallow basins whose underlying stone contains the right mineral layers. From a height they look like dark spills with elaborate edges. On approaching, the edges resolve into low cushions, then tightly overlapping rosettes, then a deep tangle of flexible stems carrying foliage like split ribbons.

The leaves are thick, matte, and cool. Some are bottle-green; others have the dark violet of a bruise beneath their pale protective bloom. Their undersides are silver. In full light most hold themselves nearly vertical, presenting narrow edges to the sky. A passing shadow makes them open. Across a basin, this can produce a broad, slow change of color, silver giving way to green as though something enormous has turned beneath the surface.

There is no open water. No stream enters these gardens, and no hidden lake sustains them. Their growth follows chemistry in the rock.

A few handspans beneath the surface, certain mineral beds release a thin, oily fluid when they cool. It travels through pores too fine to see and gathers on buried root hairs. The plants use it slowly. Within their tissues it remains mobile at temperatures that would immobilize ordinary sap; exposed to the air, it thickens into a clear skin. A torn leaf seals itself almost immediately. Fallen leaves dry without curling, retaining their form for years before the wind reduces them to a soft, dark powder.

The oldest gardens have made their own soil out of this powder. It lies deep between roots, threaded with pale, branching growths that never emerge into daylight. Press it and it yields reluctantly, like a dense felt. The surface feels lush, even indulgent, but there is no dampness to it. No smell of wet earth. Warmed by the sun, it gives off a faint resinous scent with a mineral bitterness beneath it.

Some plants spread flat against the ground, their leaves arranged in nested fans. Others rise on many-jointed trunks and carry dense, drooping crowns. These are not especially tall. The high winds keep them broad, and their lower branches gradually settle into the soil and take root. Over centuries a single plant advances outward, leaving a clear center where its first trunk has crumbled away. The largest rings enclose acres of pale stone. Younger growth crowds their margins so thickly that there is no easy way through.

Elsewhere, a whole garden consists of one low canopy stretched across a sheltered slope. Its branches are hidden. Only the foliage is visible, interrupted by small openings where cold air sinks into the undergrowth. Fine grains of white mineral collect around these openings, and delicate, colorless organisms feed along their rims. They withdraw into the soil at the slightest vibration.

The gardens do not fill all the places where shelter is available. There are perfect-looking basins that remain completely bare, and exposed slopes unexpectedly clothed in green. Underground seams matter more than the shape of the surface. A band of vegetation may stop along a line as clean as a shadow, then resume many miles away where the same stratum rises again. The continent’s living pattern is an incomplete drawing of its buried geology.

At this altitude the sunlight is fierce, but the air has little warmth to give. In shade, cold comes quickly through soles and fingertips. Sunlit stone can be warm on its upper face and sharply cold underneath. Near midday the contrast sets the slopes breathing: heated air rises from the pale flats while colder air drains out of the gardens.

These movements rarely become loud. A current may pass with nothing more than the dry turning of leaves. Farther out, on an empty plain, it gathers into a transparent column that lifts a few flakes of plant skin and carries them upward until they flash out of sight. By afternoon there can be hundreds of these columns moving across a basin, visible only where they cross dark ground.

The larger weather arrives from below the horizon.

A white bank climbs the distant escarpments, seeming at first impossibly slow. It is made of mineral crystals formed in colder air over the lower country. As it reaches the plateau, its leading edge unravels into long streamers. Some pass overhead. Others lie against the ground, flowing through shallow valleys and spilling over ledges in complete silence.

Inside one, the light remains bright but loses direction. Nearby stones become exquisitely distinct while everything beyond them disappears. Crystals settle on leaves without melting. For a little while the gardens are edged in white; then their foliage begins to work, flexing and folding, guiding the fall toward the root hollows. What they cannot use remains as a powder on the ground.

After the front has passed, the leaves reopen unevenly. A few plants have already begun to flower.

Their flowers are small and numerous, held beneath the foliage rather than above it: wax-colored cups, dark red hooks, clusters of translucent scales. Much of the flowering happens in shadow, where a bloom can preserve its volatile scent. For several hours the air near the ground becomes surprisingly rich. Then the compounds disperse, the flowers close, and the garden returns to its faint, bitter smell.

There are animals, but a person could cross a great deal of this country without seeing one.

The most evident signs are narrow passages beneath the canopy and patches where the silver coating has been scraped from leaves. A low, many-footed grazer may spend several days inside a single thicket, moving only when its chosen plants have sealed their feeding wounds. Its body resembles a worn piece of root, dark above and pale where it touches the ground. When it must cross open stone, it waits for the mineral weather. Even then it travels in short stages, remaining motionless for long intervals beside objects scarcely larger than itself.

In the mature gardens there are predators thin enough to lie between overlapping leaves. They sense the tiny flexing of stems and strike only at close range. Their presence is local. Whole basins have none; elsewhere, one old plant-ring may contain several generations of them. The emptiness between gardens is too extensive for animal life to circulate freely. Each patch has its own small history of arrivals, disappearances, and long survival.

Seeds travel farther.

Some are released as narrow strips that twist when warmed and straighten in cold air. They work themselves upward through the daily currents, rising from the garden in sparse, bright flecks. Others remain attached to dead leaves and slide over the stone, catching briefly in every seam. Most will come to rest where nothing can grow. Along certain windward ridges they accumulate in drifts so old that the lowest layers have been ground into varnish.

The few that reach a favorable bed may wait through years of inadequate weather. When they do open, they put almost all their growth downward. For a long time there is only a small, hard knob on the surface, easily mistaken for a pebble. The first green arrives after the roots have found enough to keep it.

Nothing built interrupts the country. There are no straight cuts across the slopes, no smoke, no worn crossings between the gardens. Pale bands that seem from a distance to be roads turn out to be harder strata, or the edges of old crystal deposits. The occasional upright stone has been left standing because the rock around it was softer. Its base is polished on one side by centuries of wind, and on the other a single cushion plant has grown into the shelter.

Toward evening, the brightness lowers rather than fades. The plateau’s shallow relief becomes immense. Every low ridge casts a long blue shadow; every garden gathers darkness among its leaves while its uppermost surfaces remain edged with light. Cold air begins moving downhill in steady sheets. Foliage that stood narrow all day spreads to expose its lower surfaces, and across the scattered basins there is a faint, sustained rustle.

Then the first dry clicks begin underfoot as sun-warmed plates of stone contract.

They occur singly, at wide intervals. One nearby, another too far away to place. Between them, the silence is large enough for the smallest movement to occupy it: a seed scraping across a slab, a grazer shifting under cover, brittle crystals falling from a leaf.

Far out on the white plain, a new weather bank is climbing toward the plateau. It catches the last sunlight long after the gardens have gone dark.
