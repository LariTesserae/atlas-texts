The first thing visible above the cloud is a white rim, thin and level. Then another behind it, higher by the width of a hand. As the mist loosens, the rims become the edges of shallow basins, and the basins extend across an entire upland valley.

There are thousands of them. They fit against one another without leaving ground between: long troughs, six-sided pans, broad dishes with smaller dishes set inside them. Their walls are made of pale fired clay. Some are thick enough to walk on. Others rise between adjoining waters as narrow, sharp partitions, still bearing the marks of the wooden blades that trimmed them.

Nothing grows here. The green visible in a few deeper pools belongs to the glaze.

The valley is bright enough to make you lower your eyes. Sunlight comes through a high, thin roof of cloud and returns from every wet surface. Even the undersides of ledges receive a wavering light. Only inside the drainage holes is there darkness: small, round, carefully bored darkness, repeated along the walls.

It is cool. Water gathers on anything held still long enough.

At the valley’s lower end, the basins are wide and nearly level with one another. Farther up, they climb in crowded terraces toward a ridge of white retaining walls. The ridge has no exposed rock. Its buttresses are stacks of hollow clay wedges, fitted mouth to mouth, their seams packed with a darker paste. Beyond it stand other ridges, other stepped depressions, all made of the same modest elements repeated until they possess the scale of hills.

From a distance, the slopes look finely ribbed. Nearby, each rib resolves into a gutter.

There is no single watercourse. Moisture arrives everywhere: out of the passing cloud, from beads on the underside of a slab, through a sweating joint. It collects in the shallowest bowls first. These feed larger bowls through notches cut just below their rims. Where a drop would have to fall, someone has set a sloping tongue of clay beneath it, so the water travels down in contact with a surface. Much of the valley’s construction seems devoted to this one precaution.

Consequently, it is quieter than so much water should be.

You hear your own sole peel away from wet ceramic. Occasionally a bead falls into a hollow post and makes a low, enclosed note. There is a faint rubbing sound along some walls, too continuous to be a drip: water drawing itself through a seam. When the wind passes overhead, it scarcely reaches the basins. Their packed partitions divide it into small, exhausted currents.

The workmanship is everywhere close at hand. A thumbprint remains at the base of a spout. A row of slabs has been ruled with a cord, but the cord sagged, and its slight curve survives across thirty or forty joints. Finger-width rolls of clay have been pressed into corners to keep them watertight. On one high wall, the maker smoothed the surface in sweeping arcs; another wall beside it is stippled from a cloth.

There are no machines, no pipes of metal, no glass. The few tools left in recesses are stone polishers, bone-colored spatulas of fired clay, and short mallets whose handles and heads are carved from a single dense material. The tools are dry beneath their little roofs. No other trace indicates how long they have been waiting.

Most edges are fresh. The narrow footways have not acquired a hollow from use. Even underwater, cut marks remain crisp. Here and there a firing crack crosses a basin, repaired with a butterfly-shaped piece fitted into matching sockets. These repairs, too, are unworn.

The place has the unfinished newness of a pot not yet lifted from the kiln, though it is full of cold water and large enough to disappear into weather.

Walking is possible, but never careless. The broad paths follow the thickest retaining walls, branching where the terraces branch. They have been roughened with parallel scratches. At junctions, a square platform offers room to turn. From these platforms you can look down into successive courts of water, crowded with low partitions, pedestal bowls, and upright porous slabs.

The slabs are the strangest part.

They stand in close ranks, shoulder-high, each with its lower edge submerged. Their surfaces darken slowly from bottom to top. Water climbs through them and collects along their crests, where tiny grooves lead it into basins higher than the ones below. The lift is slight, no more than a finger’s breadth at each stage. Across hundreds of stages, however, the dampness works its way toward the ridge.

The highest pools are the smallest. Some hold only a spoonful.

Up there, the cloud moves through open galleries made of perforated clay screens. Droplets form in the holes and swell without falling. Every screen has a different pattern: squares, circles, long vertical slits, irregular openings pressed through with fingertips. Behind them the light is broken into soft, luminous patches. The air smells faintly of wet pottery, with a mineral sharpness where an unglazed surface has recently split.

There is no soil hidden in the joints. A broken paving slab reveals another slab beneath, then a layer of hollow vessels laid on their sides. Through one fractured vessel you can see deeper chambers, crosswalls, pale braces descending beyond the reach of reflected light. The valley has depth, but nowhere does it disclose a foundation different from itself.

Its changes are small and widely separated. A wet line advances up a porous wall. A shallow pool finally reaches its notch. A pale flake detaches from a seam and rests on the water until it darkens enough to sink.

By late afternoon, the cloud has withdrawn from the nearest ridge. The rows of basins become distinct all the way across the valley, each holding its own piece of brightness. Far away, on a terrace you had mistaken for the horizon, a newly filled trough begins to overflow.

The water spreads along its waiting tongue of clay.

No sound reaches you.
