The plain is green almost to the horizon, though there is nothing here to drink.

Underfoot, the ground is a pale, newly divided mineral. Long plates of it lie edge to edge, their seams so fine they first appear drawn in pencil. Where one plate stands above another, the exposed edge is sharp enough to split a fallen leaf. There is no grit beneath these edges, no fan of fragments, no softening. The stone has the tender color of something just opened: cream shading into pink, with occasional translucent patches through which another layer of green can be seen.

Warmth rests evenly on everything. A hand placed in shade finds almost the same temperature as a hand held in sunlight. The air is dry enough that breathing makes the mouth immediately conscious of itself. It smells faintly of crushed stems and hot electrical insulation, although nothing is burning, and there are no wires.

The vegetation grows thickest along the seams.

There are broad leaves, narrow leaves, upright combs of dark green blades. Some plants gather into low, fleshy rosettes; others rise above head height on jointed stalks that look too delicate to bear their crowns. Between them spread mats of minute, overlapping scales. Each scale has a bright edge, so that a patch seen against the light resembles a thousand neatly closed scissors.

From a little distance it is luxuriant country. The green is convincing. There are sheltered passages through the growth, deep recesses beneath the fronds, entire acres whose upper surfaces meet in an almost continuous canopy. But close up, the resemblance to a well-watered forest weakens.

The leaves contain no sap. A broken tip shows a dry, satin cross-section, green all the way through, threaded with channels finer than hair. Nothing beads at the wound. The tip does not wilt. Held separately from its stem, it retains a slight resistance to being carried away, as though attachment were a condition that distance alone could not undo.

Most of the plants have this peculiar firmness. They yield when pressed, then hold the impression a moment too long. A broad leaf can be bent without creasing; its far edge may begin to return before the part beneath the fingers has been released.

Their tissues do the work of machinery at a scale too small to see. Light is caught, sorted, and passed into folded spaces within the green. Mineral atoms enter through the roots without leaving cavities in the rock. Heat has somewhere to go that is neither the ground nor the air. Nothing about this work is visible as movement. Only under a leaf, looking through its thinnest portions toward the sun, can one make out a pattern of tiny dark rings, each enclosing more space than its circumference ought to permit.

There are no manufacturers here. No buried conduits, no abandoned stations, no signs that the plain was planted. The same intricate arrangements occur inside the stone and in the translucent cases of the few animals. Whatever learned to do these things learned before the distinction between organism and instrument had much meaning.

The country extends for many miles at a low, almost level elevation. Its relief is slight but distinct. Broad mineral backs rise just high enough to divide one field of growth from another. Shallow depressions hold thickets of black-stemmed plants with leaves the color of pistachio flesh. Elsewhere, the vegetation parts around bare, oval flats, their surfaces smooth and unmarked. These look like dry pools until one notices that nothing slopes toward them.

No channel leads anywhere. There is no silt, no rounded pebble, no stain left by a former waterline. Even the lowest places are clean-edged.

Across the western part of the plain, pale shelves have lifted through the greenery. They stand only a few feet high, but their uninterrupted fronts run for half a mile. Plants grow right up to them and continue above them, giving the impression that the ground has been raised through an existing forest without disturbing a single stem. Beneath some shelves is a narrow blue darkness. Looking into it, one sees the undersides of distant leaves rather than supporting rock.

The light is ordinary enough to make these discrepancies difficult to accept. There is a bright sky, a sun with a warm white disk, clear shadows beneath the taller plants. A pale stalk shines on its sunward side. Green reflected light touches the ground beneath a leaf. Everything has the familiar clarity of late morning.

Yet the sun does not noticeably advance.

The shadows fit the plants precisely, but they stay where they are. A bent stalk casts the shadow of its earlier position until the hand moves away, at which point stalk and shadow agree again. In the open flats, a visitor’s shadow can become very faint without any corresponding change in brightness.

It is mostly quiet. There is no wind working through the leaves, no insect chorus, no running or dripping in the concealed places. Sometimes a small, clear note is audible from within a thicket. It has neither an attack nor a fading tail. Walking closer makes it louder; walking past takes one out of it. A few such notes overlap near the mineral shelves, forming a chord that occupies a particular stretch of ground as concretely as the shade.

Animals are scarce enough that the first may be mistaken for another kind of leaf.

One rests sideways on a stem: a flat, amber body with six long legs and a row of white points enclosed along its back. Its feet do not grip. A hair’s breadth separates each foot from the surface supporting it. Through its body one can see the stem, displaced slightly to the left.

It may remain there for hours. Then, while attention is elsewhere, it is on the neighboring plant. There is no rustle and no visible flight. Around the place it previously occupied, the leaves continue to hold a small animal-shaped interval, as though a gap in the thicket were slower to leave than the creature itself.

Farther out, above a dark stand of fronds, three white forms hang at different heights. Their wings are open. They are plainly alive, though watching them gives no evidence of effort. The space beneath them feels unusually deep.

Nothing here seems old. The tallest stems have no weathered base. Fallen leaves lie whole and bright in the understory, with their fine internal structures undimmed. There are buds beside open crowns, small plants beneath large ones, and roots emerging in immaculate white hooks from the mineral seams. It looks like a country at the height of its first season.

But there is no accumulation of fallen matter. No crowded seedbed strains toward a clearing. The buds do not need to open for the flowers to be present. Each stage has its place, and remains abundantly itself.

From one of the low shelves, the full breadth of the plain becomes visible: green fields divided by pink-white ribs, darker groves gathered in the hollows, bright bare ovals scattered between them. The density is immense, but there is room to see. Small differences in leaf height carry the eye for miles.

Nothing is waiting for rain. Nothing is drying out.

At the edge of the shelf, a new green shoot stands in a crack scarcely wider than its stem. Its folded leaves are still pressed together. Sunlight passes through them, revealing a tiny, elaborate darkness inside. The stone around it is freshly split, every edge perfect.

It has reached the surface.

It is always just arriving.
