The first impression is of a town at the bottom of an empty reservoir. There are terraces, pale fronts pierced by dark openings, something like a broad avenue leading between two districts. Everything lies well below the surrounding country. The rim catches a little light; the floor does not.

Then the town begins to call.

The noise comes from every height at once: shrieking, chattering, the rough continuous argument of birds gathered in numbers too large to distinguish. Under it runs the squeaking of bats in the deeper chambers, and beneath that, almost lost, the dry patter of droppings and fragments of nest falling onto stone. The walls return each sound several times. Near the middle of the basin, where there is nothing to obstruct the echoes, the air seems packed solid with them.

No people have built here.

The place is a chain of collapsed limestone caverns, several miles long, with roofs still bridging the narrower passages. From above it would look like a series of long, irregular holes in a plateau. From below it is a country: broad courts of gravel, low divides of rubble, shaded corridors opening into other courts too far away to see clearly. The remaining roofs are immensely thick. Their undersides disappear into blackness, though the far ends of the chambers may hold a little gray daylight.

Water made all this, and has been absent for so long that its work no longer looks unfinished. The channels on the floor have lost their edges. Old potholes are shallow dishes full of dust. Great falls of stone have settled into slopes as gentle as embankments, each block rounded by the slow loss of its own surface. There are no bright scars. Even a fracture is the color of old bone.

The air is cool and exceedingly dry. It smells of chalk, feathers, and, close to the colonies, a sharp mineral richness that catches at the back of the throat. Nothing drips. The cave formations are dull, their tips broken or softened, their flanks filmed with dust. Here and there a former curtain of flowstone stands against a wall like a folded sheet left stiff for centuries.

Most of the floor is empty.

A person could walk for a long time across its pale grit without passing anything taller than a loose stone. Fine tracks cross it: the neat stitching of beetles, the tail-drag of a small rodent, bird feet ending where the bird lifted away. In some sheltered stretches, tracks survive beneath a new scattering of dust until they become hard to tell from the grain of the ground.

But where the roof opens, there is green.

It gathers beneath the light wells in dense, separate patches, each one with a distinct boundary. Wild figs lean from cracks. Their trunks are short and gray, their crowns much wider than expected, leaves overlapping until the shaded space beneath them looks almost black. Old carobs grow horizontally from ledges and turn upward at the ends. Ivy thickens the narrow clefts where fine earth has lodged beyond the reach of moving air. On the higher shelves, fleshy little plants crowd shoulder to shoulder in pockets scarcely wider than a hand.

There is no turf linking these gardens. Between them lie hundreds of feet of bare limestone.

Their roots travel where their crowns cannot: down seams, behind slabs, into the cool interior of the rock. Almost all the scant moisture the place receives is held out of sight. Growth is economical, but its accumulated result is lavish. A fig may have spent generations filling a single opening with leaves. Fallen foliage does not blow far. It collects in the same sheltered beds, darkening them, making room for more roots.

In the dimness, these patches of vegetation have an unexpected weight. They look substantial enough to lean against. Birds disappear into them and continue making noise.

The city is on the walls.

Swifts and swallows occupy the entrance chambers, where the air is clear enough for flight and daylight persists after the floor has gone dark. Their nests fill shelves and seams in clustered rows. On rougher faces, generations of small additions have made whole projecting neighborhoods: cups tucked beside cups, old foundations strengthened with grass and feathers, narrow spaces left open beneath overhangs. Elsewhere pigeons inhabit deeper holes, each dark aperture with its own pale, dusty lip.

There are crowded districts and long vacant facades. A slight difference in exposure, invisible from below, can make one wall desirable and the next empty. Some ledges are white with occupation; others, apparently identical, have not held a nest in years.

Farther inside, bats hang beneath the surviving roofs. They gather most densely where the ceilings fold upward into warm pockets, leaving the colder vaults almost bare. Beneath them, low mounds of dry guano support a separate abundance. Beetles move over their surfaces. Pale larvae work within. Small spiders occupy fissures at the edges, and centipedes pass slowly through the loose material with the deliberate motion of animals that need not hurry.

The basin holds all these lives without appearing full. Its inhabitants use surfaces, cracks, the undersides of things. They leave the great central spaces to air.

Flight makes those spaces briefly visible. A bird drops from a wall, crosses the open court, and is swallowed by an opposite opening. At dusk, the bats begin to pour through the roof gaps in dark, wavering streams. For a while the upper air is crowded, but there is little wind below. Dust remains where it has settled. A fallen feather turns once on the floor and stops.

The loudness is difficult to reconcile with the stillness. Nothing large moves. The trees scarcely stir. No current passes through the old channels. Yet the sound is so great that a voice raised nearby becomes a mouth moving without meaning.

Only beyond the inhabited chambers does quiet return. A low arch leads into another basin, wider and less broken, where a few roots descend from a crack too high to see. The noise of the colonies follows through the opening as a single coarse roar. Walk farther and it becomes a rumble, then a faint vibration in the enclosed air.

Here the stone lies in its oldest arrangement. Dust has filled the small hollows without concealing them. A dead branch rests below its tree, bleached on one side, still dark on the side against the ground. There are beetle tracks beneath it.

Above, daylight crosses the distant rim. It takes hours to pass from one projection to the next. Down on the floor, the change is only a slight lifting of the gray, enough to reveal another terrace beyond the first, and another broad passage leading away between the walls.
