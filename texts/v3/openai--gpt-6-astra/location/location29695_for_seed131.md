Behind the pumping house, at the foot of a wall without windows, there is a hatch almost concealed by a fern. It does not lead into the machinery. It opens sideways into the thickness of the foundation, where an old inspection recess has become a small, wet room.

The room is low enough that you have to bend. Three paces take you from the hatch to the opposite wall. There is no furniture, no equipment left to inspect, nothing stored here. Most of it is air above a shallow sheet of water. The plants keep to the edges.

At first the sound prevents you from seeing much.

Water is falling somewhere beyond the back wall, not in this chamber but close enough that the wall transmits its entire weight. There is also the continuous running of large motors. Their different pitches have settled into a roar with a low, hard center, a sound felt in the jaw and the bridge of the nose. You cannot hear your clothing move. A hand placed in the pool makes a visible disturbance but no audible splash. Even a shout loses its consonants.

Once you stop trying to listen through it, the room becomes surprisingly spacious. Not large, but unoccupied. There is a clear middle, a small margin of water, a ceiling with nothing hanging from it. After the pipes and rails and fencing outside, that absence is almost an amenity.

The floor was built from long ceramic blocks bedded in concrete. Water has worn them down to broad, faintly convex shapes, their joints now shallow channels full of dark paste. No corner survives at floor level. The foot of each wall slopes into the basin as gently as a muddy bank, though there is no earth here. Everything under the plants is brick, cement, glaze, or material shed by those things.

The oldest exposed surfaces have the color of a tooth held to the light. Beside them are repairs in gray, brown, and a greenish aggregate containing crushed furnace slag. The repairs have themselves been repaired. A rectangular patch crosses a round one; both have lost their edges. At the back, a little crescent of glazed tile remains embedded in a smoother material whose original surface is gone. You cannot tell what the tile once belonged to. It may have been a lining, a drain, or the corner of a channel that no longer exists.

The recess has outlasted several arrangements of the works above it. There are filled bolt holes larger than the current hatch hinges, and a pipe socket closed with a plug so eroded that plug and wall now form one continuous hollow. Nothing here looks broken. It has gone past that stage. Whatever could loosen has loosened; whatever the water could readily carry away has been carried away. What remains is low, rounded, and firmly seated.

Light comes down a disused service shaft at one side. Its cover is a square of wired glass at yard level. In daylight the room is quite visible: not a cave, not a green gloom, but a pale chamber with a bright, slightly milky opening overhead. The glass scatters the shapes of anything passing across it. Usually there is only the sky.

Below this opening, vegetation fills a shallow ledge.

Small ferns lean out over the water with fronds longer than their supporting bed is deep. A soft-leaved creeping plant has threaded itself between them, its stems rooting wherever they touch the accumulated silt. Moss covers the ledge’s underside in thick, saturated lobes. Close up, this narrow strip is extravagant: translucent new leaves, dark mature ones, roots wrapped in fine hairs, the curved hooks of fronds still unrolling. Step back toward the hatch and it becomes a single green border around an otherwise empty room.

The plants do not spread far. The central floor is too continuously washed to hold their litter, and the darker wall receives too little light. Their lushness has a precise boundary. The longest frond reaches almost to the center, but not quite; its tip hangs over an expanse of bare water.

There is very little depth to that water. You could cover most of the floor with your palm without wetting your wrist. It is clear over the high parts of the blocks and tea-colored in the seams. Near the rear wall, it rises through an old pressure-relief joint in a broad, transparent swelling, then slips away toward a slot beneath the hatch. The flow is so even that it is easiest to see by watching a pale fragment of root travel across the floor.

The room is warm. Some of the warmth comes through the wall from the motors; some arrives with the water. The air feels close against the lips but not oppressive. Condensation gives the upper masonry a uniform sheen. Occasionally a bead becomes too heavy and runs down, joining a route already polished by innumerable beads before it.

There is no weather in the usual sense. Rain changes the blurred color of the light overhead. Summer brightens it. The pumping system holds the water level within the width of a fingernail, and waste heat takes the edge off winter. Each surface is occupied according to conditions that scarcely vary: fern near the glass, moss below the fern, algae at the waterline, bare ceramic where the current is strongest.

A single snail is sometimes visible beneath the ledge. Its shell is dull and small, its presence disclosed by a clean, winding mark through the film on the wall. There may be others inside the moss. Nothing in the chamber requires you to imagine them.

Leaf litter supplies what little soil there is. A frond collapses into the green mass beneath it, softens, and disappears. The water brings fine mineral particles through the joint. Roots retain some; the rest leave by the slot. Growth and removal have reached an old accommodation. There is no heap of debris, no advancing thicket, no suggestion that the room is gradually filling.

Standing bent beneath the ceiling, you begin to notice how little the roar corresponds to movement here. The ferns are still. The bright opening is still. Water moves, but its surface keeps the same shape. A droplet falls and is replaced by another droplet forming at exactly the same place.

Beyond the masonry, impellers turn and whole channels of water are lifted. The building trembles with that work. Inside the recess, the vibration appears only as a fine trembling along the reflected edge of the glass.

You leave by turning sideways. There is no other way to go.

When the hatch closes, the frond beside it springs back a little and rests against the metal. Inside, the shallow water continues across the worn blocks, under the same small reach of leaves, at the same depth.
