The plain is raised about a hand’s breadth above its water.

You can see this at the edges of the paths, where the woven ground ends in a tight selvedge and a clear, motionless layer shows beneath it. The paths are broad enough for six people to walk abreast. They run between beds of enormous leaves, cross shallow basins on low clay causeways, and meet at bare squares where there is nothing but light. From one square you can usually see the next: a pale opening beyond green, with perhaps a roof or a single tall stalk standing beside it.

The whole country has been made by hand.

This is easiest to believe of the paths. Their surfaces retain the pressure of palms, the shallow grooves where bundles of reeds were dragged into alignment. It is more difficult to believe of the distances. Beyond the nearest gardens, the plain continues in a succession of planted shelves, pools, low embankments, and empty courts, all the way to a bright rim too low to be called a range of hills. Yet even that rim has a turned edge. In clear places you can make out the thumb-sized indentations along it, repeated for miles.

There is no earth under the weaving. Beneath the upper mat are cross-laid stalks, then fitted clay trays, then a deeper weave visible through inspection holes beside the water gates. Each layer rests on another piece of workmanship. Look down far enough and the holes become too bright to see into.

The heat is immediate and complete. It presses against the wet insides of your elbows. A clay rail is hot on top and slick underneath; a leaf laid across it leaves a dark wet mark that will not dry. The air smells of warmed cordage, green sap, and the inside of an unglazed jar. Nothing has the scorched smell of drought. Water is always close, though there are long stretches without a pond in sight.

Along some paths it stands in narrow channels scarcely wider than a finger. Elsewhere it occupies shallow courts, spread over pale paving so evenly that you notice the water only when a bird appears to be standing ankle-deep in its own reflection. At the margins of the larger basins, droplets hang from thousands of loose fiber ends. Each drop contains a small, sharply focused version of the opposite bank.

The leaves are lavish.

They rise in separated beds, with plenty of open ground between them: groves of paddle-shaped foliage, thick fans of narrow blades, low cushions of round leaves crowded together like overlapping dishes. Some are taller than houses. Their green ranges from the yellow of soaked straw to a dark, almost blue color in the folded centers. Fat climbing stems bind the occasional posts, and flowers fill the gaps beneath them, heavy, waxy things with pale throats.

At first the vegetation seems to be the one thing that has escaped the makers. It bulges over its boundaries and conceals whole stretches of retaining wall. Then you come close enough to see the stitching.

Every leaf has been assembled around its veins. The finest ones use a translucent green membrane stretched over split fiber, sewn so delicately that the seams resemble ordinary growth. Coarser leaves show rows of knots along their undersides. The stems are rolled and joined, with collars of sticky resin at the joints. Flowers have little pleats where the material has been eased around a curve.

They are alive. Cut a leaf and sap beads along the cut. Put your hand into the center of a plant and it is warm there in a different way from the air. Roots work through the matting below, finding the water holes prepared for them. What they drink does not make them larger. Each plant occupies its finished size, down to the smallest curled tip.

The country has the freshness of something completed this morning. The clay banks have sharp shoulders. Tool marks survive on exposed corners. Where a basin wall changes direction, the angle is clean enough to catch a thread from your clothing. No silt softens the bottoms of the channels. No roots have lifted the paving. Even the luxuriant creepers stop at the last stitch allotted to them.

There are buildings, but they stand far apart.

A cluster of three or four appears beside a long pool; another occupies a platform across an expanse of empty matting. Their walls are woven panels rubbed with clay, their roofs broad folds secured by wooden pegs. Doorways are low and very wide. In some houses, sunlight enters through the walls as though the clay were thin cloth, illuminating the interior without revealing anything outside.

There are bowls on shelves, digging sticks against walls, spare lengths of cord coiled around smooth stones. The stones themselves are clay, shaped to be useful weights. A hearth holds several red coals arranged in a careful triangle. They give heat without consuming their small stack of fuel.

Nobody is working.

You find signs of hands everywhere, but no clear sign that a hand has recently moved. Beside one doorway sits a half-woven basket. Its loose strands bend outward with the exact suppleness of fresh-cut material. In another court, a wooden paddle rests in a trough of green pulp. A thumbprint on its handle is still moist.

It would be easy to think the makers had just stepped away. The longer you stay, the less useful “just” becomes.

Animals occupy the gardens in extraordinary numbers, though the openness keeps them from feeling crowded. Small lizards lie along the rims of pots. Long-legged birds stand in the pools, each separated from the next by several yards of brilliant water. Beetles cling beneath leaves like polished seeds. There are soft-bodied creatures in the channels, pale and finger-long, with dark loops visible through their sides.

The larger animals show their making more plainly. A browsing creature near a doorway has flanks of supple, closely fitted bark and a fringe of knotted fiber beneath its jaw. Its nostrils are two neat punctures. They widen when it breathes. A seam travels down the inside of each leg, almost hidden in the fold.

It turns its head to watch you. The movement is natural enough to be startling.

The birds have reed shafts in their feathers. At the shoulder, where the feathers part, you can glimpse the tiny ties holding the wing together. They preen with great care. Nothing comes loose.

There is little sound. A foot on the path makes a muted, padded contact. Now and then a beak clicks once against a clay rim. One of the grazing animals draws a leaf between its lips with a soft rasp, and when its mouth moves away the leaf is whole.

Even the insects are quiet. You see their abundance in the air as an intricate scattering of bright points, especially over the wet courts, but hear no whine. A swarm can hang beside your face without disturbing the silence.

Water occasionally falls from a lip into a basin. Watching it closely is uncomfortable. The descending thread is perfectly continuous, and the place it strikes has a small crown of raised water around it. The crown never collapses. There is no splash to hear. It is water in the shape of an event.

Other things move more freely. A lizard crosses a warm slab. Leaves yield beneath an animal’s muzzle. A bird steps out of its reflection and leaves a ring behind. Yet the ring remains at the same width until you look elsewhere; then the pool is smooth again. Motion seems permitted within each thing, provided the place itself is left exactly as it was.

The light makes these small discrepancies impossible to dismiss as shadows.

It comes from a white-gold sky with no visible sun. Every exposed surface is bright, and the undersides of bridges are luminous enough to show individual fibers. Colors have the clarity of objects washed and set out for inspection. Even far across the plain, you can distinguish a red fastening on a green stalk.

At some intersections the craftsmanship of space becomes visible.

A path may have a narrow ridge across it, like the overlap between two mats. Step over and a distant house shifts several degrees to the left. The gardens between you and it remain undisturbed. Along the edges of certain courts, distance is gathered into small, regular pleats. A beetle walks across one and is suddenly on the far side of the square.

The locals, if there are locals, would know which folds to avoid. The animals do. Birds step carefully over some seams and fly low across others. A line of grazing creatures follows a long detour around a perfectly empty rectangle of paving. At its center lies a wooden peg, driven flush with the surface.

You leave it alone.

Farther out, beyond the widely spaced houses, the planted beds become larger and the empty intervals longer. Here the low rim of the country reveals itself as an immense embankment of packed fiber faced with clay. Shallow stairs ascend it at intervals. Water shines in the joints without leaking through.

From the top, you can look back over the whole arrangement: green masses, silver pools, pale avenues, the tiny dark doorways of houses. The landscape has the spaciousness of a settlement built for more inhabitants than ever arrived. Its gardens are fully grown. Its paths are unworn. Every creature has water, forage, a dry place to rest.

On the outer side of the embankment, the clay curves downward in a smooth, careful roll. There is no broken ground beyond it, no exposed rock, no wilderness waiting to reclaim the work. There is only the underside of the plain, woven as meticulously as the top, extending away into brightness.

Near your feet, a loose end protrudes from the finished edge.

It is green, wet, and warm. A small bird has tied its nest around it. Inside are three eggs, each bearing the faint spiral left by fingers turning clay. One has a crack across its crown.

Through the crack, something breathes.
