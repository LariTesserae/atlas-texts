At this height the rain seldom arrives as rain. It comes uphill, a drift of warm droplets lifted out of the cloud layer, and gathers on the undersides of bridges. A person crossing one of the higher streets can remain dry while the street beneath them darkens, joint by joint, with water.

The city stands on the newest part of the mountain.

From a distance it is difficult to say where construction ends. The mountain rises in square shoulders and closely packed fins, its black rock interrupted by pale ceramic seams. Buildings continue these shapes upward: narrow factories, reservoir towers, apartment blocks with almost no windows on their sunward faces. Between them are deep slots of shade. Bridges cross the slots at different levels, so that looking down from a parapet means looking through several other neighborhoods before seeing stone.

Beyond the city, the heights extend for perhaps thirty kilometers. There are broad shelves, fresh escarpments, a basin filled with gray vapor, and farther off a row of summits too sharply ridged to look permanent. They have no scree around their bases. Nothing has had time to break off.

The older mountain is somewhere below the clouds.

Up here the rock is still being made.

It emerges slowly through long foundations sunk beneath the city: a dark, fine-grained material, hot enough at first to soften iron, cooling to the color of pencil lead. The rate is slight. In an ordinary year a stair acquires the height of one additional step. Surveyors leave dated lines on doorframes; old lines descend toward the floor. Where the growth is uneven, a street gradually tilts, and the city raises one end of its bridges on screw jacks large enough to house a room.

Most of the buildings are not fastened to the mountain. They hold it between them.

Their deep collars guide the rising stone into piers, retaining walls, flat terraces. This is the oldest industry here, and still the largest. There are molding houses the size of railway stations, full of immense, slowly tightening forms. Through inspection doors one sees surfaces that have never been exposed to air: almost luminous, faintly pliant, bearing the smooth impressions of the machinery.

The city has learned to ask the mountain for useful shapes.

It has not learned why the mountain continues to provide them.

***

In the middle of the day the light is ordinary and merciless. A pale blue sky, a small clear sun, shadows with hard edges. The altitude should make the air cold. Instead heat comes through the soles of shoes, through railings, through the backs of chairs left against a wall. On the western terraces, the stone remains too hot to touch well after sunset.

There are no trees. No weeds occupy the drains. No moss softens the joints where a little water escapes. The green surfaces visible from the bridges are oxidized copper, sometimes polished almost black by hands.

The absence of vegetation makes small irregularities conspicuous. A length of discarded cable looks extravagantly tangled. A cracked basin attracts attention from across a courtyard. People keep fragments of cooled rock on their windowsills because each fracture is different, while almost everything built here has been made to repeat.

The streets are dense with fittings. Pipes run beneath ceramic covers along the walls; ladders climb between them; inspection plates overlap older inspection plates. Narrow balconies hold drying cloth, water condensers, cages of spare insulators. Heavy power cables cross above the lanes in orderly bundles. In places there is scarcely a palm’s width of unused facade.

Yet the city is quiet.

The engines turn deep inside insulated chambers, at speeds too low for much vibration. Freight moves on counterweighted tracks. A loaded carriage can take most of an afternoon to cross a square, descending a gradient too shallow to see. Pedestrians step around it without impatience. At intersections a brass indicator turns several minutes before the carriage arrives.

There are sounds, but they remain separate: a latch closing; a cup set on stone; the short scrape of a sandal; water striking the bottom of an empty tank.

Occasionally a great press releases somewhere underneath the district. The noise is a soft, low concussion, felt first in the teeth. Dust loosens from a cornice. Conversation pauses and resumes.

***

Water belongs to particular places.

One alley may remain damp throughout the year while the next street never receives a drop. The difference depends on the depth of the foundations, the temperature of a buried conduit, and the route taken by the slow uphill clouds.

Under the eastern viaducts, condensation falls steadily into long troughs. Their interiors are white with mineral deposits. In the collecting courts there are shallow pools with perfectly level rims, crowded on every side by walls, stairs, and machinery. The sky reflected in them is usually only a strip.

At dawn, workers skim a thin brittle skin from the water. It folds like wet paper, then stiffens as it dries. These sheets are stacked between felt pads and taken to the glazing works. Older windows have a faint milky waviness from this material. Through them, the crowded opposite buildings seem to stand underwater.

Drinking water is condensed twice. The first condensation tastes of coins and leaves a warmth in the mouth long after swallowing.

Once or twice a season, a cloud rises high enough to pass through the city rather than below it. The streets disappear by degrees. Bridges become wet corridors with no visible sides. Doors are left open to let moisture settle on the cool interiors. For several hours the inhabitants can hear people in neighboring buildings whom they have never seen.

Afterward the sun returns, the railings steam, and the dampness retreats to its usual narrow territories.

***

There are animals, although a visitor could spend weeks here without noticing one.

The most common are small, pale creatures living behind condenser plates. They have six blunt feet and a smooth casing that beads with water. During the long dry intervals they wedge themselves against cool metal and become almost indistinguishable from fasteners. When the mist comes, they descend to feed on the translucent film inside the gutters.

Their tracks are the only soft-looking marks in the city: paired stipples left in mineral slurry.

Something larger lives in the disused lifting shafts. Maintenance workers occasionally find an egg in a cable basket, gray and warm and remarkably heavy. No one takes it. Months later there may be two shallow footprints in the dust beside an empty shell.

Far above the rooftops, on certain evenings, a single dark animal circles without moving its wings. It stays over the hottest districts. Its shadow slides across terraces and catches briefly on the smoke vents, and people looking up disagree about whether it has a head.

Nothing calls at night.

***

Along the northern edge, the last inhabited blocks overlook the formation yards.

Here the city becomes more tentative. Stairways end in air. Railings enclose terraces barely wide enough to stand on. Foundations rise around spaces where buildings will eventually be, their plans laid out in white lines on stone that is still too warm for workers to remain on it for long.

There is no rubble. Unwanted material is lowered into narrow return wells, where it softens and becomes part of the mountain again.

Survey huts stand on adjustable legs. Inside are maps with two sets of contours: black for the land as it is, red for the land expected. The red contours are revised every few years. Sometimes an anticipated square becomes a steep ridge; sometimes the mountain supplies a hollow where a hospital was intended. The planners move the hospital. They have learned to leave room around anything essential.

A street in this district is paved for half its length. Beyond the final slabs, its curb continues across a smooth, gently bulging surface of new rock. There are already sockets for lamps. At the far end stands a drinking fountain, installed early because its supply pipe needed to pass beneath the future road.

It works.

Water falls from its spout into a basin surrounded by kilometers of unfinished height.

From there the city looks very crowded and very small: stacked walls, pale roofs, tank after tank catching the sun. A freight carriage is halfway across an upper bridge. Steam lifts from a courtyard and vanishes. Below everything, the cloud plain extends to the horizon, bright enough to hide the lower world completely.

At dusk the first lamps come on in the foundation galleries. Their light shines through narrow gaps beneath the buildings, exposing a red warmth deeper down.

The new stone retains, for a while, the grain of the molds that held it. On an unfinished parapet one can see bolt heads, numbered plates, even the faint weave of an insulating cloth. Higher up, these impressions gradually relax. Corners ease outward. Flat surfaces acquire a slight inward curve.

By morning the parapet will have risen an amount too small to measure with an ordinary ruler. A bead of water will hang beneath its lip. Somewhere inside the city, a door that closed yesterday will begin to catch.
