The ocean occupies the upper half of the sky.

Its underside is visible from the dry world far below: a pale, uneven ceiling, sagging in places, trailing rain that falls for miles before reaching anything. But here, within it, there is no view of the ground. There is cool blue water, daylight, and an immense distance between one solid thing and the next.

The mountains are still arriving.

They rise through the suspended sea in widely separated groups, sometimes no more than three or four black points in a hemisphere of blue. Below those points, their flanks descend out of sight. Above them lies another mile of water, then the bright, restless surface. Almost none reach the air. Their summits are angular and wetly lustrous, without soil, scree, or the pale scars of old falls. A ridge can be followed with the eye for hours without finding a rounded edge.

Along certain faces, stone is forming quickly enough to watch. A dark seam thickens. Its two lips push apart, exposing a greenish interior that turns black on contact with the water. Whole sheets of mineral rise from these seams, thin at first, trembling under the current, then thickening into buttresses. Their shapes have the provisional awkwardness of something growing too fast: shelves with no support beneath them, narrow towers leaning into open water, a cliff whose upper third is wider than its base.

Some will become mountains. Others dissolve before they finish.

The sea is extraordinarily clear. There is little suspended sediment because almost nothing has yet been ground down. At noon, light penetrates in broad, sloping fields, and a mountain fifty miles away may show as distinctly as a nearby stone. Distance reveals itself only when a current carries a veil of bubbles across the view. The veil travels for a long time before reaching what seemed close.

Most of the landscape is this interval: water with nothing in it.

No fish turn in the light. No small bodies gather beneath ledges. There are no shells, burrows, tracks, or patient movements in cracks. A loose flake of mineral can fall through an entire afternoon without passing another visible object.

Yet the water is rarely still.

Currents come through in columns wider than valleys. They descend from the surface, turn against the growing ranges, and accelerate through gaps that did not exist the day before. Their boundaries are visible as distortions: a summit bends, separates into two summits, then becomes one again. Sometimes a current is cold enough to whiten the stone with a brief mineral bloom. Sometimes it carries a faint yellow tint from a distant region where new matter is entering the sea.

The great disturbances begin without wind.

Across a hundred miles, the water will lean. Floating mineral skins, strands of vegetation, and the slender new tips of mountains all incline in the same direction. Pressure builds. A deep note passes through the basin, felt first in the teeth and the hollows of the face, then heard as a roar that seems to occupy the bones of the rock.

A fold moves through the ocean.

It does not break like a surface wave. It travels from top to bottom, a vast change in the direction things are allowed to fall. Water rushes along its leading edge. New cliffs buckle. Loose stone streams upward for several minutes, hanging in long dark ribbons, before ordinary weight returns and the ribbons collapse.

Behind the fold, the suspended sea settles at a slightly different height.

This is how it stays aloft.

There is something in the water that corrects its position. It is too small to see, too evenly distributed to gather, and too intricate to be explained by any single property of the liquid. It measures pressure, light, mineral content, the shape of the world beneath it. It passes instructions across distances faster than the currents could carry them. Where a correction is needed, weight changes.

No machinery marks the process. There are no pylons holding up the sea, no buried chambers in the mountains, no hard bright fragments to separate from the sand. Whatever made this possible has become a property of the environment. A cupful removed from the ocean would remain inexplicably light for a few moments, then become ordinary water as its tiny agreements failed.

On the scale of the whole sea, those agreements are still being negotiated.

A region may hold steady for a season. Then a newly forming range alters the balance, and a million square miles shudder into adjustment. The mountains grow according to one set of demands; the ocean responds according to another. Between them, the world acquires its weather.

There are storms entirely below the surface. They appear first as distant silver patches where the water has torn into cavities. The cavities close with sharp concussions. More open around them. Soon a front of flashing white water is advancing between the peaks, loud enough to obscure the deeper, continuous groan of the sea’s support.

Inside such a storm, visibility falls to an arm’s length. Cold arrives in alternating blows. The water tastes sharply of fresh stone. When the front passes, it leaves a silence that is only relative: the rumble of moving water, the crack of a new mineral sheet, the long report of a fracture crossing an unseen slope.

The vegetation survives in sheltered places.

On the lee sides of a few summits, dull olive films spread over the youngest rock. They follow seams where dissolved minerals emerge, leaving the rest of the mountain bare. Closer to the surface are scattered growths of translucent ribbons, each rooted in a pinprick of stone. Their tissue is so thin that daylight passes through almost unchanged. Only where several overlap does their pale green become apparent.

They have no seasons of flowering. They extend when the current eases and draw themselves close when pressure begins to rise. A large patch may consist of a few dozen ribbons spread across a cliff a mile high. During a correction, they lie flat against the rock, their tips trembling. Sometimes the rock beneath them unfolds into a new shape and carries them out into the flow.

There is no one to eat them. Their limits are cold, exposure, and the short lives of the surfaces on which they grow.

Near the ocean’s upper skin, the daylight becomes almost white. Looking up, one sees clouds through the broken surface—ordinary clouds, surprisingly small and remote. Rain falls from them into the sea, making tiny dimples above disturbances large enough to shift a mountain. A summit approaching this boundary is lit along its knife edge. For a few hours it may touch air.

Then the water rises, or the stone changes its mind, and the peak disappears again.

Below, the new ridges remain sharp. Their debris has not yet accumulated into broad fans. There are no old valleys to guide the currents, so the currents search everywhere, pressing into fresh clefts, finding passages through stone still soft enough to yield. Far away, another black point becomes visible in the blue. It lengthens slowly while thunder travels through the water.

By the time that thunder reaches it, the point has become a ridge.
