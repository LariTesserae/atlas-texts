The hollow is entered sideways, through a break in the bank that looks too narrow to lead anywhere. A blackthorn has grown across its upper half. Below the thorns, the soil is rubbed bare where something larger than a fox once passed repeatedly, though that was long enough ago for a thin green skin to return to the stone.

Inside, there is room to stand.

There is room, in fact, for a small gathering, but nothing to gather around. The floor is a shallow oval of pale grit. The roof rises out of sight in the darkness, though a raised stick would touch it. Along one wall lie three flat stones, separated by intervals that might once have accommodated wooden uprights. Against the other is a length of split timber, so weathered that its grain stands in soft ridges. Most of the hollow is air.

The noise fills all of it.

It comes up through a fissure at the back: the sea, below and outside, working in a passage too narrow to enter. You cannot see the water. You cannot even see far enough into the fissure to tell whether it descends straight or turns. But every wave drives a mass of air ahead of it, and the rock makes that air enormous. There is a deep, continuous pounding beneath a tearing rush, with a higher note that trembles whenever the passage fills. At its loudest the sound loses the character of water altogether. It becomes a pressure against the face and chest.

A person speaking here would see the other person’s mouth move and understand nothing.

Yet almost everything visible is still.

The split timber does not stir. The grit lies in its settled little fans. A dead leaf beneath the entrance thorn has kept its curled position through many tides. Only the youngest fern tips, close to the floor, occasionally lean toward the fissure and then return.

The hollow sits inside a low coastal bank, not a cliff of any grandeur. Above it is a strip of rough pasture; beyond that, level ground and a few shallow drainage cuts. A walker could pass within several paces of the entrance without noticing it. The bank itself is an old mixture of gravel, clay, and thin beds of limestone, worn back unevenly until this harder pocket remained. Its ceiling is not smoothly vaulted. It follows the undersides of the beds, stepping down toward the mouth in broad, blunt ledges.

Nothing here looks freshly broken. Even the edges of fallen stone are rounded. On the lower walls, grit carried by ancient wash has cut faint horizontal grooves; higher up, the rock retains shallow cups where softer inclusions have weathered away. Several of these cups hold dry soil. One holds the shell of a small snail, white enough to be visible when the entrance admits a little light.

That light is scarce. The opening faces away from the sun, and the thorn shades what little sky it exposes. On a clear day there is a grey patch just inside the mouth, followed by a greenish dimness, followed by a depth in which the back wall seems to withdraw. Once your eyes adjust, it proves to be only an arm’s length farther away.

The plants occupy the threshold and almost nowhere else.

They are plentiful there: thick moss cushions on the entrance stones, hart’s-tongue ferns with glossy, undivided leaves, small fronds of spleenwort fitted into seams scarcely wider than a knife cut. Ivy has descended from the bank and spread sideways beneath the first roof ledge. Its leaves overlap densely, catching light reflected from the pale ground outside. A few nettles grow where soil has accumulated against the thorn roots, their stalks short and dark in the cold.

From outside, this growth conceals the hollow. From inside, it makes a heavy green border around a small piece of grey daylight.

Two paces inward, the vegetation thins abruptly. A last fern extends three unusually long leaves toward the entrance. Behind it, there is only a dusting of algae on one damp face of rock. Then bare stone.

The floor is mostly dry. It smells faintly of cold lime and old wood, with salt becoming noticeable only after you have been there a while. Moisture arrives in small, separate ways. Rain finds a root channel and darkens a patch near the mouth. Mist catches on the thorn and falls from its lowest tips. On a few winter nights, condensation beads beneath the roof, joining slowly until a drop becomes heavy enough to fall.

There is no stream to carry anything away. Each wet place dries within its own boundary, leaving a darker rim or a little rearrangement of dust.

Under the lowest ceiling ledge stands a shallow stone dish. Someone pecked its basin out with a harder stone; the marks remain beneath a coat of mineral scale. It is placed exactly where a ceiling drip sometimes falls. Now it holds no water, only two beech leaves blown in from somewhere inland. One has become almost transparent.

The other human traces are equally spare. A wooden peg has been driven into a joint in the limestone. A short piece of cord, stiff with age, hangs from it. Near the entrance, a few stones have been laid edge to edge to make a level footing across the slope. There is no hearth and no soot on the roof. Whatever people did here, they did without a fire, and probably without staying long. Perhaps the hollow was a place to leave a basket or wait out a squall. Its shelter is good; its noise would make any longer occupation difficult.

The cold settles into the hands first. The bank keeps the temperature of the season rather than the hour. A mild afternoon outside scarcely alters the interior, and the stone dish remains painful to touch. In winter a fine white fringe sometimes forms around the entrance roots, while deeper inside the rock stays dark and unfrozen. The ferns survive in that narrow protection, sheltered from wind without being entirely deprived of sky.

There are few animals. A wren occasionally searches the moss at the mouth, appearing and disappearing behind leaves without an audible footfall or call. In warmer months, small moths rest beneath the ledges. Their wings resemble flakes lifted from the limestone. A spider keeps a loose web beside the peg, where almost nothing seems to fly.

Farther in, there is little to eat and nowhere worth going.

The sea’s rhythm is difficult to judge from inside. The passage below distorts it. One wave produces a long, receding growl; the next arrives as three blows close together. Sometimes the fissure exhales so forcefully that the cold air reaches your ankles. Between these surges, the sound falls enough for a second, lesser noise to emerge: pebbles turning somewhere beneath the bank, drawn backward over other pebbles.

The hollow changes by such small increments that a visit reveals none of them. A fern adds a leaf. A thread of ivy finds a new seam. The timber softens on its underside. In a wet winter the stone dish fills, and in a dry spring it empties, leaving a narrower ring inside the old one. Below, the inaccessible passage grows fractionally wider.

When you leave, you must bend again beneath the blackthorn. The return to daylight is modest; outside may be no more than a sunless afternoon over cold grass. But the sound changes immediately. Released from the chamber, it becomes recognizably surf, somewhere down the bank.

Behind you, the leaves resume their cover over the opening. From a few steps away there is only a low, overgrown slope, with no visible reason for the ground beneath it to be so loud.
