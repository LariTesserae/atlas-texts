The chamber lies below the harbor, filled to the underside of its roof. At low tide a narrow pocket of air gathers between two ceiling beams; at high tide that vanishes too. There is no shore inside it, only a pale line of shell growth marking the level to which the water occasionally withdraws.

It is not much larger than a village church. From one end, in clear water, you can make out the opposite wall: three blind arches, an iron service door, and the black circle of an intake. Usually you cannot see that far. Fine material hangs in the water, turning the chamber’s length into a succession of gray distances. A column appears first as a change in the gray, then as a rounded edge, then as a structure carrying the entire darkness overhead.

Everything here was made. The floor is laid block, the walls are poured and faced, the columns have iron cores. Even the low banks of grit in the corners consist largely of the building itself: mortar grains, brick dust, rust scales, pulverized tile. There is no exposed earth. Burrowing animals enter failed joints and the spaces beneath lifted paving.

The floor slopes toward a drainage channel that no longer drains. Water has enlarged it into a shallow trough, broad enough for a ray to settle in. Elsewhere, repeated currents have arranged the loose material into small, remarkably precise features: a crescent behind a bolt head, parallel ripples across a maintenance landing, a steep little slip face below the edge of a step. These are the chamber’s hills and valleys. An old wrench, almost completely incorporated into its crust of corrosion, supports a bank of sediment on one side and a clean scour hollow on the other.

Nothing has a sharp corner anymore.

The steps descending from the service door have worn into a continuous, faintly scalloped slope. Their centers are pale where moving grit still polishes them. At their edges, the original dark surface remains beneath dense clusters of tiny tubes. The door itself has lost its lower panel. Through the opening, crabs pass into a room beyond, carrying pieces of food too large to swallow where they found them.

The place is older than the machinery that keeps it moving. Generations of fittings have been installed in the same walls. A thick ceramic pipe passes through an arch built for something larger; beside it, a narrower steel pipe rests in brackets attached to the abandoned brackets of an earlier one. There are plugged holes, sawn-off flanges, plates fastened over plates. The oldest masonry has become so evenly worn that it looks less damaged than the newer repairs. Its weakness is no longer visible in individual cracks. It has settled into broad surfaces that seem unlikely to change during any single lifetime.

Beyond the wall, pumps still run.

Their sound is the dominant condition here. A deep mechanical note travels through the columns and the water, accompanied by a rapid, loose rattling from somewhere inside the intake. It is difficult to locate either sound. Near the floor, the vibration seems to come up through the paving; beside a pipe, it seems to occupy the pipe’s whole length. Occasionally a valve changes position with a hard metallic blow. The blow crosses the chamber, returns from the walls, and leaves a brief clatter among the loose fittings.

Between these larger sounds there is a constant dry-seeming crackle: small crustaceans in the wall growth, shells shifting against shells, grit passing through a broken grille. The chamber is loud even when nothing conspicuous moves.

Most of the light arrives through a barred opening high in one wall. Outside is harbor water, green in daylight, almost black under cloud. The bars divide what enters into weak vertical bands. These dissolve well before reaching the opposite end.

Only near this opening does anything leafy grow. A few short fronds cling to a seam in the concrete. They are dark olive, thickened, repeatedly cropped at their ends. Lower down, a thin film stains the stone without softening its shape. The chamber’s apparent gardens are animals: pale anemones beneath a sill, mussels crowded along a cable, the low orange folds of sponges on the shaded backs of columns.

Much of the floor is empty. Between the columns lie several meters of bare paving, with perhaps a single shell in the middle. Life favors the boundaries. Shrimp hold upside down under ledges. Small fish gather in the lee of the intake housing, each facing the same direction, moving just enough to remain still. A conger occupies a length of pipe whose open end has been polished clean by its passage. Usually only its head is visible, the lower jaw working slowly.

The larger crabs use the open floor without hesitation. They cross it sideways, stop, raise themselves, then continue toward some scrap that has arrived in the current. Their tracks do not last long.

Every few minutes the pumps draw harder.

The first sign is in the suspended sediment. Individual grains begin to travel rather than hover. Then the fish tighten their formation, the sparse weed bends flat, and threads of debris lift from the trough. Water comes through the upper grille and crosses the chamber diagonally toward the round intake. Under the strongest flow, its damaged inner shutter trembles, producing the rapid rattle heard everywhere.

The current is not violent throughout the room. Behind the columns there are pockets of nearly still water, and near the floor a weaker return flow carries particles the other way. Animals move between these small conditions. A flatfish leaves a sheltered landing to feed in newly disturbed silt. Two fish wait beside a narrow gap where the flow delivers dislodged shrimp. The conger comes slightly farther out of its pipe.

Then the draw eases. Loose particles begin to fall. The fish spread apart.

The water remains cool, including at the end of summer. Warm harbor water enters overhead, but the masonry and the shaded passages beyond keep the lower chamber cold. After heavy rain, the incoming water carries a brown cloud that spreads beneath the ceiling before slowly mixing downward. On those days the high opening is visible only as a brighter patch, and the chamber contracts to whatever lies immediately nearby: a rounded stair, the side of a column, a crab lifting one claw into the flow.

As the cloud settles, the room reappears from the bottom upward. Paving joints emerge. The little sediment banks regain their edges. The opposite arches become distinguishable again.

There is always fresh debris beside the intake. A mussel shell, a strip of translucent skin, a length of fishing line caught on a bolt. Each stronger draw pulls it taut; each pause lets it fall. Beneath it, in a hollow worn through the surface of a paving block, a shrimp keeps clearing the same few grains from its entrance.
