The first impression is that the road has been built along the top of a wall. There is a parapet on one side, a pale drop on the other, and nothing ahead but a line of expansion joints disappearing into warm air.

Then the road widens. A freight yard appears, followed by several kilometers of empty paving, a row of transformer houses, a shallow basin full of white stones. Beyond these are districts low enough to hide behind their own boundary walls. The apparent wall is a plateau. Its opposite edge is beyond the horizon.

The country below is visible only on certain mornings. Usually the escarpment descends into layers of airborne mineral dust, each layer a slightly different yellow. When these clear, lower ranges show through: whole mountain systems worn down to the rounded profiles of knuckles. Their summits lie so far beneath the plateau that their shadows seem detached from them. No river shines anywhere. The branching marks in their valleys are the tracks of old dust slides.

Up here the air is warm, dry, and unexpectedly substantial. Flags would move heavily in it, but there are no flags. Wind is measured by the inclination of weighted copper vanes mounted above the roofs. Most point only a little away from vertical.

The rock holds heat. Sit on a step in the shade and warmth enters through your clothes.

***

The plateau is composed of alternating beds of dark ceramic stone and a softer, buff-colored mineral that powders under a thumbnail. Weather has worked on these beds for an immense time. It has rounded the hard strata into shelves and removed the softer material from beneath them, producing overhangs that extend for streets at a time. Many of the oldest neighborhoods occupy these natural recesses.

There are no fresh-looking peaks. Everything exposed has been rubbed, pitted, eased at the corners. Even fractures have small, polished lips. Wind carries very little material at once, but it has had longer than the buildings, longer than the names of the buildings, longer than the language in which the first surveyors labeled the horizon.

In places, erosion has left isolated masses standing above the plain. They look like broad-backed animals asleep under sheets. Their lower surfaces are dark with cavities. Some contain factories.

Elsewhere the ground is almost level. You can walk for an hour toward a distant pipe junction without seeming to approach it. Small objects become navigational landmarks: a broken insulator, a black boulder, an upright doorframe in a demolished district. There is so little clutter that each remains visible for an unreasonable distance.

The cities do not occupy this space continuously. They occur in knots around elevator heads, thermal wells, and the deep foundations needed for heavy machinery. Between them stretch immense municipal emptinesses, crossed by tram rails and cables on low supports. Street numbers continue through these vacant reaches. A solitary maintenance shed may stand between the addresses of two neighborhoods a day’s journey apart.

***

Industry here is mostly concerned with the height.

Elevator shafts descend through the plateau’s edge to workings far below the visible haze. Their upper houses are long, windowless buildings with rounded roofs, clad in plates the color of old teeth. Somewhere inside, cables pass over wheels taller than houses. The cages move slowly enough that arrival is scheduled by the portion of the day rather than the minute.

They bring up dense ores, heat-bearing blocks, sealed containers of gases extracted from lower strata. Outbound freight consists of machined parts, electrical equipment, thin sheets of unusually pure metal, and empty containers.

There are also engines that work on the daily expansion of the plateau itself.

Their anchor beds are kilometers apart. As the upper rock warms, it moves against the cooler mass beneath; great linkages take up the difference. A piston may travel less than a hand’s breadth between morning and afternoon. That is sufficient. The machines apply this movement to flywheels buried in insulated vaults, accumulating power through elaborate trains of gears.

From outside, an engine station looks inactive. Its cooling fins cast neat shadows on the gravel. A caretaker crosses a courtyard carrying a long-handled brush. Only a pointer creeping across a dial proves that anything is happening.

At dusk the mechanism changes direction.

The houses nearest the anchor beds have sliding thresholds. Their inhabitants learn where a cupboard can be attached to a wall and where it must stand free. Old public buildings display paired survey marks on either side of their entrances. The marks are almost aligned in the morning. By late afternoon a narrow strip of wall shows between them.

***

At first, there seem to be no animals.

Then one of the white stones beside the road turns over.

Its underside is dark and jointed. It raises itself on six short legs, advances into the shadow of another stone, and settles. All around it, the basin is occupied. Tens of thousands of pale-backed creatures lie with their shells oriented toward the sun.

They are feeding.

A thin, amber seam runs along each shell, opening as the animal warms. Through this seam it exchanges gases with the air. Beneath the shell, layered tissues keep separate mineral compounds that react when brought together. Dust supplies some of what they need; other substances are acquired by rasping the rock. They leave shallow tracks with a sheen like rubbed pencil lead.

Their bodies contain no freely moving water. Cut or cracked, they close slowly with a translucent resin.

Other creatures hunt them. Along the drainage trenches—trenches intended for dust, not rain—flat black animals wait beneath loose grilles. They can remain still for weeks. When prey touches the fine hairs at the entrance to a grille, the waiting animal folds upward with the abruptness of a sprung latch. This is among the quickest things likely to happen in the open landscape.

There are tall, delicate grazers too, though nothing resembling grass grows here. They browse the mineral blooms on warm pipes. Their feet divide into brushes that distribute their weight over fragile crusts, and their narrow heads move from side to side, testing surfaces. Around substations they gather in groups of thirty or forty. Engineers fit removable collars to the pipework so the animals can strip the deposits without damaging a joint.

Above the escarpment, broad translucent bodies ride the rising air.

From below they resemble scraps of packaging caught in a thermal. Up close, each is the size of a room. Fine ribs spread beneath a taut membrane, and a bundle of hooked limbs hangs under the center. They feed on smaller airborne animals and on the charged dust that collects along their leading edges.

Sometimes they land on the roofs of elevator houses. Their membranes collapse into tidy pleats. Roof workers leave broad clear paths around them; a grounded adult takes several days to gather enough heat for flight.

Animal abundance is measured here by occupied surface rather than movement. A wall that seems bare may carry a thousand small lives, each tucked into a pore or aligned with a vein of favorable mineral. Looking patiently changes the population of a place.

***

There is little noise.

The great machinery is isolated from its buildings by deep beds of granular stone. Trams run on smooth rails with broad wheels, traveling at an unhurried pace between stations. Most of their journey passes without anyone boarding.

In the residential courts you hear a shutter close, a brush drawing grit across a threshold, the dry ticking of shells as a sunning colony adjusts its angle. Occasionally a load is transferred inside an elevator house and a low tone spreads through the ground. It arrives in the soles of the feet before it becomes audible.

Wind is louder at the edge. There it pours across the parapets and down the face of the world, carrying the smell of hot iron and freshly broken stone.

The light is ordinary enough to make everything else stranger. Under a pale blue sky, walls have familiar shadows. A red service door is simply red. Workers shade their eyes to read a gauge. At noon, someone sits beneath an awning to eat while a room-sized animal dries its folded wings on the neighboring roof.

***

Beyond the last inhabited districts, the infrastructure continues.

There are switching stations with polished handles, maintained tracks, rows of ceramic markers. Power lines run toward installations that cannot be seen even from the tallest towers. Some cross open ground for hundreds of kilometers without serving a single building along the way.

Farther still, the plateau rises in shallow steps. Each step is the height of an ordinary mountain, but so broad that the ascent scarcely registers from the surface. Dust collects against its foot in long, motionless-looking banks. The banks advance a few meters in a lifetime, burying one row of markers while exposing another.

Old settlements emerge from them intact at roof level. Crews clear the ventilation stacks first. If the machinery beneath remains useful, the place is connected to the grid again. Otherwise the exposed roofs become basking grounds, and warm-bodied grazers stand in what were once upper rooms.

In the evening, the plateau releases its stored heat. The air above the paving trembles. Copper vanes slowly lean the other way.

Lights come on in separate districts, modest clusters with great darkness between. Near the escarpment, the roof animals unfold. One by one they step outward, membranes opening over the drop, and the rising warmth takes their weight.

Their shadows pass across the elevator houses. Inside, a cage that began its ascent before dawn is still coming up.
