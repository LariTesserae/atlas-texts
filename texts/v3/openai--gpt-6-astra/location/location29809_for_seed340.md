The ground is still deciding where to rise.

Across the basin, pale bulges lift beneath a cover of hooked leaves. Some swell slowly enough that the animals grazing them scarcely adjust their feet. Others come up in an afternoon: a seam opens, a folded ridge shoulders through, and a whole slope of vegetation tips sideways with a noise like a building being dragged across stone. There is no stone close enough to hear. Under the leaves are stems; under the stems, compressed generations of woody tissue; deeper still, hot, unfinished growth.

This is a country beneath the surface, though it has little of a cavern’s darkness. The roof stands several hundred paces overhead, irregular and heavily ribbed, disappearing behind airborne husks toward the far side of the basin. Vast yellow plates grow between its ribs. They shine with a steady, unflattering light, bright enough to show the dust lodged in every joint of an insect’s legs. Where a plate is newly formed, its light is almost white. Old ones brown at the edges and eventually peel away.

Their fall is one of the few events that briefly clears the air. Animals scatter from the shadow; the plate comes down slowly, rocking, then breaks across the vegetation with a clap felt through the soles. Within minutes, things are eating it.

There is no water here. No pools in the hollows, no damp beneath the bark, no coolness gained by digging. A split stem exposes dry honeycomb and a bead of amber material that draws itself into a thread, hardens, and snaps. The vegetation runs on heat, mineral dust, and a volatile oil held in sealed vessels finer than hairs. Bruise a leaf and the smell is sharp enough to make the jaw ache. Open a large trunk carelessly and its inner pressure can strip the skin from a hand.

Almost everything that looks like land is alive.

The broad terraces are overlapping leaves, each as thick as a mattress, their upper surfaces armored with gray scales. Ravines are gaps between bundles of stems. What appears, at a distance, to be a low range of hills resolves into the backs of enormous root-crowns pushing upward from the common mass. Their exposed tissues are ivory, pink, and a green so dark it seems black until the overhead light strikes it squarely. They have not yet acquired bark. Beetles gather along their fresh seams in such numbers that the hills appear to be lacing themselves shut.

The heat is close and persistent. It comes from below, from the growth itself, and collects under the overlapping foliage. Higher on the ridges there is a current of warm air strong enough to pull loose clothing sideways. Down in the stem-gullies, the air barely travels, but everything in it does: spores, scales, splinters, flying animals, the torn membranes of things caught and eaten overhead.

The sound has no background.

A person can shout directly into another person’s ear and convey only urgency. The low part of the noise comes from the basin’s great structural plants, whose hollow trunks lengthen in jerks. Each jerk sounds a different note. Some are deep enough to make the ribs feel loose. Above these come the snapping of seed chambers, the rasp of innumerable feeding mouths, and the metallic cries of six-winged creatures that cling beneath the luminous roof. Herds drum across the terraces. Armored bodies collide in the gullies. Somewhere, always, a stem is splitting along its full height.

The sounds move through the ground more reliably than through the air. Rest a palm against a supporting stalk and a distant collapse becomes distinct: three hard knocks, a long shudder, then the busy scratching of animals arriving at the break. The inhabitants who travel here carry thin, forked listening sticks. One end goes against a stem, the other between the teeth. They can tell whether a path ahead is merely crowded or about to unfold.

There are animals everywhere, but few remain recognizable for long in the crush.

Flat, many-legged grazers slide beneath the leaf-scales, lifting them in traveling ripples. Their bodies fit so exactly into the spaces they feed on that an empty scale is more conspicuous than an occupied one. Tall browsers move on narrow, stilted limbs, placing each foot between thorn clusters with fussy precision. Their heads divide into opposing combs which strip insulating fibers from trunks. Small bright animals ride those combs, darting inward to steal fragments before the jaws meet.

Across the higher ground travel processions of heavy, eyeless beasts. Their broad feet test the tension of each surface before committing their weight. They follow the freshest growth, crushing buds under their bellies and leaving behind a fine white powder. The plants absorb it through pores that open only after the herd has passed. Behind every procession comes a dense attendance of runners, borers, scavengers, and things hunting the scavengers.

Some of the smallest animals are also the loudest. A thumbnail-sized creature wedges itself into a hollow thorn and vibrates its abdomen until the entire branch screams. Thousands do this together. The vibration shakes loose a particular kind of pollen, and for several minutes the branch is hidden by a saffron cloud. Other animals plunge into it with their feeding fans spread. Predators follow by touch.

Nothing flies in a straight line for more than a few body lengths. The air is too crowded, and the plants move too quickly.

At intervals, a district of the basin enters a growth spasm.

The warning is a sweet, scorched odor and a tightening of the leaf surfaces underfoot. Grazers flatten themselves. Flying animals rise toward the roof. Then the ground begins to open.

A field of stiff shoots thrusts up through the old cover, carrying dead stalks and living passengers with it. Leaves unroll hard enough to knock animals from neighboring stems. Tendrils lash across gaps, catch, shorten, and haul whole masses of vegetation together. For a while the basin resembles an immense workshop in which every tool has been set working and no one is in charge.

The violence produces usable country. A new bridge draws taut above a gully. A hollow opens where two crowns have pulled apart. A slope that was impassable yesterday becomes a broad, gently inclined leaf, still warm from unfolding. Animals pour onto it before its surface has hardened. Their tracks remain embedded in the armor afterward, though the traffic that made them has already gone elsewhere.

There are scattered signs of people.

On the flanks of older root-crowns stand little shelters woven from dead ribs and tiled with shed bark. They are anchored at three points, never four. Four anchors will hold a house rigid while the ground changes shape beneath it; three allow it to tilt. The inhabitants wedge their sleeping platforms level with blocks of pith.

They keep very little loose. A dropped food scrap brings biting scavengers. A dropped cord is carried off for nest material. A basket left touching a young tendril may be woven into the hillside by morning.

Their tools are hooks, wedges, awls, and broad knives made from the glazed teeth of the great browsers. The best cutting edges are traded across the basin wrapped in insulating fiber. Metal exists in a few small pieces: a black pin at a fastening, a battered ring, a blade repeatedly sharpened until it is narrower than a finger.

Paths are marked with hanging shapes rather than scratches. Cuts heal or become attractive to feeders; a marker suspended on a long cord stays visible as the vegetation shifts. Three pierced disks mean that a supporting stem has begun to hollow. A bundle of white splinters marks a place where the overhead plates have been loosening. Some markers hang over empty space, left behind by routes that no longer exist.

Near one settlement, a row of carved posts follows the rim of a newly opened depression. The posts lean at different angles. Below them, several acres of naked root tissue flex in the yellow light. Tubes appear on its surface, fill, branch, and are drawn inward again. Animals crowd the edge but hesitate to descend. Now and then a small one ventures onto the pale floor, tests it with its mouth, and retreats.

By the next day there may be a thicket there. Or a towering, hollow crown. Sometimes the new growth closes over without making any feature at all, swallowing the boundary posts and leaving an unremarkable stretch of scaled green ground.

Even the oldest inhabitants come to watch such places. They stand with their listening sticks between their teeth, holding their baskets clear of the shoots.

Beyond them the basin extends through layers of moving foliage: broad bright terraces, dark gullies crowded with limbs, ridges laboring upward under their loads of feeding animals. The far distances are obscured by dust rather than darkness. Shapes emerge from it, become briefly distinct, and vanish behind another rising mass.

Underfoot, a small ridge presses against the sole. It is easy to mistake for an animal passing beneath the leaves. Then it presses again, harder, and the hooks along the nearest stem turn outward.

Everyone steps back.
