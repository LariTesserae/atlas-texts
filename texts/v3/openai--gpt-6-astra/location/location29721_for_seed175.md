The hollow lies high on the mountain, just below the level where clouds lose their undersides against the slopes. It is small enough to cross in a few minutes, though there is no straight dry way across it. Water occupies the middle in three shallow sheets, separated by pale backs of stone. Around them, a loose margin of vegetation climbs toward a low, rounded rim.

Beyond that rim there is mostly sky. From one particular place, between two leaning rocks, you can see the lower country: a blue distance, a river scarcely brighter than its banks. Up here, the air is warm. The stone is warm too, even under the water.

Nothing rises sharply. The hollow has been wearing down for so long that its ledges have become shoulders, its cracks broad seams. A large slab near the center has a groove deep enough to hold your forearm, polished along its whole length by water that now takes another route. The abandoned groove is filled with dark soil and six thick leaves.

The leaves belong to a single plant. Each emerges separately from the soil, on a short, fleshy stem, and opens into a shallow bowl. Rain collects in them. Their rims are amber and slightly translucent; their centers are a green so dark that the water resting there seems deeper than it is. A few have grown heavy enough to recline against the rock. Beneath them, the stone is stained brown.

There are other plants, but they occur in islands. A stand of jointed stalks occupies one end of the hollow, with bare wet ground around it. Farther along, a low tree has spread almost horizontally, its trunk thick, its branches carrying generous clusters of soft leaves. Its exposed roots travel over several feet of empty rock before finding purchase. Between these presences, the basin remains open. You can see the shape of the ground all the way to the far side.

What grows here grows abundantly, but only where it has begun.

The bare spaces are not barren. They hold a thin, clear skin of water, a little mineral sediment, and sometimes the green haze of something too low to cast a shadow. Yet seedlings do not spread into them. The edges of the planted islands are precise in places: roots stop along a faint line, moss forms a rounded lip, fallen leaves soften and disappear without anything rising through their remains.

Viewed from above, these islands would resemble the wet marks left by separate stones lifted out of a pool.

The largest sheet of water is no deeper than a hand. Its floor is pale and uneven, crossed by long bands of rusty red. Ordinary daylight reaches it, softened only when cloud comes over the rim. There is no luminous mist, no darkness beneath the surface that its depth cannot account for. You can see a fallen leaf lying on the bottom, including the little tear near its stem.

But its reflection is still attached to the tree.

The reflected branch above it carries one more leaf than the actual branch. When a breeze shifts the canopy, that extra leaf turns with the others. The leaf on the bottom does not move.

Elsewhere the discrepancy is older. A bare stone beside the water reflects a low clump of stalks, though nothing grows there now. One pool shows the rounded tip of a boulder that has weathered away above the surface. The water retains these things selectively. Most of what it reflects is present and exact: sky, cloud, the underside of a leaf. Only at the margins does it continue with something the hollow has already lost.

There is no obvious way to tell how long it keeps them.

Water enters through the rock rather than over it. Along the upper edge of the basin, moisture gathers on a vertical face and gradually joins into broad, flat beads. They swell without falling. Eventually two touch, then three, and a clear strip slides down the stone.

This is one of the few movements you can sit and watch from beginning to end.

It makes almost no sound. Occasionally a drop releases from a root and strikes a pool with a small, rounded note. The stalks at the far end rub together only when the wind reaches down into the hollow, which it seldom does. There is no buzzing over the water, no sudden disturbance among the leaves, no movement threading through the mud. Fallen wood stays where it falls and becomes soft from within.

A broken branch near the low tree has been there long enough to sink halfway into its own dark residue. Small white growths line its underside. Above it, the newer bark is smooth and damp, marked by shallow creases rather than scars.

The hollow smells of wet mineral, opened stems, and warm leaf mold. When the sun stands directly over it, the smell strengthens. Moisture rises a short distance from the exposed stone and disappears before becoming visible. The air feels close against the skin, though the open sky is enormous.

At the downhill edge, the basin narrows into a spillway scarcely wider than a foot. No one has cut it. Its sides are rounded inward, and the water passes through on a smooth convex tongue before following the outside of the mountain out of sight. There is no waterfall noise. The slope beyond must be gentle, or the water must spread very thinly.

Beside this outlet, loose flakes of stone lie in a shallow bed of silt. Some have edges fine enough to cut a stem. They have weathered out of a darker seam, and several fit comfortably in the hand. Nothing has gathered them. Their sharpness is temporary; the older ones are small, blunt ovals, indistinguishable from the rest of the gravel.

Late in the day, cloud comes level with the rim. The distant country goes first, then the leaning rocks lose their far sides. Light remains in the hollow, broad and white. The pools become difficult to distinguish from the wet stone around them.

For a while, the reflected leaves are easier to see than the real ones.

Warm droplets collect along the branches. One thick leaf slowly lowers under the weight of its water. Long before it tips, a clear swelling forms at its edge and hangs there, holding the whole pale hollow upside down.
