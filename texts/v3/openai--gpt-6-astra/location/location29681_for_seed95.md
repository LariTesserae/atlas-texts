From the middle of the plateau, nothing looks high. The land is broad and gently dished, with long, shallow rises that conceal other shallow rises. Distance gathers in bands of tawny gray. Only at the southern margin, where the ground ends without lifting into a ridge, do you see the clouds far below, their upper surfaces creased by winds that never reach this place.

It is warm enough to lie comfortably against the stone. By afternoon, even the shaded hollows hold a little heat. The air is thin, the sunlight ordinary and clear, and the horizon has the faint blue cast of something viewed through a great deal of emptiness.

There must once have been mountains here. Their roots remain as broad swirls of different rock: dull red, cream, a dark material almost purple when wet. Everything that could break away easily has gone. The harder bands stand a finger’s width above the softer ones. Across several miles, these small differences become the only ridges. You can follow one all day, placing your feet along its rounded spine, and never climb higher than your own height.

The ground seems crowded with loose stones.

Then a patch of it exhales.

***

Most of the animals are low and broad. Their upper surfaces resemble the plateau so closely that distance erases the distinction. Some are no larger than a palm, with overlapping plates and a fringe of dusty legs. Others occupy the width of a room. These larger ones have shallow domed backs, seams packed with pale grit, and heads that withdraw into a fold along whichever edge is moving forward.

They lie close together. Often their shells touch. Along the shallow drainage lines, the smaller bodies fit into spaces between the larger ones, making an almost continuous pavement of living backs.

Nothing in this abundance suggests haste. A foot unfolds from beneath a rim. A shell tips, settles, and is still. Here and there, a slow rearrangement passes through a group: one animal shifts to admit another, and that shift travels outward until thirty bodies have moved a fraction of their width. By the time it reaches the far side, you may have forgotten where it began.

Their contact makes very little sound. The shell edges are soft, padded with a velvety substance that wears smooth where neighbors rub against one another.

Walking here requires attention. Bare ground has a grain, while the backs of the animals tend to be smoother, but the oldest animals are weathered and the youngest rocks freshly exposed. If you step on a small one, it lowers itself and waits. If you step on a large one, you may feel a delicate muscular adjustment beneath your sole.

Many have shallow hollows on their backs. Dust collects there, along with shed skins, tiny white eggs, and occasionally water. Entire colonies of smaller creatures inhabit these hollows. They move between the plates as if between streets, disappearing into shade when a cloud passes and emerging again when the shell warms.

Some of the largest animals carry several generations of such residents. Their backs have the settled, particular appearance of places where life has been going on for a very long time.

***

Water arrives unevenly.

Rain may cross the western half of the plateau while the east remains dry for weeks. It falls in warm, brief curtains, darkening the ground and bringing out the purple in the buried rock. There are no rushing streams. The slopes are too slight, the surface too receptive. Water spreads into thin sheets, travels slowly through the crowded animals, and gathers in basins whose edges are difficult to find.

For a day or two these pools reflect the sky. Then they become a collection of separate mirrors, then a dark stain, then nothing visible.

But there is moisture beneath the shells.

At night, dew beads on the animals’ upper plates. Fine channels lead it toward the undersides, where it is held among folds of skin. The close-packed groups conserve it together. Lifted rims reveal a surprising coolness, a scent like wet clay, and the pale movement of creatures that never enter open light.

The plateau’s few plants grow at the edges of these living shelters. They have short, tough leaves, usually in pairs, pressed nearly flat against the ground. Their roots travel horizontally until they find the damp beneath a resting animal. A large shell may be bordered on one side by a sparse crescent of green.

When its owner moves, the exposed plants close their leaves. Some survive until another body settles over them. Others dry into little copper-colored knots.

In sheltered cracks there are also black films that soften and turn olive after rain. Animals graze them with mouths hidden underneath their bodies, leaving clean arcs on the stone. Several of the broad-backed species grow a similar film within their translucent outer plates. Seen against the light, these shells contain faint branching colors, like leaves buried in horn.

At midday, those animals turn their widest surfaces toward the sun.

***

Across the plateau stand scattered hollow structures, far enough apart that each becomes a landmark.

The nearest is waist-high and shaped like an overturned bowl with one side lifted. Its wall is chalky, thick, and full of narrow chambers. The outer surface bears hundreds of fine horizontal lines. Around its foot lies a skirt of older material, softened almost into the ground.

Inside, the air smells faintly of salt.

These are communal nesting places. They grow by accumulation: each season the occupants add a thin secretion around the entrances and over the roof. No single animal makes much of the structure. The same small motions, repeated by thousands of bodies, slowly lift a wall.

Some nests are still inhabited. Their entrances are occupied by small, blunt faces that remain motionless for hours. Others have been empty long enough to lose their roofs. A few have worn down into rings that resemble the natural bands in the bedrock.

Along a shallow saddle, seven of them stand in an irregular line. From a distance they could be the remains of a settlement. Up close, each has its own proportions, its own pattern of repaired cracks and sealed openings. Warm air emerges from one doorway. Another holds a little pool of clear water, with a single pale animal suspended upside down beneath the surface.

The occupied nests are quiet. Inside their walls, young animals press together in stacked chambers. Their movement produces a dry, intermittent whisper, audible only with an ear close to the shell.

***

There are so many lives here that the eye eventually stops counting bodies and begins noticing intervals.

A bare patch of red stone.

A narrow passage between resting herds.

A hollow where nothing lies.

Such vacancies draw attention. Some are newly opened feeding grounds. Some cover underground chambers whose roofs are too thin to bear weight. Others remain empty for reasons that do not become apparent during a visit.

The animals avoid one broad oval near the plateau’s center. Its surface is perfectly ordinary: pale stone, a little dust, three low plants growing along a seam. Yet the crowded bodies form a clean margin around it. Even the smallest creatures, working through debris at its edge, turn back.

At sunrise, the empty oval is the last place to receive dew.

By noon, it is the first to cool.

Beyond it, the land continues for miles.

***

The larger roaming animals are easier to recognize. They stand on narrow, jointed legs and carry their bodies high enough to pass over the resting shells. Their feet open into soft discs when they touch down. They browse the sparse plants, inspect nesting walls, and lower long faces into the temporary pools.

Often one stands with a foot raised, waiting for room to place it.

Their routes persist even when no visible trail marks them. A procession may take most of the afternoon to cross a shallow valley. Those at the front pause; those behind pause in turn. Between them, smaller creatures continue their minute traffic.

Occasionally a shadow moves over everything.

High above, broad-winged animals circle on the weak rising warmth. Their wings hardly change shape. They descend only after long consideration, settling into an open space with an awkward folding of limbs. On the ground they seem less like birds than another kind of shelter. Small animals approach the shade beneath them almost at once.

Death is absorbed quietly into this arrangement. A shell whose owner has died remains useful. Its damp underside becomes a nursery; its plates loosen into separate roofs. Fine white scavengers gather beneath it, working over months. Eventually the empty back sinks flat, retaining its outline until weather and passing feet have made it indistinguishable from the ground.

There are places where living animals rest upon the shells of ancestors, which rest upon older shells already becoming a chalky layer between the rock bands.

***

Toward evening, the plateau releases its heat.

A faint smell rises from the damp pockets: mineral, bodily, a little sweet. The plants lift their paired leaves. Along the eastern drainage lines, hundreds of shell rims rise together by the width of a finger, admitting cooler air.

This is the busiest hour, though you might overlook it. Tiny bodies cross the open stone. An adult eases aside while its young pass beneath it. In one of the distant nesting bowls, something knocks softly three times against an interior wall.

Then the sound stops.

The southern cloud field turns pink below the edge of the land. Up here, the light lingers across the low rises, picking out each shell and worn stone separately. What looked like an unbroken surface at noon becomes an immense gathering of particular bodies.

You sit on a patch of warm rock until the warmth begins to leave it.

Nearby, an animal has been approaching throughout the afternoon. It reaches the shadow of your leg, pauses, and lowers itself into the small cool place you have made.
