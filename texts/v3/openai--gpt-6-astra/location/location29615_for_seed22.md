The plain is so open that the weather can be seen happening in separate places.

Far to the west, a short brown rain falls beneath a cloud scarcely larger than its own shadow. Eastward, something is lifting dust in a straight line. Between them lie miles of bare, dark ground, faintly luminous at the cracks. The air is warm. There is little wind at walking height, though high overhead long filaments of cloud are being pulled apart with considerable speed.

The noise is everywhere.

At first it resembles an ocean heard from inside a pipe: a deep, continuous concussion, with sharper sounds passing through it. Then the ear begins to distinguish layers. A grinding underneath the feet. Vast leathery claps from the south. A shrill, wavering chorus that advances across the plain and recedes without quite becoming quiet. Occasionally there is a single report so heavy that the loose cloth of a sleeve lifts against the arm.

Nothing immediately visible seems large enough to account for any of it.

The ground has only recently become ground. It is made of a black mineral substance pushed upward in broad, shallow sheets, each sheet retaining the folds and thin curled edges of its arrival. Where two meet, a seam rises to ankle height, sharp enough to cut a boot. There are no rounded stones. Almost no soil. Dust collects in the lee of these seams, and in the dust lie pale insect cases, fragments of leaf, and little transparent teeth.

Some of the cracks give off warmth. Others draw air inward.

Looking down into one, you can see a green depth.

The vegetation begins an inch beneath the surface and continues out of sight. Fernlike fronds crowd the walls, glossy and intricately divided. Thick runners loop across the fissure, bearing soft orange growths on their undersides. There are leaves with translucent margins and leaves folded around themselves into long, moist throats. Everything leans into everything else. Where there is space for a root, a root has found it.

Yet the open ground between fissures remains almost perfectly bare.

From above, this country would resemble a dark plate with green fractures. At ground level, the fractures become ravines, and the ravines occasionally open into shallow basins so densely grown that it is impossible to tell how far down they go. Their rims are edged with low trees whose branches spread flat over the plain. The crowns are lush, heavy, and motionless until an animal moves inside them.

There is always an animal moving inside them.

Small six-limbed browsers climb along the undersides of branches. Copper-colored creatures pour in ribbons through the leaf litter. Something with a long flexible snout probes the throats of the folded leaves, withdrawing each time with a wet click. Across one basin, pale bodies hang from silk so fine that they seem suspended without support. They turn together when a louder pulse passes through the ground.

Water is handled carefully here. The plants breathe into the hollows, and the hollows keep their breath. Condensation forms under leaves, beneath bark, on the cool bellies of animals sleeping through the warm hours. A drop may pass from leaf to fur to mouth to root without ever reaching open air.

After rain, the bare plain dries in minutes. Its seams stay dark for days.

The city begins without an outskirts.

A polished rail emerges from the ground, runs beside you for a hundred yards, and disappears again. Farther on stands a doorway, taller than a tree, casting a thin green shadow. Through it is the same plain, but seen from a slightly different direction. Walk around the frame and the discrepancy is gone.

Beyond this are other structures, widely separated: a white cylinder on three legs; a low building with a roof like a folded wing; a sequence of steps descending into an unbroken surface. They are so far apart that each can seem the only building in the world until you turn and find another at the horizon.

Nevertheless, this is unmistakably a city. Curbs follow the mineral ridges. Small lights mark crossings where no roads are visible. The edge of each planted basin has been finished with a narrow strip of pale material, warm and faintly yielding under the hand. Here and there, a sign changes as you approach, arranging its dark marks according to the direction from which you have come.

There are districts. You can feel the boundaries in your teeth.

In one district, the prevailing roar acquires a high metallic edge, and all the broad leaves face east. In another, footsteps reach the ear just before the foot touches down. A third contains no echoes at all. The sound is still immense there, but curiously close, as if every distant event were occurring just beyond a wall.

The buildings are not connected by continuous streets. Distance is one of the things the city maintains.

At intervals, the doorway frames darken. Their interiors fill with a dense, trembling grey, and the thunder rises until it seems to occupy the skull itself. Across the plain, leaves close. Animals grip their branches. Loose grit makes brief geometric arrangements on the ground.

Then something arrives.

Sometimes it is a narrow tram carrying no passengers. Sometimes a stack of sealed containers. Once, a whole stand of trees passes through, its roots held in a shallow black vessel, its resident birds spilling out ahead of it in a frantic cloud. The vessel glides a few yards beyond the frame and settles into a waiting depression. Fine tendrils immediately extend from the neighboring vegetation.

The immense noise subsides to its usual immensity.

The spaces between structures are necessary. A building brought too close to another begins to borrow its rooms. A basin moved without sufficient warning loses some of its shade, which remains behind on the ground and has to be collected later. There are old mistakes in the city: a balcony projecting from empty air, a line of windows visible only when rain passes through them, a stair whose lowest step is several miles from the rest.

Most have been colonized.

The window line carries nests. The orphaned balcony supports a tree with scarlet fruit. At dusk, which is only a slight deepening of the permanent dimness, animals gather beneath it to catch what falls. They wait with their faces turned upward while the balcony slowly rotates.

The sky gives little light. Its cloud cover is thin but dark, a smoky amber through which no distinct sun can be found. Buildings show their edges with subdued illumination. The fissures provide more: greenish light from leaves, dull red from the new mineral below, occasional white flashes from creatures startling one another in the roots.

Those flashes can spread for miles.

One basin flares, then another. A luminous shiver runs along a crack toward the horizon. With it comes the shrill chorus, swelling as each colony takes up the alarm. Winged animals lift out of the vegetation in sheets, briefly making the vacant air seem crowded. They are small, broad-winged, almost weightless. Their cries are magnified by sacs in their chests and by the hollow ground beneath them. A flock no larger than a drifting patch of smoke can produce the sound of a collapsing cliff.

Under that cover, predators move.

You see only the disturbance: leaves pressing down in a traveling furrow, a hanging body suddenly absent, a long tail slipping over the rim of a crack. The city’s low lamps brighten when a large hunter approaches a crossing. This seems to be for the animal’s benefit. Nothing stops it.

Later, warm air begins to flow out of the northern seams. The mineral sheets tick and shift. A new ridge rises very slowly across an empty plaza, breaking its immaculate surface into sharp black petals. Dust falls into the opening. The nearest trees bend toward it.

By morning, there will be threads of green along the fresh edge. Within a few days, leaves will hide the glow.

For now the fissure is bare, breathing noisily into the dim air. A maintenance machine approaches across the wide vacancy, low and silver, traveling on no visible means of support. It pauses beside the opening. Behind it, at generous intervals, the city lights come on, marking a new route around the place where the ground has begun.
