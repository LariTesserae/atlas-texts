The place is a shallow notch just below the summit, small enough that a person standing at its center could throw a stone against either wall. Beyond its open end there is no visible slope. The mountain drops away too steeply, and the clouds lie far below, their upper surface bright and apparently solid. Other summits show through them at long intervals. From here, they look less like mountains than stones left on a white floor.

Inside the notch, everything is close.

Thin slabs of pale rock stand on edge, crowded together in rows that bend with the mountain’s grain. Some rise above head height; others have worn down to blunt teeth. The passages between them are shoulder-wide, then hand-wide, then too narrow to admit anything but light. Their upper edges are rounded by old weather. Along the bottom, where frost has loosened the softer layers, they are undercut into little shelves and hollows. Loose flakes fill these hollows, arranged by repeated thawing and freezing into surprisingly neat stacks.

There is nowhere level to put a foot without looking first.

The stone is mostly a milky gray, but freshly opened surfaces are blue. A fracture that happened this morning has the deep, clean blue of ice seen through a hole, although it is dry rock. Over years the color fades. The older breaks become chalky; the oldest acquire a faint yellow skin, especially where water has passed over them. You can read a little of the notch’s history by these colors: a blue chip beneath a gray wall, a yellow channel interrupted by a younger fall, pale fragments lodged above the cracks from which they came, lifted a fraction at a time by freezing water.

Nothing grows in the cracks. There are no roots holding the rubble, no moss darkening the seepage, no wiry grass at the sheltered edges. What looks from a distance like a patch of lichen resolves into a crust of greenish mineral, hard and cold beneath a fingernail. The small black specks on the sunward faces are crystals.

The light is almost excessive. It comes directly from the bare sky, rises again from the cloud field, and scatters off the pale walls. Even the undersides of ledges are visible. Shadows survive only deep between the slabs, where they make narrow purple cuts. At midday the brightness flattens distances so thoroughly that a stone within reach can seem to belong to the far wall. Then a small movement of the head separates everything again.

Despite the light, the air remains cold. A hand placed on a sunny slab finds a little warmth in its surface; a hand slipped behind it meets winter. That difference keeps the notch occupied.

Frost retreats from one side of a fissure while thickening on the other. A bead of water forms on a lip, swells, and does not fall. It draws itself along an almost invisible seam, darkening the stone by degrees. Farther down, it joins another bead. Together they reach a shaded shelf and freeze into a clear, flattened lens. By late afternoon the lens has lifted one grain of rock from its bed.

Most of the water arrives without rain. Clouds rise against the mountain in the hours before dawn, entering the notch as a fine, fast-moving mist. They leave droplets on the exposed edges and feathers of rime on the coldest faces. When the cloud sinks away, the place emerges glittering. Much of the frost vanishes directly into the dry air. A little melts, and that little is enough to feed three shallow basins near the rear wall.

The largest basin would hold a helmet’s worth of water. Its rim is white with deposits. Its center is so clear that the surface becomes visible only when a drop strikes it, or when a thin plate of ice turns slightly and catches the sun. Two lower cups receive its overflow during the brief thaw. The last has a crack through its floor and is usually empty.

Someone worked on these basins once.

The upper one has been deepened with blows that left overlapping thumb-sized hollows. Beneath a projecting slab, a low wall of fitted stones keeps falling grit out of the water. No mortar remains because none was used. The builder chose pieces whose irregularities locked together, inserting tiny wedges where necessary. Several wedges have fallen out. Others have become fixed by mineral crust.

A narrow channel leads from the basin toward a shelter assembled against the rear wall. Shelter is perhaps too generous a word: two upright stones, a heavy roof slab, and enough room underneath for one person to sit with knees drawn up. The roof is blackened in a patch near its lower edge. Whatever was burned here had to be brought from far below.

There is no door, no bedding, no container left behind. On the inner wall, at seated eye level, six short grooves have been cut into the rock. Their bottoms are weathered. A seventh mark is shallower and may be only a scratch.

The wind passes mostly overhead. You can hear it pulling across the summit without feeling more than an occasional cold pressure in the face. Within the notch, sound is local and spare: a drop entering a basin; the faint scrape of a flake settling; a dry click from somewhere among the slabs.

The clicks come most often after sunlight leaves the floor. Each sounds distinct in the quiet, though it can be impossible to locate. Some are the release of warming or cooling stone. Others come from ice taking hold in the wet seams. Once in a while a chip falls, strikes two ledges, and stops. There is no long cascade. The rubble has found places to rest.

Late in the day, light enters through a split high in the western wall. For perhaps twenty minutes a bright bar crosses the crowded slabs and reaches the shelter. It illuminates the cut grooves, the smoke stain, the tiny wedges in the basin wall. The wet channels shine above and below it.

Then the bar climbs out of reach. The water skins over. In the deepest basin, a bubble travels beneath the new ice until it catches against a submerged ridge.

The notch does not become dark yet. It remains full of light reflected from the clouds below, while the first stars appear over its narrow opening. All around the shelter, in seams too small to see, the remaining water begins its slow expansion.
