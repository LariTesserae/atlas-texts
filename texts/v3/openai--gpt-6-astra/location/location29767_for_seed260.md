The heights are still being made.

Across a region perhaps two days wide, pale ridges rise from an underside that cannot be seen. There is no continuous ground beneath them. Each ridge hangs with its own slopes and hollows, its own burden of loose stone, while enormous intervals remain unfilled. Through those intervals you can see the lower faces of neighboring mountains: angular, unfinished, threaded with dark seams that end abruptly in the air. Far below, more distant surfaces show as faint planes. It is difficult to tell which are land and which are places where the darkness has acquired an edge.

The light comes from the exposed interiors of the stone. It is weak, gray-violet, strongest along a fresh split. There is no sun to give the slopes a common direction. A recess may be brighter than the ridge above it; the underside of a boulder may illuminate the soles of your feet. Across a whole valley, one newly appearing fault supplies enough light to reveal terraces, sparse buildings, and patches of vegetation that were present in the dark for centuries.

Nothing is wet. There are no streams in the gullies, no ice in the high shade, no dampness under a lifted rock. The air is cool and so dry that woven fiber stiffens within a few hours. What looks from a distance like frost is a growth of white mineral needles. They emerge point first, very slowly, without ever having been dissolved in anything.

The stone itself has scarcely begun to age. Edges remain sharp. Slopes are arrangements of planes rather than the rounded consequences of weather. There are basins, but they have not been hollowed by water; the surrounding heights simply came into existence earlier. On some escarpments the lowest layers are younger than the summit. You can see the pale new material pressing upward against older, darker strata, lifting entire ledges without cracking them.

This process makes a terrible sound.

It is present everywhere: a sustained grinding pressure, broad and deep, with so much high metallic shrieking inside it that individual notes cannot be followed. At first it resembles an avalanche. After a while you realize that no avalanche could last this long, and that nothing visible is moving quickly enough to account for it. The sound is the ridges acquiring depth. It comes through the ground, through teeth and breastbone, through the handles of tools left standing in a shelter. Sometimes it seems to arrive from a mountain that has not yet appeared.

Across a narrow gap, a person could be plainly visible and entirely inaudible.

The vegetation grows in separated, extravagant gardens.

A shelf may remain bare for half a mile, all chalk-colored blades and dark cracks, and then open into a hollow crowded with folded leaves. The plants are low, heavy, and richly layered: broad fans of dull green, purple-black cushions, long silver fronds coiled close to the ground. There is enough growth in these hollows to hide the rock completely. Leaves overlap leaves in a thick, cool abundance. Beyond the lip of the hollow, the naked ridge resumes.

The gardens occur where the noise gathers.

A concave wall, the space between two leaning slabs, the undersurface of an overhang—anywhere the grinding concentrates, plants take hold. Their leaves are stiff membranes crossed by fine mineral ribs. Their roots fasten to the stone without entering it, spreading across the surface in flat, copper-colored webs. They do not draw up moisture. They flex almost imperceptibly under the weight of sound, extracting something from that endless compression.

Close to a thriving garden, the roar becomes a little less complete. You begin to distinguish separate impacts within it. A leaf as wide as a sleeping mat may tremble once over the course of a morning. Along its edge, small dark beads accumulate: dense, dry nodules with a waxy interior. These are the plants’ stored food. Split one with a sharp flake and the exposed center remains faintly warm for several minutes.

There are no flowers that need visitors. No wings move between the fronds. Nothing burrows under the cushions or grazes their margins. Growth passes from one ledge to another when a mature fan loosens a small folded plate from its underside. Most plates lodge nearby. A few travel into the gaps, suspended there as though settling through a substance too thick to see. Years later, they may reach a cliff.

Some are visible between the ridges now: little green angles in the violet gloom, each casting a shadow on no surface.

The highest gardens are the lushest. Their thick leaves gather in ascending tiers around isolated columns, making dark green collars just below the raw summits. Yet the cold increases with height, and beyond these gardens the stone becomes brittle enough to break beneath a careful step. Fragments do not always fall. Some remain where the foot displaced them, slowly turning. Others descend a short distance and stop level with the oldest exposed layer of rock.

Weight is reliable only near a surface.

The builders understood this well enough to leave broad paths. Their routes keep to the middles of shelves and climb in shallow, laboriously cut steps. Where a path approaches an edge, three upright stones mark the last dependable footing. Beyond them, a dropped object may go downward, sideways, or back toward the hand that released it. The returning objects are dangerous. A shard can come back with the force it would have gained from a very long fall.

There are a few shelters, never enough to make a settlement. One stands in a garden beneath a tilted wall, another on an exposed saddle, a third so far across the open interval that it is visible only when fresh stone lights the opposite ridge. They are built from thin slabs laid into low arches, with strips of fibrous leaf packed tightly between them. No mortar, no metal fittings. The entrances turn twice before reaching the interior.

Inside, the noise is merely overwhelming.

The walls are lined with dried fans harvested from the gardens. Their ribs still flex. Bundles of them hang above shallow sleeping niches, and flat stones lie over the bundles to keep them under tension. A visitor can feel the difference by putting a head close to one: the grinding loses its sharpest edge, leaving a heavy pulse in the jaw. On the floors are chipped blades, stone hammers with split wooden handles, bowls carved from a porous black mineral. Some bowls contain the warm-centered nodules. Others hold nothing but powder.

No one is visible in the shelters. There are no tracks to follow. On a newly made surface, a footprint has no particular reason to outlast the next stage of the stone’s development.

Still, certain steps have been widened. A cracked lintel has been propped with a fresh wedge. Beside one doorway, a row of handprints has been rubbed into the rock with green leaf dust. Their spacing suggests a measure, though what was being measured is unclear. The first few prints are ordinary. Farther along the wall they become shallow depressions, then openings. Through the final hand-shaped opening you can see a ridge that is not outside the building.

Changes here are slow enough to be mistaken for errors of attention.

A ledge gains a little width. A distant spire, watched for weeks, reveals an additional face. A basin grows deeper because there is now more space between its floor and rim, although neither seems to have moved. The paths require occasional repair not because they wear away, but because the terrain develops new obligations. A stair that once joined two shelves may come to a level landing halfway up, with another flight necessary beyond it. Builders leave spare slabs at such places.

The great gaps are the least settled parts of the landscape. Their distances are provisional. You can look across one and clearly see a small hammer lying on a threshold, yet be unable to distinguish the shelter around it. Sometimes two opposing cliffs fit together exactly, every projection answering a recess, though they are separated by miles. Between them the roar has an unusually even pitch.

These are places to avoid. The cliffs are still deciding whether they are separate.

Near the center of the region is a wide, shallow saddle, bare except for three gardens and a roofless stone enclosure. From there the country feels open: ridge beyond ridge, long descending shelves, immense dark spaces crossed by no bridge. Nothing hurries across the view. The hanging seeds barely change position. The mineral needles lengthen too slowly to watch. Even the freshest wound in a cliff brightens over decades.

Yet the air pounds without pause.

At the enclosure, one garden has spread over the wall and down its inner face. Thick black-green leaves conceal the corners. Beneath them, where their overlapping membranes drink most deeply from the noise, is a patch of fine pale dust. A narrow wooden scoop rests there, its handle polished by use.

If you lift it, the dust in its bowl keeps the shape of the hollow it came from.

For a moment you are holding a small, silent piece of the place before it became stone. Then the bottom of the scoop begins to grow heavier.
