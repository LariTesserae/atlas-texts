At first the upland appears empty. Its distances are so large, and the daylight so weak, that you mistake the nearest ridge for the limit of the country. Then a break in the cloud reveals another ridge above it, pale as the underside of a fish, and beyond that a third: not higher, merely farther away, lifted by the curve of the plateau into a different layer of weather.

Nothing here comes to a sharp summit. The mountains have had time to spread. Their crowns are broad gravel surfaces; their flanks descend through worn benches into valleys too wide to hold an echo. Dark stone shows through the frost in long, low ribs. Between the ribs lies a powder of ground mineral, gray when dry and almost black wherever a little moisture has entered it.

The cold is steady. There are colder places, but few in which cold seems so much a property of the material itself. A stone turned over is cold on both sides. The deeper earth gives no appreciable warmth. Even at noon, when the cloud thins enough to cast a shadow, the light scarcely changes what a bare hand can endure.

Water occurs locally, almost privately. A seam beneath an overhang beads for an hour and dries. A patch of snow survives in the lee of a boulder until it has been reduced to a hard, translucent scale. In certain valleys there are streams only a handspan wide, flowing beneath roofs of old ice. You find them by the slight sag of the gravel above them, or by a dark opening no larger than an eye.

Rain falls somewhere on the plateau every day, but it may be several years before it falls on the same stone twice.

There is vegetation, though it rarely rises high enough to disturb the outline of the ground. Brittle cushions of black-green leaves occupy cracks that face away from the prevailing weather. Each cushion has a dead center and a living rim, expanding by fractions of an inch through decades of dim summers. In sheltered hollows, a pale grass grows sideways along the earth. Its roots travel farther than its blades. Much of what looks like an isolated tuft is the exposed tip of a plant that has occupied the gravel beneath you for centuries.

The plants are cropped close.

This is the first indication that the emptiness is misleading.

What looked like scattered slabs on the valley floor begin to change their spacing. One uncovers a strip of darker earth; another turns, so gradually that you notice only because a white mark has moved from its eastern edge to its western one. They are animals, broad and low, with backs plated in the same weathered gray as the country. Fine sediment gathers between their plates. On older individuals, lichens have established themselves in the joints that move least.

A large one might cover a room. Most are smaller, but size is difficult to judge when there is nothing upright nearby.

They graze by pressing their undersides against the ground. Beneath each body, rows of soft ridges sift the mineral powder, taking in the films of life that grow around buried moisture. A feeding animal advances a few yards in a day. Its passage leaves no furrow, only a swept patch and several shallow depressions that fill with frost before the next morning.

They are widely spaced. For a long time you may see only six or seven. Then your eye learns the slight asymmetry of their outlines, the particular way frost collects around a breathing edge, and the whole visible valley becomes inhabited.

There are hundreds of thousands of them.

Beyond that valley, the same conditions continue for distances that cannot be taken in from any height available here.

Their abundance has not made the landscape busy. It has made stillness densely occupied.

Under stones, smaller animals fold themselves into the available darkness. Transparent jointed things crowd the undersides of ice. There are worms in the damp dust and colonies of pinhead-sized grazers on the black leaves. The dry crust between two apparently bare ridges may shelter more living bodies than all the large animals in sight. Their lives proceed in narrow intervals: a little condensation, a faint rise in light, the slow thaw of one buried lens of ice.

Almost nothing calls.

The plate-backed animals have no voices. Their breathing passes through many small openings rather than a single throat, and makes no sound you can separate from your own blood. Most of the smaller creatures have neither the need nor the means to advertise themselves across a distance. Predators find prey through pressure, warmth, and dissolved traces in the damp earth.

Occasionally a pale hunter moves along a ridge. It is narrow-bodied, with long feet that spread before touching down. It pauses above one patch of gravel, lowers its face, and waits. You might watch it for half an hour without seeing it strike. When it finally does, the action is small: a mouth opening into the soil, a brief tightening of the shoulders.

The silence survives.

There is an industrial order to the life here, though nothing has been built.

Within the larger animals, food passes through banks of nearly identical chambers. Each chamber performs one small part of a long extraction: softening the grit, separating organic films, taking up particular salts, precipitating what cannot be used. Their internal fluids carry minute metallic grains. Muscular valves move these fluids through repeating circuits, with the economy of machinery designed to run continuously under a poor supply of power.

Their plates are grown from the waste.

Along an old animal’s flanks you can read changes in diet as layers of dull iron, chalk-white carbonate, and a greenish mineral that darkens in wet weather. A fresh fracture shines briefly. Within days it tarnishes to the color of stone.

The animals do not warm the valleys. They retain almost everything they produce. What little heat escapes is enough to keep a thin working layer moist beneath the belly, enough for digestion, enough for the next inch of movement. In severe weather their internal circulation slows from a pulse to a succession of separate events. One valve closes. Much later, another opens.

Across a continent of upland, these processes continue: sifting, sorting, pumping, plating. The visible output is a faint alteration of the soil.

There are places where generations have followed the same mineral seam. The ground there is unusually level and carries a muted metallic sheen. Long before a valley’s stream changes course, its animals have begun redistributing the finer sediment, carrying it inside themselves toward some other favorable ground. The old feeding routes can be seen from a ridge as broad, dull bands. They divide and rejoin around outcrops, then disappear beneath frost.

Nothing supervises this movement. A body turns toward a slightly wetter patch; another follows a concentration of salt. Given enough years, the separate adjustments become something you can mistake for a plan.

The plateau has room for several kinds of weather at once. Far to the west, snow hangs beneath a cloud without visibly reaching the earth. To the south, a belt of thin fog rests in a depression so broad it has its own horizon. Overhead the sky may remain clear and darkly blue for days, while frost forms on the stones from moisture arriving too sparsely to be called mist.

The wind is usually high above the surface. You see it in the cloud before you feel it on your face. Near the ground, the broad ridges shelter one another, and the heavy cold air settles into their worn contours. Loose grains seldom move. The landscape has already lost most of what could easily be carried away.

Death is slow to become distinguishable from rest. A plate-backed animal may stop for an entire season. Only when its plates begin to separate do the small scavengers enter, drawing the soft tissues down into the soil. The back remains, sometimes for centuries, settling as its supports diminish. Eventually frost opens it along the old growth lines.

Some of the stone-like shapes are animals. Some are shells. Some are stones.

At the dimmest hour, when the sun passes behind the plateau’s distant rise, the differences become impossible to see. A low cloud touches the next valley and leaves a silver deposit on every exposed surface. Beneath the living bodies, a few square feet of earth remain dark and damp.

That is where the landscape is working.

By morning, one animal will have reached the edge of a grass cushion. Another will have finished separating a mouthful of grit. A trickle under the ice will have advanced its thawed passage by the width of a finger.

From the ridge, nothing will appear to have happened.
