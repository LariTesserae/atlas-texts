From the signal roof, the country looks almost level: a low, crowded plain under a pale winter sun, with no mountain behind it and no sea at its edge. The distance is divided by factory stacks, stands of dark trees, and the pale backs of buildings being carried elsewhere. Farther out, these become a single uneven line. Beyond that line are more districts, more nurseries, more ground.

The plain is still arriving.

You can see it happen in the unoccupied places, though few places remain unoccupied for long. Between a freight siding and a cabbage house, the soil swells into a smooth, low cushion. Its surface is the color of the inside of a mushroom. Over several hours it hardens, develops grains, and separates into angular plates. Sometimes it produces clay. Sometimes a dense greenish stone, warm for a little while and then cold forever. Surveyors drive numbered pins into it while it is still soft enough to accept them.

There are no rounded pebbles here. Nothing has traveled far enough to become rounded. The stones have corners so fine that a dropped glove can catch on one and stay hanging.

Much of the city stands on ground younger than its inhabitants. Some streets stand on ground younger than their street trees.

***

The buildings are narrow and very close together, but they rarely touch. A hand’s breadth of clearance is maintained between walls, packed with sliding metal strips, brushes, and overlapping skirts of black felt. Foundations cannot be trusted to hold the same shape overnight. Houses stand on adjustable legs. Larger buildings rest on rafts of girders with hundreds of screw feet beneath them, each foot fitted with a gauge.

At street level, behind locked grilles, these gauges tremble.

An entire apartment block may rise by six inches while its neighbors settle. Doors are fitted high, with folding steps. Bridges between buildings telescope. Water pipes make generous loops before entering a wall, and the electric cables hang in dark festoons, with enough slack to let a whole row of shops lean west without losing power.

There is machinery everywhere. Small engines under pavements. Traction motors in the bases of towers. Long, low factories whose roofs disappear beneath gardens and ventilation equipment. On the outskirts, walking cranes move across the plain, placing road sections ahead of themselves and lifting the old sections from behind.

Yet from the signal roof you hear almost nothing.

The cold carries an occasional bell with extraordinary clarity. A loose sheet of metal clicks once, then again much later. Nearer at hand, a bird worries a seed against a gutter. The great movement below proceeds with the subdued intimacy of furniture being shifted in a distant room.

The new ground drinks vibration. A hammer strike is sharp in the air, but through the floor it arrives as a faint pressure in the soles. Engines are mounted deep, where their noise disappears. On factory streets, workers can speak across a loading bay without raising their voices. Warning lights are more important than sirens. Every vehicle has a lamp low on its front that throws a moving rectangle ahead of it, so pedestrians see the light coming around a corner before the machine appears.

***

There is remarkably little water for so much green.

Rain falls briefly, perhaps for an hour, and leaves the city looking varnished. Then the gutters are empty again. Pools survive in the cupped tops of new stone or under pipe joints, where animals have worn little approaches through the vegetation. There are no broad rivers. The drainage channels are usually dry, their concrete beds occupied by roots, fallen leaves, and the elaborate white husks of insects.

Most moisture comes with the cold.

Before dawn, the foliage gathers a fine silver nap. The plants hold it until daylight loosens it into droplets, which run down grooves in their stems. Leaves are thick, crowded, and dark on top, with pale undersides that show when the wind turns them. Many grow in funnels. Others have fringes like combs, each tooth carrying its bead of condensation.

A mature thicket can be taller than a tram and so densely interlocked that a person cannot push an arm into it. Beneath the outer leaves is an almost dry interior, crowded with woody stems and old nests. The roots descend only a short distance, then spread sideways in flat, muscular nets. Deep roots would be broken by the ground’s changes. These plants hold one another instead.

They occupy every strip the city cannot defend: the space between paired railway lines, the corners of factory roofs, the narrow courtyards where ladders have been left too long. Branches enter broken windows and continue growing inside empty rooms, arranging their leaves toward the glass. On some residential streets, the trees have woven a continuous canopy from one end to the other. The winter sun shines through in small, steady patches. There are no leaves drifting down. Dead foliage is retained around the stems, insulating them in layers.

The gardeners carry knives, pruning hooks, and small electric saws. They move in teams ahead of the road crews.

***

By midmorning, the district below the signal roof is changing too quickly to take in all at once.

A bakery is being drawn sideways on its foundation rails. The shop remains open; customers cross a little hinged gangway to reach the door. Behind it, fresh mineral has risen through the courtyard and lifted the communal oven off level.

Three streets away, a tram waits while workers lower a new length of track into a shallow trough that did not exist at breakfast. Its passengers look out at a hedge being rolled up around a steel drum. The roots cling to their blanket of soil. Small brown animals spill from beneath it, cross the road, and vanish into the hedge opposite.

Along the freight yards, a warehouse wall unfolds. What appeared to be a single long building separates into six short ones, each moving independently as a seam opens beneath them. Through the widening spaces you glimpse workers, piled crates, a yellow machine, strips of daylight. Later, the gaps will be roofed and become passages. By evening, someone may have set a stall in one.

No change happens cleanly. A cable catches in a branch. A step comes down on a nest. Fresh ground pushes a buried drain upward, and the drain splits, releasing a few gallons of water that draw birds from every roof nearby. For five minutes the broken pipe is the busiest place in the street.

The city’s maps are printed on thin, cheap paper. Public boards display revisions under glass: a bridge closed, a square divided, an address moved seventy yards south. People give directions using things that can be followed—a tram line, a district heating main, the red lamps on the side of a mill—rather than distances.

***

Animals make use of the disturbances with practiced speed.

Small, heavy-bodied birds nest inside the open steelwork beneath houses, where cats cannot easily reach them. Their feet are broad, and they walk on frost without breaking its crust. They follow the foundation crews, taking pale larvae from newly exposed soil.

The cats are long-haired and mostly silent. You find them asleep on motor housings or standing motionless beside the little basins of water. Larger creatures live among the root mats: blunt-faced grazers with close gray coats, narrow enough to pass through spaces that look impassable. At dusk they emerge into the service lanes and crop the tender shoots growing around lamp bases.

There are insects even in the cold. Some spend most of their lives near steam fittings. Others open flat, black wing cases to the sun and remain there for hours, warming themselves before they move. Their larvae favor the first skin of new earth. Lift a plate of stone shortly after it forms and you may find them already underneath, translucent, turning slowly away from the light.

Nobody has established where they come from.

The same uncertainty attends certain plants. A sealed survey plot may produce a low forest of jointed green stalks in a week, without any recognizable seedlings. Sometimes the ground arrives with roots in it. These are carefully cut and tagged. Most die. A few continue growing after both ends have been severed.

***

Outside the oldest wards, the city thins only by changing its contents. Buildings give way to storage racks, propagation beds, transformer yards, stacked road panels, and dense belts of vegetation planted to catch airborne moisture. There is still very little bare space.

Here the ground changes in larger gestures. A shallow basin lifts until it is a broad table. A mile-long ridge, no higher than a person, appears across roads and allotments. Its crest splits lengthwise, showing a dark interior that smells of iron and cut stems. Crews mark it with lamps and wait to see whether it will harden or fold back into itself.

Under the ordinary daylight, these events have an exposed, workaday strangeness. There is no fog to soften them. You can see a fence suspended above a new hollow, every nail and wire distinct. You can see the roots beneath a tree as the land withdraws from them. Nearby, laundry hangs from an apartment balcony.

By late afternoon the light turns yellow on the windows. The cranes keep walking. A row of greenhouses glides almost imperceptibly toward its new foundations, carrying its humid interior, its hanging tools, its seedlings in numbered trays. Birds ride on the roof vents.

Then the sun drops behind the distant factories, and frost begins to collect on the freshest stone first.

At the foot of the signal tower, a maintenance worker finds that the lowest stair no longer reaches the ground. She brings out a short wooden step and places it beneath the iron one. Before leaving, she presses it down with her boot, testing for a wobble.

All across the plain, lights are coming on.
