The ground is still taking the shape of the hands that made it.

Across the low plain, thumb hollows hold warm water. Some are small enough to step over; others extend beyond sight, their far rims lost in the brown dimness. Between them lie broad, slightly raised seams where one piece of ground has been pressed against another. Nothing has weathered smooth. Every softened edge was softened deliberately, with a wet palm or a strip of hide drawn along it. On the higher seams, you can still see where grit caught under the tool.

There is no rock beneath the clay.

Where a basin has split open, its walls reveal more workmanship: slabs laid on slabs, plugs rammed into old gaps, wedges of pale fired earth supporting floors that were never meant to be exposed. Deeper down are hollow spaces with rounded corners. Heat comes out of them. A dropped fragment falls for a long time, then makes a small, damp sound, as though someone far below has caught it in a folded cloth.

Everything here has been made, but very little has been finished.

The plain extends on a scale that defeats the modesty of its construction. A ridge assembled from pinched rolls of clay runs toward the horizon and continues into the haze without gaining much height. Beside it, a trough of shallow water is interrupted at regular intervals by little cross-walls, each carrying the marks of a wooden paddle. There must be millions of these walls. They have no fittings, no gates. Where water is meant to pass, somebody has simply pushed two fingers through the soft material.

The work is crude in the way a well-used bowl is crude: the maker knew exactly how much irregularity the thing could bear.

Here and there, far apart, stand heaps of unused pieces. Curved tiles as broad as valleys. Hollow cones lying on their sides. Bundles of straight lengths that might become shorelines, if laid end to end. Their broken surfaces show coarse temper mixed into the clay—crushed pottery, mostly. The newer world contains fragments of an older made world, including the occasional intact rim or handle projecting from a bank.

There are no plants binding any of it together. No roots enter the joints. No moss darkens the damp sides of the troughs. The black patches visible at a distance are places where the firing went too far.

Underfoot, the surface is hot enough to discourage standing still.

Water appears without much announcement. It gathers along a seam, darkening the clay, then forms a row of beads. These join and spill. A dry hollow becomes a pond over the course of an hour, though nothing flows into it from outside. Elsewhere, ponds sink back into the floor and leave behind a faint skin the color of milk.

The water smells of warm pottery. In the deepest basins it is almost clear, with a copper-brown cast where the dim light passes through it. Along the margins it becomes thick with slip. It does not support any visible life. Nothing wriggles in it; nothing comes to drink. Even the finest settling mud is undisturbed except by bubbles rising from below.

Some bubbles break normally. Others retain their shape after the water drains away, leaving hollow clay blisters that harden in the heat. A field of these can form between one crossing of a plain and the next: knee-high domes, widely scattered, each with a tiny round aperture at its summit. They breathe out steam in turns.

Their timing is not regular enough to be a mechanism.

Overhead, the sky hangs low, although there is no way to judge how low. It has the texture of damp plaster rubbed almost smooth. Broad bands overlap across it. At the joins, a dull yellow light seeps through, illuminating the ground unevenly, so that a distant stretch of empty plain can be brighter than the place immediately around you.

Occasionally, a band sags.

Then the heat increases. Moisture condenses on every exposed surface, including surfaces that were dry and powdery a moment before. The nearest horizon lifts slightly. Water tilts within its basins. For several minutes the whole landscape seems to be held at an angle, though there is nothing outside it against which that angle could be measured.

This is the beginning of its weather.

A storm here may have almost no wind. It starts with adjustments: a trough lengthens; a bank acquires an extra corner; a shallow basin draws itself into a long, narrow channel. The changes pass outward in great intersecting fronts. A seam that lay a day’s walk away approaches until it is close enough to touch, bringing all the land on its far side with it. Between the advancing seams, new ground spreads in broad, wet sheets.

It spreads quickly. The surface wrinkles, flattens, and takes impressions from tools that are nowhere visible.

You can watch a line being scored across a plain. The groove advances toward you through the clay, pauses at a harder patch, then begins again with greater pressure. On either side of it the ground rises by the thickness of a fingernail. By the time the line reaches the horizon, it has become a watershed.

The sound is slight: a long scrape, a sticky release.

That is what makes the changes difficult to comprehend. So much happens without the noise its size ought to produce. Whole expanses fold against one another with the sound of a pot being settled on a shelf. An immense bank collapses into its own hollow interior, and the only audible consequence is water trickling through newly opened cracks.

There is no continuous roar to become accustomed to. Between events, the quiet returns completely.

A drop falls.

Somewhere beyond visibility, a wooden implement knocks twice against a rim.

The stillness afterward can last half a day.

Not all of the construction holds. On the margins of newly spread ground, distance is unreliable. A narrow drainage cut may be easy to cross in one direction and impossibly broad on the return. The clay on its two banks is the same color, bears matching pressure marks, and plainly belonged together moments ago. Yet the interval between them has not been made to the same measure.

In other places, depth has been applied too thinly. A pond looks shallow because it is shallow, until something breaks its surface. Then the bottom withdraws. Its pale floor passes down through successive layers of dimness, and the water above it remains perfectly still. Once the disturbance ends, the bottom rises again, sometimes carrying a different arrangement of handprints.

The world’s rules have been fitted locally. They meet badly at the joints.

Water generally runs downhill, but certain little cross-walls persuade it to climb. Heat rises from most openings; from a few it falls outward and spreads along the ground like a weight. Shadows cling correctly to the sides of objects until they reach an unfinished edge, where they may continue on their own, darkening a stretch of ground that has not yet acquired anything to cast them.

The oldest districts are recognizable by their consistency. A pace remains a pace there. Basins retain their volume. A thrown shard follows one clean arc.

These districts are not old enough to be worn. They are merely dry enough to keep their agreements.

Their surfaces are dusty, pale, and faintly resonant. Scattered among them are tools: stone pounders, wedges, shallow mixing dishes with hardened material still adhering to the inside. A paddle lies beside a ridge bearing its unmistakable pattern. It is an ordinary length of wood, split at one end, with a grip polished by use. The ridge it shaped crosses half the visible world.

No workshop surrounds it. No dwelling stands nearby. There are no footprints leading away.

Farther out, beyond the dependable ground, lie the soaking fields. From a distance they resemble a continuous shallow sea, but they are separate basins divided by lips too low to see through the vapor. Light travels over them in broken strips. Sometimes the sky’s seams and the seams below align, and for a while the entire region appears to be one immense object folded loosely around a layer of hot water.

Here the newest pieces are kept wet.

Unfired headlands slump slowly toward one another. Broad plates of land float just beneath the surface, pale and incomplete, their undersides turned up in places. Water slips over their edges without wearing them. Before it can cut a channel, the edge is pressed back into shape. Before sediment can gather, the floor is lifted and laid elsewhere.

Nothing has time to become a ruin.

At the farthest visible margin, the water ends against a straight, soft lip. Beyond it there is no distant country, only a brown vacancy of the same dim value as the sky. The lip trembles. Small portions detach, hang briefly without falling, and are drawn back into place.

Then a new length of shore is pressed onto the end.

The join is smoothed poorly. A little water begins to leak through it.

All across the low, hot plain, the basins quiver in response. Steam rises from fresh ground. The yellow light narrows overhead, and for a moment every wet impression shines—the paddle strokes, the dragged fingers, the deep heel of a palm—before the next adjustment passes through and changes what they were making.
