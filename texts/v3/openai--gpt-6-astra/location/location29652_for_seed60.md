The hollow is only a few minutes across, though crossing it takes most of an afternoon. There is no bare ground. Underfoot lies a thick braid of roots, polished where feet have passed, furred elsewhere with the pale tips of smaller roots seeking purchase. Between the braids, leaves grow edge upward in such close ranks that they resemble the gills beneath a mushroom. A dropped object might remain visible for a moment, held among them. Then the leaves lean together.

The hollow sits low in the surrounding country. You can see its rim through the upper foliage: a modest rise, scarcely higher than a house, densely stitched with stems. Everything slopes toward the center, where water collects beneath the living floor. Its presence is felt rather than seen. A step depresses the roots; several feet away, a bead of water swells out of a joint. Sit long enough on one of the broad, upholstered buttresses and your clothes become damp.

It is warm here. Sunlight enters through a canopy of ordinary green leaves, bright enough to distinguish the red seams of new growth from the brown seams of old repairs. The humidity softens distant outlines, but there are few distant outlines to soften. Almost every view ends against something close: a pleated trunk, a curtain of hanging rootlets, the swollen underside of a branch that has sagged and taken root again.

The plants have occupied the shapes usually left empty. They grow through forks, fill hollows, bridge the space beneath arches. A passage exists only where something keeps passing.

Near the entrance stands a little brick building with a corrugated roof. It is perhaps large enough for two workers and a boiler. Most of the bricks remain visible, though they have been separated by cushions of growth, each held in its own green socket. The wall has expanded without falling. Its windows are wider at the top than at the bottom, and the door hangs several inches above its original threshold.

Inside is a pressure gauge.

Its glass is cloudy around the edge, clear in the center. The needle moves so slowly that you have to choose a mark and watch it. Copper pipes leave the back of the gauge, disappear into the wall, and emerge outside as lines of blue-green staining along a root. Farther on, the copper ends. The plant continues its bore.

There are other pieces of machinery in the hollow: a flywheel embedded upright in a buttress; three ceramic insulators carried aloft on a stem; a length of toothed rack whose missing teeth have been replaced with hard, amber-colored growth. The machinery was once arranged around a small industrial purpose. There are platforms, drainage channels, the remains of rails no farther apart than a person’s shoulders. Whatever was made here could have been carried out on little trucks.

Now the whole place maintains the pressure.

You hear it first as an intermittent thumping beneath the floor. The sound is substantial, close, almost domestic—the knock of a heavy pipe in a wall. After a while you distinguish several sources. A deep double beat comes from the center. A hollow rattle answers near the brick building. Somewhere overhead, a valve whistles for half a minute, stops, and begins again at a lower pitch.

Nothing visibly hurries. The big leaves barely move. Water gathers patiently on their margins. Yet the hollow is loud enough that a quiet voice does not carry across it. Sound travels much better through the substance of the place. Rest your palm against a trunk and you can feel a sequence of impacts arriving from elsewhere, slightly ahead of what reaches your ears.

The central feature is a squat growth about the size of a chapel, with a low crown and six or seven supporting lobes. Calling it a tree does not quite account for its shape. Calling it a building overlooks the soft, translucent fringe around every opening. Its surfaces are layered with old bark, leathery leaf bases, and strips of darker material as smooth as worn drive belts. Broad channels descend its sides. They are dry much of the time, stained black by years of brief discharges.

A narrow iron stair climbs one lobe. The lower steps have been lifted out of alignment. Higher up, the stair disappears into a succession of shallow pockets, each lined with fresh green tissue. There is still room for a boot in them.

From the top, you can look down into the crown.

Water stands there in a round basin, perhaps twelve feet across. The surface is open to daylight. Around it, upright leaves overlap into a watertight rim, and beneath the water a pale diaphragm rises and falls. Its movement is too gradual to follow directly. You notice instead that a rusted bolt on the basin’s inner wall has become submerged, or that a floating leaf has come within reach.

At the highest point of the cycle, the floor of the hollow becomes firmer. Small passages widen. The brick building gives a faint series of clicks. Then water spills into the black channels, and the overhead whistle begins.

This is the easiest time to leave.

During the falling half of the cycle, the lower roots relax into the paths. They do it without urgency, but with considerable weight. A gap that admitted your shoulders in the morning may admit only your arm by evening. The old rail bed is the safest route: the plants have grown around the rails so completely that the metal now serves as a permanent pair of splints.

There are very few animals. Occasionally a small gray snail appears on the pressure gauge, leaving a clean track through the film on its glass. Something with wings sleeps behind an insulator. No birds forage in the canopy, though there seems to be food enough. Seeds hang in dense clusters, and bruised leaves give off a rich smell of cucumber, yeast, and warm flour.

Much of that scent comes from the seams.

Where two great masses press together, their surfaces slowly soften, adhere, and become continuous. Old boundaries remain visible as wavering lines. You can trace them for yards: the place where a fallen limb joined the floor, where a wall of stems engulfed a handcart, where one side of a path closed against the other. Some are many decades old. Their edges have rounded; smaller growths have settled into them. The hollow has had time to accommodate its own crowding.

By late afternoon the sun reaches the brick building and warms the exposed metal of the gauge. The needle trembles. Drops begin falling from the roof even though there has been no rain.

Under the central growth, a discharge gate opens with a long, low scrape. For a few minutes there is the smell of water from deep underground. You glimpse a dark opening, an iron lip, a current running over roots worn smooth as knuckles. Then the gate settles shut.

The hollow resumes its slow filling. Along the rail bed, the narrow leaves lift their edges, and water shines briefly in the spaces between them.
