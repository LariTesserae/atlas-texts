The whole district fits beneath a single sheet of ice. From the central square, if the water is clear, you can see its four boundaries: a retaining wall, the backs of three houses, a bank of pale rubble, and the iron grille through which the tide enters. Above them the ice rests on the roofs. There is no sky between.

The square is scarcely wider than a workshop floor. Six streets feed into it, although none runs farther than a dozen doorways before narrowing into pipes or ending against stone. Everything is submerged. Water occupies the rooms, the stairwells, the little covered passage behind the public scales. It stands inside lamp globes and behind clock faces. When a window finally breaks, nothing floods; a pane simply leaves its frame and settles among the others.

The cold has made different waters here.

Under the ice is a clear, nearly fresh layer, fed by a slow thaw somewhere beyond the district. Lower down lies green seawater. At ankle height above the paving, a heavier brine creeps through the square in broad folds. Looking down through these layers bends the houses: their upper windows remain where they should be, but their thresholds seem to slide sideways. A straight drainpipe has three elbows that are not present when touched.

There is almost no exposed soil. The bottom consists of worn flagstones, roofing slate, iron filings, and a gray deposit fine enough to enter screw threads. Where a street has subsided, the paving does not gape open. It sags in close-set scales, each stone still fitted to its neighbors. The place has had a long time to accommodate its failures.

Even the debris is settled. Broken handrails lie neatly in gutters because the currents have turned them there. Bottles have lost their necks and become thick, dull rings. Around the square’s drain, ceramic fragments have been sorted into concentric bands: white nearest the mouth, then blue, then the heavier brown pieces from electrical insulators. The pattern changes, but so slowly that a small chip may spend years arriving at its proper distance.

The buildings are crowded with additions. External stairs cross one another without meeting. Cable trays pass through bedrooms. A row of balconies has been enclosed in sheet metal, then pierced by a main pipe, then braced against the weight of the ice with timbers now soft on the outside and black-hard within. There are service hatches beneath service hatches. Someone once needed every available surface.

Much of the machinery still works.

Behind the public scales, three pumps turn in a room whose door has swollen permanently shut. Their housings are broad and low, painted a green visible only where something has rubbed them clean. They do not hammer. Their shafts move with the patient speed of a minute hand, driven by a pressure difference between the upper water and a buried reservoir of brine. Each revolution gathers itself through a sequence of small movements: a leather diaphragm bows; a counterweight rises a finger’s breadth; a valve opens; a dark ribbon enters a pipe.

The pumps serve no distant city. The pipes return into the same few buildings, cross the square beneath its paving, climb the retaining wall, and descend again. They keep the lower water moving. Without them, salt would crystallize in the narrow passages and close the district from the bottom upward.

Near each working bearing there is a faint warmth. Not warmth enough to feel comfortable, only enough to keep a patch of iron bare. These patches are the place’s gardens.

A short, almost colorless growth clings to the pump outlets. Its fronds are no longer than eyelashes. In the weak glow from a maintenance lamp they look green, but when the lamp goes dark they are indistinguishable from the mineral fuzz on the neighboring bolts. Farther from the machinery, vegetation survives only in sheltered seams: a strap of kelp threaded through a roof gutter, a crust beneath a stair, a little fan growing from the felt lining of an old boot.

Most surfaces are occupied by other things. Salt feathers. Rust blisters. Tiny transparent tubes whose inhabitants are seldom visible. The district is dense less with life than with places life might fit. Every grille has another grille behind it. Between two touching pipes lies a black channel wide enough for something slender to pass.

Occasionally something does.

A white shrimp appears on the lip of a washbasin, holding itself perfectly still while its feelers inspect the water. Several streets may be empty, yet this single basin contains enough drifting food to keep it there for weeks. In the municipal office, a flat fish rests beneath a fallen ledger cabinet. Its skin has taken on the cabinet’s rectangular stains. It leaves at long intervals, crossing the floor close enough to disturb the sediment without lifting a cloud.

There are no shoals, no busy grazing along the walls. An animal entering view feels like an arrival.

Light comes from five lamps, though never all five at once. They are fixed low on the buildings, beneath the reach of the roof ice, and supplied by a cable thick as a wrist. Their glass is smoked with deposits. One illuminates a handrail and nothing beyond it. Another throws a narrow yellow cone across the square, ending at the suspended veil where fresh water meets salt.

At intervals, the lamps dim together. The pumps take the current for some deeper operation. In that darkness, a few seams in the brickwork shine with a faint blue light, then fade. The effect is too weak to reveal the buildings. It merely shows how many cracks they contain.

The district’s strangest feature is behind the iron tide grille.

From the square, the grille appears backed by black water. Close to it, however, the openings show another view of the square, seen from a little higher up. The public scales are there. So are the three houses and the rubble bank. The lamps in that view are usually dark when the real lamps are lit.

Water passes freely through the bars. Objects do not. A length of wire pushed between them emerges from a drain beneath the scales, still advancing at the same speed. This has been used rather than explained. Several pipes end at the grille and resume elsewhere, saving bends and lengths of metal. The oldest have collars built specifically to fit the bars.

Sometimes the tide carries a little sediment inward that has not yet been disturbed on the square outside.

Nothing announces these events. Sound is scarce. There is no surf under the ice and no air in which a loose shutter can knock. The machinery’s vibration reaches the paving as a faint, steady tension. If you rest a hand on a wall, you can feel a valve close two houses away. Once in a long while a grain of stone comes free beneath the ice and clicks against a roof. The water seems to hold the click for a moment before letting it go.

Yet the place is continually adjusting. Brine descends the steps of the empty shops. A clear current threads upward through a chimney. Ice grows along one roof edge while melting along another. In the pump room, a counterweight completes its rise, hesitates, and starts down.

Beside the square’s drain, a blue ceramic chip turns over. Its underside is still glazed and bright. For a few seconds it catches the low lamp, showing a small piece of a painted leaf, before the current seats it between two older fragments.
