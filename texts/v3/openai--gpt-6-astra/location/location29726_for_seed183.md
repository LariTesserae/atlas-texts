From the lowest basin, you can see weather passing beneath the floor.

It shows through the cracks: white cloud moving rapidly across a blue distance, sometimes opening onto another, much lower layer of cloud. The cracks are narrow enough to step across. Their edges have been worn smooth by centuries of runoff, and in several places someone has fitted flat stones over them, making little bridges no wider than a pair of feet. Water slips between the paving stones and falls out of sight. You never hear it arrive.

The basin itself is only a few miles across. It lies among pale ridges whose summits are blunt with age, high above any country that could support a forest. There is no snow except in the deepest north-facing clefts. The air is cool, the sunlight fierce. Wet surfaces shine so brightly that looking down can be harder than looking at the sky.

Much of the ground is wet.

There are pools in the hollows, curtains of seepage on the scarps, broad shallow streams crossing beds of fine gray grit. The ridges sweat through the morning. Water gathers along their bedding planes until each slope appears to have been ruled with a hundred silver lines. By noon these lines have joined into runnels. By evening the runnels have cut temporary channels through the gravel and filled the basins below.

Nothing grows beside them. No grass occupies the wet margin, no moss softens the stones. Even the oldest pools are clear to their bottoms. The dark material under their rims is a dense mineral varnish, purple when wet, almost black when dry. It flakes away in scales with a bright copper underside.

There are many such colors, but they must be approached closely. From a distance the country looks nearly white. Up close, the rock is banded with lilac, rust, smoky green, a yellow as thin as old fat. Small crystals crowd the sheltered undersides of ledges. Some resemble grains of rice; others form interlocking blades so fine that a drop of water can bend them. Where the drops fall repeatedly, the blades have grown into little combs. Each comb gives a faint, hard ticking as water passes through it.

These sounds accumulate.

A single seep makes almost no noise. A whole wall of seeps chatters. Gravel rolls in shallow channels with the steady rattle of someone sorting beads. Farther off, falling water strikes hollow stone and booms. Wind catches the mouths of vertical cracks, making notes that swell and fail as you move between the ridges. There is no birdcall, no insect whine, no rustle of anything feeding. Even so, it would be difficult to call the basin quiet. On some afternoons the noise is so continuous that the closing of a nearby stone door cannot be heard.

There are doors.

The shelters are hard to distinguish from fallen blocks until you come close enough to see the workmanship. They have low walls of fitted stone, slab roofs weighted with rubble, entrances facing away from the brightest part of the sky. Most contain a raised sleeping shelf and a shallow floor channel leading outside. There is no timber. The doors are thin sheets of laminated rock, hung on stone pivots that turn in cups polished by use.

A few are still balanced well enough to move with one hand.

Inside, the light is blue and merciful. Tool marks survive beneath the roof: short parallel scars where a pointed stone was used to dress the wall. There are grinding hollows, stacks of unused wedges, bowls pecked from the softer layers of the local rock. One shelter contains a pair of sandals made from many small perforated plates. Their ties have vanished. The plates lie in the shape of feet beside the sleeping shelf.

Outside each occupied-looking ruin is a standing stone, usually waist-high, with a long groove cut down one face. At its base sits a bowl.

The bowl and groove were instruments. You can understand this by watching one through the day.

In the morning, water poured into the groove runs down and fills the bowl. Later it slows. For a little while, generally when the sunlight has become intolerable, the water rests in the groove as a trembling vertical bead. Then it begins to move upward.

This happens to the whole basin, though not everywhere at once.

The height of things changes here without their position changing.

A ridge that has been above a pool all morning may become lower than the pool shortly after noon. It remains plainly visible in the same place, its summit cutting the same outline against the sky. Yet the pool drains toward it. Water spreads across the intervening slope, climbing every feature you can see, and pours over the crest. Loose grit follows in hesitant little rushes.

Your body notices after the water does. The soles of your feet become light. A slope that looked exhausting becomes easy to ascend, while a flat stretch of paving asks for effort. In some places the change is strong enough that you have to sit down and hold a stone until it passes.

The old paths accommodate this. They wander away from apparently easy ground, follow shelves around modest humps, and double back through narrow passages worn glossy by hands. Along the most exposed stretches, holes have been bored through solid rock at shoulder height. Some retain the remains of braided mineral fiber, stiff and fraying, threaded through them as handlines.

The standing stones indicate when to travel. Their grooves face the routes that leave each shelter. As long as the water is moving toward the bowl, the path ahead will bear weight in the expected way.

The changes have gone on long enough to shape the land. Every ridge has been eroded from more than one direction. Gullies meet at crests. Potholes occur on the undersides of overhangs, containing smooth pebbles that sometimes remain there through the morning and sometimes fall. Great aprons of gravel lie plastered against slopes too steep to hold them for most of the day. Their grains are extraordinarily round.

There are no young, sharp mountains in sight. These heights have had time to wear down, but no lasting agreement about what down should be.

In the western part of the basin, the stone stands in crowded fins. Between them are lanes of water and pale shingle, narrow enough that the reflections of opposite walls almost touch. The fins rise thirty or forty feet, with edges rounded like the backs of old knives. Small rooms have been built into their bases. A few are joined by covered passages; others open onto courts containing cisterns, work slabs, and heaps of broken vessels.

Here the afternoon shift produces a kind of tide. Water rises along one lane while draining from the next. It passes through holes in the fins, briefly linking the channels with clear horizontal streams. The streams are strong enough to turn small stone wheels mounted in sockets. Most of the wheels are broken. One still revolves, slowly, carrying its chipped paddles out of the water and back into it.

No machinery is attached. Whatever work it performed has ended, but each afternoon it turns for an hour or so.

The brightest place is beyond these ruins, at the basin’s eastern lip. A shallow lake extends almost to the edge of the country. Its bed is white silt, its water scarcely ankle-deep. Through it, innumerable small shadows move against the direction of the sunlight.

They belong to stones that are no longer there.

Lift a pebble from the lake and its shadow stays on the bottom. Carry it away, and the dark oval continues along the route the pebble would have taken under the day’s changing pull. After several weeks it fades. Larger stones leave longer traces. Near the middle of the lake, the shadow of a collapsed shelter still has a doorway you can recognize.

The water moves these remnants gently toward the lip.

By late afternoon the eastern shore is stippled black. Shadows gather against the last low ridge of silt, overlap, elongate, and go over the edge. There is no corresponding fall of stone. Only the pale lake behind them, briefly cleaner, and the cloud far below receiving nothing you can see.

As the sun descends, the basin settles. Water returns to familiar hollows. The standing grooves empty into their bowls. A few pebbles drop from the ceilings of gullies, making sharp reports in the cooler air.

Then a deeper sound travels through the ground.

It comes from below the shelters, below the cracked floor, beneath even the cloud visible through it: a prolonged grinding, immensely heavy. The water in every bowl trembles. Fine dust sifts from roof joints. For several minutes, the old door pivots turn more easily.

By the time the first long shadows reach the western fins, the sound has stopped. The paths are safe again. Across the empty courts, streams draw fresh lines through the grit, and the little stone wheel gives one final turn.
