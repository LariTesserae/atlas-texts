The height is apparent first in the boiling water. It stirs in its clay pot long before it grows hot enough to soften roots. Then in the light: even under cloud, every edge is unusually clear. A stone a day’s walk away has a visible crack. Distance offers no blue veil until it becomes enormous.

There are no peaks.

Whatever mountains raised this country have worn down into a high, almost level continent of stone. Their roots remain beneath the soil, folded and compressed, but the surface has lost the gestures of upheaval. A rise takes three days to climb and is scarcely perceptible beneath your feet. Valleys are shallow enough to look like changes in color. Only at the outer margins, several seasons away, does the land end in an escarpment whose lower reaches cannot be seen from above.

Across this height lies a city.

You can travel for a week through it without entering a room.

Its districts are separated by miles of pale pavement, gardens, frost plains, and unbuilt ground. Each district consists of a few hundred low structures gathered around an open court. Beyond them stand solitary storehouses, roofed cisterns, and long walls built to intercept the wind. Streets continue far past the last door. Some cross empty country for hundreds of miles before arriving at another settlement; some gradually lose their curbs and become rows of stones, then a slight difference in the way moss grows.

The construction is simple enough to understand by touch. Flat stones fitted without mortar. Wooden lintels blackened by age. Roof slabs resting on pillars broader at the foot than at the top. Doors of woven stems, backed with felted plant fiber. There are bronze tools in some houses, but most work is done with stone blades, bone-colored hardwood wedges, cord, and patience.

Nothing here was built quickly. Even the smallest threshold has a repair history.

In the inhabited courts, new slabs lie beside old ones, waiting for a day when the ground is willing to accept weight.

That day does not always come.

Sometimes a paving stone set down in the morning remains an inch above the street until dusk. People pass around it. They do not push it into place. By evening it may have settled, or the gap beneath it may have deepened into an opening through which a different season is visible. There are practical signs for these occurrences: a piece of white cord across a doorway, three pebbles on a windowsill, a rake laid with its teeth toward the sky.

The city has few written warnings. Most hazards repeat often enough to have become household knowledge.

Do not plant along a shadow that persists after the wall has been removed.

Do not carry water across a street while the two sides of the street are at different hours.

If a stair has more steps coming down than it had going up, leave something on the last step. A leaf is sufficient.

The air is cold enough that these instructions are often delivered through layers of cloth.

For much of the year the temperature stays just below freezing, though the ground retains narrow seams of warmth. Moisture collects there. It beads on the undersides of stones, darkens joints in masonry, hangs in the curled tips of leaves. Rain is occasional and very fine. A weather front may spend weeks crossing the plateau, delivering a brief mist to each district in turn. Behind it, frost lifts from the ground in the afternoon and returns before sunset.

There is little running water. The streams are short, intensely clear, and given to vanishing beneath sheets of vegetation. Most terminate in hollows rather than joining a larger river. Their beds preserve the shapes of much older drainage systems: broad gravel fans, polished channels, potholes large enough to contain a neighborhood. In these places, a person can stand ankle-deep in the surviving thread of a river that once carried half a continent.

The gardens grow in its abandoned bed.

From a distance they look almost black. Close up, they are richly differentiated: blue-green cushions, leaves with copper undersides, pale branching stalks crowded with translucent bladders. Moss climbs a wall and spills over its other side in heavy folds. Low shrubs spread in tightly interlocking mats, their foliage dense enough to support a dropped cup. Ferns keep their new growth enclosed in woolly sheaths until the few warm hours arrive.

There are no broad forests. Nothing tall survives the cold wind for long. Instead the vegetation puts its abundance downward and sideways. Beneath a garden runs a deep, tangled felt of roots, old stems, stored water, and decomposing leaves. The green surface may be only knee-high, but the living bed beneath it can fill an ancient ravine.

On bare country between the gardens, a single lichen patch may occupy the visible face of a hill.

Plants are in motion here, though the motions rarely announce themselves. At noon, dark leaves turn their undersides toward the sky. Near evening, mats contract around their stored moisture, exposing small pale borders of stone. Over the course of several days, a garden shifts toward a damp wall and withdraws from a dry one. The paths must be cut back regularly. The cuttings are stacked under weighted screens, where they continue rearranging themselves for a while before becoming still.

There are no animals to scatter seed, graze the cushions, or eat the roots. No insects gather at flowers. No wings cross the courts. The rich beds remain strangely untroubled on their surfaces.

Pollen travels close to the ground in thin, colored drifts. Some plants release it only when a shadow passes over them; the gardeners walk prescribed routes between the beds carrying wide wicker frames. Other plants set seed during the intervals when a district briefly overlaps another part of the plateau. A fruit opens beside a cistern and drops its contents onto a hillside far beyond the horizon.

This is one reason the city is so dispersed.

A district needs room around it for what it may become adjacent to.

On most days, distances behave. The eastern storehouse is a quarter-hour from the court. The nearest neighboring settlement is two days north. The horizon stays where it belongs. But during certain weather, the empty avenues shorten unevenly. A distant colonnade appears at the end of a local street, every pillar distinct. Cold air pours through carrying the scent of crushed vegetation unfamiliar to the neighborhood. Residents go out with baskets and exchange goods across the temporary junction.

They work slowly despite knowing it may close at any moment. Hurrying across an uncertain distance makes it less certain.

When the conjunction ends, the pavement extends again. Someone who has stayed too long may have a month’s journey home. There are guest rooms in every district, and the same basic loom, hearth, and garden tools everywhere. A traveler can usually find work while waiting for the roads to become favorable.

Beyond the inhabited courts are older precincts.

Their roofs have settled almost flush with the ground. Streets have become parallel depressions holding frost. Here and there, a stone basin remains perfectly level while everything around it has tilted or subsided. The oldest masonry is difficult to distinguish from bedrock. It has the same rounded grain, the same shallow erosion channels, the same slow tendency to split into slabs of useful thickness.

Quarries sometimes uncover finished interior walls.

No one agrees whether the buried rooms were built before the rock formed around them, or whether the rock has been gradually learning the shapes of the city. The quarry workers treat both possibilities the same way. They knock before opening a chamber. If the sound returns promptly, they continue.

Very little sound carries elsewhere.

The wind presses continuously against clothing but seldom whistles; the land has few sharp edges left to give it a voice. In a court you hear a scraper passing over wood, a lid being placed on a pot, the soft tearing noise of a root mat lifted for division. Conversations are quiet. At long intervals frost splits a stone with a dry report, astonishing in the stillness.

Sometimes a sound arrives without its event. A slab falls tomorrow, but its impact is heard today. The masons pause, locate the direction, and inspect what they can. Often they find nothing.

Meanwhile the continent remains busy.

Under the pavement, ice grows through old cracks. Moisture migrates toward buried warmth. The gardens thicken, divide, and advance. Entire drainage basins slowly change which way they slope, without any measurable change in elevation. A distant district passes through a season of white growth while its neighbor remains dark green. Clouds accumulate above certain empty squares and disperse when their shadows touch the correct stones.

In the evening there is ordinary sunlight, low and gold, touching ordinary things: a damp sleeve hung over a wall, bundles of stems laid out to dry, the pale interior of a freshly split log. Smoke rises from a few roofs and then flattens in the cold.

Far away, beyond days of empty avenues, other hearths begin to show.

There are so few lights that each seems isolated. Yet from a high roof you can watch them appear in succession across the enormous level country, following the retreat of daylight. Some belong to settlements currently within reach. Some will not be reachable again for years.

Below the roof, someone draws a woven door shut. Water gathers on its cold outer fibers. The street is swept, the garden tools are stacked, and a white cord has been tied across the mouth of the western passage.

By morning, there may be nothing unusual there.
