The chamber is brightest at its lowest point.

You descend toward a white basin through galleries of honey-colored stone, following the warmth rather than a draft. The passage walls sweat. Water gathers on the undersides of ledges, swells into trembling beads, then falls onto steps worn so shallow that they are almost a ramp. At the last turn, the light becomes strong enough to show the bones of your fingers through the skin.

Below lies a space perhaps sixty paces across, irregularly oval, with a ceiling too low at the edges and unexpectedly high in the middle. There is no view beyond it. Every opening in the rock bends away within a few yards. Yet the chamber has the brightness and noise of a place exposed to violent weather.

Water bursts upward from the basin in broad, collapsing sheets.

It rises three or four times a minute, not regularly. First the pool flattens, acquiring a hard white sheen. Pebbles tick against one another in the shallows. Then a portion of the surface lifts, sometimes as a smooth dome, sometimes as a wall taller than a person. The wall opens at the top. For a moment there are windows through it, little clear holes fringed with streaming water. Then the whole thing crashes down.

The sound fills the chamber so completely that distance is difficult to judge. A splash on the opposite shore seems to strike just behind your head. Smaller sounds remain close and sharp: grit sliding under a heel, droplets hitting a metal plate, the brief suction of mud releasing your boot. Between the great collapses there is still a rushing from the passages beneath the floor. The quietest interval would be loud enough to make conversation tiring.

Despite all this, the shore looks settled.

Its curves are old, rubbed smooth by repetitions of the same violence. The basin has a wide, shallow lip on one side and an undercut edge on the other. Low islands of stone rise out of the water, their upstream faces polished, their sheltered hollows packed with pale sand. Nothing here has a fresh fracture. Even the ceiling scars have softened. Where waves strike hardest, the limestone has been pared into long, rounded grooves that fit a palm.

The floor slopes in broad steps around the basin. Each holds a different depth of water. On the highest, a warm film runs over bare rock, thin enough to show every grain. Lower down are ankle-deep shelves, crossed by traveling bands of brightness. The deepest water is opaque, not with sediment but with light.

There are devices down there.

From the edge, you can see three of them whenever the pool draws low: narrow black uprights arranged without apparent symmetry. They are scarcely taller than fence posts. Their surfaces remain perfectly dry as water climbs around them. At the base of each, light shines from a gap too thin to account for the illumination of the whole chamber.

Whatever they were installed to do, they are still doing it.

Occasionally a droplet falls toward one of these gaps and slows. It hangs long enough for the next droplet to catch it, then the next, making a short, bright chain. The chain tightens into a thread and vanishes sideways. A moment later the basin heaves.

The uprights show no joints, no corrosion, no accessible controls. One has a limestone collar halfway up its length, where centuries of spray have deposited a creamy crust. Another leans at an angle that makes it look broken, though the rock surrounding its foot has grown to fit the slant. It is impossible to tell whether the machinery has adapted to the chamber or the chamber has spent a very long time adapting to the machinery.

There are a few more ordinary traces of work. Four sockets cut into a dry ledge. A length of ceramic conduit exposed in the wall and disappearing again beneath mineral flowstone. Near the entrance, a waist-high screen of perforated metal shelters a patch of level ground. The holes in its lower half are clogged with stone. Behind it lies a single bench, narrow and backless, with no inscription except a row of depressions at one end, polished by fingertips.

It is a good place to sit. The screen breaks the spray, and from there you can watch the water without having to brace against it.

The air is warm enough that wet clothes never feel cold. It smells of wet chalk, heated metal, and something faintly sweet from the cracks above the flood line. Humidity beads on the fine hairs of your arms. Close to the basin, each collapse sends a pressure wave through the air, a soft blow against your chest followed by a harder scattering of droplets. Farther back, the chamber breathes in long, damp exhalations. Condensation forms even on stone that feels warm to the touch.

Very little grows here, but what does grow is distinct.

A handful of plants occupy the higher seams: low rosettes with thick, translucent leaves, no larger than an outspread hand. Their roots enter cracks rather than soil. They lean toward the basin’s light, pale green at the edges and almost colorless in the middle. Each leaf has a deep central channel that carries intercepted spray back to the root.

In one recess there is a hanging mat of fine brown stems. It looks dead until a wash of brighter light passes over it and reveals minute green tips. The mat is rooted behind a curtain of mineral lace, where neither the violent spray nor a careless hand can reach it. A few detached threads turn endlessly in a nearby eddy.

The rest is stone, water, light, and room between things.

Even the living films on the wet floor occur only in sheltered patches, narrow amber crescents on the downstream sides of rocks. Their edges show how far a quiet pocket extends. Move a stone and you would erase an entire small habitat with the next surge.

Animals are easy to miss. There are flat, transparent creatures beneath the ledges, each with a dark knot of organs near its head. They hold to the rock with a fringe of tiny gripping feet. When the basin begins to flatten, they withdraw into holes scarcely wider than a blade. They react before the pebbles start to tick.

Once, after several minutes of watching, you may see a larger animal cross a shallow shelf: a cream-colored crab, or something close to one, with long feelers and a carapace dusted in mineral grains. It moves sideways through the glare, pauses beside a strand of plant debris, then carries the strand under a stone. Its entire crossing takes place between two upheavals. The next sheet of water passes over its hiding place without disturbing so much as a grain.

The turbulence has a geography. After a while you begin to recognize it.

One corner always receives the first retreating current. A low arch on the left fills with water just before the center rises. The smooth island nearest the entrance remains dry through most events, but every so often a narrow jet strikes it squarely and sweeps its top clean. There are places where standing would be exhausting and places a few feet away where you could leave a cup undisturbed.

The inhabitants use these differences. So did whoever placed the bench.

Only the light refuses to stay put. Brightness runs along the ceiling in trembling nets, concentrates in a fissure, dissolves across a broad slope of rock. Shadows turn upward when the basin swells. At the height of a surge, the plants throw enormous, wavering silhouettes against the roof. Then the water falls, their shadows shrink, and the chamber returns to its true, modest size.

On the bench you find a little grit, a shallow puddle, and a place worn smooth by bodies that sat there before you. The stone beyond the screen is streaming. Another pressure change reaches you; the transparent animals have already disappeared.

You have time to draw your feet back.

Out in the basin, the white surface pulls taut.
