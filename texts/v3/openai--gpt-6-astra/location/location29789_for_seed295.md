At night the terraces resemble the side of a mountain, but there is no exposed mountain here. Every slope is laid brick, every hollow a former court. The ridges are the backs of walls, broadened by fallen masonry and covered in vegetation. Even the loose red earth underfoot is mostly brick dust, leaf rot, and the droppings of animals.

The place occupies a high shoulder of the uplands. Beyond its outer parapet, the ground drops into a valley you cannot see. Warm air rises from that depth, carrying the smell of distant rain. Sometimes a cooler current slips down from the higher country and moves through the leaves, but the masonry gives back its stored heat well into the night. In sheltered passages, the air feels almost bodily warm.

There is very little light. A strip of clouded sky shows above the widest courts; elsewhere, foliage closes over the gaps between buildings. You distinguish a stair from a slope by the repeated catch of its edges under your feet. Wet leaves make brief, uncertain reflections. The interiors of doorways are darker patches within darkness.

Nothing is quiet enough for you to hear yourself walking.

Crickets occupy the dry walls in such numbers that their calls merge into a continuous, abrasive ringing. Frogs pulse from the cisterns. One begins with a low, hollow knock, another answers, and then an entire courtyard fills with the sound, each call striking the plaster and returning slightly altered. Under a vaulted roof, bats squeak and jostle. Their wings make a close, papery commotion that swells whenever something disturbs the roost. Farther away, a night bird repeats three hard notes from the same broken tower.

The sounds disclose the rooms. A deep, rounded chorus means water and a high ceiling. A thin metallic rasp comes from a dry stairwell. There are corridors where the noise seems to arrive from beneath your feet, conducted through old drains.

Almost everything you can touch was shaped and placed. The great retaining walls, the shallow basins, the covered walks, the courses of brick beneath the roots: all belong to a settlement that once extended from one end of this high shelf to the other. At its widest, it takes much of a night to cross, especially now that the direct routes are blocked. There are no working mechanisms, no lights, no inhabited houses. The objects that might once have furnished the rooms are gone. What remains is the heavy arrangement of the place—levels, enclosures, channels, shelter.

Animals use that arrangement thoroughly.

The undersides of the vaults are crowded with bats, hanging in dark, uneven masses. Small rodents travel inside the double walls. Geckos wait at openings where insects emerge. Ants keep narrow routes clear across roots and fallen leaves, their traffic scarcely interrupted by the larger creatures passing over them. In the upper vegetation, sleeping birds fill branches that appear empty until one shifts and sets off a brief chain of objections.

There are feathers in the drains, scales beneath the figs, pale insect wings pasted to damp plaster. Every ledge has a history of occupation written in stains.

The vegetation is thickest where the masonry meets itself: in joints, in the corners of courts, along the buried rims of tanks. Figs have taken possession of several roofs. Their roots descend through rooms in bundled ropes, spread across the floors, and disappear into cracks too narrow to admit a finger. Ferns crowd the shaded stairs. Broad-leaved shrubs lean over parapets, their leaves chewed into lace at the edges. Creepers cross the lanes between buildings, first as a few suspended stems, then as tangled bridges holding dead leaves, twigs, and more seedlings.

From an upper terrace, these patches join into something like forest. At close range, their dependence on the old construction is obvious. One wall is lush from top to bottom because a hidden tank leaks through it. The next is almost bare, its brick warm and powdery beneath a sparse fringe of grass.

Water is present in pockets rather than everywhere. The highest courts dry quickly after rain. Their paving is convex, worn smooth in the middle, and the surviving gutters still carry water away. Beneath them are tanks that hold it for months. Some contain only a shallow black pool; others are deep enough that a falling seed makes a small, delayed sound.

Around each tank the air changes. It becomes cooler and smells of soaked leaves. Snails graze the green film at the waterline. Mosquitoes hang in sheltered clouds. Frogs sit in the old overflow mouths with their toes spread against the ceramic lining.

A brief rain can rearrange the noise of the whole district. For a few minutes, water runs down stairs that have been dry for weeks. It gathers in roof hollows, spills through root mats, and falls into courtyards in heavy drops long after the clouds have passed. The frogs begin calling from places that had seemed empty. Then the upper surfaces dry again, while the moisture remains below, shaded by several layers of brick and leaf.

The ruins are old enough that their collapse has become difficult to distinguish from their settled shape. A fallen arch supports a bank of ferns. A room filled with rubble holds a tree whose roots now bind the rubble together. The principal paths belong to animals and avoid unstable edges with an accuracy no visitor initially understands. Broad, inviting stretches of paving may conceal a hollow roof; a narrow route along a wall may be firm for its entire length.

Most changes are small. Mortar softens. Roots thicken. A little more soil collects behind a fallen beam. Once in a long while, a section gives way, and for several nights the creatures around it are restless. Then new routes form.

Near the outer edge, the vegetation lowers. Wind reaches the crowns of shrubs and turns their leaves together. Here the settlement opens into a succession of broad platforms, their parapets rounded by weather, their paving interrupted by raised seams of roots. Beyond them lies the unseen valley. You can feel its size in the air moving upward, and in the way the animal noise loses its echoes at the brink.

Behind you, the courts remain loud and close. Ahead, scattered bats pass out over the drop.

Toward dawn, the smallest details begin to separate: a shell on a stair, the red flank of a wall, hundreds of ants crossing a pale root. The bats return through the high openings, folding themselves into already crowded spaces. Birds stir in the figs. The frogs continue for a while beneath them.

Only when the sky lightens does the extent of the place become clear: terrace after terrace following the contour of the upland, every one built, weathered, and occupied, with warm mist caught in the deeper courts and the first birds rising above the roofs.
