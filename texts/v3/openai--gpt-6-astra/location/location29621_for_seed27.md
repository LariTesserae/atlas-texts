The first impression is of a high, empty country under a low ceiling of cloud. Long-backed hills run away into the dusk. Between them lie shallow basins, broad enough to hold towns, and several do: pale towers standing far apart, roads that catch a little light, the faint red squares of occupied windows.

Then the ground shifts beneath your foot.

Only a little. Enough to release your footprint after you lift your heel.

There is no soil here. What looks like a weathered plateau is the exposed back of a plant, or of so many plants grown together that the distinction has ceased to matter. Its surface is gray, finely ridged, dry as an old seedpod. In sheltered places it has the softness of cork; on the ridges, the hard polish of something handled for centuries. Split seams disclose layers of cream-colored fiber, then a dark, flexible tissue that slowly closes against the air.

The plateau stands well above the cloud deck of the lower world. From its western edge you can look down through immense gaps and see lightning moving silently in the overcast below. Up here the light comes mostly from the sky’s long twilight, supplemented by a faint green emission from freshly exposed plant tissue. There is little warmth in either. The wind makes your teeth ache.

Nothing drips. Nothing glistens.

The native vegetation has no use for water. Within its fibers, charge moves through branching mineral chains; gases pass from chamber to chamber through valves thinner than moth wings. Its closest equivalent to sap is a fine, mobile dust, held in suspension by the tissue around it. A cut stem exhales with a smell of pepper and scorched insulation. Then it seals.

Everything underfoot is alive, but very little stands upright. Hundreds of paces may separate one tall stalk from the next. Each rises alone from the low continuous growth, carrying a few black blades at the end of a bare, jointed shaft. They turn slowly, presenting their edges to the wind. Between them, the view is almost unobstructed: miles of rolling, close-cropped surface, worn hollows, distant buildings, the moving shadows of things in the air.

The age of the place is clearest in those hollows.

Once there were trunks here large enough to have divided the horizon. Their bases remain as rings several miles across, leveled almost flush with the surrounding tissue. You cross one without noticing until the grain underfoot changes direction and follows a curve too broad to see all at once. Old branches have become low ridges. Former crowns are open basins, their intricate growth worn down by windblown husk and the passage of animals.

The plant does not restore its old grandeur. It repairs only what it needs. New growth follows ancient contours, replacing a worn layer with one almost exactly like it. Even the broken places have become customary: a collapsed arch is lined with healthy tissue; a split that once nearly severed a district is now a permanent avenue for the wind.

There are no ruins in the ordinary sense. There are injuries that have acquired addresses.

The nearest town occupies one of these.

Its buildings grow along the sides of a wide fissure, connected at intervals by narrow bridges. Some rise eight or ten stories, though the stories are not level with one another. Their walls have the warm gray color of sunflower shells. Broad windows open behind lattices that tighten when the wind becomes dangerous. At ground level, the houses separate into roots, leaving sheltered arcades where people walk beside grazing animals and carts with soft, muscular wheels.

The streets are bare, clean plant tissue. Along their centers runs a dark stripe that contracts in slow pulses, carrying fallen husks, shed skins, and household waste toward public digestion chambers. Nothing piles up for long.

The town is spacious. There are plazas with only a bench at one edge and a single flowering pole at the other; neighborhoods where the next inhabited building is a five-minute walk away. Yet the utilities are everywhere. A slight thickening in a wall is a heat supply. A comb of short fibers beside a doorway is a communications terminal. Freight travels beneath the streets as visible bulges, passing one another in the deep tissue with a low, abdominal rumble.

At one end of a square, two freestanding doorframes face each other across fifty yards of open ground.

A porter rolls a load of sealed seed cases through the first and emerges from the second before the rear wheels have entered. For a moment the cart occupies both places, its central shaft stretched into a line too fine to see.

The frames are living grafts. They have been persuaded that they are neighboring cells.

This is the local form of distance engineering. It requires decades of cultivation and constant tending. Each evening, workers climb the frames and remove stray buds with silver jaws. If allowed to branch, the connection begins admitting other destinations. The oldest transit station in town has a sealed third doorway from which a cold, steady wind blows, carrying pollen no one has identified.

Despite the open spaces, the plateau is loud.

Wind travels through the buried remains of great stems and comes out as an organ note too low to locate. The solitary black-bladed plants clack at their joints. In the town, shutters strike their frames in complicated sequences, opening and closing to maintain the pressure inside. Every few minutes a distant report rolls across the country: a seed capsule releasing, or a sheet of dead outer tissue tearing free.

The animals add sharpness to this continuous bass.

There are broad, six-legged grazers whose backs scarcely rise above your knees. Seen at a distance they resemble loose patches of the ground, until they turn and their pale undersides show. They feed with vibrating mouthplates, shaving the surface into curls. Herds move widely dispersed, each animal keeping itself within hearing of the next by giving a hard, two-part knock with its jaws.

Smaller creatures occupy the freshly grazed paths. Some are nearly transparent, visible chiefly because the dark dust in their bodies travels in quick, regular bursts. Others wear seed husks as armor and drag long, sensitive tails through the grooves. They gather by the thousands wherever a new seam opens.

Above them hunt thin-bodied fliers with fans of stiff vanes. The fliers do not flap. They catch the electrical gradient between the plateau and the clouds below, turning on their sides to rise or fall. When one stoops, its vanes sing. A whole hunting flock makes a sound like a blade being sharpened against an enormous wheel.

Much of the day you see only a few animals at a time. Then a pressure change passes through the plateau, and life comes out of every fold.

The grazers stop feeding. Their jaw-knocks accelerate. Fine slits open in the ground, releasing warm gas and a blizzard of flat, copper-colored spores. Burrowing animals pour from the vents ahead of the discharge. The fliers descend to meet them. People in the town close their shutters and draw cloth screens across the street arcades.

For twenty minutes the entire basin rattles.

Spores strike the walls, the bridges, your coat. They collect in the lee of your boots, restless as filings near a magnet. Farther out, the tall black blades have spread into fans, intercepting the release. Their shafts bend almost to the ground. A few seeds explode overhead with white flashes, scattering hooked filaments across the grazing country.

Then the pressure falls. The vents close. Street fibers rise and begin combing the drifts away.

In the quieter interval that follows, you can hear chewing.

There are rules to walking here. Do not sleep on a surface that has become warm. Do not cut across a field whose grain is turning toward you. On the old pale roads, keep to the center: their margins are where the plateau tries new things.

The inhabitants mark safe routes with rows of small red buds. They are maintained by gardeners who travel with stools, instruments, and little cases of sleeping insects. Roadwork is patient, intimate labor. A damaged junction may require one woman to sit beside it all afternoon, feeding a beetle into an opening every few minutes while traffic walks around her.

Toward evening, the buildings lower their upper floors.

It happens so slowly that you first notice the skyline has changed. Towers shorten; balconies settle into recesses; bridges slacken and then pull taut at a lower level. The town is reducing its exposure to the night wind. Out on the plateau, the tall collectors fold their blades along their stems.

The horizon remains dimly visible. Beyond the last houses, a herd crosses the pale ring of an ancient trunk. Their calls travel clearly through the cold.

Under the road, something large is being carried from one district to another. You feel its progress through the soles of your shoes. A gentle rise approaches, passes beneath you, and continues toward the town, where windows are closing one by one.
