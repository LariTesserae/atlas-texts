The mountains are still soft beneath the water.

Their ridges rise through the drowned valley in folds of pale mineral paste, firm enough to hold a footprint, not yet firm enough to keep it. Here and there a harder crest has formed: a translucent blade, a shelf of porous white stone, a knuckle of black glass still warm from whatever is making the mountain underneath. Nothing has been rounded by weather. The edges that exist are new edges. Most surfaces have not quite decided where to end.

Above them lies nearly a hundred fathoms of green water.

This is a high sea. Through gaps between its outer peaks, far below the level of the valley, clouds move across the lower world. The water does not fall through those gaps. It bulges between them, dark and convex, with a faint skin that gathers wrinkles when the weather outside changes. From within, one can approach this boundary and see daylight distorted through it: distant dry ranges, lightning underfoot, sometimes the minute dark mark of a bird moving in air.

A hand pressed against the boundary meets something like the resistance of a leaf. Push harder and the fingers emerge cold and suddenly heavy.

The people of the valley keep their houses well back from it.

Their town occupies three terraces on the sheltered side of a forming ridge. From a distance it resembles a collection of seed husks caught in roots. The houses are woven from broad, pliant waterwood strips and weighted with stones, their pointed ends facing the slow current. Between them stand thicker communal chambers built of stacked mineral slabs. These have no mortar. Over years, the mountain grows into the joints.

There are perhaps six hundred houses, though it is difficult to count them. Gardens obscure the lower rows. New chambers are continually being enclosed within old ones, and some apparently solid buildings are only trellises thick with leaves. The streets pass over roofs, beneath hanging beds of vegetation, through narrow places where two walls almost touch. Most have a rope running along one side. People travel by pulling themselves hand over hand, letting their feet trail clear of the unfinished ground.

The ropes are polished by use. At junctions, knots identify the different ways.

A knot with three long loops leads toward the upper gardens. A hard round knot means there is an air chamber nearby. Loose tassels mark a passage that is moving and should not be trusted in darkness.

There is darkness enough to make this necessary. The sun reaches the valley as a broad, uncertain brightening overhead, seldom strong enough to cast a shadow. Beneath the garden canopies, afternoon has the color of the inside of an olive. The town’s few lamps are shallow stone cups of luminous culture, tended with scraps of root and covered when no one needs them. Their light shows a hand, a doorway, the first turn of a stair. Beyond that, things return gradually to green.

Warmth comes from below. It rests against the belly of the valley and rises so slowly that a suspended strand of hair may take an hour to change direction. Near the newest rock, the water feels almost feverish. Farther up it is merely warm, pleasant against skin, carrying a faint mineral taste.

The town’s air chambers are tucked beneath overhangs or inside inverted clay bells. Their entrances are underneath. A person rises into one, lifts their face out of the water, and finds a low ceiling stippled with droplets, a ledge for elbows, perhaps two neighbors already resting there. The air smells of damp fiber and the bitter leaves grown to freshen it. Conversation happens in these small pockets. Out in the streets, people use their hands.

Occasionally a laugh escapes a chamber in a necklace of bubbles. It rises between the houses, expanding, and is gone.

Most of the valley is quieter than this.

There is the faint rasp of rope through a wooden guide. The soft tapping of someone shaping a slab with a stone adze. At long intervals a new seam opens in the mountain, making a low sound felt in the teeth before it is heard. Work stops for a moment. Hanging leaves tremble. A dusting of white mineral settles on the roofs.

Then the adze begins again.

The vegetation favors whatever has recently become solid. Along the ridges, broad red-brown leaves stand upright on short stems, crowding one another like hands held close to a fire. Their undersides are velveted with tiny clear bladders. Taller plants grow in loose columns, each leaf separated from the next by several feet of bare, flexible stalk. Looking through these groves, one sees the valley in layers: leaf, house, pale ridge, another leaf, a distant terrace fading into suspended green.

Near the settlement the growth is tended. Edible stems are braided sideways to keep them within reach. Root mats are stretched over wooden frames so that new stone cannot swallow them. The most carefully guarded gardens contain small, almost colorless plants whose hollow leaves collect breathable gas. Each morning someone empties these with a clay vessel, slowly, taking care not to bruise the seams.

Beyond the last cultivated terrace, the plants grow less generously. There are clear expanses of raw mineral, smooth slopes glazed with bacterial color, and hollows full of such fine white particles that entering them erases the body from the knees down. A dropped tool can disappear without reaching anything that could be called a bottom.

Animals are uncommon. Now and then a translucent grazer moves across a roof, its many small feet lifting and lowering in sequence. Its passage leaves a clean band through the green film. Children watch for these tracks more often than they see the creatures themselves.

Once or twice in a season, a large dark swimmer crosses above the valley. It travels alone. Its shadow takes several minutes to pass, and the garden leaves fold inward before it arrives. No one has seen it feed. People bring their baskets close and wait beneath the eaves until the leaves reopen.

The settlement changes more slowly than its inhabitants, but not in the way an old town on dry land changes. Foundations swell. Rooms become shallower. A threshold rises by the width of a finger over a winter. The builders leave generous spaces beneath floors and hang their storage baskets rather than setting them down.

In the oldest quarter, a meeting chamber has almost filled with stone. Only the upper part of its doorway remains, a curved opening just large enough for a child. Inside, the last foot of space is still dark, still furnished with a row of pegs. People touch the lintel as they pass. Eventually they will fasten a new rope above it and the street will continue.

At the outer terraces, work proceeds in the other direction. Slabs are propped around places where the mountain is expected to rise. Young plants are tied to frames above bare water. A staircase ends halfway down a slope, its lowest steps hanging unsupported, waiting for their foundation.

From there, the whole inhabited valley can be seen: the close-set roofs, the garden columns, the pale unfinished ridges extending into dimness. Overhead, the high sea holds its quiet weight. Below, clouds brush the underside of the water’s far boundary.

A builder sits on the unfinished stair with an adze secured to her wrist. Beside her floats a basket of wooden wedges. She places her palm against the soft white slope, testing it, then settles back to wait a little longer.
