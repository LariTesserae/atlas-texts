The hollow is small enough that, in clear weather, someone standing on the pump-house roof could see its whole extent: a basin of dark water and worn stone, enclosed by a low rim of shale. There is no clear weather now. Mist has settled among the rocks, leaving their upper surfaces visible as separate black backs. The far side lies perhaps a hundred paces away. It looks much farther until something moves there.

There are no trees, no reeds, no grass holding the edges together. The banks descend in blunt shelves, each shelf rubbed smooth by water that has occupied slightly different levels over a long time. Some ledges are only a finger’s breadth wide. Others hold pools deep enough to conceal an animal. Between them, clay lies in folds like soaked cloth. The ground is firm in places that look treacherous and gives way in places that look firm; a pale crust often means hollow mud beneath, while the darkest patches are usually exposed stone.

Water enters almost everywhere. It beads out of the shale, runs from cracks in threads, and gathers beneath overhangs before dropping. Little of it arrives as rain. Even on a rainless day, the underside of a handrail grows wet, and the pump-house walls shed enough water to keep their foundations submerged. The air is cold without being sharp. It settles inside sleeves and stays there.

At first the basin appears crowded with rubble. Then the eye begins sorting it.

There are stones, certainly: thin plates tipped against one another, rounded nodules, low stacks left where softer rock has washed away. But between them stand the shells of animals that have scarcely moved in years. Some are no bigger than thumbnails. Others rise to the knee, tapering, ribbed, their openings turned away from the nearest drip. Mineral deposits have joined old shells to stone and stone to old shells. Living ones can be recognized by a narrow rim of pale flesh, or by the slow passage of grit across their lips.

The large ones feed on the water itself, drawing it through chambers where a gray bacterial paste grows. Their shells are black near the base and chalk-white at the mouth. When disturbed, they close in succession rather than all at once: a small click nearby, another under a ledge, then a series of distant clicks fading into the fog. Afterward the basin seems quieter than before, although nothing loud has stopped.

Under the shelves, the water has a faint yellow cast. Here it rises from below, cold and rich in dissolved salts. White films gather where this water meets the darker water from the walls. They hang in loose curtains, wrinkle, tear, and form again. Almost everything alive in the hollow eats the films, eats something that eats them, or waits beneath a stone for scraps.

Flat, many-legged grazers work the curtains from the bottom upward. Their backs resemble little flakes of wet slate, but their undersides are pink and surprisingly soft. They crowd into the shallow runnels until each one is touching two or three others. Where a runnel narrows, they climb over one another without hurry. Their feet make a faint brushing sound, easiest to hear with an ear near the stone.

Occasionally the whole procession stops. A threadlike predator is passing through it, too thin to see except where its body bends the reflection. The grazers flatten and grip. One fails to hold. There is a quick disturbance, hardly more than a wrinkle, and the procession resumes with a gap in it.

The basin has depth as well as surface. Every shelf is undercut. Every large stone rests above a black interval. The water that seems to occupy a single shallow pool is connected to other water behind the bank, below the pump house, inside layers of rock no thicker than a closed book. Small pale swimmers emerge from one crack and vanish into another. They have broad heads, blunt fins, and eyes reduced to dark points under the skin. In the dimness they look unfinished. They are not: their bodies fit these passages so closely that they can reverse through them without turning.

Near the center stands the pump house, built on a square of concrete that has become an island only by a few inches. Its brickwork is almost the same color as the shale. One wall has bowed outward, and the roof is held down by iron strips, each strip fastened with bolts larger than seems necessary. The door remains shut, but water comes through the bottom of it.

An elevated pipe once carried whatever the pump lifted across the basin. Most of the supports remain. The pipe itself survives in three disconnected lengths, dull iron above the water and orange beneath it. At the broken ends, mineral growth has narrowed the bores to uneven circles. Little animals shelter there. One length has filled with shell colonies so completely that it looks packed with pale teeth.

There are other traces of work: two steps descending into a pool, a handwheel half embedded in a bank, a short rail ending against bare rock. Nothing here was grand. The place was fitted out for a task, and the task required only a few people, a machine, somewhere to stand. Weather has softened the distinction between their work and the basin’s own forms. A drainage groove cut with a chisel now joins a natural fissure without any visible seam.

The pump still runs.

It starts at irregular intervals, usually when the lowest pool has become very still. A tremor passes through the concrete. Water quivers inside the broken pipe, though the pipe is no longer connected to anything. Then comes a slow turning sound from behind the door: metal moving under load, stopping, taking hold again.

It is not loud. Beside the building, it can be felt through the soles more clearly than heard. Across the basin, it resembles an animal shifting its weight in sleep.

With each stroke, the water withdraws a little from the eastern shelves. Dark bands appear on the stone. Shells lower their mouths. The pale swimmers leave the shallows and collect in deeper openings. After six or seven strokes, water begins rising on the western side.

No exposed conduit connects the two sides. The old pipe points north.

The transfer continues for several minutes. It never empties one half or floods the other. It merely moves the shoreline by the width of a hand, sometimes two. This is enough to reorganize the hollow. Grazers follow the retreating edge. Small translucent scavengers come out to examine newly exposed seams. A thick-bodied hunter, the color of dirty porcelain, pushes its head from beneath the pump-house foundation and waits beside a shrinking puddle. Its eyes sit far back on its head. Its front feet are spread wide on the concrete, each toe tipped with a black pad.

It catches a swimmer with a short sideways movement, then backs under the foundation to eat.

When the pump stops, the western pools keep rising for a little while.

The water returns slowly through the rock. In some channels the flow reverses at once; in others it continues in the old direction for an hour. There are crossings where two thin streams run side by side, separated by a ridge scarcely higher than a coin, moving oppositely. Animals gather at these crossings. The bacterial films are thickest there, layered cream over gray, and the stone is almost hidden beneath feeding bodies.

Up close, the hollow is densely occupied. There are eggs fixed inside abandoned shells, transparent sacs trembling with movement. There are mites on the eggs, larger scavengers hunting the mites, and soft tubes protruding from drill-holes in the rock. A single fist-sized nodule may carry more activity than an entire bare stretch of bank. Yet from the rim, all this resolves into a few patches of different texture. The animals conserve their movements. The water muffles what they do.

The most audible things are often drops.

One falls from the pump-house gutter into a deep iron vessel beneath the surface, producing a low, hollow note. Another strikes a ledge with a flat tap. Along the southern wall, seepage descends behind a loose sheet of shale; the sheet ticks faintly as the cold water changes its shape. These sounds overlap without becoming a steady rhythm. There are long pauses in which a distant click of shell seems deliberate.

Light enters from a narrow portion of sky. The basin is not deep, but its rim leans inward in places, and the mist catches what little brightness reaches it. At midday, the pools show dull silver between the dark stones. Under ledges, visibility ends abruptly. A hand pushed into one of those shadows disappears before it reaches the wrist.

After dusk, a few submerged films begin to glow. The light is weak and blue-gray, visible only after the eye has given up looking for the path. It traces the water routes rather than illuminating them: a line beneath a shelf, a brief patch inside a pipe, three descending marks in a crack. When a grazer passes, the film brightens around its mouth. Feeding animals become visible as little moving interruptions in the light.

Nothing grows tall here. Nothing can afford to expose much of itself to the air. Life spreads sideways, enters cavities, coats undersides, takes possession of the gap beneath something else. Even the pump house is inhabited chiefly between its bricks, under its floor, and inside the double thickness of its walls. On the roof, the wet iron is almost bare.

Toward morning, condensation thickens. The rocks seem to draw closer as their reflections disappear. Water covers the eastern shelves again, erasing the dark bands left by the pump. The porcelain-colored hunter comes out for a drink, stepping carefully around a cluster of shells. It laps twice, pauses, and presses one front foot flat against the concrete.

There is a tremor below it.

The animal withdraws before the first stroke. Around the basin, mouths begin to close, swimmers gather at the cracks, and the grazers turn toward a shore that has not yet started moving.
