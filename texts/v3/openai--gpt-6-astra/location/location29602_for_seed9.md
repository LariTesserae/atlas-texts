The garden is higher than the clouds, but only by the height of its outer wall. When the wind changes, cloud comes over the coping and moves between the plants, rubbing moisture onto the undersides of leaves. The far end disappears first: a yellow maintenance door, three steps, the pale crown of a tree. Then the nearer pools go blank. You can still see your feet through the shallow water on the walkway.

It is a small place, perhaps forty paces across. Beyond its wall there is no neighboring slope, no forest climbing toward it. Through the drainage slots you see blue air, sometimes the white backs of clouds, and, very far below, a dark country divided by rivers. The garden stands on the top of a constructed column. How far that column descends cannot be seen from here.

Everything underfoot has been made.

This is easiest to recognize at the entrance, where the floor is ribbed iron and the pools have numbered depth marks. Farther in, the iron disappears under cream-colored mineral shelves, black growing felt, and the interwoven roots of plants. What looks like a low ridge has a straight inspection seam along its crest. What looks like a boulder is warm, hollow, and faintly vibrating. Beneath a curtain of fern roots, six bolts hold it to the floor.

The ground is being manufactured while you stand on it.

Water issues from narrow brass mouths around the perimeter. It comes up warm from inside the column, carrying dissolved lime, iron, and nutrients, and passes slowly over a lattice of ceramic ribs. On contact with air, it begins to leave its burden behind. The newest deposits are translucent, almost soft, with an opalescent edge where a film is still thickening. Older deposits have become the pale shelves that divide the garden into pools.

There is almost no wear. Corners remain corners. The channels do not meander because water has cut them; a worker bent their supporting mesh that way. Even the little rounded stones on the paths have wire eyes hidden underneath, where they were hung in a precipitation tank.

Here a hill begins as a frame. It is plumbed, wetted, allowed to gather weight. Only later will it be expected to support a tree.

Several new hills are at this first stage along the eastern wall: low cages shining through moss, each no bigger than a sleeping animal. Water beads along their wires. Tender roots have already found them and are crossing the gaps before there is anything solid to grip.

The vegetation makes the place feel older than it is. Broad leaves overlap above the paths, some as long as an arm, their stems standing in collars of brown fiber. Tree ferns rise from cylindrical beds. Creepers pass from one support to another and have to be unwound from valve handles. In the wettest corner, a thicket of glossy canes has lifted the lid of its own nutrient trough. There are flowers, but few showy ones: little green cups held under leaves, waxy bells close to the water, a single pale inflorescence that smells of pepper when the air becomes still.

The planting is lush without being continuous. Between two heavy masses of growth there may be a patch of bare grating, a recessed gauge, a clean ceramic surface on which the first green points are only now appearing. Those unfinished patches are bright in the ordinary daylight. You can see the separate drops on them, each containing a magnified piece of mesh.

Sun reaches the garden for much of the day. There is no permanent roof. Fine steel ribs curve inward from the wall, carrying rolled storm cloth and the tracks of a traveling service arm, but above those is open sky. At this elevation the sunlight is sharp. Leaf shadows have clear edges until vapor passes through them.

The warmth comes from below.

It is strongest beside the central rise, where three small trees grow around a capped shaft. Their trunks lean away from one another, leaving a narrow triangular opening through which you can see the shaft’s pressure dial. Heat enters the garden through the floor, through the irrigation water, and through squat porcelain vents disguised by no more than a loose screen of stems. Kneeling beside one, you smell hot metal beneath the smell of wet roots.

Outside the wall, the air is cold enough to make the windward rail uncomfortable to hold. Inside, moisture collects at the back of the neck. The boundary between the two climates is only a few feet wide. Leaves that stray across it develop brown edges, so someone has tied the outermost branches inward with soft red cord.

Much happens here, but little of it is loud.

Under the walkway, a pump gives a low, steady pressure to the soles of your feet. Every so often a relay clicks inside a wall box. Water falls from one shelf to another in threads rather than streams. A leaf slowly filling at its cupped base tips and empties, making a brief, surprisingly large splash. Then the leaf rises again.

The loudest regular sound is the service arm beginning its circuit.

It moves on the overhead track with a short metallic complaint, then proceeds almost silently. Its end carries a brush, a narrow watering nozzle, and a pair of blunt shears. It pauses over each bed. Sometimes it does nothing visible. Sometimes a fine spray darkens the plants below, or the brush touches a cluster of small flowers and comes away yellow. A cut tendril falls into the water. The arm waits until it has drifted clear before moving on.

There are very few animals. A small snail lives under the third step of the maintenance door, where the metal stays cool. Its trail is visible on mornings when the rest of the step has dried. Once in a while a winged insect arrives above the wall, hangs in the rising warmth, and vanishes into the foliage. Whether it stays is difficult to tell. Pollination cannot depend on visitors.

The garden’s litter is caught in little combs fitted across the drains. Root ends, shed leaves, and the occasional spoiled flower collect there until an attendant takes them down into the column. They return eventually as part of the dark slurry fed through the planting collars. Nothing has been given enough time to become deep soil. Beneath the loose surface fiber, a probing finger soon meets felt, then perforated plate.

Yet the plants are already making arrangements the builders did not specify. Roots have crossed between separate irrigation circuits. Moss keeps one overflow damp long after its supply shuts off. A vine has woven itself around a pressure pipe and condensed enough water onto its own leaves to support a row of small plants growing in its forks.

Near the central shaft, one pool rises and falls against the cycle of the pumps. No one has corrected it. Its water is exceptionally clear, and beneath it a white mineral lip advances over a black seam in the floor. At the advancing edge are tiny stairways, too regular to be accidental but too small to have been set by hand. Each step holds a still bead of water. New steps appear underneath.

By late afternoon, the stored heat begins lifting a faint mist from the beds. The traveling arm returns to its recess. Valves close in sequence around the wall, and for a few minutes the pools settle enough to reflect the ribs overhead. The reflection makes a roof where there is none.

Then the evening feed begins. Warm water enters under the lowest shelf. It rises without splashing, covers a fresh band of pale stone, and reaches the fine roots waiting at its edge. Along the eastern wall, drops join on the unfinished cages and run down into shapes that are becoming, very slowly, ground.
