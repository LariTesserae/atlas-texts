The street lies two floors below the streets still in use. It is not quite a tunnel. A narrow opening runs along the crown of its roof, crossed by girders and the undersides of newer buildings, and through this opening comes a little weather: rain, pale air, the occasional dry leaf that has traveled farther than anything down here ordinarily travels.

There are seven doorways, a turning bay, and a pumping station. You can see nearly the whole place from the bottom stair, although the far end is difficult to distinguish from its reflection. The street stops there against a retaining wall whose stones have swollen out of alignment. Water comes through the joints without haste.

The paving is laid in shallow terraces. Each terrace holds a sheet of water, ankle-deep at most, separated from the next by a worn granite lip. Once these were curbs, steps, loading platforms. Now they govern a slow descent through the street. Water slips over them in broad, clear folds, so thin that it makes almost no sound. The stones under those folds are green-black and smooth enough to show the grain within them. Elsewhere they have a pale, soft-looking rind that scrapes the skin if touched.

Nothing here is newly ruined. The settlement of the roofs, the widening of mortar joints, the rounding of the lowest stair: all have gone far enough to acquire a kind of dependability. Loose things fell a long time ago. What remains has found somewhere to rest.

Above the waterline, the brickwork carries old shopfronts. Their windows were filled with masonry, then opened again for pipes, then partly filled around the pipes. A bakery’s tiled threshold leads into an electrical switching room. Across from it, three large ceramic insulators occupy the display niche of a former jeweler. There are still hooks for a shutter, a boot scraper, the narrow slot of a letter box. Small civic fittings persist among the industrial additions, though there is no longer a reason to post a letter here.

The pumping station takes up the largest frontage. Its doors are open by the width of a hand. Inside, two vertical engines descend through the floor into black wells. Their upper flywheels scarcely move. You must watch a spoke against a crack in the wall to be certain that they are turning at all.

The machinery works under the weight of the city overhead.

Every hour or two, a building settles by an amount too small to trouble its inhabitants. The movement passes down through rods inside the walls and advances one of the flywheels. A piston draws water from beneath the foundations and delivers it into a high iron tank. The tank empties elsewhere, through infrastructure inaccessible from this street.

There is electric power too. Thick cables hang on porcelain saddles above head height, their tarred cloth coverings wet with condensation. Three lamps burn behind wired glass. One has gone brown, one greenish, and the last retains a small cold whiteness. There is enough light to place your feet. Colors have to be inferred from objects brought close.

The air is cool against the face and colder around the ankles. Metal is unpleasant to hold. Yet there is no sharp draft, no stirring chill: the temperature seems stored in the masonry. A coat left on a hook becomes damp but never quite wet enough to wring out.

Plants occupy very little of the available surface. A fern grows where the roof opening briefly widens, its fronds held almost flat to receive the sky. Short reeds have rooted in the grit behind a drain cover. They are colorless at their bases and green only in their last few inches. Their roots make a dense comb in the current, gathering fine rust and the fibers shed by ropes.

Most of what appears to be moss is animal.

The velvet along the waterline consists of minute filter feeders, each with a ring of transparent arms. Seen from the stair they form a quiet brown margin. Kneel close and the margin separates into thousands of distinct movements. They withdraw beneath a passing shadow and emerge again very slowly, the patch recovering its texture from the edges inward.

There are fish under the loading platforms. Long, pale ones, nearly as narrow as the gaps between stones. They remain suspended with their bellies just clear of the bottom, moving only their gills. Smaller fish rest in the dark hollows of their mouths. This arrangement does not appear to inconvenience either creature. When a large fish changes position, the little ones turn together to keep facing the current.

Along the handrails, snails graze the condensation. Their shells are flattened on one side so that a whole row can fit beneath a rail without being visible from above. There may be a hundred on a single span. Only their tracks betray them: clean, winding lines across the dull metal.

The larger animals are few enough to leave the street feeling empty.

A white, blunt-headed creature sleeps in the turning bay, half in the water, its folded limbs resembling scraps of heavy hose. It is about the size of a dog. Two others occupy recesses beneath opposite doorsteps. At long intervals one lifts its head, listens to something below the paving, and puts it down again.

They keep clear of the round iron covers set into the street. Even the filter feeders leave a bare circle around each cover.

Those covers rise.

Not often, and never very far. First the standing water draws away from the rim. Then the iron lifts perhaps an inch, without tilting, exposing a dark seam. No vapor escapes. Nothing can be heard moving beneath it. After several minutes the cover lowers, and the water resumes its former level.

The pumping station’s flywheels stop while a cover is raised. A notice beside the station doors, painted in large white letters, instructs maintenance workers not to restart them. The rest of the notice has washed away. There is no explanation on the surviving wall.

Human visits are infrequent but continuing. A dry pair of gloves sits in a glass-fronted cabinet. There are fresh tool marks on a valve whose wheel is older than the brick around it. Someone has replaced a stair tread with galvanized grating, and beneath this bright rectangle a dense congregation of small shrimp has gathered, feeding on whatever the new metal gives off.

A maintenance path crosses the shallow terraces on stepping blocks. They are widely spaced and each has a hollow worn into its upper face. Some hollows fit a boot. Others are too long, or face the wrong way. The most comfortable route changes slightly between visits; a block remembered as steady may now rock, while another has seated itself firmly in the silt.

Mostly the place is quiet enough to hear water forming before it falls. A droplet swells at a bolt, stretches, and strikes the flooded street with a small, rounded note. Much later, another answers it. Somewhere inside a wall, a relay closes.

Near the far retaining wall, the water is perfectly clear. You can see a lower course of stone beneath the street, and below that the top of an arch. Small fish pass through it in both directions. From time to time a faint amber light shows beyond, too weak to illuminate anything but the curve of the opening.

It remains for a while after the lamps above have dimmed, and fades before they brighten.
