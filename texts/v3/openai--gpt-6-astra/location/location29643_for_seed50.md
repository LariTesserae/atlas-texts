Just below the summit, where the mountain is still too young to have scree, there is a split in the pale stone. A hand will fit through it sideways. Beyond it lies a flooded hollow no larger than the inside of a bread oven.

The valley is far beneath this place. Even the highest birds usually pass below it. On clear mornings, sunlight travels through the split and illuminates the entire hollow: its low ceiling, its unfinished floor, the few things fastened to its sides. Nothing here belongs to the deep sea’s darkness. The water is bright enough to show the shadow of a hair.

It does not drain.

Outside, meltwater runs down the mountain in ordinary threads, finding the steepest route. Inside, water presses against every surface and bulges slightly through the entrance. Its exposed face trembles in the wind without breaking. A finger pushed into it comes back cold, with a clear bead clinging to the nail; the bead eventually falls, but the water behind it stays. There is no basin holding it, no downward-facing lip. The hollow is simply full.

And it is enormously loud.

The first sound, heard from outside, resembles a river passing through a narrow gorge. Lean closer and separate noises appear within it: heavy impacts, a fine grinding, the abrupt concussion of something broad and flexible being shaken. Through a jaw pressed to the rock comes a lower sound that seems to originate behind the teeth. It is difficult to remain there for more than a few breaths. Mountaineers who know the place wrap their heads in wool before approaching, although this does little for the vibration conducted through their hands.

There is almost nothing inside to account for it.

A shallow rib of stone rises from the floor. Two thin green strands cling beneath an overhang. Near the entrance stands a small arrangement of worked bone. The rest is water, clear and cold, with occasional grains moving through it too quickly to follow.

The mountain is forming here.

It forms in starts. A patch of water clouds over, usually near the floor, though sometimes halfway up a wall. For an instant the cloud resembles breath on glass. Then its particles align, and a wafer of pale mineral appears with six or seven edges, each sharp enough to divide the sunlight. Other wafers lock against it. A ridge grows by the thickness of a fingernail.

The sound of that growth is a crack large enough to belong to a glacier.

Sometimes the ridge persists. Sometimes, before it has quite become stone, a change in pressure shakes it loose into water again. The floor can acquire a step and lose it in the time it takes to blink. A needle rises, branches once, and disappears from the tip downward. There is no rubble. Failed stone leaves only a momentary distortion, a place where the light bends as if passing through warmer water.

Yet nothing is warm. The cold here is steady, penetrating, just short of ice.

The oldest surface is the ceiling. It has the faint translucence of an unpolished tooth and carries no scratches, sediment, or rounded edges. Small facets meet at exact angles. In places it looks less like a roof than the underside of a crystal still deciding how much room to leave beneath itself.

This decision is what keeps the water moving. Each new projection displaces a little of it. Each vanishing projection makes room. Currents strike one another in the middle of the hollow and flatten into invisible sheets. At the edges of those sheets, the few suspended grains whirl in tight circles, then shoot sideways. There can be three opposed currents across the width of a thumb. A loose object will remain motionless for several seconds before striking a wall with startling force.

The water never becomes frothy. There are no bubbles to soften the view. Everything happens in perfect visibility.

The green strands survive in a crease too narrow for these currents to enter directly. They are each shorter than an eyelash, rooted to a dull patch of older mineral. Their tips sweep back and forth, sometimes together, sometimes in contrary directions. They put out a new cell during the brief pauses between the deepest impacts. Over a season, this may amount to very little growth.

Beneath them is a brownish stain that appears lifeless until the sun reaches it. Then several minute cups open in its surface. They remain open while the beam passes and close when it has gone. Nothing grazes there regularly. The stain has kept the same irregular outline for longer than the people who visit can remember.

There is one animal they occasionally see.

It is translucent, jointed, scarcely longer than a grain of barley, with a dark fork inside its body. It emerges from a hole behind the stone rib and walks rather than swims. Its feet adhere so firmly that the currents flatten its body without carrying it away. It moves during the loudest intervals. When the hollow falls quiet, it folds itself into a seam.

Its shell bears tiny opaque flecks. Seen closely, these are pieces of the floor, incorporated while the floor was still soft. With each molt it leaves behind a nearly invisible cast containing a few grains of mountain.

The worked bone near the entrance is a gate, though it blocks very little. Two pegs have been driven into sockets in the sidewalls. Between them hangs a crosspiece lashed with dark hair, and from the crosspiece descend three short teeth cut from the same bone. The teeth do not reach the floor. Water passes freely around them.

People made this with a stone blade. The cut marks remain visible.

During the worst convulsions, the crosspiece knocks against its pegs. Its small, dry clatter can somehow be distinguished through the surrounding roar. Whoever installed it must have discovered that the bone keeps a narrow region behind the entrance stable. There, mineral grows slowly enough to be removed. Thin flakes are taken away in a folded hide and used lower on the mountain for cutting, piercing, and making the sharpest part of a tool.

No one takes much. Once, a tooth was removed from the gate. By the next visit, the hollow had narrowed so far that the remaining apparatus was embedded in the walls. Months passed before it opened again.

Beside the entrance are eleven shallow notches. Some are fresh and white; others have filled with the same stone into which they were cut. A little ledge beneath them holds a wooden wedge, a frayed length of cord, and nothing else. There is no room for a shrine or shelter. A person working here must lie on one shoulder with both legs braced against the slope.

At noon the sun shines directly through the water’s face. The bone gate casts three wavering shadows. For a little while, every newly forming edge lights up, and the hollow seems crowded with structures: shelves, fins, a flight of minute terraces climbing a wall. Then a single deep impact passes through the peak. Most of them vanish.

The green strands whip flat. The jointed animal comes out of its seam. Behind the gate, a new flake begins to thicken.

Outside, a few drops of ordinary snowmelt tick down the rock. There is blue sky, thin wind, and an immense distance to the valley. Inside the hand-wide opening, the clear water heaves against its boundaries, leaving just enough space for the mountain to continue arriving.
