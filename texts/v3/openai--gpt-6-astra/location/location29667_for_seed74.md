The town occupies a shallow fold in the ground, low enough that the morning cold collects there, but not low enough to keep a stream. Water comes and goes. After rain, a narrow runnel threads the main street for an hour or two, carrying pale grains of pith. By afternoon there is only a dark seam in the paving, and moisture under the thresholds.

From the rim of the fold, you can see almost the whole place: a square, two long workshops, a row of houses, and the blunt green wall of a reservoir. There are perhaps thirty buildings. They stand well apart, with broad courts between them and streets wider than the traffic requires. Nothing rises above three storeys. Beyond the last houses, the ground lifts into a low bank, and the town’s roofs merge with it.

It takes a little time to understand that the ground is living too.

What looks like worn flagstone is a continuous surface of compressed rootwood, divided by old growth seams. In the street it is gray and hard, polished along the shallow wheel tracks. Under eaves it retains a brown nap. Where a doorstep has been cut back, its exposed edge shows layer after layer of pale fibers, some dry and structural, others still carrying sap. There is no clear point where a house stops and its foundations begin.

The buildings are not tangled or lush. Their growth has been trained for so long that it follows the economy of masonry. Walls are thick, corners rounded, windows small. The older houses have a low, settled look, with broad bases and roofs worn into gentle hollows. Rain has followed the same routes down their faces for generations, darkening channels that are now almost ornamental. Every sill has a drip notch. Every outer stair slopes slightly toward the street.

Leaves are mostly overhead, or on surfaces kept for that purpose. Roofs bear a close, dull-green fleece, no higher than cropped grass. The south wall of the larger workshop is covered in flat leaves arranged like overlapping hands. Elsewhere the town presents bark, cork, polished lignin, and tough membranes stretched across frames. It is almost entirely vegetable matter, yet there is little undergrowth. The open courts remain open. A single shoot emerging in the square is conspicuous enough to draw a worker across from the mill.

They kneel, turn it gently toward a joint in the paving, and fasten it there with a wooden peg.

The square is empty for much of the day. Its center has subsided into a dish no deeper than a washbasin, too old and gradual to seem damaged. On damp mornings it holds a thin sheet of water that reflects the windows of the surrounding buildings. Small bubbles rise through it at irregular intervals. Below are the town’s breathing spaces: a network of dry chambers, drainage passages, and root channels, each maintained by growth along one surface and slow decay along another.

The square can be crossed in half a minute. Nevertheless, its emptiness gives it the composure of a much larger civic space. There is a bench, a covered weighing platform, and a clock mounted under a hood of thick bark. The clock has metal hands. When it advances, the mechanism makes a small, distinct click.

Most of the metal in town is inside the workshops.

Through an open door you can see a line shaft turning below the ceiling. Narrow belts descend to presses, cutters, and a machine that draws long, pale fibers through a row of progressively smaller holes. The machinery is old iron, repeatedly repaired. Its feet are sunk into living sockets that thicken around them and absorb the vibration. There is no factory roar. The belts make a soft brushing sound; bearings give a low, steady note. Every few minutes, a press closes with a padded thump.

The power house stands against the reservoir wall. A little electric motor there runs almost continuously, supplied by a cable that disappears through the outer bank. It drives circulation pumps, the line shaft, and the pruning rollers on the roof. Their loads are staggered. When the pumps start, the drawing machine slows; the person tending it lifts a hand from the feed until the belt regains its speed.

The town makes packing cloth, insulation board, and hollow sections of rootwood grown to standard sizes. In one yard, straight stems rise through a set of iron gauges. In another, flat mats of fiber dry on racks beneath a translucent canopy. They scarcely move in the cold air. Finished pieces are stacked with generous gaps between them, each stack raised on small feet that are still alive and must occasionally be trimmed.

Nothing here grows especially fast. What gives the place its activity is the number of things proceeding at once.

A wall slowly closes around a repaired pipe. Roof leaves lift as the cloud cover thins. A drainage flap opens beneath a stair and releases half a cup of water. Inside the reservoir, pumps move moisture from the cool side to the sunlit side so that the outer skin does not split. Workers go between these processes with gauges, knives, grease tins, and little bundles of cuttings. There is always maintenance underway, but rarely a crowd around it.

The air smells cleanly of shaved wood, with a faint fermented sweetness near the drains. It is cool enough to make an iron railing unpleasant to hold. Sunlight reaches the courts for several hours, ordinary white daylight, brightening the walls without warming them much. Under the streets, the root mass preserves the chill of the previous night.

Water is stored out of sight. A cistern may look like a swelling in a wall or a broad pier beside a doorway. Condensation gathers on its skin and is drawn back through fine pores before many drops can fall. The few open pools lie in places where the town has ceased trying to maintain a dry surface: a hollow behind the weighing platform, a disused loading bay, the bottom of a stair that now leads nowhere. Their water is shallow and amber. There are no reeds, no clouds of insects. Once, a small gray bird comes down to drink, leaving six sharp footprints on the damp rim.

Along the northern boundary are the oldest buildings. Some have been opened to the weather and allowed to become terraces. Upper floors have sagged into the rooms beneath them; partition walls have thickened into buttresses. You can still find a door latch embedded sideways in what is now a low garden wall. The town does not clear these places all at once. Usable fibers are taken away, drainage is redirected, and whatever remains is encouraged to settle.

The outer bank is made partly of such settlements. It encloses the town without looking like a fortification: a long, smooth rise of weathered bark, furred on top with small leaves. Narrow paths cross it where generations of feet have kept the growth down. From there the streets below look pale and spacious, and the workshops seem almost idle.

Then a rack begins to travel out from under its canopy. A roof section tilts to catch the last sun. Someone opens a sluice the width of a hand, and water runs briefly beneath the square.

At evening the leaves on the roofs draw closer together. Lamps come on behind the thin window membranes, turning them the color of warm paper. The machinery continues, more slowly now. In the cooling street, the living paving contracts just enough to open its oldest seams, and the moisture hidden beneath it gives off a faint, dark smell.
