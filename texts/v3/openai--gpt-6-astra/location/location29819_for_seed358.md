The land lies below the river.

From the top of the retaining bank, you can see the difference: brown water passing high on one side, almost level with the roofs of the pumping houses, and on the other a basin of wet ground extending several miles into haze. The basin was excavated in sections. Now it is being filled in sections, though not with anything you could yet walk across. River silt comes through broad pipes and settles into shallow water. What looks from above like land is mostly a skin.

The descending service road has a dry center no wider than a person’s shoulders. Its edges darken, soften, and disappear under water without a definite shoreline. Halfway down, the air becomes warmer. At the bottom it smells of wet iron, disturbed mud, and the faint saltiness of an estuary too far downstream to see.

There is very little empty space.

Water occupies the wheel ruts. Snails occupy the water. Small fish crowd the narrow overflow between one settling bed and the next, holding their heads into a current so slight that a floating feather scarcely moves. Under each submerged board are shrimp, pale worms, and crabs packed joint against joint. The mud itself is punctured with breathing holes. When a footstep shakes the road, the nearest holes close in a sequence too quick to follow.

Beyond the road, the basin is divided by low clay ribs. Some were pushed up by machinery; others are forming where the slurry loses speed. They make a broad, unfinished pattern of pools and pale tongues of sediment. Nothing has had time to acquire a weathered face. The banks are blunt. The little channels have not yet chosen permanent courses. Water seeps through a ridge, undermines a handspan of its edge, and spreads into a neighboring hollow. A place that was a pool last week is now a glossy flat. A place marked as solid ground on the survey board has begun to tremble under its own weight.

The pumps can be heard everywhere.

Close to a pumping house, the sound is too large to separate into parts: motor whine, the hammer of a loose casing, water dragging stones through a pipe. Farther out it becomes a low mechanical weight beneath the other noises. Frogs call from almost every margin. Their voices overlap until they resemble a single rough vibration rising from the ground. Birds stand in dense lines along the clay ribs, screaming over disputes measured in inches. On a sheet-metal roof, hundreds of webbed feet make an irregular drumming.

Conversation is conducted beside someone’s ear. Even then, the machinery takes away pieces of it.

Yet very little seems to be happening.

A discharge pipe runs steadily into one end of a settling bed. The incoming water is thick and yellowish, with a smooth, bulging surface. Fifty yards away it has become brown. At the far end it is clear enough to show the backs of fish. Sediment accumulates too slowly to watch. A painted stake records the change: yesterday’s wet mark, last month’s crust, the height the ground is eventually supposed to reach.

There are hundreds of these stakes. Seen through the haze, they give the flats the appearance of an enormous, sparsely planted field.

Actual plants are uncommon. A few reeds have established themselves beside the older banks, their lower stems coated in dried slurry. Tough grass grows on heaps of broken brick around the buildings. Elsewhere the vegetation consists of thin algal films, sometimes green, sometimes almost black, lying so close to the mud that they seem more like a finish applied to it. Too much is still being buried. Too much of the soil remains fluid. Seeds wash in constantly and settle among feathers, fish scales, and fragments of shell, but only a few find a surface that stays where it is.

The animals need less permanence.

Wading birds follow the fresh discharge, stepping delicately through material that would swallow a boot. They work close together, stabbing and lifting, with brief quarrels when their feeding paths intersect. Egrets stand beside the darker birds like scraps of clean cloth. Ducks gather in the older pools. Water rats travel along the pipe supports, using the same polished routes beneath the flanges. At dusk, bats come down from the retaining bank and skim through the insects above the warm water.

There are insects even in the loudest, most exposed places. Midges form low pillars beside the survey stakes. Flies settle on the sheltered side of a motor housing. Mosquito larvae hang in puddles caught in the folded tops of discarded sacks. Every small difference in height or shelter has its occupants.

The buildings are few and widely spaced. A pumping house on concrete legs. A switch shed with a rusted fan turning behind wire mesh. A flat-roofed hut where workers leave their boots upside down on poles. Between them run power lines and elevated pipes, crossing expanses that have no paths.

The structures look provisional, but they are better founded than anything around them. Their piles go down through the new sediment into older ground. Around each pile, the forming landscape leaves a dark wet collar. In places the mud has risen almost to the underside of a maintenance platform. In others it has compacted and fallen away, exposing another foot of stained concrete.

Light enters the basin reluctantly. The sky is low with rain cloud, and the high banks hide the brighter edges of the day. Water reflects a pewter color. Yellow lamps remain lit over the switches well into morning, making small, blurred doubles in the puddles below. When rain comes, it is warm enough that the workers do not immediately put on their coats. It strikes the pools, softens the newest ridges, and makes the whole basin seem briefly to fizz.

Afterward, droplets continue falling from the pipes for hours.

Near the middle is an older settling bed where pumping stopped several seasons ago. It is only a few inches higher than its neighbors. Its surface bears fine cracks, tiny shell deposits, and the first faint branching marks of runoff. Here the mud can hold a bird without closing around its feet. Here, in a sheltered corner, a small patch of reeds has survived two rounds of flooding.

From that slight rise, you can look across the basin and see its different ages: open slurry near the outlets, trembling flats farther away, firmer ground under the birds. The machinery roars without interruption. Water shines between every ridge. Under it, almost imperceptibly, the bottom is coming up.
