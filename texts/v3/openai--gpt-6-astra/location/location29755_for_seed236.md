The place occupies the broken crown of a mountain: a shallow enclosure, no wider than a village square, lifted far above the dust plains. Its outer walls are new stone. They rise in pale, straight fractures, with corners sharp enough to catch a loose strand of fur. Nothing has yet rounded them. Here and there, a slab has split so recently that the inside is still a different color from the surface—a faint pink beneath the sun-yellow crust.

There is no stream, no pool, no dark seam where moisture collects. Even the deepest shade is dry. The warmth comes partly from the sun and partly from below; lie against the ground and you feel the mountain holding a second, steadier afternoon inside itself.

Yet the enclosure is thick with growth.

Broad, overlapping fans push out of the fissures. Their upper surfaces are dull green, their undersides the color of old copper. They do not flutter. Each is as stiff as a thin tile, and where several plants meet, their fans fit over and under one another, building roofs. Beneath them rise pale stems, jointed and hollow, crowded close enough that the spaces between become passages. Smaller growths fill the low places: rust-colored cushions, ivory knobs, tight coils of something like fern tips that take years to uncurl.

The green is not soft. A hand laid on it would find warmth, a fine grain, and a slight give, like thick leather stretched over ribs. A broken edge does not bleed. It shows a waxy, amber interior that seals itself over several days. These plants keep almost everything they take in. Their buried threads enter the newly opened rock, feeding along bands of sulfur and iron, drawing on compounds exposed by the mountain’s recent splitting. The least trace of vapor is caught under the fans at night and passed inward before morning can reclaim it.

From the rim, the growth looks like a compact town.

There are districts of tall stems and low districts roofed almost to ground level. Openings recur at similar heights. Narrow routes bend around the bases of the oldest plants, widening at intersections into little courts of bare mineral. Along one wall, shelves of growth step upward in five irregular tiers, each sheltering the one below. On the opposite side, a row of swollen trunks has grown together, leaving oval doorways through which the warmer air passes.

No one laid out these routes. A stem thickens where it has room; a root withdraws from a place that is repeatedly trodden; neighboring fans overlap until one shades the other into a new direction. Over long intervals, use becomes architecture. The whole enclosure has arrived at the crowded practicality of a settlement without anyone ever drawing a line.

It is inhabited at nearly every height.

Small animals cling beneath the roofs, flat-bodied and pale, each with six short limbs folded into grooves along its sides. Their backs carry patches of the same green as the plants. At first they seem to be part of the ceiling. Then one turns its head, or draws a foot slowly out of its socket of shadow.

On the ground are broad, blunt creatures with mineral-colored shells. They move along the passages in single file, not because they follow one another but because there is scarcely room to pass. A journey from one end of a court to the other may occupy most of the morning. They stop at the copper undersides of fallen fans and rasp away films too thin to see.

Finer lives occupy the vertical surfaces. Little white bodies, like grains of rice with dark seams, assemble around the stem joints. A few slip into the hollow stems. Others remain motionless for so long that fresh tissue begins to grow around their feet. There are winged animals too, though flight here is usually a brief release: a small, gray shape lets go from an upper shelf, opens four translucent vanes, and settles somewhere lower.

Despite all this, the place is almost silent.

There are no calls, no chorus hidden in the foliage. The animals find one another through warmth and scent, through contact, through movements conducted along the stems. What looks like stillness is full of messages passing too slowly and too quietly for a visitor to notice. A small creature presses its underside to a roof rib. Several feet away, another lifts its head.

Occasionally a shell touches stone with a dry tick. Once in an afternoon, a fan may ease against its neighbor and produce a sound like a page being carefully turned. The wind is audible only beyond the wall. Inside, the dense roofs divide it into currents too small to whistle.

Light enters plainly. There is no perpetual dusk, no colored haze. At noon the upper fans are bright green, the stone nearly white, and the open courts hold hard-edged patches of sun. Lower down, light arrives through narrow slots and seams. It catches a copper underside, then a pale animal, then the amber seal on a damaged stem. A creature standing in one of these shafts casts a surprisingly crisp little shadow.

The oldest route leads to the center, where a single fracture runs across the floor.

It is only a hand’s breadth wide. Its edges match exactly, tooth to tooth, as though the rock might be pressed shut again. Warm air rises from it, carrying a dry metallic smell. Around this opening the vegetation is tallest and most densely layered. Stems lean inward, but none quite crosses. Their roots descend along either side into depths hidden from the sun.

Here the inhabitants gather without crowding into a heap. Each species keeps a particular height or distance from the crack. The flat animals occupy the lowest undersides; the shelled grazers rest along the outer margin; the white grains fill the joints above. At the warmest point, where the fracture narrows beneath an overhang, several translucent eggs adhere directly to the stone. Nothing sits over them. The mountain does the warming.

There is very little loose soil. Most of the ground remains exposed rock, with a soft accumulation only in the sheltered corners: shed membranes, powdered shell, the dark residue of old roots. Even the dead are slow to come apart. A fallen fan may form a bridge for decades, its surface gradually polished by feet. When it finally breaks, the passage beneath it becomes brighter, and the small growths there begin their patient adjustment. One court closes; another opens. A doorway narrows until only the youngest animals fit through.

Beyond the rim, the world is much larger and almost empty. Dry slopes drop away in long, unbroken planes. Far below, dust lies in the hollows like a surface that has forgotten how to move. There are other peaks, but from inside the enclosure they are visible only through a few gaps in the wall.

Most of the inhabitants never approach those gaps. Their lives take place among warm ribs, overlapping roofs, mineral courts: distances measured in stems rather than mountains. At the western edge, however, one pale shelled animal rests with its forefeet on the newly broken lip. Sunlight falls on its back. It stays there while the shadow of the wall lengthens across the entire settlement.

By evening it has moved no farther than the width of its own body. Behind it, deep in the growth, a new fan is opening. Its two halves have separated enough to admit a thread of light, and a little of the untouched copper beneath is showing.
