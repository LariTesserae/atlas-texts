The hollow lies just behind the shore bank, low enough that water rises through its floor before rain reaches it. It is not quite a pond. There are too many surfaces inside it: shelves of gray clay, submerged brickwork, iron plates furred with pale mineral deposits, narrow islands whose edges darken and sag while you watch. From the bank, the whole place could be mistaken for an abandoned works yard after a wet week. You can see the opposite boundary, a bowed concrete wall, and beyond it the upper windows of three houses.

A flight of seven steps goes down into the hollow. The bottom two are underwater. On the fifth, someone has cut a drainage groove that now carries water toward the bank rather than away from it.

The daylight is ordinary and sufficient. Clouds pass, and the details remain clear: a washer on a ledge, a feather stuck to a valve casing, tiny bubbles collected beneath the lip of a broken tile. Nothing requires darkness here. Even at noon, when a little sun enters the hollow, the wet surfaces keep their cool color. Your hands become cold if you rest them on the rail.

There was a pumping installation here once. Some of it still works.

A small brick house stands against the eastern wall, its roof patched with two different gauges of corrugated metal. Inside are a motor, a pressure vessel, and a control cabinet with a green indicator lamp. A cable leaves through the wall and disappears into the shore bank. The motor turns almost without sound, transmitting a faint tremor through its concrete footing. It does not run continuously. Sometimes it stops in the middle of a rising flood; sometimes it starts when the yard appears nearly dry.

There is no posted explanation. The old instructions inside the cabinet door concern lubrication, frost, and the order in which to close three valves. Only two valves remain.

Outside, the pipes are much more numerous than the building seems to require. They lie across one another in the mud, descend into square wells, emerge farther away with different-colored paint. Broad flanges nearly touch the wall. Thin service lines occupy the gaps beneath them. Between the pipes are settling trays, slabs, short lengths of rail, and low masonry partitions rounded by decades of immersion. Everything has been fitted around something older.

This is what makes the hollow feel crowded despite the lack of growth. There is hardly a clear square yard. Looking down through shallow water, you find another arrangement beneath the first: a line of bolt heads, the mouth of a drain, a grid of small chambers filled with different shades of silt. Under a cracked slab, more water moves in the opposite direction.

The plants keep to a few reliable places. A tuft of coarse grass grows where a downpipe has split. Small leaves cling to the mortar beneath the steps. On the highest island, six or seven rushes lean together, their roots enclosed in a lump of clay scarcely wider than a bucket. Most of the ground is too frequently disturbed for anything to establish itself. A seed might settle in a promising patch of grit at breakfast and be buried under smooth gray sediment by noon.

Yet the place does not look newly damaged. Its shapes have the assurance of long use. Every exposed brick corner has softened. The clay banks have worn into overlapping lobes, each with a small vertical face and a shallow apron below it. The iron is thinnest along certain waterlines, thick and dark above them. Even collapses seem to have happened many times in the same places. Beneath a crumbling edge there is already a hollow shaped to receive it.

Stand still for a few minutes and the floor begins to change.

A pale patch in the water widens. Fine sediment is rising from below, spreading beneath the surface without disturbing its reflection. Nearby, a little channel runs brown, then clear, then empties so abruptly that the pebbles at its bottom shine in air. Water wells up beside a pipe and divides around its bolts. One stream enters a crack. Another crosses a patch of mud, cutting three channels, abandoning two, deepening the third.

At the same time, the western pools are draining.

There is no single water level to consult. Two adjacent compartments may stand a handspan apart in height, divided only by a low ridge of packed silt. The ridge trembles, sheds a thin sheet from its face, and gives way. Water slips through. For several seconds the breach is busy with curling sediment and small, fast eddies. Then both pools sink, leaving a wet saddle between them.

Almost none of this is loud. The water is shallow, the drops are short, and there is little loose stone. Mud folds into mud. A submerged plate shifts with a soft knock. Air comes up through a bolt hole in three round pulses. The loudest sound may be your coat touching the handrail.

Once, from under the brick house, comes a brief metallic ticking, like a cooling radiator. A whole row of puddles dimples in response.

The shore is close, but you cannot see it from the bottom of the steps. You can smell salt when the wind crosses the bank. At other times the hollow smells of wet iron and the cool, faintly sweet interiors of old drains. The sea presumably supplies some of the pressure, though the changes here do not follow the tide in any simple way. In one inspection well, water begins rising several minutes before it rises in the inlet outside. A pencil mark inside the pump house records this. Beneath it are three later measurements, each confirming roughly the same interval.

The mark has not been turned into a warning. It is simply there among dates of bearing replacements and a note about the roof.

There are a few animals. A small shorebird sometimes walks the concrete rim, waiting for the channels to empty. It descends only after they have done so, taking something too small to identify from the exposed silt. Under a piece of roofing felt live pale, many-legged creatures that scatter slowly in the cold. No fish are visible. Now and then a pool shows a quick disturbance that might be life, but the ground makes so many movements of its own that this is difficult to decide.

The safest way across is along the old foundations. They form no obvious path until you notice the polished places where other feet have landed. A heel-width strip beside a settling tray; the rounded top of a partition; three embedded blocks leading to the pump-house door. The open mud is not necessarily deep, but it is unreliable. Firmness passes through it in patches. A footprint can remain sharply defined while the ground on either side liquefies.

By late afternoon, the rush island has lost part of its southern edge. The fallen clay is already softening into the next basin. Behind it, water has withdrawn from a curved iron cover you could not see earlier. Its lifting ring stands upright, clean on one side.

You might expect the hollow eventually to fill, or drain, or break through the shore bank. But the wall bears old stains at much the same heights as today’s, and the brick house has survived with only a slight lean. There is room here for an extraordinary amount of movement without much alteration to the whole.

When you climb back up, you can take in its boundaries again: the wall, the bank, the patched roof, the seven steps. Below, a narrow tongue of clear water advances across a tray of black silt. It reaches a rusted fastener, separates around it, and begins lifting the sediment grain by grain. A few feet away, another channel closes. The surface smooths over so completely that, without having watched, you would not know where it had been.
