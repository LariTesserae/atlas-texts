The floor is pale enough to hold the daylight.

It lies forty metres down, a plain of cast concrete extending beyond the distance at which water allows anything to be seen. Near at hand there are seams, bolt sockets, a dropped length of cable. Farther away, these disappear and the floor becomes a blue-white slope, though it is almost level. Farther still, there is only blue. A fish crossing that last visible distance loses its markings, then its outline, then the slight disturbance its body makes in the light.

Nothing here is bedrock. Even the grit collecting in the joints has come from somewhere else: crushed shell, rust, aggregate shed by new concrete, sand carried through the intake gates. Beneath the floor are drainage galleries and inspection passages. Beneath those are piled foundations. The water presses on a construction all the way down.

The basin occupies what was once a shallow reach of sea. Its enclosing works are too far apart to belong to the same view. You can spend days following the bottom without encountering a wall, moving through administrative sectors whose differences are marked only by numbers stencilled on small enamel plates. At intervals a steel mast rises toward the bright surface. Most carry a lamp, a current meter, and a ladder intended for use when their compartment is drained. Underwater, the ladders seem extravagantly long.

Between them there is almost nothing.

This emptiness is not stillness. Water moves steadily across the plain, cold against the face and stronger a few metres above the bottom. The operating current carries suspended particles in long, level paths. Now and then a pump station changes its load, and all the particles turn together.

The pump stations stand in trenches.

You first find one by its sound: a low mechanical throb felt through the jaw, overlaid with a higher, uneven rattling. In water the noise gives little useful direction. It seems to come from the whole floor. Only when the trench opens ahead does the source become clear.

Its edges are sharp. The chamfers still show the smooth imprint of their moulds, and the yellow paint on the corner guards has scarcely faded. Three enormous intake mouths occupy the trench wall. Their screens are divided into rectangular panels, each panel large enough to pass a truck if it were lifted out. Behind them the impellers cannot be seen. Their presence is expressed by the pull of the water, by weed bent flat along the bars, and by the continuous trembling of anything loose.

A cleaning rake travels down one screen. Its teeth lift plastic film, frayed rope, a dead ray, a compact brown wad of kelp. For a moment the debris billows around the mechanism. Then the rake rises into its guideway, carrying the whole untidy collection toward machinery above the surface.

Fish hold in the current just beyond the dangerous draw. They face the screens, tails beating, eating what the disturbed water brings them.

There are many fish in the basin, but they do not fill it evenly. Whole hectares seem vacant until a school arrives. Then the water acquires a moving grain: thousands of small silver bodies turning along the same invisible boundary. They pass over the joints in the floor like a low cloud crossing a road. Behind them come larger fish, widely spaced, their movements less synchronized. Occasionally one accelerates and the school dents around it.

Afterward the plain is empty again.

Most life attaches itself to an interruption. Mussels crowd the underside of a bracket. Anemones occupy the sheltered recess of a lifting eye. A gasket projecting from a panel joint supports a thin beard of green algae. Along some seams, where a little sediment has accumulated, narrow eelgrass blades have rooted and spread into strips no wider than a hand. Their runners encounter clean concrete and stop.

There are no broad meadows yet. The water is cool, the current persistent, the surfaces young. Much of the available light falls on bare material.

On clear days, sunlight reaches the bottom in moving nets. A passing surface wave gathers it into brilliant bands that run up a mast, break over a valve housing, and slide onward across the slab. The concrete is warm grey rather than truly white. Fresh steel is almost black. Galvanized handrails shine faintly through their first skin of slime.

Clouds pass over the basin, and their shadows are immense. Under one, the visible world contracts. Details remain close by, but the distant floor withdraws into blue-grey water. When the cloud moves on, the plain seems to open again.

The basin is broad enough for wind to build a substantial sea inside its perimeter. Storms begin overhead as a disorder in the light. The bright surface roughens; wave troughs deepen into travelling green hollows. Lower down, the first sign is the movement of loose cable ends. Then silt lifts from the seams. Long strips of the floor vanish beneath low, running clouds of sediment.

The pumps continue working through this weather. Their steady flow meets the back-and-forth surge of the waves, producing confused currents around anything that stands above the bottom. A service gantry sheds vortices from its legs. On one side of a partition, grit scours the floor clean; on the other, it accumulates in a soft fan. During a long storm, shell fragments can travel several kilometres before settling.

The landscape is beginning to acquire wear, but has not yet lost the certainty of its construction. Corners remain corners. The slopes of the overflow channels are uniform. Expansion joints run straight to the limit of sight. Where a plate has buckled, its deformation looks like damage rather than terrain.

Some sectors have been flooded only recently. Their floors retain chalk marks and boot prints sealed beneath the water. Bundles of spare fasteners lie in corners where workers left them. Fish inspect these places cautiously at first, then return because the new obstructions offer shelter. Within weeks, translucent growth softens the bolts. Small shrimp take up residence beneath washers. The first patches of rust appear as delicate orange runs.

Older sectors are recognizable by their scattered heaps of shell. Crabs carry mussels away from the masts and break them open on the plain. Rays settle into sediment gathered behind low curbs. Flatfish lie on concrete dust and change colour badly enough to remain visible. There is food here, and shelter, but not always the kind their bodies expect.

Far across the basin, a maintenance vessel starts its engines. The sound arrives before the vessel can be seen: a coarse, rising vibration, then the regular percussion of a propeller. Its shadow eventually crosses the floor, accompanied by the shadow of a suspended work cage. Two divers descend beside a guide wire. Their bubbles climb through the water in widening, bright columns.

They are replacing a sensor on a mast.

Around this small task, the basin continues at its own scale. Water enters through distant gates and leaves through others. A pressure change opens a bank of weighted flaps. A travelling screen clears another load of debris. Fish gather in the lee of the maintenance vessel and scatter when a tool strikes metal.

The strike carries an astonishing distance.

So do the other sounds: chains working against guides, a loose panel clapping during a surge, the brief hammering of a pump drawing air. There is no quiet foreground with noise somewhere beyond it. The water holds the machinery close, even when its nearest moving part is kilometres away.

At evening, the floor darkens slowly. The surface remains luminous long after the seams below have vanished. Lamps come on along the service routes, illuminating widely separated circles of concrete. Small fish gather at the edges of those circles. Predators wait just outside them.

Between the lamps are long reaches of open water, cold and occupied mostly by current. The next mast is a dim vertical line. Beyond it, another cannot yet be seen. The pale floor continues underneath, newly made, already collecting shells.
