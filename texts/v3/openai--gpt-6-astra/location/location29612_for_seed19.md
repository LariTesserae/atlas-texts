The height is apparent first in the lightness of everything that casts a shadow.

Stones lie loosely on the pale ground, yet their shadows seem pinned beneath them. A broken piece of shell could weigh almost nothing here. The air is cool and thin, without the sharpness of approaching frost, and the sun has the ordinary brightness of late morning. There is no mist to soften the distances. Across a basin several miles wide, you can distinguish the individual knobs on a ridge, then realize that each knob is an animal.

This is the top of a very old elevation. Whatever raised it did so before the rocks acquired their present surfaces, before grain and fracture and bedding had been rubbed down into the same mild texture. There are no peaks left. The whole country consists of broad, shallow dishes, rounded shoulders, and passages between low stone swells. Only at the outer edges does its altitude become unmistakable. There, the land ends in drops so extensive that the lower portions cannot be seen—not because of cloud or darkness, but because perspective ceases to provide them. The eye follows the wall downward and eventually finds itself looking across the plateau again.

A few trails reach these edges. None has a turning place.

The ground is a close mosaic of gray mineral plates, ivory grit, and patches of something brown that resembles old leather. It is dry all the way through. Broken stones disclose no darker heart; hollows hold neither damp nor salt bloom. There are depressions shaped like pools, with gently shelving margins and smooth bottoms, but even the idea of filling them feels mistaken. Their emptiness has the completeness of a solid material.

Almost everywhere, there are animals.

They occupy the hollows shoulder to shoulder. They stand along the shallow ridges in such numbers that the ridges look fringed. Small ones fill the intervals beneath larger ones, and smaller ones still are visible in the spaces between toes, under the rims of shells, along the undersides of bellies. At first this seems like a congregation—a migration arrested at a pass, perhaps, or a gathering around some resource too subtle to notice. But the distribution has no center. Farther away there are simply more bodies, fitted to another mile of the same terrain.

Some are recognizable enough to make the rest difficult. There are low, thick-necked grazers with flattened horns and coats the color of ash. There are long-legged creatures with bare knees, small heads, and ears held horizontally. Pale beetles lie packed into seams in the rock. A branching antler rises out of a mass of backs, carrying a dozen little birds on its worn tines.

Others require longer looking. A shelled animal has its opening on top, and within it stands a smaller animal whose feet cannot be seen. Something like a hare possesses the same foreleg twice, both versions resting comfortably on the ground. Along one ridge, a row of narrow heads seems to belong to a single body lying beneath the stone.

None of this has the strained quality of a deformity. Each creature is well accommodated. There is room for its weight, its limbs, its particular arrangement of attention.

They are alive. That is the most immediate and least explicable fact of the place.

Their eyes are clear. The flesh at a nostril has the softness of living flesh. Fur does not shed, shells do not flake, and the fine membranes between certain toes retain a faint translucence. Yet no flank rises. No jaw works. A bird holds one foot curled against its breast, not in the precarious instant before setting it down, but as a complete way of standing.

There is no next movement waiting inside these bodies.

The quiet is correspondingly deep. Not the quiet of animals hiding from an intruder, and not the silence after a disturbance. There is a faint, close sound like the contact of two dry seeds. It has no identifiable source. Listen beside a horn, a stone, or your own sleeve, and it seems equally near. Although it resembles a succession of tiny clicks, you cannot isolate one click from another. It is simply the texture the silence has here.

Vegetation survives in narrow allotments. A gray rosette occupies a thumb-wide gap between mineral plates. A thorn bush spreads flat beneath the belly of a tall animal, each branch following the space available between its legs. On sheltered slopes grow sparse stems with dark, thickened tips, more like blunt awls than leaves.

Nothing reaches eagerly toward the light. Roots enter the stone through holes precisely their own diameter. There is no loose soil around them and no evidence of roots having forced anything apart. One small tree, scarcely higher than a person, grows sideways from a rounded outcrop. Its six branches support six dusty leaves. Beneath it lie no fallen leaves at all.

The grazers stand among these plants with their mouths closed.

Scattered through the basin are signs of hands.

A low shelter has been made by leaning three slabs against a fourth. Its workmanship is plain and competent: stone wedges, a lintel chosen for its natural flatness, a threshold rubbed shallow at the middle. Within lies a mat woven from split stalks. Beside it are a bone needle, a stone scraper, and a bowl whose rim has been repaired with cord threaded through drilled holes.

The bowl is empty and clean. The cord is neither fresh nor rotten.

Elsewhere, little walls cross the ground for ten or twenty paces and stop. Some enclose nothing. Some pass beneath animals that stand astride them. On a saddle between two basins, a line of upright stones marks a route broad enough for a single walker. Their upper surfaces have been worn level, though there is no prevailing wind and nothing blows across the ground.

Age here is not a quantity of damage. It is the thoroughness with which everything has found its shape.

Even the animals have that quality. The horn tips are rounded; the pads of feet spread into shallow sockets; the backs of old, low creatures repeat the contours of the hills. You might mistake them for formations if not for the eyes. You might mistake the hills for sleeping animals if the distinction continued to matter.

From the highest shoulder, the country opens into three adjoining basins. Each is densely occupied, pale backs and dark shells laid out in innumerable close groupings. Between them run narrow corridors of bare stone. The corridors are continuous, though you cannot work out how: one passes between two touching bodies; another disappears beneath a curled tail and resumes several yards away without any intervening space.

There is always somewhere to put a foot.

This courtesy makes the landscape stranger than an obstruction would. It admits a traveler without rearranging itself. Every passage is already present. At a constriction, the room you need proves to have been concealed by the angle of approach, even where no change of angle should have helped.

Near the far edge stands a solitary post of dark wood, fixed in a cairn. A strip of hide is tied near its top. It hangs in a shallow curve, its free end lifted slightly outward.

Beyond it is the enormous descent, the inaccessible lower world, the light continuing without haze into distances that do not resolve.

Turn back, and the nearest animal is looking at you. So is the next. Far across the basin, among thousands of clear, motionless eyes, there is no visible adjustment of a head.

Nothing has turned to watch you.

There was a place for your arrival, too.
