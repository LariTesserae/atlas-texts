Behind the last bank of cooling fans, high on the roof of the plant, there is a space too small to have been given a name. Two steps down from a service hatch, it opens between a duct casing and the concrete wall of a water tank: a narrow, roofless court, scarcely wider than a room. You would not find it by looking up from the ground. Even from the maintenance walkway, it is hidden by the folded lip of a galvanized windscreen.

Everything underfoot has been poured, pressed, welded, or bolted into place.

The floor slopes in four shallow planes toward a drain. Their intersections make a little watershed, exact and almost new. There are no softened edges yet. The concrete still carries the fine grain of its formwork; the sealant in the expansion joints is black and slightly glossy. Along one wall, stainless-steel flashing rises in a series of abrupt steps, each holding a thin ledge of water. A discarded spacer, no larger than a matchbox, divides one of the flowing sheets. Downstream of it, two clear currents reunite over a ridge of grit.

The grit is mostly from construction: pale drilling dust, a few grains of aggregate, tiny curls of metal whose edges have begun to turn orange.

Water arrives from several directions. Mist drifts down from the cooling cells overhead. Condensation gathers on a cold supply pipe and drops from the lowest bolt of its support bracket. When a valve opens inside the casing, a short discharge runs through a side channel, crosses the floor, and fills the drain faster than it can empty. For a few seconds the whole court becomes a shallow pool. The bolt heads on the floor disappear, one after another. Then the valve closes and they emerge again, each wearing a bright ring.

The water is cool enough to numb fingertips. The air is cooler. At this height the wind brings little warmth from the buildings below, and the wet steel takes what warmth it can from a hand or knee.

Beyond a slot in the windscreen, there is occasionally a piece of distant mountain: dark rock, a line of old snow, nothing close enough to judge distance by. More often the slot is white with cloud. The court seems to hang inside that whiteness, attached to the world chiefly by the vibration in its floor.

The noise is enormous.

There is no quiet corner behind the duct. The fans force a continuous, heavy roar around it, while the concrete returns a lower sound from machinery deeper in the building. A narrow whistle comes and goes at the edge of a loose panel. Water striking metal ought to make a distinct racket, but here it is visible rather than audible. You can watch a stream hammer a hollow cover plate and hear nothing separable from the surrounding force.

A dropped tool would be noticed by its movement. Speech would have to be delivered close to an ear.

Yet the noise has changes. After a while the body learns them: the slight easing when one fan slows, the pressure against the chest when another takes its load, the brief roughness that travels through the rail before the discharge begins. The place has a schedule, but it is the schedule of demand elsewhere. Somewhere below, a controller asks for more cooling, and weather happens in this little court.

The mist thickens. A warm breath escapes a louver. Droplets accelerate sideways. Then the draft turns cold again.

There is very little growing. On the sheltered underside of the drain rim, a green film follows the reach of the water with exquisite accuracy. A thumb-sized patch of moss occupies a recess where the installers left a broken piece of packing felt. Three grass blades lean out of a seam beside the hatch. Their roots have found no soil, only accumulated dust and the damp fibers of something once used to wipe a joint.

The grass is pale at its base, greener where it bends toward the opening. Its longest blade has a sharp crease from being shut in the door.

Nothing moves here under its own small, animal power. No wings pass between the walls. No insect circles the maintenance light. The light itself burns behind a ribbed cover over the hatch, yellow and insufficient, giving the wet floor a short, trembling reflection. Most of the illumination comes from the cloud above: a dim silver that reaches the upper flashing and weakens before it gets to the drain.

There is plenty of empty surface. A pipe bracket, the spacer, a single washer in a corner—each stands apart, easy to keep finding with the eye. Even the stains are young. Rust has traveled only a finger’s length below a cut edge. Mineral deposits are no more than a cloudy outline around yesterday’s puddle. On one panel, a strip of blue protective film remains caught beneath a screw, fluttering too rapidly for its individual movements to be seen.

For now, the fabricated geometry still controls everything. Water turns where the floor tells it to turn. It falls from a machined edge in evenly spaced threads. The moss stops at the boundary of the sheltered recess.

But one thread has begun to run backward beneath the flashing. It clings to the underside, passes a screw, and drops into a corner intended to stay dry. Each time the mist returns, that corner darkens a little sooner.
