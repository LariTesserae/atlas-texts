The city begins well below the place where you first see it.

From the western approach, the road runs across warm, bare upland, between shallow pools and outcrops of gray stone. Water stands in the wheel ruts. It beads on the guardrails and seeps from horizontal seams in the rock, though there has been no rain since morning. The land appears almost level until you come to a parapet and discover that the next several miles are roofs.

Beyond them are more roofs, lower and paler in the wet air, then a long escarpment carrying windows, outfall pipes, and the dark mouths of tunnels. Another district occupies its foot. Beyond that, another escarpment. The city descends through the plateau in broad, irregular steps, following valleys so old that their original rivers have become difficult to distinguish from the municipal drainage system.

There is no skyline in the usual sense. Distance swallows the lower districts before the buildings end. Far to the south, a bank of cloud rests on a quarter of the city that has its own afternoon rain.

Above it all, the sky is ordinary: bright, lightly overcast, with enough sun to put shadows beneath balconies. The altitude reveals itself in the deep blue of clear patches, in the hard brilliance of distant metal, and in how often a newcomer must pause on a staircase. The warmth comes from below.

***

The plateau is made of successive lava flows, weathered for so long that even its broken places have lost their sharpness. Basalt columns stand exposed along the terrace edges, their joints widened by centuries of seepage. Loose stones have accumulated in stable slopes beneath them. Some are as large as houses and have houses built against them; others have been fitted into retaining walls without much alteration.

Almost everything is rounded where people can reach it. Stair treads sag in the middle. Canal copings are smooth enough to sit on comfortably. The brickwork near public taps has been rubbed into shallow hollows by buckets and shoulders. Older buildings lean slightly into one another, their movement arrested generations ago by iron ties whose anchor plates have become part of the street ornament.

Nothing here looks newly ruined. A collapsed warehouse may have served as a covered fish market for two hundred years. Its surviving arches carry pipes installed in several different periods, all still in use.

Water is frequent rather than deep. It lies in courtyard basins, runs along gutters, beads beneath bridges, and gathers behind the lips of mineral shelves. There are broad reservoirs farther inland, but most of the visible water is only ankle-deep. You can see pale sediment on the bottom and the trails of small animals crossing it.

Hot springs feed the upper channels. By the time their water reaches the inhabited terraces, it is warm enough to wash in. Some branches pass through mills and heat exchangers; others are diverted into long, low cultivation halls. Cooler water arrives through tunnels from the wetter eastern heights. The two systems meet in mixing basins, where a worker can spend an entire shift turning a valve by fractions.

The city smells of damp masonry, warm iron, salt, and animals. Near certain drains there is a sulfurous trace. Near the processing halls, something richer: broth, wet shells, the fatty smell of a crowded rookery.

There is no smell of leaves.

***

The absence of plants is most noticeable in the small places.

No grass grows between paving stones. No weed takes hold in the broken gutter of an abandoned house. A wall left untouched becomes streaked with mineral deposits, then pitted, then perhaps occupied by a colony of shell-bearing grazers; it never turns green.

Courtyards have shade roofs rather than trees. Their central features are usually water basins or large stones worn glossy by use. In prosperous districts, chains hang from the eaves to guide rain into tiled channels. The sound is pleasant, and the water is kept away from the foundations.

Life begins in the warm conduits.

Pale microbial films coat submerged stone where mineral-rich groundwater meets air. They are cream, rust-red, faintly lilac; in some channels they form thick, folded skins. The city cultivates them on stacked ceramic plates in humid galleries built into the rock. Water passes slowly over the plates. Air is drawn through by enormous fans that turn too gradually for their blades to blur.

Every few weeks, a rack is lifted and scraped.

These galleries feed much of the plateau. Their harvest goes into fermentation vats, communal kitchens, and the long feeding troughs of the animal houses. More escapes than the engineers once intended. Beneath every leaking joint and downstream of every settling tank, smaller versions of the same ecology establish themselves.

The abundance increases as you descend.

At the uppermost terraces there are only a few birds on the parapets and dark beetle-like animals in the drains. Three levels down, every ledge has occupants. Ten levels down, the buildings seem to have been designed around them.

Broad-bodied waterfowl cover the settling ponds so densely that, from above, the water looks paved in overlapping feathers. Long-legged waders stand along the overflow weirs. Small white birds occupy window lintels, signal gantries, and every unused handrail. Under the bridges hang colonies of leathery-winged scavengers, shoulder to shoulder, their slow shifting audible as a continuous dry rustle.

There are heavy, seal-shaped animals on the warm spillways. They lie in rows with their heads downhill, allowing a thin sheet of water to pass under their bodies. Workers step around them. A sleeping adult can block a service gate for most of a day, and there are hooks on the wall for hanging a notice when this happens.

In the channels, shelled grazers rasp the mineral films from stone. Flat fish wait beneath them. Eels occupy the deeper conduits. Translucent larvae gather around warm discharges in such numbers that a bucketful of water appears to contain cloudy gelatin.

The city has made room for all this through thousands of practical adjustments. Ventilation grilles are sloped so birds cannot nest directly over them. Bridge bearings have smooth metal collars to exclude burrowing animals. Freight sheds have separate low doors through which the larger scavengers can be driven without opening the loading bays.

Some balconies belong wholly to nesting colonies. The rooms behind them remain inhabited. Their residents use another window.

***

The noise is considerable, but little of it is sudden.

Pumps throb through the bedrock. Water passes over hundreds of spillways, each audible from a particular street corner. Fans breathe behind brick walls. Somewhere below, a freight train is moving through a tunnel, its passage taking several minutes to complete.

Over these sounds lie the animals: hoarse calls, low communal grumbling, the ticking of claws on sheet metal. In the largest rookeries, individual voices disappear into a thick, level roar.

You can still hear a person speaking beside you, provided they face you. Conversations on bridges tend to happen close to the ear.

Industry moves at a similar pace. Mineral wagons travel on cable railways from the lower extraction works to the upper factories. Their wheels are taller than a person, their speed scarcely greater than a walk. A wagon comes into view between two buildings, crosses the opening, and is gone after a long interval. There will be another.

Inside the processing halls, cylinders revolve steadily in warm mist. Salts precipitate in shallow vats. Metal is recovered from brines. Porous ceramic is fired for the cultivation racks, pipe linings, and filter beds on which the whole settlement depends.

Many machines have occupied the same foundations for centuries. Their components are replaced individually, carried through doors whose sills bear the grooves of earlier replacements. The oldest workshops keep wooden patterns for castings no one has redesigned because the neighboring equipment would also have to change.

At shift changes, people emerge in waterproof aprons and walk home along the canal sides. They carry lunch tins, folded boots, cages of purchased shellfish. They stop to let wagons pass. They know which staircase is wettest, which tunnel holds its warmth through the night, which nesting bird will strike at a hat.

***

From a high maintenance balcony, the scale becomes difficult to hold in mind.

The nearest roofs are detailed and wet. You can see a person clearing an inlet with a rake. Farther away, buildings merge into bands of masonry, broken by reservoirs and the white threads of descending water. Beyond them are factory districts large enough to have rain at one end and sunlight at the other.

Cloud forms above the warm basins and drifts against the higher terraces. Sometimes it fills a valley completely, leaving chimneys and lift towers standing in a level white field. The terrace you are on may remain in sunshine for hours while fog presses against the walls below.

There are places within the city where no one has a reason to go: old settling courts reached only through flooded passages, disused rail cuttings packed with nesting animals, entire roofs hidden behind later construction. They have not returned to wilderness in any green sense. Water continues to cross them. Minerals collect. Animals breed among the obsolete fittings.

Change can be measured there, but it takes patience. A crust thickens on a pipe. A channel shifts a finger’s width. The outer edge of a rookery advances along a cornice over several seasons.

Toward evening, the masonry gives back the day’s warmth. Workers open roof vents, and mild white vapor rises through the birds. Electric lamps appear along the towpaths before they are strictly needed, reflected in every shallow runnel.

The last direct sunlight catches the upper plateau: bare stone, wet road, a few low structures beside the approach. From down here it looks almost unoccupied.

Below it, the pumps continue. Water moves from one terrace to the next, carrying heat, mineral traces, shed feathers, and the waste on which something farther down will feed. Along miles of warm spillway, the large animals settle more comfortably against one another. The night shift crosses the bridges.
