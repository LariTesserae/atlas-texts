The place fits beneath a lifted paving stone, although there is more daylight in it than there ought to be.

Light comes down between the stone and its frame in four clean strips. By noon these have moved onto the floor, illuminating the whole hollow: a shallow basin perhaps two armspans across, crowded with pale ridges, black grit, little copper-colored terraces. Nothing is far away. From any ledge you can see the opposite wall, but reaching it would mean crossing a country of obstructions.

The floor has risen recently. You can tell by the edges. Mineral shelves end in thin, unchipped lips; small cones retain the delicate points they had when they first hardened. Even the loose rubble looks new, with flat faces that flash when an animal overturns it. There is almost no soil. What seems like brown earth in the recesses is a mixture of iron flakes, shed skins, and the granular waste of things that have been eating the iron flakes.

Under everything runs a warm pipe.

It enters through one wall and disappears beneath a mass of cream-colored deposits. A little of its original blue paint survives on the exposed elbow. The heat is modest but dependable: enough to keep the hollow warm after sunset, enough to dry a fallen drop from the higher shelves before it can reach the bottom. In the morning, the pipe ticks once or twice. At irregular intervals a vibration passes through it, felt more clearly in the feet than heard.

The basin is packed. Every level carries another level. A shard supports a crust; beneath the crust is a nest; the nest is roofed with scales shed by something living on the next terrace up. The few open patches are passageways kept open by traffic. Even a hairline crack has tenants arranged by depth, with the pale ones near the mouth and the transparent ones farther in.

Yet it is mostly quiet.

The animals are soft-footed or too small to make much noise. Their movements register as changes in texture: a patch of gravel loosens and becomes six bodies; a white seam folds itself away from the light. Occasionally there is a crisp little click, like someone testing a fingernail against glass. It may be a jaw closing. It may be a fresh ridge splitting as it grows.

Water arrives from several sources, none generous. Rain seeps around the paving stone, but only when the wind pushes it from the right direction. Moisture condenses on a short iron bracket embedded in the cooler wall. A valve on the warm pipe leaks one clear bead at a time, with long pauses between beads.

Each source makes a different landscape.

Below the bracket lies a cold pocket, barely larger than a thumbprint, where the mineral is dark and smooth. The valve feeds an amber channel bordered by sharp white teeth. Rain spreads thinly across the upper shelves and leaves a bitter gray rim when it dries. Animals that drink from one source sometimes refuse another. After a heavy shower, the rainwater creatures descend into places they ordinarily cannot reach, and for several hours the hollow becomes dangerous in unfamiliar ways.

There are plants, but you have to look past the abundance of other life to find them.

Three stiff green blades grow from a screw hole near the pipe. Their roots have followed the thread into the wall. On the north side of the basin, a flat liver-colored growth occupies the underside of a translucent flake, receiving its daylight through stone. It has held that position through many cycles of wetting and drying, slowly pushing the flake upward. Its exposed margin is bitten almost straight.

Nothing here can afford much foliage. Anything tender is eaten; anything broad interrupts a passage. The plants survive in inconvenient positions, hanging over drops or growing behind hot metal, where feeding requires effort.

The grazers carry houses made from the place itself. They resemble low, jointed seeds, with dark legs tucked under roofs of mineral plates. A young one begins with a single plate. As it grows, it collects more, sorting them against its back until their edges lock. Some are tiled in white, some in rust, some in fragments of blue paint. When one dies, its house remains upright. Smaller creatures move in. Eventually the floor incorporates the whole arrangement.

This is one reason the terrain is so young. It is continually being assembled.

Another reason is less easy to account for.

Along the amber channel, a ridge sometimes forms ahead of the water rather than behind it. A dry patch brightens, stiffens, and lifts into a low wall. A minute later the next bead reaches it and turns aside. You can watch this happen. There is no hidden stream beneath the surface, no animal pushing upward. The basin seems to make provision for water just before receiving it, though not always correctly. Here and there stand immaculate little dams across channels that have never been wet.

The inhabitants use these errors. Eggs are laid in the empty reservoirs. The smallest predators wait at the ends of unnecessary bridges.

One predator lives beside the valve. At rest it is almost indistinguishable from a curled shaving of brass. When a grazer passes, it unfolds two long forelimbs and lifts the animal clear of the ground. There is a brief struggle in the air. Usually the grazer retreats inside its plates, and the predator tests the seams, patiently, before setting it down again. Successful attacks leave little to see: an empty tiled roof and a dark wet mark that soon contracts.

The valve itself is the only elaborate structure. A square brass body, a slotted adjustment screw, a bent lever no longer connected to anything. Beside it are the remnants of a service platform: two stamped-metal steps, a broken guard, a bolt sunk so deeply into new mineral that only its head remains visible.

The steps were made for fingers, perhaps, or for a tool. Now they support a colony of long-legged animals that hang beneath them in rows. When the pipe begins to vibrate, all the animals lift their bodies at once and remain suspended from the tips of their legs. They settle only after the vibration has passed.

Above, ordinary life continues. Shoes cross the paving stone. A wheeled bin goes past on certain mornings, producing a thunder that loosens grit from the ceiling. Once in a while someone washes the pavement, and a smelling flood enters one corner. Then the whole basin moves upward. Roof-backed grazers climb the walls; the hanging colonies crowd onto the bolt; eggs swell in their dry reservoirs. For an hour, the warm pipe becomes an island.

Most days are less dramatic. A bead fattens beneath the valve. Antennae touch the air around it. On a dry shelf, two animals rotate a mineral chip between them until it fits into a gap. The green blades lean toward the passing light.

Eventually the bead falls.

It strikes a ledge, divides, and sends three narrow movements through the crowded hollow. Bodies turn toward them. Bodies withdraw. A film advances under a shell, a new edge whitens, and somewhere beneath the nearest step there is room, briefly, for something to hatch.
