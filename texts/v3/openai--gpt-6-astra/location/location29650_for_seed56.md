The hollow lies just below the frost line, beneath a meadow that has not yet unfolded. From above, there is only a pale swelling in the ground, a little larger than a pond, with cold vapor gathering along one side. The way down is a seam between two roots. It opens by degrees over the winter, never wide enough to suggest an entrance until you are standing in it.

Below, there is room to stand, and then room for something much taller.

The ceiling hangs in deep green folds. They look black until your eyes adjust. Each fold is a leaf several feet thick, still furled around its own paler interior; where two folds part, blue light seeps through the packed vegetation above. The light has traveled through so much living tissue that it casts no clear shadows. Things become visible first at their edges: the silver hairs on a stem, the lip of a pool, the frost on an animal’s whiskers.

There is no exposed stone. The floor is a compressed tangle of roots, broad runners, and leaves flattened by the weight of later leaves. Even the grit underfoot consists of hard seeds. Some have split, putting out translucent threads that attach themselves to whatever passes close enough. A few catch on your soles. If you stand still, they begin to grow.

The whole hollow is young. Nothing here has been worn into shape. Its depressions are places where growth has paused; its ridges are where growth is still arriving. At the far end, a rounded rise lifts perhaps the width of a hair each day. It has the fresh, faintly greasy shine of a bud. Water beads on it but cannot yet cut a channel. Each droplet wanders among changing dimples until the surface opens a pore and takes it in.

It is bitterly cold. Moisture freezes in the outer layers of your clothes, and breathing makes the skin inside your nose ache. Yet water is everywhere: held in leaf cups, trembling along root undersides, spread in thin films over the floor. The water contains enough dissolved sugars to move sluggishly at temperatures where ordinary pools would be solid. It smells faintly of cut cucumber. Along its edges, clear ice grows in hollow needles, leaving dark syrup between them.

The largest pool is no wider than a bed. It occupies the center of the hollow, under a low knot of roots, and seems shallow until something swims beneath the visible bottom.

What looked like the bottom is a submerged leaf, transparent except for its veins. There are more below it. Between them move pale, jointed animals, folded almost flat to fit the spaces. They have too many limbs to swim gracefully, so they travel by gripping the underside of one leaf and walking upside down. Their eyes are small red points. Occasionally one lets go and descends, opening its legs in a slow, complicated bloom.

Other animals pack the walls.

Small furred bodies sleep in the warmest root junctions—warmest meaning only a little less cold. Their coats carry green filaments that continue into the bark around them, so it is difficult to tell where a nest ends and an animal begins. They feed without fully waking, drawing a leaf margin between their teeth and chewing for several minutes. When one shifts, the filaments stretch, release, and fasten again.

There are snails with shells like tightly rolled petals. There are long white grazers, thin as shoelaces, that travel inside the ceiling leaves and show through as slowly changing knots. Beneath the floor, something heavy moves once in a great while. You hear each foot settle, then feel a soft upward pressure through the roots.

For such a patient place, the hollow is loud.

The sound begins as a low drumming, too close and continuous to locate. Thousands of animals knock against the leaves they inhabit. Some are feeding; others are answering one another. The thick leaf chambers deepen these tiny impacts until the hollow seems filled with distant hoofbeats. Above that runs the ringing of ice needles. They grow until they touch, hold tension for hours, then snap with a bright, glassy note. Water makes a sucking sound as it passes through tight bundles of vessels. At intervals, a deep wooden report comes from the ceiling, and a folded leaf loosens by an amount you cannot see.

The noises overlap without much variation. After a while, they begin to feel less like events than weather.

Along the western wall—though direction becomes unreliable here—hang several clusters of small, dark leaves. They are narrower than fingers and unusually still. Frost settles on everything around them but never on their surfaces. Their veins carry brief points of white light, each point stopping at a junction before continuing along another path.

These leaves regulate the hollow’s cold.

You can feel their work by bringing a hand near them. One side of a leaf is intensely cold; the other gives off no corresponding warmth. The heat is going somewhere else. Far above, in the unopened meadow, a bud may briefly steam. Sometimes the receiving bud has not grown yet. The leaves maintain that connection anyway, holding a fine silver blemish along their margins until the future tissue is ready.

Nothing has been installed here. The roots learned these transfers as they learned to draw water. Their cells contain structures smaller than the pigments that make them green, structures that fold distances together and keep the folds supplied with sugar. A severed leaf loses the ability immediately. Under magnification it would yield only damaged tissue, ordinary salts, the beginnings of decay.

The living arrangement matters.

This is also why the hollow remains small. Its outermost roots turn inward, linking back through themselves. Energy, water, and the slow chemical messages of its inhabitants circulate within a boundary roughly thirty paces across. A root that grows beyond that boundary becomes an ordinary root: cold, blind, subject to the long route between one place and another. Few extend far.

Near the entrance, a broken runner shows what happens outside. Its exposed end has frozen white. Farther in, the same runner remains green and bends around a sleeping animal’s ribs.

Deeper in the hollow, distances have slight irregularities. A dripping stem can be close enough to touch while its reflection lies several paces away. Two adjacent roots may take water from the same pool, though one draws it this winter and the other draws it next. The animals negotiate these differences by scent. Their trails follow precise, apparently needless curves. A snail approaching a certain patch of floor turns back, circles a seed, and crosses safely a finger’s breadth to the left.

The place is still developing these capacities. There are mistakes: a leaf whose two tips occupy the same point, a root drinking from an empty depression while water accumulates in its shadow. Around each fault, new tissue grows very slowly, testing another shape.

At the center, beneath the layered pool, a bud is forming.

It is visible only when the swimmers move aside: a blunt green body enclosed in a clear membrane, with a seam down one side. Bubbles gather on the seam and remain there for weeks. Every animal in the pool leaves a little room around it.

Over many winters, it will rise until it touches the ceiling. The folded leaves above are already adjusting, easing their weight outward, making space. For now there is only the cold water, the crowded life, the continuous drumming, and this small unfinished chamber growing around something it has not yet learned how to open.
