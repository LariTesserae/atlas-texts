The hollow lies just beneath the hillside, close enough to the surface that roots come through its roof, but deep enough that the afternoon arrives as a change in temperature rather than a change in light.

It is not quite a cave. Part of the roof has fallen in, long ago, leaving three narrow openings above a chamber perhaps forty paces across. Through them one sees slices of white sky, the undersides of leaves, sometimes the pale belly of a lizard crossing a branch. The openings do not line up with one another. Light enters in separate places and at separate hours, moving over the interior so slowly that a patch of fern may wait most of the day for its few minutes of brightness.

Below these openings, the ground rises in heaps. Fallen stone has weathered into blunt shoulders, its original fractures packed with red earth. The heaps meet and overlap, dividing the hollow into small courts and passages. There is no point from which the whole place can be seen. A person standing at its center would have a wall close to one shoulder, a hanging root near the other, and several ways forward that all appear to end in leaves.

The heat is held in the stone. Even before sunrise, the walls feel like something recently occupied by a large animal. By noon, warm air presses down through the roof openings and settles into the passages, bringing the smell of sun-struck soil. There is little wind. Occasionally a leaf turns over without anything else moving, and that is enough to reveal an air current.

Most surfaces are dry to the touch. The damp is local and exact.

It persists under the lips of rocks, inside the sleeves of old leaves, along one dark seam in the eastern wall. There, water comes through the limestone one bead at a time. Each bead swells until it reflects an entire roof opening, then slips down a polished track into a basin no wider than two cupped hands. The basin has a mineral rim and a floor of black leaf fragments. Several pale snails live beneath that rim, beyond the reach of most mouths.

Rain on the hill does not immediately reach the hollow. Sometimes it arrives hours later, after the sky visible overhead has cleared. The roots begin to shine. Dark patches spread across the ceiling, join, and release a brief, uneven rain of their own. Other storms leave nothing here. Their water goes elsewhere through the hill.

The plants grow according to these differences.

Beneath the broadest opening, glossy leaves stand shoulder-high, overlapping so densely that their stems disappear. Farther in, the leaves become larger and thinner, held almost horizontally to receive the scattered light. Vines cross between them on taut, woody stems. Some have grown around fallen branches that have since rotted away, leaving loops with nothing inside them.

The darkest recesses hold few green plants. They are occupied instead by roots, fungi, and the pale ribs of leaves brought down from above. A shelf of small brown mushrooms follows a buried log through the soil; its course can be traced from one passage to the next. In places, the mushrooms emerge beneath a stone and lift it almost imperceptibly. Elsewhere the log has collapsed, and the ground above it has begun to sink.

There are more animals than the stillness suggests.

Geckos lie lengthwise in cracks, their toes spread against the stone. Small frogs occupy the folded bases of leaves, each in a shallow reserve of warm water. Under almost every loose object, something is feeding or waiting: beetles with earth-colored backs, flattened centipedes, translucent young spiders, mites moving over the abandoned shell of a larger creature. Land crabs live in the deeper soil. Their burrow mouths are often plugged with leaves, but fresh crumbs of red clay accumulate outside them overnight.

Nothing here wastes much effort on calling. The frogs breed after the heavier seep-rains and are quiet for weeks between them. The lizards hunt by sight. The crabs pause for a long time before crossing open ground.

A dry click may come from beneath a root. A leaf may scrape another as something passes behind it. Near the water seam there is an occasional small sound, too soft to distinguish a falling drop from an animal entering the basin. The hollow absorbs these noises in its soil and foliage. Even an abrupt movement seldom carries beyond the next mound of stone.

Along the southern wall stand a few low courses of fitted rock. They are old enough that roots have entered the joints and thickened there, pushing some stones outward while holding others in place. The courses form two shallow terraces and the remains of a narrow step. Nothing else suggests a building. Earth has accumulated behind the walls, and the plants on those terraces are unusually thick.

One flat stone still has a bare center. Animals use it as a crossing place. Fine tracks appear in the dust there: five-toed prints, a beetle’s dotted line, the dragged punctuation of a tail. Around its edges lie seed husks carried in by small rodents. Several have germinated in the joints, though few seedlings survive beyond their first pair of leaves.

The hollow is busy with such small failures and renewals. A vine finds an opening and shades the plant that supported it. A crab pulls a fallen leaf underground. A gecko takes a moth from the wall, leaving a faint powdery outline. A root thickens behind the old masonry. None of these events needs to disturb the whole place.

Toward evening, the light withdraws from the floor before the heat does. The last bright patch climbs a mound of stone and catches the underside of a leaf, making its veins briefly visible from below. Then only the roof openings remain distinct.

In that dimness, movement becomes easier to notice. Bodies cross the bare stone. Antennae extend from crevices. Something small descends a hanging root, stops above the hand-sized basin, and waits for the larger animal already drinking there to move away.
