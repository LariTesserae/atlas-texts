The first impression is of a city built above the weather. Then the heat reaches you, and the impression becomes harder to keep.

These heights ought to be bare. Far below, through openings between the terraces, you can see the blunt summits of other mountains, their snowfields reduced by distance to pale scratches. Here there is no snow. The stone is hot enough that you cannot comfortably rest a palm on it. Water beads on its shaded faces. The air presses warm moisture against the eyes and inside the nose, carrying the smell of soaked bark, mineral salts, and the sweet, almost edible odor of thick green stems.

Across the plateau, the city extends from one precipice to another.

It has avenues, courts, narrow passages, something like aqueducts. Its taller structures stand in close groups, their broad feet joined beneath the paving. Everything is green or brown or the dull cream of exposed inner tissue. Nothing has been cut. At a distance, the regularity looks architectural: arcades following the contours, bridges spanning ravines, tier after tier of roof gardens. Closer, you find that the columns are trunks whose grain runs sideways into the lintels. The paving is a continuous rind, cracked into handsome, uneven polygons. What seemed to be mortar is a pale film growing between darker surfaces.

There are no doors. The openings have rounded lips, and the chambers behind them are humid and empty.

Some are the size of cupboards. Others could hold a crowd. Inside the largest, rows of buttresses rise into a dim ceiling furred with roots. There are recesses at a comfortable sitting height, shallow basins along the walls, broad slopes leading to upper galleries. It is difficult not to suppose an intention behind these provisions. Yet no path is more worn than another. No soot marks the ceilings. No edge has been polished by hands.

The city has the accommodations of habitation without any evidence that habitation has occurred.

It is also deafening.

The sound is there before you distinguish its sources: a deep, continuous concussion, filled with a higher rushing that bears against the teeth. You feel it in the soft underside of the jaw. Speaking would be possible, but hearing yourself would not. Under the great arcades, certain notes thicken until the ribs seem to contain another set of ribs, vibrating a little out of alignment.

Water accounts for some of it.

It lies everywhere near the surface. The avenues have dark, saturated centers. Pools fill the courts, leaving only narrow rims of living pavement around them. High on the trunks, clear seepage bulges from pores and joins into sheets. Whole districts stand ankle-deep in water transparent enough to reveal the fine white roots beneath. The roots spread in dense, formal arrangements, turning at the boundaries of adjoining structures as though respecting property lines.

At the eastern edge of the plateau, several channels converge and pass over a cliff.

The falls descend farther than the eye can follow. Through their lower portions you see distant mountain slopes, blue with depth. Their roar comes back from those slopes and from somewhere farther down, enlarged by an immense space that the mist conceals.

But the water does not visibly move.

A divided strand hangs clear of the main fall, threaded with droplets. Each droplet has a bright point on its upper edge. You look away, walk the length of the parapet, look back. The strand is still divided in precisely the same places.

Nearer at hand, a leaf holds a bead of water at its tip. The bead is heavy enough to pull the leaf downward. It never falls. Along a gutter, a thin white foam has accumulated behind a knuckle of root. Each little bubble remains intact.

The noise has no such stillness. It enters the body with the violence of water in motion. There is no pause in it, no repeating sequence you can learn, but after a while you notice that it has no developments either. No splash rises above the rest. No distant collapse changes its weight. It is possible to listen for a very long time without hearing anything happen.

Beyond the wet districts, the ground lifts into a ridge occupied by the tallest growths.

Here the streets are steep and narrow. Walls nearly touch overhead. Long leaves overlap in shelves, their undersides purple and glossy, their upper surfaces the deep green of foliage seen through water. Lesser plants occupy every seam: fleshy rosettes, mats of minute translucent cups, moss as dense as velvet. Pale roots hang through the interiors of the towers without quite reaching the floor.

There is no flutter, no scratching, no wingbeat. Nothing has eaten the leaves. Even the broadest fronds are entire. In the basins there are neither larvae nor the quick dark marks of swimming things. The richness feels strange for a while before its missing element becomes apparent: all this abundance has no appetite moving through it.

Nor is there much rot. Fallen leaves lie beneath their parent growths, softened and brown, but their edges remain distinct. A great trunk has come down across one court, and smaller stems enclose it on both sides. Its exposed end is weathered silver. It appears to have been falling into decay for longer than the surrounding mountains have existed, without surrendering another fiber.

From the ridge you can see the plan more clearly.

The city occupies a system of stepped basins perhaps several miles across. Each basin has its own flooded center and its own encircling streets. Raised causeways connect them. Huge arches cross the deeper cuts, sometimes carrying a grove on their backs, sometimes only a shallow ribbon of water. The forms are old and settled. Corners have rounded; broad surfaces have acquired the shallow hollows that rain makes over ages. Nothing is raw. Even the precipices possess an established, almost domestic shape.

Ordinary daylight rests on it all. There is no colored sky to account for the strangeness, no luminous fog. The clouds are white. Shadows are gray-green. Sunlight shines plainly on wet surfaces and reveals the small, practical irregularities of the place.

Then you notice a distant court through an arch below you.

You have stood in that court. You recognize the fallen trunk, the three cream-colored basins, the tall split column. From here it ought to be hidden behind the ridge. Instead it lies openly in view, several hundred feet beneath a district that was below it when you passed through.

Other connections become doubtful. A causeway crosses a ravine and arrives at a terrace lower than its departure, although every visible part of it rises. Two neighboring towers share a wall near their bases but stand on opposite sides of a gorge at their crowns. Through a low passage you can see the outer mountains, and between those mountains, very small and very high, the same passage with daylight showing through it.

These discrepancies do not spread or resolve. You can use them as landmarks.

The split column always overlooks the wrong basin. The ascending causeway always takes you down. The city permits a route to be learned.

Farther out, construction thins into groves and then into low vegetation clinging to hot, porous rock. There is no clear boundary where the streets cease being streets. The last avenue narrows into a seam of moss. The last vaulted chamber is a hollow beneath a root, full of clear water and ordinary reflected sky.

At the brink, the air is as wet as it was among the buildings. Small plants crowd the rim, packed tightly against one another, their leaves swollen and untroubled. Beyond them is the enormous drop.

You can sit there with the heat coming through the living stone, looking across the lower summits. The roar is so complete that it eventually loses the quality of interruption. It occupies the distance, the ground, the spaces inside your clothing.

Beside your knee, a droplet hangs from a leaf. Within it are the terraces, upside down, and a minute bright portion of the cliff.

Nothing arrives. Nothing departs. There is room in the city for all of it.
