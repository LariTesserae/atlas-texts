At first the country looks level. The horizon lies so far away that its curvature is easier to notice than any hill. Pale ground rises almost imperceptibly toward it, passing through shades of gray and buff until the distinction between distance and sky becomes difficult to hold.

Then, somewhere ahead, a cloud moves below the ground.

You are on the upper surface of a continent-sized uplift, high enough that most weather happens beneath its edges. Through a gap in the rim, you can see the tops of storms: white, densely folded, lit by the same ordinary sun that falls on your boots. Lightning spreads inside them without a sound. Beyond the storms are glimpses of a lower world, blue-black with atmospheric depth. Nothing down there resolves into a recognizable landform.

Up here, the light is clear and slightly severe. Shadows have sharp edges. A stone turned over reveals a cool underside, and even the sunlit ground holds little warmth.

The mountains that once occupied this height have worn almost completely away.

Their roots remain as broad differences in color: an iron-colored oval several hundred kilometers across, a long greenish seam, patches of white mineral with the dull translucence of old teeth. There are no young scree slopes, no bright fractures. Ridges have become shallow swells. Former valleys are wide depressions that you enter and leave without noticing, except that a distant object disappears for half a day and then returns.

Occasionally, a hard inclusion has survived as a low knob. Its windward face is polished enough to reflect a blurred patch of sky. On its sheltered side, grains have gathered in little shelves, each shelf only a finger deep. It might have taken a century to make one. It might have taken this morning.

The air is too thin to carry much sound, but it is never still.

You see its violence in the dust.

A narrow line begins on the horizon and travels toward you, wavering at its edges. As it approaches, it becomes a sheet of grains no higher than your knees, moving faster than a running animal. The sheet divides around a stone, closes again, twists upright into a translucent wall. For a moment the sun shines through it in a dirty amber disk. Then the wall collapses sideways and races away, leaving the ground almost exactly as it was.

There is a faint ticking against your clothes.

Farther out, whole districts are in motion. Dust spirals intersect and shear apart. Low fronts overtake one another. A patch of dark ground turns white as fine mineral settles on it, then dark again when the next current lifts the coating. Sometimes the particles rise in tall, widely separated columns whose upper ends bend in different directions. They look substantial from a distance. Within one, you can still see the horizon.

There is very little material involved. The same ancient powder keeps traveling.

Electrical charge supplies what the sparse air cannot. Dust climbs surfaces against the wind. Fine grains stand on end before releasing together. On certain afternoons, a pale branching pattern races across the plain, tracing buried mineral seams. You feel a tug at loose threads; the hairs on your wrist lift. Several kilometers away, a low dune unfolds into the air and becomes a long, revolving veil.

The silence makes these events difficult to judge. Something that appears leisurely can arrive before you have finished watching it.

Life stays close to the stone.

In shallow cavities grow small black rosettes, most no larger than a coin. Their leaves are thick, tightly fitted scales with a faint metallic bloom. Beneath them, roots enter cracks too narrow to see. They draw minute reserves of moisture from salts that surrender it only during a few cold hours before dawn.

A healthy colony may contain six plants.

Around each colony is a nearly invisible stain where the stone has been chemically softened. Given enough time, the plants deepen their own shelter. The oldest occupy smooth cups, protected from the passing dust, their exposed leaves worn flush with the rim. When a charged front approaches, the scales close. You can watch the movement if you kneel very still.

Once in many days, something crosses between these cups.

It is low and translucent, with too many small feet to count while it moves. Its body is full of dark grains. It stops at the edge of a rosette, scrapes once beneath a leaf, and settles into a fissure. The entire encounter leaves you uncertain whether you have seen an animal or a loose piece of the colony carrying itself elsewhere.

The buildings are easier to miss.

Most are shallow, dark rectangles embedded in the plain, widely separated. Their roofs have the same worn sheen as the surrounding rock. There are no roads, but near some of them the dust lies in parallel strips, as though repeated passage has changed how the ground holds charge.

A building might contain one room. Inside are a bench, a depression in the floor, and a narrow opening facing the distant rim. No controls are visible. Nothing glows.

Yet the air inside is dense enough for you to hear your own movements clearly.

A boot scraping the threshold makes a startling noise. Clothing rustles. If you set a small stone on the bench, it settles slowly through the last few centimeters, meeting the surface without a click. Outside, a dust front can pass directly through the open doorway and lose its shape, the grains hanging briefly before drifting back out.

The structures are still working.

Their foundations extend farther than their walls suggest. At sunset, when the ground is seen in grazing light, extremely straight seams become visible between them. Some cross a whole day’s walking distance. Where a seam meets a natural fracture, the fracture has been prevented from widening: its two edges remain perfectly aligned, though the rock around them has subsided and worn away.

Near the continental rim, the purpose becomes harder to ignore. Great portions of the plateau project over the lower atmosphere with nothing beneath them. Their undersides are smooth, rounded by an erosion that must have occurred before they reached this height. In places, detached slabs hang just beyond the edge, separated from the mainland by a few meters of empty sky.

Dust streams across the gaps.

At night the air cools quickly, and the mineral plain releases the charge it gathered all day. The busiest weather often begins then. Under starlight, low curtains of powder wheel across one another, visible only when their surfaces catch the light. A pale flash travels along a seam. Far below, the cloud decks brighten and fade.

Inside a shelter, you can hear the faint settling of grit on the floor.

Outside, the oldest exposed ground in the world is being swept clean again.
