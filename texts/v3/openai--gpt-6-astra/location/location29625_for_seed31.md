The town occupies a high basin, broad enough that its far side is blue in the afternoon. Beyond it, a wall of dark mountains rises in sharp, uneven steps. There are no rounded foothills. Rock passes almost directly into level ground, as though the basin and the mountains have not yet had time to wear themselves into one another.

Water lies close beneath everything.

It appears in the bottom of an unfinished foundation, in the seams between paving stones, in clear beads along a freshly cut bank. A footprint beside the road fills slowly from below. Yet most of the basin is not a lake or even a marsh. It is firm ground laid thinly over wetness: coarse volcanic soil, beds of pale ash, angular gravel that has scarcely traveled from the rock that made it. The drains run clear. There is little mud except where people have worked the same patch of earth for years.

The air is mild and damp. At this elevation the light has a clean strength, but cloud often rests on the mountain wall, feeding narrow threads of water down its face. Sheltered against dark stone, it is warm enough to sit barefoot. In the open, the same warmth is spread more thinly, with a little coolness coming up from the saturated ground. Clothes hung beneath an eave dry eventually. Clothes left against a wall do not.

From a distance, the town looks unfinished. Its houses stand in groups separated by wide stretches of green, shallow water, and bare stone. A long paved avenue might pass only three compounds before arriving at a square. Across the square, another avenue continues toward buildings so far away that their doorways are small dark marks.

But the spacing is deliberate. The town has been built around the water rather than over it.

Each occupied rise carries a neighborhood: a few dozen low houses, a public court, a place for grinding grain, and a roofed platform where people work in the shade. Between these rises, the ground is left open. Gardens occupy the drier margins. The lowest strips hold sedges, reeds, and broad-leaved plants rooted in black, shining soil. Channels connect them beneath short stone bridges. The town is one settlement by its roads and its shared waterworks, though no roof touches another across the wet ground.

The buildings are made of what can be lifted, split, pressed, and tied by hand. Dark stone forms the foundations. Above it are walls of earth mixed with chopped stalks, carefully protected by wide eaves. Roofs carry overlapping wooden boards weighted with flat rocks. Some older houses have stone walls all the way up, their faces rough and their corners exact. There is little ornament beyond a band of pale clay around a door, a carved post, a row of finger impressions beneath a windowsill.

Every threshold is raised. Every courtyard has a drain.

The care given to these small differences in height is the town’s most conspicuous skill. A walkway remains dry where the ground on either side is soaked. A channel turns around the foot of a house with no wasted fall. Flat stones can be lifted to expose the narrower drains beneath them, and at intervals there are shallow settling basins with steps down into the water. The tools used to maintain all this are simple—wooden spades, stone-edged hoes, baskets, poles—but the work is precise. A person kneeling at a sluice can change which garden receives water without noticeably changing the stream beyond it.

There are no fountains. Where water must descend, it is led along an inclined slab or a succession of low steps. Even the larger channels make little sound.

Vegetation grows densely where it has found a place, leaving much of the basin open. Beside a seep, leaves overlap in thick, glossy layers. A few paces away, a shelf of black rock supports only small tufts in its cracks. Young woodland follows the sheltered foot of the mountains, its trees slender and widely separated, with fern beds between them. The exposed uplands carry shorter growth: coarse grasses, low shrubs, patches of flowering plants whose stems hold droplets long after the cloud has lifted.

Within the town, trees stand singly in courts or in small groups along a drain. Their crowns provide broad pools of shade without hiding the surrounding country. From most streets it is possible to see a mountain, a wet meadow, and another part of the settlement at the same time.

The cultivated plots are especially rich. Leaf crops grow on raised beds edged with stone. Taller stalks occupy the sunny ground; tubers are planted in loose ridges where excess water can escape. Cut vegetation is laid between rows and worked back into the soil. There is no grazing land, no need for fences against browsing animals, no sheds or pens at the edge of town. Low boundary walls mark ownership and keep loose soil from washing into a path.

Nothing calls from the reeds.

The pools have no fish, and their surfaces are not disturbed by insects. There are no birds along the rooflines or tracks crossing the soft ground beyond the gardens. Beneath leaves, the damp litter breaks down slowly into dark material threaded with fungus. Wind moves pollen across the open plots. Seeds catch in cracks, spread along drains, or are carried in baskets to prepared ground.

Human sounds consequently travel with unusual clarity. A wooden bowl set down on a sill can be heard from the other side of a court. Somewhere a stone pestle strikes at long intervals. Two people talking under a roof seem nearer than they are. Much of the time even these sounds are absent, and the town rests in the faint noise of water touching stone.

The main square is an extensive pavement on a natural shelf above the basin floor. Its surface is uneven but not broken: large slabs fitted together, their joints packed with small chips. Along one side stands an open hall, its roof supported on heavy timber posts. Along another are storehouses with high floors and deep porches. The square has room for many more people than are usually in it. On an ordinary afternoon there might be someone sorting pale stalks on a mat, someone carrying two baskets on a pole, a few figures seated at the hall’s shaded edge.

Beyond the square, a processional road—or simply a road made wider than necessary—crosses the basin on a low embankment. Water passes beneath it through square openings. It leads to a quarry at the foot of a fresh escarpment, where the exposed rock still has clean fracture surfaces. Blocks lie close to the places from which they were separated. Chips have sharp edges. There is little rubble softened by weather, little depth of accumulated debris.

The surrounding land has the same youth. A cliff ends in a narrow apron of fragments rather than a long slope. Small streams occupy shallow grooves; they have not cut ravines. On the higher benches, old lava retains its folds and abrupt lips beneath the vegetation. Rain darkens these surfaces, gathers in their hollows, and drains away without yet having remade their shapes.

Above the quarry, the basin can be seen whole. Its settlement spreads across several low rises, substantial in extent but light in its occupation of the ground. Gray roofs repeat among green beds and narrow reflective channels. Long roads hold the parts together. Between them are spaces large enough to seem like countryside until one notices a stone-edged drain, a stair cut into a bank, or a small shelter positioned exactly where a path meets the water.

There is no great river entering the basin. Its supply comes from many modest sources: springs at the mountain’s foot, seepage through porous beds, cloud condensed on the upper slopes. Water gathers gradually and leaves through a broad, shallow outlet at the far end. The outlet has a sill of hard rock. It keeps the wet ground wet without allowing the whole basin to deepen into a lake.

Most days resemble one another. Cloud touches the heights, withdraws a little, returns. The sun warms a wall. The shade beneath an eave shifts across a doorstep. Water holds nearly the same level against the bridge piers, marked by a dark band and a fine edge of green.

At evening, the far neighborhoods remain visible across the basin. Their roofs lose their highlights, while the channels continue to catch the sky. A few people take the raised paths home. From the quarry road they can be followed for a long time, small figures moving between stretches of still water, until a stand of trees or the corner of a house receives them.

Then the paths are empty again. The mountains retain a little light along their sharp upper edges. Beneath the nearest bridge, a thin sheet of water slides down a fitted stone into a pool, making a sound that can only be heard from close beside it.
