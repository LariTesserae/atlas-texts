The first thing you see is the opposite shore, although there is no lake between you and it.

A kilometer away, beyond a low expanse of wet gray flooring, a line of lights marks the foot of a retaining wall. Their reflections lie in separate puddles. Some are long enough to join; others stop at a raised seam or break against the black square of a drain. In the dimness these disconnected strips of water suggest a much deeper body, something you might need a boat to cross. Then a maintenance vehicle moves through the middle distance, its wheels visible all the way down to the ground.

The basin is shallow, broad, and entirely manufactured.

Under the water are overlapping plates of ceramic-faced steel. Under those are heat-exchange channels, pressure cells, service passages, and further floors. There is no soil to reach by digging. A loose plate reveals another made surface, usually warmer, sometimes moving. Even the pale grit gathered along the puddle edges is industrial: fractured gasket material, beads of insulation, a chalky precipitate shed by the overhead condensers.

The ceiling is high enough to disappear in its own weather. You occasionally glimpse a truss through a thinning bank of vapor, or the red point of a crane traveling where the sky ought to be. Most of the light comes from low fixtures fixed along drainage berms. They illuminate the undersides of things. Handrails cast shadows upward into mist.

It is cold enough that exposed metal takes skin. Not everything freezes. Long lanes of water remain open above circulating pipes, while the ground beside them carries a brittle white crust. At the margins, ice forms in narrow combs aligned with the grooves of the floor. Every few minutes a valve opens somewhere below, the temperature shifts, and a whole comb lets go with a delicate crackling almost lost beneath the machinery.

The loudness is spacious. It does not come from a single engine you can locate and walk away from. There is a deep, continuous loading sound underfoot, the effort of pumps lifting water through an unimaginable vertical distance. Fans turn above the vapor. At intervals a hollow report crosses the basin as a pressure door closes in a neighboring chamber. The sound travels along the plates faster than through the air: first a tremor in the soles, then the blow itself, then echoes arriving from several directions.

Between these events you can hear water everywhere.

It ticks from cable trays. It whispers down the faces of tall baffles. It spills in thick, transparent ropes from a broken collector lip and lands hard enough to keep a circle of floor clean. Elsewhere the rain is so fine it seems suspended until it touches your sleeve.

The basin was level once, and not long ago. Its new terrain has risen through a combination of frost, pressure, and deliberate alterations to the water system. Broad sections of flooring have buckled into low ridges. Some rise only to the knee; others make long hills with a dark, accessible hollow beneath one flank. Their crests are sharp. The coating has split there, exposing clean metal without rust, and warning stripes painted on the former flat ground now climb the slopes at absurd angles.

These are young landforms. Nothing has rounded them yet. A drainage trench ends at a ridge that did not exist when the trench was cut. Water gathers, finds a bolt hole, and begins enlarging its new route. Around the hole, powder-white deposits have already formed a small fan. Farther downstream, the flow carries flakes of blue paint into a sheltered depression, where they settle by size like gravel.

Across the basin stand widely separated structures: a pump tower, three sealed lift housings, a gantry with no visible load. Each has an apron of bare floor around it. There are no crowded streets or banks of machinery packed shoulder to shoulder. The industry here works through distance. It requires room for water to spread, for air to cool, for pressure to dissipate before the next set of intakes takes it up.

A few raised walkways connect the installations. Many stop short of one another. Their missing sections have been removed to allow the floor beneath to rise.

Vegetation occupies the quieter margins. On strips of exposed filter felt grow dark, low plants with stiff leaves, scarcely taller than a thumb. Their roots enter the weave and follow it under the cladding. Where a seam stays warm, they make a thin continuous line; where the circulating water turns cold, the line ends as neatly as if cut with shears.

Near an abandoned sampling station, something reedlike grows out of a bed of tangled synthetic fiber. Its hollow stems rattle whenever the ventilation changes. Most bear no green, only small bronze collars at the joints. Once during each lighting cycle, the collars open and release dust into the fanward air. By morning the nearest wet handrail has acquired a faint brown velvet.

Animals are easier to hear than see. A quick series of taps may come from beneath a plate, followed by silence when a pump changes speed. Once, under a walkway, a pale many-legged creature crosses the light carrying a shred of filter material. It is gone before its size is clear. The large pools remain almost empty. In one, a few translucent swimmers hold themselves against the current beside a warm outlet, their insides showing as single crooked threads.

The maintenance vehicles do not disturb these places unless a channel becomes obstructed. They travel slowly, amber lamps turning on their roofs, and leave precise tracks through the mineral film. At a plant colony they may pause, extend a probe, and continue without cutting it back. Nobody visible directs them.

Sometimes one stops before an empty stretch of floor.

After a while, the floor rises.

It happens gradually: a shallow dome developing beneath standing water, reflections sliding apart, loose grit creeping toward the edges. The vehicle waits until the movement is complete, then paints a narrow yellow boundary around it. Similar boundaries cross the basin in older, fading loops. Some enclose ridges. Some enclose nothing at all.

The place has a tide, though no sea reaches it. Every six hours the collectors empty. Water advances across the lower flats, quietly at first, then with a thin rushing sound as hundreds of separate puddles connect. Islands of dry plating shrink around the towers. Drain covers begin to hum. The reeds bend toward the outflow, and the maintenance vehicles take to the raised routes.

For twenty minutes the far shore becomes a shore in earnest.

Then the pumps deepen their note. The water withdraws into slots too narrow to see from a distance, leaving bubbles caught against the new ridges and a fresh bright rim around every bolt head. Mist thickens overhead. Somewhere beyond the retaining wall, another chamber receives the water with a long, muffled roar.

The lights remain low. The exposed floor steams briefly, then begins to freeze.
