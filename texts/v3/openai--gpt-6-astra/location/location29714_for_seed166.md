The hollow lies four metres under the lake, close enough to shore for daylight to reach its floor without losing its shape. It is about the size of a large room. A low limestone lip encloses it on three sides; on the fourth, the bottom continues outward as a pale shelf until the water makes distance indistinguishable from depth.

There is no roof. Looking up, there is only a broad, silver-grey brightness, too diffuse to show the exact position of the surface. The water is clear and cold. It does not have the dense blue of open sea. Near the rock it is almost colourless, becoming faintly green across the width of the hollow. Small details remain visible: a dark seam in the limestone, individual grains on a ledge, the thin brown edge where a patch of growth gives way to bare stone.

The basin was worn into the lakebed long before the present shoreline settled into place. Its walls are low and rounded, with none of the fresh fractures of a recent collapse. Water has enlarged the joints between beds of stone into shallow horizontal recesses. Fine sediment occupies these recesses without quite filling them. Elsewhere, harder bands stand out a finger’s breadth from the wall, their edges softened enough to look comfortable to touch.

Most of the floor is uncovered rock. Silt gathers only in the lowest part, a grey oval a few metres across, so smooth that its boundary is easier to see than its surface. Three loose stones sit well apart around it. They have the same pale colour as the floor, but beneath each is a narrow shadow where the water has access to its underside.

Nothing has been placed here.

Beyond the wooded headland above the lake, a pumping station runs continuously. Its intake is far outside the hollow. There are no pipes here, no cables, no concrete footings, no grilles darkening the water. The station’s presence arrives through the limestone instead: a low mechanical note that is scarcely audible in the middle of the basin but becomes distinct close to the floor.

It has two parts. One is a deep, nearly featureless hum. The other is a finer vibration resting above it, steady enough that after a while it seems less like a sound than a property of the stone. The bedrock carries the motors more readily than the open water does. Against a ledge, the entire hollow feels faintly connected to a machine much larger than itself.

Yet there is no local agitation. No stream of bubbles marks a leak. No current scours a channel through the silt. The pumps draw from deeper water, and this small indentation remains outside their circulation. Industrial work proceeds somewhere beyond the visible world, registering here only as a fixed undertone.

Vegetation occupies a few favourable inches rather than whole surfaces. On the upper lip, where the light is strongest, short charophytes grow in widely separated tufts. Their jointed stems are stiffened by a pale mineral coating. They do not form a meadow. Between one tuft and the next lie broad reaches of naked limestone.

A thin olive film follows some of the shallower grooves. In places it is so sparse that the colour appears to belong to the rock itself. The deeper recesses are bare.

There are no animals in the hollow. No fish interrupt the long view toward the shelf. No snails stipple the stones, and no small swimmers gather over the sediment. The floor has neither tracks nor feeding marks. Its fine surface is intact all the way to the foot of the wall.

The light is ordinary daylight, softened by an overcast sky and by the water above. It is sufficient, even generous, but without sparkle. There are no restless nets of sunlight running over the bottom. The limestone shows in muted cream, grey, and a little rusty yellow along one mineral seam. Shadows remain close to the things that cast them.

Cold groundwater seeps through the lower joints so slowly that it produces no visible plume. It keeps the hollow cool even when the upper lake warms. Water leaves as gradually as it enters. There is exchange, but nothing that bends the plants or lifts the settled grains.

Nearly everything here has found a resting place. The silt lies below the reach of ordinary surface disturbance. The loose stones have settled into shallow seats. Growth remains close to the better-lit rim. The worn walls shelter the floor without closing it off from the lake.

From just above the basin, its whole arrangement can be taken in at once: the enclosing lip, the three stones, the small grey bed of sediment, the sparse growth along the upper edge. Beyond it, the shelf fades quietly into green water.

Stay longer and little is added. The light holds. The stems remain upright. The low vibration continues through the rock, with no audible beginning or end.
