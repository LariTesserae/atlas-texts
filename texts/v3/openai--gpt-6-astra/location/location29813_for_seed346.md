The district occupies a shallow depression in a plain of pale, newly formed stone. You can walk around its outer wall in twenty minutes, provided the northern corner is present. Beyond it, the ground continues almost level to the horizon, interrupted by a few low ridges with edges too sharp to have endured a winter. There are no valleys yet. Rain has made tentative dark lines in the dust, but none is deep enough to keep the next rain in the same course.

It is very cold. Not the cold of altitude or wind: the air is nearly still, and the roofs stand only a little above the surrounding plain. The cold comes through the soles of your shoes. It collects in door handles and the undersides of steps. Even in full daylight, an ungloved hand leaves a faint, warm discoloration on a railing, which fades reluctantly.

From outside, the place resembles a single enormous building into which streets have been cut.

Inside, it is plainly a town.

There are bakeries, closed workshops, apartment entrances, a small square occupied almost entirely by ventilation housings. Buildings stand so close that their gutters interlock. Some share walls without sharing a boundary; you can find a stairwell belonging to one house entirely inside another, with its own weather showing through the landing windows. The narrower alleys are roofed by successive generations of footbridges. Looking up, you see glass, pale masonry, hanging utility bundles, a strip of sky, then another strip of sky slightly nearer.

Most surfaces are light enough that the streets remain well lit. The illumination is ordinary and useful. You can distinguish a brown button on gray cloth at the far end of a passage. There is nothing theatrical about the light, and this makes certain things harder to dismiss: a drainpipe passing behind its own shadow; the same woman’s coat hanging in six windows, moving only in the fourth.

The buildings are made from a dense ceramic that has not finished deciding what mineral it is. In sheltered corners it is smooth as crockery. At ground level, where boots and handcarts touch it, crystals are slowly arranging themselves into a tougher, darker substance. Small square patches remain soft enough to take a thumbprint. These are marked with municipal warnings.

Construction here precedes geology. The foundations were installed first, suspended at the required coordinates, and the ground is being allowed to develop around them.

You can see this most clearly beneath the eastern arcade. A maintenance trench exposes a row of footings and, between them, an opaque gray interval about the thickness of a book. It is not a cavity. Light does not enter it, and a probe pushed into it acquires extra length without advancing. Over several months, grains of matter collect along its margins. They begin as something finer than frost and harden into rock with no ancestry: no buried organisms, no old riverbed, no previous mountain.

The workers brush away the first loose grains. The later ones are left in place.

There is little water, but its habits matter. Fine condensation gathers beneath lintels and along the seams of certain pipes. It freezes into short, cloudy teeth. Once or twice a week a thin rain falls between the buildings, sometimes without wetting the plain outside. The paving absorbs most of it. What remains drains toward a courtyard where a pool, scarcely larger than a dining table, occupies the town’s lowest point.

The pool has a stone curb, three benches, and a brass depth marker.

It is usually two fingers deep.

The marker extends down eleven meters.

On cold afternoons, something resembling a reflection of the district appears far below the bottom. Its roofs are flatter; its streets are wider; a little steam rises from chimneys no one has built. This does not draw a crowd. A caretaker comes with a long-handled scraper and removes the clear ice before it can thicken. He lays the pieces in a covered cart. You can sometimes see staircases in them.

The town’s machinery is compact and densely distributed. There are no monumental reactors or glowing towers. Power arrives through ceramic rods no thicker than pencils, bundled beneath the eaves. Junction boxes are lined with a black material that exposes too much depth when opened. The repair crews wear gloves with one extra finger, kept folded back unless needed.

Heating is expensive here, though energy is not. The difficulty is persuading warmth to remain associated with a particular room. Kitchens manage best. A stove used every day gradually establishes a reliable region around itself: the table, two chairs, the hook where a towel hangs. Unoccupied rooms chill rapidly, even with their heaters running. Residents close them off with thick curtains and eat close together.

At one end of the square stands a public warming cabinet. People put their hands through felt sleeves in its front. Inside, unseen, there is a summer afternoon extending for about sixty centimeters in every direction. It smells faintly of hot grass. The cabinet has been in service for thirty-one years, and the afternoon is approaching four o’clock.

There are plants, but you notice them individually.

A wiry shrub grows from a crack beside the laundry. Its leaves are dark, small, and folded tightly along their midribs. Meltwater from an exhaust pipe reaches its roots for a few minutes each morning. Someone has built a little iron guard around it, not against animals but against alterations to the alley. The guard establishes that this patch of ground must continue to be here.

Farther in, under a glazed passage, shallow trays hold moss the color of old green wool. It grows in the condensation from a bakery’s cooling equipment. The baker trims it with scissors and packs the cuttings around vulnerable valves. A few pale shoots have emerged beside the courtyard pool. Nobody has identified them yet. Their roots show through the paving as narrow dark lines, although the paving is not transparent.

Birds rarely enter. When one does, the close walls seem to confuse it less than the open sky. It lands, turns its head, and flies down a covered street rather than up.

There is also a small gray animal in the service passages. You might mistake it for a long-bodied rat until it pauses across a threshold and its front and rear halves remain in different stages of movement. It feeds on the brittle residue scraped from electrical contacts. Only one has been seen at a time. There may be one, or the maintenance network may supply several appearances of it. The workers leave it alone.

For so much habitation, the district is quiet. Footsteps carry briefly, then stop at a corner even when the walker does not. Doors close with padded clicks. Every so often a utensil rings against a sink, unexpectedly clear. Beneath these domestic sounds is a low, intermittent ticking from the foundations, heard better through a wall than through the air.

That is the ground becoming load-bearing.

Nothing happens quickly enough to watch with confidence. A passage grows narrower over a season. A building gains the need for an additional stair. One morning, a tenant finds that a window has always looked into a small courtyard, and spends the next week troubled by an unexplained memory of distance.

The municipal surveyors make their rounds carrying folded paper plans. Electronic maps adapt too readily. The paper records disagreements: a pencil line where the western wall stood last year, a stain marking a water main that now supplies an address rather than a location. At uncertain corners the surveyors nail down broad metal plates and stamp them with the date. Residents step over these carefully.

Near the northern boundary, the houses become shallower. Rooms end just beyond their furniture. A cupboard may contain only the fronts of its shelves. Here the outer wall is faced on both sides, but in places has no thickness between them, and the neighboring doorways are kept locked.

A narrow observation platform overlooks the plain. From it, you can see how little land has formed around the town: the unrounded ridges, the scattered grit, the delicate first marks of runoff. Close to the wall, the stone is solid. Farther away, its surface has a faint nap, like paper. At the horizon, a low ridge occasionally shows through another ridge in front of it.

Behind you, someone opens a bakery door. Warm air reaches the platform, carrying the smell of yeast and a little moisture, then dwindles.

Below, along the base of the wall, that moisture settles into a seam. By morning it will be ice. In time it will widen the seam, loosen a grain, move it a few centimeters downhill.

For now the grain is still attached.
