High above the last cultivated terrace, in a town built from the same brown stone as its mountain, there is a space behind a fallen lintel. From the lane it looks like a dark joint in the wall. You would have to kneel, turn your head sideways, and wait for your eyes to adjust before you saw how much was living there.

The whole place is scarcely wider than an outstretched arm.

Its floor is fine, warm dust, banked against the back wall and combed into little ridges near the entrance. Nothing here is sharp. The lintel has lost its corners; the old chisel marks have softened into shallow hollows that hold pollen. Even the broken pottery buried in the floor has rounded edges. The mountain’s stone was worn down and compressed and lifted long before anyone quarried it. Now the blocks are slowly losing the distinction between worked surface and natural rock. A thumb-sized arch, built accidentally where two fragments lean together, resembles the larger doorways outside, though it is likely to outlast several of them.

Beyond the opening, the town steps down a dry mountainside. Flat roofs, parapets, narrow stairs: almost everything is below this height. But from inside the recess there is no panorama, only a bright, crooked strip of sky and the underside of a roof beam.

It is late afternoon. The beam ticks as it cools. The stones have not begun to cool at all.

There is no wet surface anywhere. No seep, no water vessel, no dark patch beneath a leaf. Dust remains loose even in the deepest shade. Yet the back half of the recess is crowded with plants.

They grow from the wall joints, where their roots can follow cracks into the thicker masonry. Little stonecrops press against one another in cushions of blunt green fingers, red at the tips nearest the light. A houseleek has wedged itself into the neck of a broken jar; its offspring spill over the rim in a chain of tight rosettes. There are wiry stems with almost no leaves, small pale flowers held well away from the fleshy growth below, and a low mat of grey foliage so densely felted that dust rests on it without reaching the green.

The plants have stored the brief spring rains in themselves. By this season their skins are drawn close, their growth slow, but from the entrance they still give an impression of extravagant leaf upon leaf. Old leaves remain beneath the living ones as dry papery skirts. Seed husks lodge between them. A dead stalk becomes a bridge, then a scaffold, then the support for another generation of webs.

Almost none of this vegetation is unoccupied.

At first the ants are the only animals you notice. A dark line crosses the lintel’s inner face, disappears behind a rosette, and emerges on the floor. Look longer and the line resolves into several kinds of traffic. Large workers carry pale fragments of insect shell. Smaller ones run empty-mouthed against them. Two struggle with a seed that catches at every grain of grit; a third arrives, pulls in the wrong direction, and leaves. Where the path passes under a fallen leaf, the leaf trembles continuously.

There are mites on the stalks. Beetles under the leaf skirts. A narrow wasp backs out of a hole in the mortar, turns in the air, and goes straight back in. Several other holes have been closed with plugs of earth, each plug a slightly different colour according to where its maker found the material. One is being opened from within.

The faint scratching there goes on for a long time.

Overhead, the underside of the timber is furred with old silk. Fresh webs occupy the spaces between the abandoned ones. Tiny spiders sit at their centres, while a larger jumping spider moves across the beam without using a web at all. It stops at each knot and lifts its front legs to investigate the next dark depression.

A gecko lies farther back, almost indistinguishable from the plaster. Only its throat moves. Around it, insects pass through a zone that is safe until, abruptly, it is not.

The enclosure makes small sounds surprisingly large. A beetle falling from the beam lands with a distinct tap. Dry leaves scrape together when something pushes beneath them. The wasps produce an intermittent, close-bodied buzz that changes pitch as they enter their holes. Above the recess is a pigeon chamber, one of six built into the wall with flat stones and mud mortar. The birds walk heavily on its floor. Their claws rasp; their wings strike the sides when they turn. A low coo fills the cavity as if the stone itself had begun to sound.

Feathers descend through a crack. So do flakes of nesting material and grains of spilled seed. This slow untidy rain feeds much of the life below.

The building was made with simple tools and has been repaired with simpler ones. A split twig reinforces a patch of plaster. Someone pushed a shard into a gap too wide for mortar alone. Straw shows in an old repair, and each exposed end has been chewed, bored, or stripped. Human maintenance has left a succession of habitats: a smooth patch for the ants’ road, a hollow behind a loose stone, a sheltered shelf where the pigeons cannot reach. The most recent repair is already inhabited.

Toward evening, the activity changes without diminishing. The ants shift their route away from the cooling entrance. A small moth emerges from under the jar rim, walks up a stem, and hangs there while its wings expand. The wasp at the sealed hole finally breaks through. For a moment only its face is visible in the ragged opening; then it hauls itself out, leaving crumbs of the plug on the sill.

Outside, footsteps pass along the lane. A wooden latch falls. Someone sweeps a roof, and dust drifts across the strip of sky.

Inside, the gecko moves forward by the length of one foot.

The recess has survived summers in which the exposed plants shrank almost to nothing, winters when snow lay briefly on the roof, and years when nobody repaired the pigeon chambers. Its floor rises by amounts too small to notice: a little powdered mortar, a little leaf, the remains of innumerable meals. Lower passages close. New ones open above them. Roots enter the buried jar through a crack in its base.

When the last light leaves the lintel, warmth still comes out of the wall. The green cushions become dark masses, touching and overlapping. Between them the narrow paths remain busy. Somewhere beneath the dust, something loosens a grain of stone, pushes it into an older tunnel, and continues digging.
