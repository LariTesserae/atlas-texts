The hollow is behind a loose panel in the uppermost waterworks, high enough that clouds sometimes pass below its drain. From the maintenance side, it looks like a defect: a bowed grille, a brown seam, a little water finding its way out around a fastening. There is room to put an arm through. Beyond that, room to crawl, and then enough space to stand with your shoulders touching both walls.

It is very loud inside.

The sound is not the splash of the water you can see. It comes through the structure: a continuous, crushing roar from the main circulation shaft, with a higher rasp that shifts whenever a valve opens far below. Loose things cannot remain loose here. A dropped washer walks across the floor. Water trembles in its shallow basins, every surface stippled by vibration. Even the dark has the restless quality of something being shaken.

The hollow is perhaps four paces long, though its folds make this difficult to judge. Its floor descends in rounded shelves toward a narrow outlet. Overhead, thick conduits cross and recross, almost touching, leaving low passages between their undersides and the shelves. Everything is warm. Some surfaces are warm enough that the hand draws back; others carry a film of cooler water, freshly condensed.

There is no rock. The shelves are old structural ceramic, once part of a precisely stepped assembly. Their corners have worn into lips. Water has cut finger-width gullies through the softer joint compound, exposing dark reinforcement that curves across the channels like roots. The pale material between these ribs has a fine, sugary texture where it stays damp. Elsewhere it is polished to the smoothness of a used stair.

Several generations of protective coating lie exposed in the gullies: cream, gray, a thin rust-red layer, then cream again. None was intended to become a surface.

Water arrives everywhere but seldom in quantity. It beads on the conduits, creeps along seams, wells from a crack at ankle height. One droplet grows for a long time beneath a coupling before falling onto the same small patch of floor. There is a hollow there, deeper than the surrounding wear, with a single black pellet rolling in it. Every drop knocks the pellet up the side; every pause lets it settle. Over the years, the two have worked together.

The main stream is no wider than a hand. It runs down three shelves, spreads into a shallow pan, and disappears beneath a swollen gasket. Its apparent direction changes near the back wall. There, the water climbs a short section of channel to reach the drain.

This is a remnant of the station’s weight-management system. The field boundary has drifted since the hollow was built, and what was once a level conduit now contains a little uphill reach. Nothing here hesitates at it. Water animals swim through the bend. Sediment collects along the line where weight changes direction. A small snail grazing across that line adjusts its foot, almost imperceptibly, and continues.

Light enters through the drain.

Not much: a weak silver wash that brightens when cloud gathers outside and fades when the surrounding air clears. In that light, the lowest basin looks deeper than it is. Occasionally a moving shadow crosses its surface, cast by something enormous on the exterior of the waterworks, but the opening is too narrow to show what passed.

Closer to the entrance, a failed service strip gives off a dull amber glow. Its transparent cover is furred with mineral deposits. Tiny insects shelter behind it, their bodies making a row of dark commas against the light.

At first, the hollow seems nearly bare of life. There are only two patches of vegetation: a short, stiff turf rooted in the porous insulation above the warmest pipe, and several translucent leaves growing flat against a wet wall. The leaves do not reach toward the drain. They follow the amber service strip, each new one slightly nearer to it. Their oldest edges have been neatly eaten away.

Once your eyes adjust, the animals become difficult to separate from the surfaces.

Flat brown grazers crowd the undersides of the ledges. Pale segmented bodies thread through the moss. Along the mineral crusts, little jointed creatures stand so closely together that their legs resemble a fringe. Some remain still for minutes, then all turn at once when a richer pulse of water comes through. They feed with feathery mouthparts held into the flow.

The basin has its own depth of activity. Transparent swimmers flick between settled flakes of coating. Dark larvae anchor themselves beneath the inlet, flexing against the current. A predator the length of a thumbnail waits inside a discarded valve sleeve. Its forelimbs are folded against its face. When it strikes, the sleeve rocks once on the ceramic floor.

Above the basin, small winged animals cling in overlapping layers to the dry backs of pipes. There are hundreds. Their folded wings shine softly, like old varnish. They launch themselves only for short flights: across the water to seize an insect, down to drink from a bead, back beneath the pipe. The air is crowded, but no flight is long enough to become a journey.

They cannot call over the machinery. Courtship and warning pass through the walls instead. A male taps a sequence into a conduit with his hind feet; nearby bodies lift and settle in response. The tapping is too slight to hear. If you rest your fingertips on the pipe, you can feel it inside the larger vibration.

The hollow has weather, though it has no sky of its own.

During the afternoon circulation peak, the pipes grow hotter and the upper shelves dry. The grazers withdraw beneath the lips. Wings gather on the cooler side of the chamber. Then a purge cycle sends cold water through the overhead lines, and condensation thickens until it falls in a brief, heavy shower. Dust accumulated in the dry hours washes into the basin. Everything comes out to feed.

These changes have repeated long enough to shape where bodies wait, where eggs are laid, which seams remain open. The station still repairs its principal structures, but this cavity falls between its tolerances. A slow repair film advances a few millimeters from one fastening, sealing hairline cracks. Grazers scrape its softened edge. Water opens a neighboring seam. The hollow keeps approximately the same shape.

Near the drain, the floor is cooler. You can crouch there and look out through the narrow slot at a strip of white cloud, astonishingly far beneath you. Warm runoff slips past your shoes, climbs its modest rise, and spills into open air.

Behind you, in the crowded heat, the pipes change pitch. The first cold beads appear overhead. Small feet begin moving across every ledge.
