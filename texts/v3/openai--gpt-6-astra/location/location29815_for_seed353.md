The ground gives a little underfoot, then hardens around the impression.

It is hot enough that you cannot leave your hand against it. Across the plateau, the surface has the dull polish of wood handled for centuries: black, brown, a deep red visible only where something has recently split. Broad seams curve through it. Some are stitched shut with roots as fine as wire; others are opening, very slowly, to expose layers of pale compressed fiber. Warm air rises from these openings, carrying the smell of scorched cork and bitter resin.

There is no soil. Beneath the walking surface are older walking surfaces, folded galleries, bundled trunks, the flattened remains of chambers. The plateau was built upward from these things. Its edges stand high above a basin you cannot see into, and its foundations extend down through the darkness in close-set columns. Nothing exposed is stone. Even the grit that collects in the seams consists of seed coats, carbonized pollen, and little hard granules shed by the machinery.

From a rise near the edge, you can make out the country’s arrangement. It extends for several miles: low ridges, shallow depressions, long smooth embankments, occasional blunt towers. Everything has been worn toward the same soft profile. Towers have rounded crowns. Embankments join the ground without a sharp angle. What might once have been a monumental staircase now descends in slopes so shallow that a step takes several paces to cross.

The place is old enough to have lost the shapes it was built in.

It has not lost the ability to build.

Beyond the nearest ridge, a tract of ground lifts. No crash accompanies it. The surface stretches, grows taut, and rises into an elongated mound perhaps the length of a street. Adjacent ridges lean aside to make room. On the mound’s far slope, a row of narrow apertures opens in sequence, each showing a brief line of dark orange. Heat rolls across the plateau. Then the apertures seal and the whole formation begins to settle, leaving two new embankments where there had been one.

This happens elsewhere while you watch. A tower shortens. A depression fills from below. A dark band travels across a hillside as thousands of overlapping plates turn edge-on. The landscape changes too continuously to be counted, yet its broad silhouette remains almost the same.

There is no vacant ground on which to put a new thing. Every addition must pass its displaced volume onward.

Much of that motion takes place beneath the surface. The ridges are pressure mains, their walls made of interwoven living trunks. The shallow basins are compression beds. The towers carry exhaust through layers of folded leaf, stripping out heat and useful gases before releasing what remains. Thick roots run between them in ordered bundles. Where a bundle enters a junction, it divides into smaller and smaller strands until it becomes a dense, velvety lining.

The factories were grown to their dimensions. Their tolerances are maintained by growth.

Inside a broken inspection slit, you can see a shaft revolving in a wooden bearing. It turns slowly, under immense load. Its surface is slick with a transparent wax that does not run even in this heat. As the shaft wears, the bearing adds new cells. As those cells thicken, a ring of rasping fibers trims them back. Fine shavings fall into an intake below.

There are no workers. There are no deserted workstations, either. The machinery has never needed a place for someone to stand.

The plants here have almost nothing of a forest about them. They do not reach toward light. Their leaves are buried in exchange stacks, pressed into conductive sheets, wrapped around hot conduits. Their flowers occur inside closed chambers, where pollen passes between fitted surfaces without exposure to the air. The few structures that open above ground are valves and heat vanes. They unfold, perform their task, and return to the mass.

Green is a color of illness. Occasionally it appears along a damaged seam, where an old chemistry has been accidentally awakened. The surrounding tissue closes over it.

Water survives only in minute, guarded quantities. There is no dew, no damp underside to lift, no coolness below the bark. A rupture produces dust or a sticky filament of resin. The active tissues hold their fluids in microscopic compartments, renewing them from gases recovered deep within the works. Most transport is accomplished by hot oils, electrical gradients, and muscular contractions that move dry material from chamber to chamber.

A seed is scarcely more than a sealed instruction. It waits inside a socket until the socket becomes vacant.

The darkness makes distances uncertain. Overhead there may be cloud, or another layer of construction, or simply a sky without useful light. A few points glow overhead, but they shift too regularly to be stars. Along the horizon, exhaust vanes sometimes show a red margin. These offer just enough illumination to reveal how crowded the plateau is.

Every apparent gap contains something finer.

Between two buttresses lie vertical curtains of root. Between their strands hangs a mesh of hairlike shoots. Across that mesh are membranes thin enough to tremble in the convection currents. Their surfaces are stippled with buds, each bud fitted against its neighbors. Even an abandoned borehole will contain a soft plug of new growth several feet inside.

The air passages remain open only because their walls keep withdrawing.

This makes travel peculiar. A route is not a permanent absence of obstruction. It is a maintained sequence of permissions. A broad lane can close behind you without any visible barrier moving across it: the walls simply swell, their layered edges meeting at the center. Elsewhere, a passage opens as a hundred narrow ribs fold flush into their housings.

The safest ground is often the most heavily worn. Along certain routes, generations of maintenance growth have produced a recognizable texture, a fine crosshatching that grips the foot. These paths follow the old load-bearing lines. They rise and fall with the working terrain, sometimes carrying you gently sideways. To leave them is to risk stepping onto a panel that has already begun to turn.

Despite all this activity, the place is mostly quiet.

There is a low strain in the ground, felt through the legs. Close to a seam, you hear fibers sliding over fibers. A valve releases a short dry breath. Once in a while a brittle surface breaks with a sound like a fingernail pressed through paper. The dense construction absorbs almost everything. Great displacements occur within padded walls. Pressure is handed from one living chamber to the next without an impact.

At intervals, the quiet becomes complete. The vibration stops. Heat still rises, but every visible vane holds still.

Then a pulse arrives from below.

You feel it first in the soles of your feet. A ridge tightens, and the tension runs away into the distance. Vents open across the plateau in branching lines. Far off, entire districts heave and rearrange themselves, their dim red interiors exposed for a second before the outer layers draw shut. The pulse passes beneath you and continues toward the edge, where the foundation columns bend together by an almost imperceptible amount.

The works have completed another stroke.

What they manufacture is difficult to separate from what they are. In the lower chambers, heat is converted into pressure, pressure into current, current into organized tissue. Finished beams are carried upward inside hollow trunks. Insulating bark is rolled into thick sheets. New pump organs mature in rows, already connected to the pipes they will serve. Worn components are reduced to dust and gas and fed back through the growing beds.

There may once have been another product. A few of the oldest embankments terminate at sealed loading mouths facing the empty basin. Their lips have fused. Their approaches have been consumed by more recent construction.

Now the output is absorbed entirely by repair, replacement, expansion inward.

Near one of those mouths, the wind has polished a stretch of dead outer bark until its grain stands in long, low ripples. You can follow individual fibers across the rounded surface. They curve around an ancient bolt hole, merge, divide, and disappear beneath living tissue. Above them, younger layers are creeping forward at a pace too slow to notice. Below, something is being dismantled much faster.

A warm flake loosens beside your foot. Before it has finished curling, fine roots emerge from the exposed layer and fasten it down. Far across the plateau, a blunt tower sinks from view. The ground under you inclines slightly toward the space it has left.

For a moment, the oldest polished ridges catch the exhaust glow. Then the valves close, and you stand in the dark, listening to the faint, close sound of the path adjusting.
