The hollow is small enough that you can see its far boundary through the rain: a low bank of pale stone, a little higher than a person, with water sliding down its face. On this side, the bank has worn almost level with the ground. You enter without quite noticing, stepping from warm, coarse grass onto a surface that looks dry until water gathers around your foot.

There is no stream feeding the hollow. Water rises through the floor in broad, slow pulses. For an hour it may lie ankle-deep between the stone shelves; then it withdraws, leaving small creatures stranded only briefly, already making their way toward the next depression. The rhythm does not match the tide, and the nearest sea is many days away.

At midday, everything is bright. Clouds pass thinly over the sun, whitening it rather than hiding it. The wet stone gives back so much light that the undersides of leaves are illuminated. Shade beneath the few trees is green and warm, with none of the usual relief of darkness. Even the water looks lit from below, though this is mostly the pale sediment, fine as flour, that has collected in basins too shallow to cast a shadow.

The rock has no sharp edges. There are places where it folds up into something like a knee or the back of a sleeping animal, but even these shapes have become uncertain. What were once separate layers have weathered into a single yielding-looking surface. A fallen branch can make a deeper impression in the eye than the oldest ridge.

Here and there, dark pebbles sit embedded in the pale floor. They are the last hard parts of something the size of a mountain.

Vegetation grows in separated gatherings. One clump of broad leaves occupies a whole shelf, each leaf large enough to shelter a crouching person. Across several yards of open water stands a tree with seven trunks, its roots exposed in a low, complicated arch. Farther on is a patch of fleshy grass, intensely green, leaning outward from a dry center. Between these islands there is space: stone, luminous mud, shallow water.

Up close, the islands are lush almost beyond distinction. Ferns root in the forks of other ferns. Creepers pass through the dead centers of older creepers, using them as conduits. The tree’s trunks wear a soft coat of minute plants that open when wet and fold when merely damp. Fallen leaves do not accumulate. Their margins are eaten while their middles are still green.

At first you might think there are few animals.

Then the surface of one pool moves against the wind.

What seemed to be sediment is a congregation of translucent bodies, each no longer than a fingernail, arranged head to tail in loose spirals. They lift together when the water rises. Beneath them are flat, copper-colored grazers. Beneath those, visible where a footstep has disturbed the bottom, pale worms work through the mineral flour in such numbers that the exposed patch closes like an eye.

The animals do not fill the open air. They occupy its boundaries. Every waterline has a fringe of legs. Every underside is crowded. The clean trunks are clean because soft-mouthed creatures travel over them continuously, removing growth almost as soon as it forms.

On a single broad leaf, hundreds of clear, jointed animals rest with their limbs aligned along the veins. You see them only when one turns and the reflected sun passes from body to body. The leaf briefly glitters, then becomes an ordinary leaf again.

There are larger creatures too. Something broad and gray lifts out of a pool that appeared only inches deep. Water drains from the folds beside its head. It has small eyes set far apart, a blunt mouth, and four limbs that unfold with surprising delicacy. It walks to the next basin, pausing to let a procession of young animals pass beneath its belly.

The basin it leaves behind is shallow. You can see the bottom.

Three structures stand in the hollow.

The first resembles a roof without a room: a thin, cream-colored slab held above the ground by two unequal supports. Its edges are rounded by age, or were made that way. Rain passes through the slab but slows inside it, each drop appearing briefly as a dark bead before emerging underneath. In dry weather, the slab continues raining for several hours.

Animals gather below it well before the sky clears.

The second structure is a ring set flush into the stone. Nothing grows across its circumference, although roots approach it on every side. Once in a while, an insect crossing the ring disappears for the width of a finger and returns farther along its course. Water does the same. A thread of runoff enters one side and comes out at the other, warmer, carrying grains of black sand.

The third is scarcely a building anymore. Four low walls enclose a room open to the sky, with a doorway too narrow for the gray pool animals. There is a bench along one wall and a square hollow in the floor. The bench is dry in all weather. If you sit there, the humid air slowly loosens around your skin.

Inside the square hollow lies a heap of broken shells.

Some are recent. Others have become stone. Their shapes repeat through the layers with small alterations: a ridge migrating, a hinge thickening, the same delicate point disappearing and returning. Whatever uses this room has used it for a very long time.

There are no labels, lights, or visible controls. The structures do not hum. Their materials have survived long enough to acquire habitats of their own. Tiny burrowing animals enter holes in the supports of the rain roof; the holes close behind them and reopen elsewhere. On hot afternoons the roof bends slightly toward the tree. Whether it is measuring something, resting, or following a much older instruction is impossible to tell.

The hollow is mostly quiet.

You hear a leaf tip releasing water. A soft drag as a large body crosses wet rock. Now and then, a small hard click comes from the shell room. There is no sustained insect whine, no chorus from the pools. The few flying animals have broad, fringed wings and travel low, slipping from one bright patch of vegetation to another.

Communication happens in brief changes of color, in tremors through roots, in scents that arrive distinctly and vanish: bruised citrus, warm iron, the sweetness of a cut stem. A disturbance can pass across the whole hollow without making an audible sound. The translucent creatures flatten. The grazers stop moving. Every leaf-borne animal turns toward the same empty stretch of bank.

Then something beneath that bank shifts, and the water begins to rise.

By late afternoon, the stone shelves are islands. The tree’s exposed roots disappear one by one. Small animals climb the supports of the roof, while others descend them to meet the water. The gray creature settles into a basin and becomes difficult to distinguish from its rim. Across the hollow, flowers open close to the surface, their heavy petals resting on the water rather than lifting into the air.

For a few minutes, their reflections make the place seem much deeper.

The bank is still visible all around. You could walk its circuit before the light changes. Yet the water keeps bringing things up: a soft white egg, a frond with no apparent stem, a little animal carrying another inside its transparent body. Each finds a place almost immediately. The egg catches among roots. The frond spreads flat. The transparent animal descends beneath the flowers.

On the dry bench, a bead of moisture forms beside your hand. It stays perfectly round. Long after the rising water has covered the doorway, it begins to roll toward the shells.
