At first there seems to be no ground, only a warm dimness and, very far below, another warm dimness with a green cast to it. Something broad passes overhead. It takes several minutes to recognize the movement as the turning of a leaf.

You are standing on its root.

The root is wide enough to have its own horizon. Its upper surface has been planed, scored, and trained into a shallow crown, so that water runs toward either edge. Underfoot, the grain is soft and longitudinal, polished by passage and by the slow migration of the surface itself. Here and there a cut made centuries ago has grown into a low ridge. Rain gathers behind these ridges in narrow, warm pools. Small transparent animals graze upside down beneath the water, their feet dimpling the surface without breaking it.

There is no soil. The dark matter in the grooves is powdered bark, shed skins, pollen, and the remains of things that lived here. Beneath that lies living fiber; beneath the fiber, larger fiber. If you cut down far enough, you would eventually come out into air.

Most of the world is air.

Across it run the works: root causeways, woven slopes, great bent ribs of wood carrying sheets of foliage. The intervals between them are enormous. A neighboring span may look near enough to reach by afternoon and remain in the same place in the view for the rest of your life. It is difficult to judge distance because the atmosphere is full of moisture, and because certain faraway structures enlarge as they recede. The old routes allow for this. Their makers knew which crossings had to be approached sideways.

Nothing visible is unworked.

The smallest tendril is likely to bear a knot. The broad walls hanging beyond the causeway have seams, each seam a line of grafts, each graft once held together by a splinter and a strip of wet bast. Even the smooth undersides of leaves show the faint square impressions of frames that guided their expansion. What appears from a distance to be a mountain is a stack of living mats, pinned at the edges and left to thicken. Its rounded summit was laid out with stakes. Its valleys began as drainage cuts.

The tools used to make all this could fit in a basket: wedges, scrapers, bone needles, a stone blade with a thumb-worn handle. The immensity came from repetition, inheritance, and the willingness to begin a structure whose useful shape would not appear for forty generations.

Some of those structures are still being begun.

On the margin of the root, where the surface curves away into the void, a row of pale shoots has been bent through little wooden hoops. Each carries two leaves and a drop of reddish sap. Their shadows extend in the wrong direction. Far out in the haze, occupying the space these shoots have been trained toward, stands a faint, mature arcade.

It is visible only in damp weather.

The people who tend the shoots say this means the bindings are holding.

***

The height is apparent in the behavior of breath. You can walk comfortably on the level, but an incline soon brings a thin ache behind the eyes. Small cuts heal slowly. A flying animal that strays too far from the living surfaces must return and cling there, sides working, until it recovers. The green structures breathe a heavier air along their skins, and nearly everything inhabits this shallow layer.

Beyond it, emptiness.

There are creatures that cross the emptiness, though not by ordinary flight. They unfold membranes almost without substance and let themselves be taken by the differences between one span’s warmth and another’s. Seen from beneath, they resemble flaws in the gloom. Some will spend a year crossing a single hollow. Their young hatch along the way, feeding on the parents’ fringes, and arrive at the destination before the bodies that carried them.

It is warm despite the altitude. Heat comes through the floor in broad, uneven patches. Where old grafts meet, the wood can be hot enough to dry cloth. Elsewhere a cool sweat forms continually on the grain. The air smells of bruised stems, fermented fruit, and the mineral tang of sap that has circulated through living tissue for too long.

There is very little open water. Water is too valuable, and an unbroken sheet of it too heavy. Instead it beads, films, drips, and travels in the woven capillaries under the paths. It collects in the cups of joined leaves. It hangs in long pockets beneath platforms, visible through green membranes, where fishlike larvae turn slowly among pale roots.

After rain, the entire place leaks for days.

A bead swells beneath a crossing, trembles, and falls. You follow it with your eyes until it vanishes. Much later, from somewhere below, comes a sound too soft to establish whether it was a landing.

The rain itself is made here. Moisture rises from the lower works, cools beneath the high leaf vaults, and returns along the same worn belts. Some causeways have been under a fine drizzle for thousands of years. Their sides are rounded and furred with miniature plants, each rooted in the decay of the larger one. Their carved gutters have shifted as they grew, becoming deep, comfortable channels with soft lips.

Elsewhere it rains upward. Under certain floors, droplets lift from hanging reservoirs and hurry into the breathing pores above. Animals shelter on the dry undersides of branches during these showers. A traveler learns to check both faces of a leaf before choosing a place to sleep.

***

The light reaches this altitude from somewhere higher still.

You never see its source. Layers of trained foliage intercept it, turn it, spend it. What comes down is olive-colored and diffuse, except along an occasional cut in the ceiling where a narrow amber brightness descends for hundreds of miles. Around these light shafts the architecture becomes finer. Platforms extend inward on elaborate braces. Flowers open on long stalks over the brink, each trying to take a little more light without casting too much shade on what supports it.

The oldest regulations concern these openings.

You may prune your own shade. You may not move another span’s morning. A new walkway must leave enough light beneath it for the old one to heal. No fruit may be harvested from a load-bearing branch until the fruit has cast its second shadow.

The rules are maintained with cords tied in particular patterns at junctions. Some junctions have so many cords that the living branch has grown around them, preserving the law as a hard interior tangle. When a crossing begins to fail, tenders tap its surface with sticks and listen for trapped knots.

That is one of the few deliberate sounds.

The place is otherwise remarkably quiet for the amount of life it holds. Soft materials drink up footsteps. Wings are broad and fringed. The grazers have padded mouths and peel their food rather than bite it. Even a breaking limb often gives only a low, prolonged sigh, the surrounding fibers taking its weight as it separates.

There are abundant animals, but they are distributed along surfaces so thinly spread through the immense volume that you can look outward for hours and see none. Then you put your hand on a rail and find it warm with a procession of tiny bodies.

Flat, many-jointed browsers move through the bark creases. Their backs carry gardens of minute red leaves. Pale climbing beasts sleep inside folds of the great walls, fastened by all four feet, their long tails tucked against their stomachs. At the edges of pools, predatory flowers and flower-colored predators wait beside one another. One has roots; the other occasionally changes pools.

Far below, a herd travels along the inside of an arch.

You notice them first as a gradual change in its color. There are thousands: broad-backed, moss-coated animals, each taking a few steps and then pausing to feed. The whole procession moves with the deliberation of shade crossing a room. Their weight bends the arch by a degree too small to see, but water begins flowing through a different gutter at your feet.

By evening, a dry district several days away will receive rain.

***

Age is written here in softened geometries.

No sharp corner lasts. A flight of steps becomes a ribbed slope. A square doorway becomes an oval, then a slit, and must be opened again. Abandoned baskets merge with the shoots stored inside them. Handrails thicken until they are walls; walls split along their old weaving and become colonnades.

The landscape has been repaired so often that repairs are its principal material. Every broad surface contains buried ladders. Every easy road has narrower roads folded within it, the paths of the people who tended its first growth. At exposed edges you can see the succession: a wrist-thick vine, a braid of roots, a lattice, a solid living beam, another lattice strapped around a wound.

There are no ruins in the usual sense. There are works no longer being persuaded to keep their shape.

In those regions, floors curl toward the light. Rooms slowly turn inside out. A reservoir lifts its rim and becomes a grove of drinking stems. Sometimes an entire district folds shut, quietly and over several seasons, with its paths and feeding grounds and abandoned tools enclosed. The outer skin continues to flower.

Travelers leave such places early. The animals generally leave later.

At the boundary of a closing district, you might find fresh ladder notches cut into a stem that otherwise shows no sign of habitation. Someone has been up to check the bindings. Someone has decided there is another year.

***

As the light weakens, the high leaves begin their slow turn.

You feel it first through the soles of your feet: a steady drawing tension, followed by release. Across the gulf, enormous planes of foliage tip toward the last brightness. Warm air spills from their upper surfaces. Mist moves through the open works in long, level streams, catching on the fine branches and leaving them strung with water.

The distances alter at this hour.

A landing that was visible all day disappears behind an interval of space. A remote woven slope comes close enough for you to distinguish its individual knots. Paths do not always shorten with it. There are bridges whose two ends spend the night in different weather, and one old stair that must be descended before dusk because afterward its lower steps belong to the following morning.

Along the safe routes, seedpods begin to glow. They give barely enough light to show the next handhold.

The transparent pool animals turn upright. Small hunters emerge from the drainage grooves. Somewhere in the vastness a tender strikes a hollow stem three times, waits, then strikes it once more. The signal arrives through the living floor long before you hear it in the air.

Under your feet the root goes on growing, carrying the shallow pools with it, rounding the oldest cuts, closing around a wooden peg driven by a hand that has been gone for centuries.

And beyond its edge, in the warm, nearly empty dark, the next span is being drawn out: a few taut fibers, wet at their tips, extending toward supports that are not yet there.
