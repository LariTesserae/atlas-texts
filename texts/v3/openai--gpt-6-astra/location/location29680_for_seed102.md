Beneath the salt floor, no deeper than a buried hand, there is a hollow the size of a small room. Its entrance is a split between two slabs, narrow enough to overlook, black enough to mistake for a stain. Warmth comes through it without a draft. Put an ear near the opening and the ground sounds crowded.

Inside, there is room to sit, but nowhere to stand.

The ceiling is low and faceted, the underside of a crystal bed that has separated along fresh planes. Some edges are thin enough to be translucent. They hold a dull apricot light whose source is hidden farther inside the salt. Nothing drips. There are no stains left by dripping, no rounded channels, no sediment arranged by an old current. The hollow has never held water. Its floor consists of pale, angular grains, each resting where the stone first released it. Even the dust has corners.

Across this dry floor grow five luxuriant thickets.

They are separated by broad patches of bare mineral, so that each has its own space, its own complicated silhouette. From the entrance they resemble plants seen in the last light of evening: broad leaves crowded around thick stems, hanging tassels, pale new growth folded tightly at the tips. Nearer, their substance is less familiar. The leaves are flexible sheets of dark graphite, with undersides the color of old copper. At their edges, fine transparent teeth interlock without touching. The stems divide into fluted columns, hollow and warm.

There is no sap. A broken stem would show dry chambers partitioned with gold-colored membranes.

Their roots enter the salt along boundaries between crystals. What feeds them is held there: tiny differences in the way adjacent faces meet, pressures trapped when the surrounding bed formed. The plants have grown around these small reserves with extraordinary extravagance. A root no thicker than a hair supports a fan of leaves wider than a face. Nothing needs to be carried upward. The root and leaf share the same strain, as the two ends of a bent bow share one tension.

They do not grow now.

Every curled tip has reached the exact degree of openness the hollow permits. Each fruit—a ribbed, papery thing enclosed in a cage of bracts—is full, but will not fall. The thickets have the abundance of midsummer without its urgency. There are no dead leaves underneath them.

Most of the room is empty.

This becomes apparent only after the foliage has drawn the eye for a while: the clean breadth of floor, the low ceiling extending over it, the spaces through which nothing passes. There is a little ridge near the center, freshly split, its crest as sharp as the edge of a struck flint. Beyond it, the floor drops into a shallow hexagonal basin. The basin contains neither liquid nor loose salt. It is lined with a black surface that reflects shapes but very little light. Seen in it, the plants look farther away than the room allows.

Animals occupy the undersides of the leaves.

There are hundreds in the nearest thicket alone. Flat, six-legged creatures fit between the ribs like small inlays of ivory. Others have clear shells through which a single dark coil is visible. Along one stem, a row of narrow mouths remains open against the bark. Under the largest leaf hangs something soft and many-jointed, folded around three smaller versions of itself.

They are not corpses. Their eyes have the unmistakable depth of attention. The soft animal’s skin yields slightly where its young press against it. Yet no foot shifts; no antenna completes a sweep. An ivory creature rests with its forelegs extended across a gap it has never finished crossing.

The noise is enormous.

It has none of the broken rhythm of an ordinary colony. There are no separate calls to count. Each body holds a note: a rasp, a whistle, a low wooden knocking so continuous that it has become a texture. Together they fill the hollow with a dense, hot chord. The broad leaves carry the lower sounds. The salt ceiling contributes a thin screaming overtone. Beside the central ridge, the whole floor seems to hum behind the teeth.

And still there is no vibration that a fingertip can detect.

Sound is simply one of the conditions here, like warmth or the angle of a crystal face. It does not need to be made again from moment to moment. A mouth opened once is sufficient. In the bare spaces between thickets, the chord is less crowded, and one can distinguish the deep voice of the folded animal from the sharp, almost metallic voices of the little shelled ones.

Someone has made things here.

Between the two farthest plants lies a bridge fashioned from three slivers of bone, lashed with pale fiber. It crosses a crack scarcely wider than a thumb. Its handrail is absurdly careful: a bent twig fixed at either end, polished where tiny hands might have rested. Nearby, five clay cups stand in recesses scraped into the floor. All are empty. One bears a thumbprint, and another has been repaired with a stitch of root passed through paired holes.

A comb cut from shell is lodged among the graphite leaves. Its teeth separate them into even intervals. In that thicket the sound is clearer, almost a single voice.

There is also a place to sit: a flat stone, a mat of braided husks, a slight hollow in the mat. Above it, the ceiling has been rubbed smooth. Whoever used the seat could not have raised their head fully.

Nothing is ruined. The bridge has not sagged. The exposed fibers of the mat are neither brittle nor fresh. These objects have the intimacy of things just set aside, without any promise that their maker will return.

Deeper in, the warmth settles against the face. It is the warmth of a covered kiln long after the visible fire has gone, except that here it does not diminish. There is no cooler corner, no movement of air toward the entrance. The little room is complete in its heat.

Only the split overhead connects it to weather, footsteps, the slow wearing down of the world above. From below, that opening is a crooked pale line. A single root reaches toward it and stops short, its tip enclosed in a translucent sheath.

The distance remaining is very small.

It will always be there: the pale line, the waiting tip, the untouched interval between them. Below, the five gardens hold their leaves open over the listening animals, and the empty cups stand ready in the salt.
