The shelf is small enough that a person standing at its inner wall can see all the way to its broken edge, but there is nothing beyond that edge to help the eye judge distance. Only bright air, and far below, a bank of cloud with blue hollows in it. Occasionally a mountain shows through—a dark triangle so low in the view that it seems to belong to a different country.

Up here the light arrives almost without softening. Every grain in the stone has a shadow. White feathers, scraps of old bone, the undersides of leaves: anything pale becomes difficult to look at. Even in the shelter of the wall, where the frost survives all afternoon, the shadows are lit by the immense sky.

The shelf was exposed recently. A weight of ice withdrew from it, leaving a shallow stone basin about the size of a hamlet and a lip that tilts slightly outward. There are no rounded boulders. Loose pieces have clean planes and edges sharp enough to cut a boot sole. Some fit visibly into scars in the wall above them. Others lie balanced on corners, waiting for the next thaw to make their decisions.

Most of the basin is bare.

Its emptiness is not barren in the ordinary sense. It feels occupied by distances: six paces from one flake of slate to the next, a long strip of white gravel between a green bank and its shadow. Nothing grows upright enough to interrupt the view. There are no trees, no tall shrubs, no crowded skyline. The tallest living thing is a flowering stalk, little more than a hand high, shaking where the ground appears perfectly still.

The vegetation gathers along three seams.

One follows the back wall, where condensation beads on cold stone and descends so slowly that it often freezes before reaching the soil. Another crosses the basin diagonally, following a crack too narrow to admit a finger. The third occupies a shallow depression beside the lip, fed by a thumb-sized patch of snow that persists beneath an overhang. Between them they make a broken green enclosure around the naked center.

Close to the ground, these seams are extravagant. Moss swells into cushions. Tiny willow branches creep under their own leaves, making forests no higher than a person’s ankle. There are thick-leaved plants with red rims, silver-haired rosettes, flowers whose cups turn toward the sun and close as soon as shadow touches them. Their roots occupy every available fold. Lift the margin of a cushion and there is scarcely any soil: a dark, springy weave of living fibers holds powdered rock, feathers, insect shells, and the remains of earlier leaves.

In places the green hangs over the fracture that supports it, rich enough to conceal the thinness of its foothold.

Water is present mostly as evidence. A blackened run on the wall. A clear collar around a moss stem. Damp grit under a stone whose upper face is dry and warm. At noon, droplets begin to fall beneath the snow patch. They strike the same shallow pit, irregularly, making the only sound that persists long enough to be anticipated.

There is never a stream across the whole shelf. The water disappears underground within a few feet, moving through cracks where the light cannot follow. By evening its entrances are rimmed with ice.

The animals know these wet places more precisely than any path could record them.

Small, round-eared mammals travel beneath the willow mats, lifting leaves from below. Their tunnels emerge at the bases of rocks in clean little doorways. They carry clipped stems backward into the dark and return almost at once. At first the shelf seems to contain only two or three of them. Then a shadow passes over the ground, and a dozen separate movements stop.

High above, a hawk turns without calling.

When it has gone, the work resumes.

There are birds along the outer lip: compact, pale birds with black eyes and feet the color of old horn. They settle facing the sun, each separated from the next by several yards, as though the ledge were much larger and they had no reason to gather. Beneath the rim are their nests, tucked into downward-opening cracks. Now and then a bird simply steps off. It drops out of sight and reappears a moment later far below, crossing the luminous air on stiff wings.

The apparent emptiness holds a surprising amount of life. Beetles shelter under every sun-warmed shard. Minute jumping creatures crowd the damp borders of the snow. Moths rest flat against stone, invisible until they lift and expose a brief flash of yellow beneath their wings. In the flower cups, dark flies wait for enough warmth to move. When the sun reaches a green seam, the seam begins to flicker.

All this happens quietly. The insects seldom rise into the cold air. The small mammals communicate in soft clicks that carry no farther than the neighboring cushion. A bird scratches once against rock. Somewhere under the plants, dry stems are being cut.

Near the inner wall stands a shelter made of stacked slabs.

It is too low to enter upright. The roof consists of three overlapping stones, weighted with smaller pieces, and one end is built into a natural recess. There is no chimney. A shallow hearth outside contains pale ash protected beneath an inverted bowl of rough clay. Beside it lies a wooden paddle worn thin at one edge, used for scraping snow or lifting turf; its handle has been repaired with sinew.

Whoever comes here brings fuel.

There are no stumps, no cut bushes, no heap of local branches. The creeping willows have been left intact. Even the path from the shelter to the lip avoids the green seams, making an awkward detour over sharp gravel. Three stepping stones cross the narrowest strip of moss. They are placed carefully enough to suggest repeated visits rather than permanent occupation.

Inside, the wall bears a row of short incisions. Some are fresh. Others have filled with mineral bloom. A bundle of feathers hangs from a root cord, barely moving in the draft.

The shelter faces the blank center of the basin, not the view.

By afternoon, that center becomes active in ways too small to notice all at once. Sunlight warms the upper surfaces of the slabs while their undersides remain frozen. Meltwater enters a fracture. A grain loosens. A flake tilts, then slides a finger’s width with a dry tick. Along the back wall, clear ice separates from stone and falls in fragments fine as broken glass.

The plants take advantage of each hour. Flower cups open wider. Leaves uncurl from their protective hairs. Warm air collects within the cushions, and the insects move between them in low, hurried flights. The small mammals carry fodder while keeping close to cover. At the far end of the shelf, a bank of greenery has begun to split under its own weight. White roots show in the opening, holding together two pieces of damp earth that the frost is slowly prying apart.

Nothing here is settled, though much of it is patient.

A cloud can end the afternoon in a minute. The bright stone turns gray, the air loses its borrowed warmth, and movement withdraws into cracks. Flowers close. Birds lift from the rim and vanish underneath it. Water thickens around the mouth of the little melt pool.

Sometimes the cloud passes below without touching the shelf. Then the whole place remains in sunlight above an apparently solid white plain. The shelter, the three green seams, the handful of pale birds and fresh-edged stones stand exposed in a brilliance with no visible source of comfort.

At dusk, the last lit part is a narrow band high on the inner wall. Beneath it the moss has already stiffened with frost. A final animal crosses the gravel carrying a stem longer than its body, pauses beside the stepping stones, and pulls the stem underground.

For a while there is only the occasional ticking of cooling rock.

Then, under the overhang, one more drop falls.
