Beneath the lifted root of a tree, there is a hollow no wider than a person’s shoulders. You could pass within a pace of it and see only a dark seam under the wood. Its floor lies almost level with the surrounding forest, but a low lip of fresh earth keeps fallen leaves from sliding inside.

The light comes sideways. It touches the lip, reaches a few centimeters beneath the root, and fades across a warm, bare clearing.

Everything on that clearing is newly placed.

The soil has been brought up from below in grains and soft crumbs, some pale yellow, some the deep red of the ground beneath the leaf litter. Nothing has had time to settle into a crust. Tiny heaps stand at their original angles. A damp pellet, dropped onto the side of one, sticks where it lands. Even the narrow routes between the heaps are still changing: a passage open in the morning may be banked on one side by evening, its traffic diverted around a new mound.

This is the exposed edge of an ant nest.

There are three entrances beneath the root. One is no more than a puncture. Another has a short, roofed approach made from soil packed against the underside of the wood. At the third, workers are widening the opening, emerging backward with loads held beneath their heads. They carry the excavated earth beyond the doorway before releasing it. The settlement grows by being emptied out; its visible landscape is what could not remain inside.

Between the entrances stretches a surprising amount of vacant ground. A seed husk rests in it like a large, overturned boat. Farther back, a fallen twig has been left untouched, too large to move and apparently not worth going around. The ants cross it along a polished strip of bark.

Most of the green is gathered at the margins.

On one side, a fern has rooted where the tree meets the soil. Its few fronds spread broadly enough to roof half the hollow, each leaflet heavy with the warm, dim color of plants that seldom see direct sun. Opposite it, several seedlings rise from a single pocket of black humus. Their leaves overlap lavishly, though their stems are thin and widely spaced. Moss occupies only a thumb-sized patch near the rear wall. Beyond that patch, the wood is dry enough to shed a fine brown dust when something walks across it.

Moisture arrives unevenly. After rain, water follows a groove down the living root and darkens one corner of the floor. A shallow bead gathers there, held between wood, earth, and a curled leaf. It may last until noon. Elsewhere, the new spoil remains loose and almost dry beneath its shelter.

The ants maintain their routes around the wet corner. A worker approaching with a cut fragment of leaf pauses, turns, and takes the higher ground. Behind it, others make the same adjustment until the altered path becomes clear without anything visibly having been built.

The leaf fragments are the brightest things here. Fresh green, sometimes edged with the white of a severed vein, they move across the dark soil in irregular procession. They disappear beneath the root into chambers where the colony tends its fungus. None of that pale growth is visible from the clearing. Its presence is suggested by the work: green material carried inward, exhausted scraps brought out to a separate place, workers repeatedly cleaning themselves before returning underground.

There is other life, much of it nearly still.

A tiny spider waits beneath the curve of the seed husk. Springtails gather along the damp edge of the moss and scatter when an ant passes too close. A translucent snail rests against the back of a fern stem, its shell scarcely larger than a grain of the excavated earth. Beneath one loose flake of wood, mites move through a softness of decaying fibers.

The bare spaces remain bare because they are used. A small shoot leaning into a busy entrance has been bitten through. Its two leaves lie a little way off, still fresh. At the quiet end of the hollow, another shoot of the same kind is opening undisturbed.

Almost none of this makes a sound you could hear. Occasionally a falling crumb taps a dry leaf. Water thickens at the tip of the root, lets go, and lands with a small, dull click. Beyond the hollow, the forest’s sounds arrive softened by wood and foliage.

All afternoon the ground changes. A doorway broadens. A mound lengthens. The shaded bead of water shrinks until the curled leaf lifts free of it. Under the fern, an ant struggles with a fragment too wide for the entrance, turns it twice, and draws it inside.

By dusk, there is a new ridge across the clearing, scarcely higher than the ants themselves. Its crest is sharp and loose. The last light catches individual grains that were underground a few hours before.
