The place lies behind a loose inspection plate, nine centimeters inside the summit of the mountain.

Outside, the ridge rises above almost all weather. Its last few meters are black stone, freshly split, too steep to retain much snow. Wind carries ice past it without letting it settle. Below are the backs of clouds, and below those, other mountains, diminished by distance into something like debris.

But the inspection plate faces away from the prevailing wind. Behind it is a space about the size of a shallow drawer, built into the housing of an old atmospheric instrument. Here the wind becomes a series of pressure changes. Here a cold that would kill exposed flesh in minutes is moderated by the waste heat of a transformer. Here, between a ceramic mounting block and a folded strip of aluminum, there is room for a landscape.

Its floor is not level. A fracture in the rock enters through the back of the housing and divides the enclosure diagonally, making a gorge beneath the lowest circuit board. On one side, milky feldspar rises in a clean, almost vertical face. On the other, a shelf of mica overlaps itself in dark reflective leaves. The instrument was bolted over this rock rather than fitted around it. Consequently, some of its deepest spaces are neither natural nor manufactured: narrow ravines roofed by sheet metal, a stone ledge passing through a cable bracket, a blind cavern with a screw tip hanging from its ceiling.

Everything is close to something else.

Copper windings crowd behind a perforated screen. Bundles of wire pass overhead in stiff, dusty curves. The undersides of circuit boards make successive low skies, each carrying its own pattern of blunt solder hills. Between them are stacked passages, sheltered courts, steep drops bridged by a single insulated conductor. It has the density of an old factory district built on terrain nobody could afford to level.

There is no soil. The loose material is powdered stone, ceramic grit, fragments of insulation, insect shells, and a fine gray meal worn from a motor brush. Some of it is so recent that it still has a sheen. New fragments have straight edges. Fresh cracks in the mounting cement are white inside.

The housing has been moving, very slightly, ever since it was installed.

Each night the metal contracts around the stone. Each day, if sunlight reaches the ridge, it expands. Once in a while the difference produces a new break. A chip falls into a passage and becomes a wall. A flake lifts from the floor and opens a route beneath it. There are old, rounded accumulations of dust in protected corners, but most exposed surfaces remain angular. Nothing runs over them to soften them.

Water almost never appears as a substance here. It is an ingredient withheld inside bodies, bound into food, scavenged from the little air that leaks through the seals. Along the coldest wall there is a faint white residue where a few crystals of frost once formed and vanished. No channel leads away from it. No dark stain spreads below it.

Life keeps to the machinery.

The warmest neighborhood surrounds a resistor no larger than a grain of rice. Its heat reaches only a short distance, but that distance includes several ledges, the sheltered underside of a washer, and a seam in the cable insulation. Pale mites gather there in such numbers that the seam seems to possess a slow, granular lining. They separate when a larger animal approaches, then close again behind it.

There are dark mites, too, armored and deliberate, with dust packed into the joints of their legs. Small scavenging insects move along the wires. Their antennae sweep the surfaces ahead, testing each crossing before the body commits. In the tightest cracks live almost transparent young, visible mainly because the contents of their guts are darker than everything around them.

They arrived in packing material, in the felt of an original gasket, on a technician’s glove. Generations later, the enclosure contains more animals than the entire exposed summit.

Their food comes from similarly small inheritances. A trace of grease. Dead skin sealed into a joint. Adhesive slowly breaking down. Airborne particles drawn through the vent. Fungal threads occupy a few protected seams, not enough to make a visible bloom, only enough to bind crumbs together and soften the edges of old organic debris. The animals eat these, eat one another, eat what death makes available. A shed skin can occupy several feeders at once.

Beyond the heated components, the population thins abruptly. Empty passages continue toward the outer plate. They look habitable. Their floors carry the same grit, their ceilings offer the same shelter, but they are several degrees colder. Occasionally a body lies there, perfectly preserved, at the end of a journey that exceeded its reserves.

The light enters through three flaws.

One is a pinhole near the upper screw, projecting a small, hard spot onto the stone. One is the uneven lower edge of the plate, which admits a dull strip of reflected sky. The third is a green indicator on the instrument itself. This burns continuously, staining the nearby legs and pale bodies green as they pass.

Farther in, there is only the suggestion of depth: copper catching a little light, a screw thread emerging and disappearing as the eye adjusts. When cloud crosses the sun outside, the pinhole spot goes out. The green district remains.

Most of the time there is very little to hear. The transformer hums below the threshold of ordinary attention. A feeding animal makes no audible sound. Dust lands without announcement.

Then the sampling pump starts.

Its first movement travels through the mounting frame as a single knock. Every loose particle jumps. Animals flatten themselves into seams or lock their feet around the edges of solder joints. The diaphragm begins its rapid strokes, and air pulls through the enclosure toward a narrow intake at the rear.

For twelve seconds the place is violently rearranged.

A drift of carbon powder lifts and streams under a board. A dry shell tumbles down the rock cleft. The crowded animals around the resistor compress into sheltered strips, while others cross the exposed floor in sudden, urgent runs. A mica flake rocks on its edge. A hanging fiber lashes the same little patch of metal over and over.

The pump is well muffled. Outside the enclosure, on the mountain, nobody would hear it.

When it stops, the particles descend into different streets.

Some crossings have vanished. Others have opened. A carcass formerly out of reach has lodged beside the warm washer. A small animal, displaced two levels downward, follows the underside of a wire back toward heat. Traffic resumes before the last dust has settled.

This happens every four minutes.

Between cycles, slower disturbances continue. The resistor warms and cools with the instrument’s workload. The mountain presses against its bolts. Gusts bow the inspection plate inward, narrowing a passage for a moment, then releasing it. A predator waits at a junction made favorable by the most recent fall of grit.

At the bottom of the enclosure, directly above the gorge in the rock, a loose nut has worked halfway along its thread. Beneath it lies the busiest route between the warm center and the feeding seams.

The nut has been moving for years. Since morning, it has turned a little farther.
