The town occupies the lip of a hollow stone, high enough that clouds sometimes pass beneath its drains.

From the approach path it looks like a low green bank. Ferns lean over the parapets; thick-leaved creepers cover the outward faces of the walls. Only when you reach the gate do you see the space inside: a pale bowl, perhaps a hundred paces across, with buildings arranged around its upper edge and nothing at its center. No monument, no market, no tree. The floor curves down into an expanse of clean, faintly shining rock. Its emptiness seems extravagant in a town where the outer walk is so narrow that two people must turn sideways to pass.

Beyond the parapet are other heights, blue in daylight, and intervals of white cloud. There is no view of a valley bottom. The path comes up through a cleft packed with dark leaves, then stops at the gate. This is the whole town.

The stone is still forming.

You can see it most easily beneath the water spouts. Each spout projects over the hollow, and beneath each one a translucent shelf has begun to grow out of the wall. Water beads along its lip. Within the newest shelves are little cloudy fans, like frost caught in thick glass. Older shelves are opaque and strong enough to stand on. Older still, they take the pale gray color of the bowl.

There are no loose stones here. Nothing has yet broken down into gravel. At the foot of a wall you find neither scree nor a soft accumulation of dust, only the wall continuing into the ground through a rounded seam. Cracks are uncommon and strangely brief. A split that appears during a cold night will hold water by noon; by the end of the season it may be filled with a milky vein.

The builders allow for this. Their doors hang on wooden pins set in deep sockets, with room above the lintels. Steps are made too shallow at first. Gutters are cut broad, almost absurdly broad, because their bottoms rise a little each year. A house is not finished once and then repaired as it decays. It has to be kept open.

Along the inner rim runs a continuous arcade of square pillars. Behind it are the rooms: kitchens, sleeping chambers, storerooms with reed doors, a bath no larger than a trough. Above them, another narrow level carries gardens and a covered walk. There are stairs to platforms on which no building stands, and several rooms have a window, a drain, a beautifully fitted threshold, but no roof. The town has more passages than its few inhabitants need. Its people have inherited work begun for a larger population, or perhaps they are preparing for one. Nothing in the daily rhythm makes the distinction clear.

In the morning, someone moves slowly along the arcade with a basket of wooden wedges. Farther off, two hands emerge from a window to shake out a length of wet cloth. That may be all you see of the inhabitants for an hour.

Yet the place is loud.

The hollow gathers every sound made around its rim. Water falling from a spout strikes its growing shelf with a hard, hollow knock. Several spouts fall at slightly different intervals; their patterns meet, separate, and meet again. A door being pulled across its stone sill produces a roar that seems much too large for the action. The scrape of a hoe travels around the bowl. From a room you cannot locate comes the beating of plant fibers with a wooden mallet: three strokes, a pause, three strokes. Long after the worker stops, you remain aware of the silence where those strokes were.

Wind passes through the drains beneath the houses. The larger channels make a low blowing sound; small ones whistle. In certain weather the whole outer wall gives a steady, rough note, loud enough that people lean close to speak. Inside the rooms, behind thick walls and hanging mats, it is quieter. You can hear a spoon touch a cup.

Water is everywhere available but seldom deep. It shines in channels beside the walks, gathers under leaves, wets the hems of anyone passing through the gardens. There is a shallow pool at the lowest point of the bowl, no wider than a table. Most of the time it looks like a patch of different-colored stone. The water is so clear, and its floor so smooth, that its depth becomes apparent only when a falling drop sends a ring across it.

Rain arrives in short, cold veils. Between showers there is ample light. The sky opens abruptly, and the pale floor throws brightness up beneath the arches. Leaves cast crisp shadows on the pillars. Everything wet flashes for a moment; then the surfaces darken as the water draws into them. Even at noon, the shade is cold enough to make fingers stiff.

The vegetation grows thickly where the inhabited rim catches windblown matter. Roots lie in beds of leaf mold held behind woven branches. The oldest gardens have deep, springy soil, made from generations of cut stems, kitchen scraps, and the leaves that people carry up from the cleft. Their edges bulge over the retaining work. Ferns uncurl between cabbage-like rosettes; vines with purple undersides climb the roof poles. Small, low plants bear waxy yellow flowers close to the warmth of the stone.

From across the bowl these gardens merge into one lush band. Close to them, each bed is distinct. One smells sharply of crushed pepper. Another is a tangle of pale edible stalks tied upright with fiber. Beside a doorway grows a shrub whose leaves are harvested a few at a time, leaving bare lengths of green branch between tufts. Damp moss swells around the water jars.

Nothing grows on the floor below.

There is no soil there, but that alone does not explain it. Seeds reach the bowl. You find them at the edge of the little pool, caught in translucent rims of new stone. A fallen frond can remain recognizable for months while the surface slowly gathers over it. Its leaflets lose their green, become silver, then settle into the floor as a shallow pattern. The inhabitants sweep the bowl from time to time with very long brooms, working barefoot and gathering everything into baskets. It is slow work on the curve. They begin at opposite sides and spend much of the day approaching one another.

Animals are scarce. A narrow-winged bird occasionally crosses the opening without landing. There are small snails beneath the garden boards, their shells nearly the color of the stone. On a clear evening, one moth may circle a lamp in the arcade. Its shadow passes huge over a pillar, disappears into the hollow, and returns.

The town’s tools hang in dry recesses: stone adzes, bone awls, saws with short dark metal blades, bundles of replacement handles. Most of the fresh marks you see are cuts made to preserve a channel or free a hinge. Beside one stair, a line has been scored across a pillar at shoulder height. Below it are older lines, each slightly lower than the last. The writing beside them is too small to read from the walk.

Toward evening the bowl’s eastern wall goes blue while the upper gardens remain sunlit. Someone closes the irrigation sluices with flat pieces of wood. The intermittent knocking of the spouts dwindles, exposing the wind’s lower note. Cooking smoke rises through the leaves and bends away over the parapet.

Down on the empty floor, the small pool holds a bright piece of sky. At its edge, where the day’s water has spread thinly and withdrawn, there is a new skin of pale material. It is soft enough to mark with a fingernail. By morning it will be harder.

Above it, a resident kneels in a doorway with a chisel, carefully shaving the sill so that the door will close.
