The place fits behind a square inspection plate near the top of a mountain elevator. The plate has been painted so many times that its lower screws are buried, and the number stamped into it is almost filled with enamel. Beyond it lies a cavity no deeper than an arm’s reach. It is very high up. The air admitted through the screw holes has the thin, metallic cold of air above the clouds.

There is no mountain inside the cavity. Everything that resembles ground has been laid down by machinery.

A shelf of pale ceramic rises from the back wall, thickening into little shoulders and bluffs. Beneath it are stacked sheets of lead, woven copper, black rubber, and a gray insulating felt so compressed that its fibers have become a grain. These layers show along the shelf’s edge like the strata of a miniature escarpment. They have never broken or weathered. Each exposed edge is exact, and each ends in a soft bead of moisture.

The ceramic is unfinished. Across its surface, ridges stand only halfway raised, with the rounded, tender profiles of teeth beneath a gum. A small extrusion nozzle points into the largest ridge. Between nozzle and ridge hangs a neck of white material, still glossy, still apparently on the verge of being deposited. There is no crust around it. No waste has accumulated beneath it. The landscape has reached the instant at which it is beginning to acquire a shape, and remains there.

Water occupies almost everything that has not been given another purpose.

It fills the shallow terraces, lies under the ceramic in narrow bright seams, and swells over the lips of little metal cups. On the ceiling it forms a dense inverted field of drops. Some are large enough to contain the inspection plate in their reflections: a hundred dark squares, each with its own clogged screws. None falls. A continuous film climbs the woven copper at the rear, turning every crossing of wire into a lens.

The water is colder than water should comfortably remain. The pipes keep it liquid by pressure, dissolved salts, and a low electrical current. Where it touches the rubber, it looks thick as clear oil. A fingertip placed in one of the pools would ache almost at once.

There are only three patches of anything green. Each grows in a punched hole in a zinc strip. The plants are short, stiff, and nearly black, with leaves pressed close around a central bud. Their roots pass straight into manufactured nutrient gel. No soil has ever been brought here. One leaf bears a bead of water along its whole upper edge, magnifying a line of pale pores. Even this small amount of vegetation seems extravagant, something for which space had to be found.

The animals have no such restraint.

At first the chamber looks furred. Then the fur resolves into antennae, legs, gills, whiskers, jointed paddles. Every wet ledge is occupied. Transparent crustaceans lie side by side in the channels between ceramic ridges, their bodies so closely fitted that their overlapping shells make a second, flexible lining. Under them are flat, milk-colored worms. Beneath the worms, visible where the water meets the glass of a pressure gauge, smaller animals form a fine red felt.

The ceiling drops are inhabited too. Each holds a curled creature with six dark eyes and a fan of translucent limbs. Their bodies remain entirely within the water, though a claw or the tip of a feeler sometimes reaches its surface and dimples it outward. The drops are their whole depth of sky.

Larger creatures occupy the bolt recesses. They have the blunt heads and folded forelimbs of very young mammals, but their sides open into soft combs of gill tissue. Their skin is pale blue where it presses against the steel. You can see the little roots of their teeth. Each animal fills its recess closely, cheek against one wall, spine curved around the bolt, with a few smaller organisms packed into the remaining crescent of space.

Nothing has the loose arrangement of a nest or a swarm. The animals are fitted as densely as components on a circuit board. Flesh touches shell; shell touches ceramic; the ceramic sweats into the flesh. There is hardly a bare surface wide enough to lay a fingernail.

And the sound is enormous.

It reaches through the inspection plate as a solid, unvarying roar. Inside, it is almost impossible to distinguish the industrial noise from the animals. A compressor under the lowest shelf supplies a deep note that seems too large for the cavity. The copper mesh carries a shrill vibration. The little gilled mammals have their mouths open around a continuous, breathless cry. Thousands of finer voices occupy the intervals, so densely that no individual call can be picked out.

The noise has the pressure of a factory floor heard from inside a helmet. It would fill your jaw and blur your sight. Yet a drop hanging a hair’s breadth from another drop does not quiver.

That is the governing peculiarity of the chamber: it can sustain an intensity without allowing an event.

The compressor’s gauge holds one position. The cry never reaches the end of an exhalation. No animal advances, withdraws, hatches, feeds, or dies. Their eyes are clear. Their skin is living. Their limbs carry the unmistakable tension of limbs about to move. In the transparent bodies, tiny organs are visible at some intermediate point in their work.

A worm holds its mouth against an egg without breaking the membrane.

A larva protrudes halfway through a casing that has opened cleanly around its head.

Along one ceramic ridge, a colony of minute shelled animals has assembled at the boundary between water and air. The foremost has raised itself on its legs. Its underside is exposed, delicate and intricately pleated. Behind it, the others press close. They have the posture of a migration compressed into less than an inch.

There is light from two sources: a green indicator buried behind the copper mesh, and a short heating filament glowing dull red inside a glass sleeve. Neither illuminates much. The green passes through bodies and reveals their internal partitions. The red reaches the unfinished ceramic and gives its wet whiteness a faint, raw blush. Between these patches the cavity is blue-black, crowded with details that emerge only when the eye settles on them.

No dust lies anywhere. No stain marks a former water level. There are no shed skins, no empty shells, no corrosion beneath the pipe collars. Even the felt along the lowest edge retains the sharp impressions of the tool that pressed it into place.

Far below, the elevator serves floors where doors open and close, where people carry wet coats into heated rooms. Here, behind the small painted plate, the manufactured earth is still arriving. Its first ridges are crowded with life. Its first pools are full. The noise of its beginning is deafening, and has never diminished enough for there to be a second sound.
