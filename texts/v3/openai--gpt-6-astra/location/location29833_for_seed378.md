At this height, the rain arrives warm.

It comes out of a sky so bright that the clouds can only be distinguished by their undersides: broad, faint bruises above the plateau. Between showers, sunlight falls through almost unchanged. The wet ground shines. White mineral banks shine. Even the shadows beneath the buildings contain a little reflected glare, enough to show the pale animals resting there.

The plateau is a shallow basin several miles across, suspended between bare mountain shoulders. Its far edge drops away into a depth usually filled with cloud. Water escapes over that edge in dozens of narrow falls, but there is no great river feeding them. It comes up everywhere: through hairline cracks in the paving, through old drill holes, through porous stone that darkens each morning as if breathing out. The rain adds little to this supply. Mostly it rinses the surface and temporarily makes the water cool enough to touch.

Underfoot, the rock is gray where recently broken and ivory where it has been exposed for years. Its broad slopes have settled into a sequence of shelves, shallow basins, and rounded lips. Water crosses them in sheets too thin to ripple, then gathers in pools clear enough to conceal their depth. Some are ankle-deep. Some descend between nearly vertical walls to a blue point that shifts when you move your head.

There is almost no loose earth. Nothing has roots here. In cracks where a weed might grow elsewhere, a little fan of crystals opens instead.

The town occupies the higher shelves, though “occupies” suggests more completeness than there is. A pumping house stands alone at the end of a paved square. Six apartment blocks face a basin half a mile wide. Beyond them, a long machine hall runs parallel to the escarpment, its roof supported on columns thick enough to contain staircases. Between these structures lie stretches of bare stone, open water, and disused foundations filled flush with white sediment.

The buildings are spaced for the ground beneath them, not for convenience. You build only where the water has remained obedient for several generations.

Raised walkways connect one patch of reliable rock to another. Most are roofed with thin porcelain panels that turn direct sunlight into a dense, milk-colored glow. Their undersides drip continuously. Along the railings, shallow gutters carry condensation toward drinking-water cisterns, each cistern shaded and sealed. The water running naturally across the plateau is rich in dissolved metal. Some of it leaves orange stains. Some plates iron objects with a fragile blue skin. The clearest pools are not necessarily the safest.

Doors have high thresholds. Electrical cables hang overhead in pale ceramic loops. Every occupied room has a drain.

The heat is strongest before noon, when the sun and the ground seem to be working toward the same purpose. A handrail left uncovered can burn the palm. Water gathers at the ends of sleeves. Farther out on the shelves, rising air distorts the town until the apartment blocks appear to stand on narrow, flexible legs.

Yet the place is very quiet.

The pumps are deep below ground, hung inside flooded shafts. Their pressure reaches the surface as a slight trembling in a cup, or a momentary thickening of the water spilling over a basin’s rim. The machine hall has no open windows. Its turbines turn within double walls packed with wet mineral felt, and the freight line that serves it is a slow cable railway: low carts moving on smooth tracks, each spaced far enough from the next that one can cross between them without hurrying.

Occasionally a coupling takes its load with a small click.

Rain makes a faint, even hiss. A loose gutter drips. Someone closes a door carefully across an entire square, and the sound is distinct without being loud.

The animals are quieter still.

There are so many that at first their abundance is mistaken for texture. What looks like a scattering of wet pebbles below a culvert is a colony of low, jointed grazers. Each has a translucent shell the size of a thumb joint, with a dark body folded beneath it. They advance over the warm stone in overlapping rows, scraping the colorless living films that form wherever mineral water meets air. Behind them, the rock is a little duller.

No green enters this food chain. The films live on what the springs bring up, thickening fastest around a fresh seep. In certain light they reveal themselves as an oily silver sheen. In others, their presence is visible only through the traffic of mouths.

Long-bodied swimmers hunt the grazers in the pools. Their skin is the color of watered milk, almost transparent along the spine. They pause beneath the surface with their limbs spread, then close those limbs and travel several yards in a single smooth impulse. The water scarcely breaks above them.

On the higher banks lie larger creatures, flat-bellied and broad-headed, with six short legs and eyes sheltered under ridges of chalky skin. They choose places where warm runoff passes over their backs. A dozen may occupy one shallow channel, aligned head to tail, while the next hundred yards of bank remain empty. When a cable cart passes, they lift their heads together. Once it has gone, they settle in the same order, the movement running along the line.

There are flying animals too, though they seldom fly far. In the afternoons they detach themselves from the shaded sides of the towers: thin, translucent bodies with wide ribbed membranes. They rise on the heat above the roofs and drift toward the springs, collecting the minute soft-bodied things carried in the vapor. Against the sky, only their veins are visible. Against a white wall, they become faint amber stains.

Nothing calls to mark territory. Courtship takes place through touch, brief changes of color, and vibrations transmitted through stone. At the edge of an occupied basin, a patient observer can see rings forming on the water without anything touching its surface. Beneath the bank, two armored animals are tapping replies through the rock.

The town’s maintenance crews know where these territories are. Some joints in the walkways are deliberately left soft so that footsteps do not travel into the breeding pools.

Other accommodations are less gentle. Fine copper combs guard the turbine intakes. The drinking-water tanks stand on isolated feet surrounded by dry channels. At the machine hall, workers remove shells from the cooling screens every morning, carrying them out in shallow trays. Most of the animals are still alive. Set down beside a warm seep, they wait until the tray’s shadow moves off them and then begin to crawl.

By late afternoon, cloud rises from the country below and catches against the plateau’s rim. The distant mountains disappear from the bottom upward. For a while, the town stands between a brilliant upper sky and a white depth with no visible floor.

This is when the springs change pressure.

The shift is small but general. Water withdraws a finger’s width from some pools and swells in others. Old inspection pipes exhale. The hanging chains inside the gauge houses move against their scales, and someone records the differences in a ledger. Across the basin, the animals leave certain channels before the hot water arrives.

A fresh flow spreads over the abandoned stone. It steams only at its edges, where the cloud has begun to cool the air. Within hours a new film will form there. By morning the grazers will have returned.

Nothing in the town is quite level anymore. Decades of mineral deposition have raised one end of a square; slow dissolution has lowered the other. Steps have been cut into old steps. Doorways open onto small platforms added after the water climbed. Beneath a transparent crust in the central plaza, an earlier set of road markings remains visible, leading toward a bridge that is now little more than a white ridge across a pool.

The place is maintained by subtraction. Workers shave stone from spillways, bore out pipes, cut the thresholds free. Where they stop, the water patiently rounds the edges of their work.

At evening, shutters open along the apartment blocks. People come onto the roofed walks to stand in the slight movement of air. There are lamps, but for a long time they do very little. The basin holds the daylight in every wet surface, every glazed wall, every pale animal gathered at the margins of the pools.

Only when the sky finally darkens do the deeper shafts become visible from a distance. Small red service lights burn far down inside them.

Above, the town rests widely spaced across the stone. Below, the pumps continue turning.
