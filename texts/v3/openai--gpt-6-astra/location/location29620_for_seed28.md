The city fits beneath a doorstep.

Not beneath the whole house: only in the shallow place where the underside of the stone has worn away, between two supports of yellow brick. From outside, the entrance looks like a flaw in the mortar. A blade of warm air comes out of it. If you kneel close enough, there is a smell of crushed pepper and heated plaster, and a noise that makes your teeth ache.

Inside, it is noon.

Sunlight enters through three hairline fractures in the doorstep. Small plates of mica, fitted into clay sockets, turn these narrow arrivals into broad fans of light. The plates have been adjusted by hand over generations. Some are shimmed with splinters; others lean against old roof tiles. Light passes from one to the next until the underside of the stone shines white, and the city below it has almost no shadow.

There is no sky, although there is room enough overhead to acquire a longing for one.

Most of the hollow is empty. The buildings keep to its edges, adhering to the brick supports and following the low curves of the ground. Streets run along the walls in pale, closely spaced bands. There are stairways, landings, arcades scarcely taller than the grain of a brick. Hundreds of doorways open onto the central space, which contains nothing but a shallow slope of fine mineral dust. From a high window, you could watch a single seed cross that emptiness for half a day.

The buildings are made of the hollow itself: chalk scraped from the ceiling, grit sorted by size, thin bricks pressed in wooden molds and hardened against the hot outer face of the step. There is no metal. Door pivots are polished pebbles. Balcony rails are braided root. Where a wall needs strengthening, its builders stitch it with fiber through holes drilled by a pointed tooth.

Some houses are no more than facades against stone. Others extend deep into the mortar, joining chambers that were once separate. The oldest neighborhoods have grown around abandoned neighborhoods, leaving interior streets that go nowhere and public stairs ending against living wood.

For there is a great deal growing here.

It grows sideways, mostly, keeping to surfaces. Across every roof spreads a close, springy vegetation, green-black at its roots and pale gold at the tips. Leaves overlap like little hands shielding something precious. Thick creepers descend through empty rooms. Fern-shaped fans emerge from cracks in the plaster, each leaflet edged with clear mineral teeth. On the undersides of balconies hang orchards of amber bladders, translucent in the glare.

Nothing drips from them.

The plants have no use for water. A wet fingertip brought into this place would cause a local catastrophe: leaves whitening, roots withdrawing, whole gardens shedding their outer skins. Their working fluids are warm resins, held in fine branching channels. They take nourishment from dust and from the oily vapors that creep down through the old stone. Heat keeps them supple. Light hardens their new growth.

Touch a leaf and it bends slowly, with the resistance of softened horn. Break one and the wound closes beneath a fragrant bead that stiffens almost at once.

The gardens are lush because they have had time, and because almost nothing eats them.

Their abundance stays close to the city. Beyond the last root, the central hollow is bare. A fallen leaf out there becomes brittle before it can turn over twice. Its edges curl upward; wind carries it a little farther; eventually it breaks into flakes that collect against a distant wall. The garden receives them back as soil.

The wind is also the reason you cannot hear yourself think.

Air forced through the entrance slit strikes a natural lip in the stone. The entire hollow roars. It is a continuous, bodily sound, low enough to press against the chest, with a bright tearing note inside it. Every building participates. Empty cistern-shaped chambers deepen it. Narrow alleys whistle. Loose shutters chatter so rapidly that they seem to be ringing.

There are quieter districts, but quiet here means being able to feel the sound in your soles rather than in your jaw.

The city was built around this. Doors face away from the entrance. Sleeping cells have double walls packed with shed leaves. Public squares are fitted with upright slabs behind which people can meet and sign to one another. Messages are scratched on soft clay strips and slid along covered channels between houses. A person crossing a street carries a small flag, because no warning cry could reach someone on a balcony.

Now and then a larger change in pressure arrives from outside. The roar rises. Garden fronds flatten, mica mirrors tremble in their sockets, and dust lifts from the central slope in long, low veils. Hinged screens close under the force of the air. Roof vents open. Somewhere, counterweights of stone descend through shafts, pulling broad leaf-shutters over the most delicate beds.

Much of this happens without anyone attending it.

At first the city seems deserted. Then a figure appears beneath an arcade, dragging a shallow tray of sifted grit. Much later, another crosses a rooftop with a knife of dark glass. There may be a dozen inhabitants. There may be fewer. The gardens conceal the occupied houses, and nobody needs to show a lamp at a window.

The abandoned quarters remain in use by roots. A council chamber has become one thick tangle of climbing stems; its rows of benches are still visible between them. In a marketplace, plants have followed the seams of old awnings and preserved their shapes after the cloth decayed. Gold-green canopies stand on brittle stalks above counters worn smooth by hands.

Only one animal is likely to be seen: a flat, ivory-colored creature living beneath the warmest mica plate. It has six feet and a transparent shield over its head. Once in a long while it ventures down to crop the powder from a flowering stem. The flowers close behind it, one after another.

The ground is old. Corners have rounded under generations of windblown grit. Steps sag in their centers. Fallen plaster lies beneath root mats, incorporated into the next terrace. Even the entrance slit has worn to a stable curve, so that the great noise changes pitch only when something outside moves.

A foot on the doorstep briefly lowers the ceiling’s note. Dust loosens from the stone. Across the city, gardeners look up.

Then the weight passes. The mica holds its light. In the hot, deafening hollow, someone resumes the careful work of lifting a root away from a doorway.
