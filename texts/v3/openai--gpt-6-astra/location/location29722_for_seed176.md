The garden sits so high that the clouds have undersides.

Through a notch in its enclosing rock, you can look down onto their level backs: gray-white, faintly wrinkled, extending to a horizon broken by two distant summits. On clear afternoons the clouds open and reveal a much lower range, blue with distance. The inhabited valleys are farther down still. Nothing of them is visible from here.

The garden itself is scarcely larger than a village square.

It occupies a shallow cup in the side of a volcanic pinnacle, sheltered on three sides by walls of fresh black rock. The fourth side ends at a stone lip and an almost vertical drop. There is no broad approach, no hillside that gradually becomes this place. A maintenance ladder comes up through a crack beside the lip. Its upper rungs are orange with rust, and the last few disappear beneath a heavy fringe of moss.

At this altitude the exposed mountain is pale, dry, and murderous with cold. Inside the cup, your collar grows damp.

Warm water rises everywhere.

It beads along hairline fractures, shines beneath loose slabs, seeps out from under the roots of ferns. Some of it is too hot to touch where it first emerges. A few inches farther on, divided into threads across the rock, it is merely warm. The threads collect in shallow basins lined with green velvet, then travel through a sequence of pools toward the open edge. Nothing here makes a waterfall. The water goes over the lip as a continuous wetness, clinging to the mountain until it is lost below.

The stone still shows how it broke. Sharp shelves project through the growth; cracks meet at clean angles; small cavities preserve the bubbles of its cooling. Where a flake has recently come away, the exposed surface is blue-black and almost glassy. Mineral crusts soften the wetter edges, but there are no deep channels yet. Water spreads across the rock, gathers against an obstruction, and finds a different way around.

In places, roots are already opening the fractures.

The vegetation is low and extravagant. Thick moss occupies every surface that stays wet without becoming hot. Ferns rise from it in overlapping tiers, their newest fronds copper-colored, their older ones dark enough to seem almost black in the shade. There are plants with broad, corrugated leaves that hold spoonfuls of water along their central ribs. Pale roots pass over stones and descend into steaming holes. A few woody shrubs lean outward from the sheltered walls, their trunks no thicker than wrists, their crowns knitted together above the pools.

Most of the soil is caught rather than accumulated: a pocket behind a root, a wedge beneath a fallen slab, a dark cushion in the fork of a pipe bracket. There is very little level ground. To cross the cup, you follow a series of stones with their upper faces worn smooth by boots. The plants nearly meet across them. Every passage wets your trousers.

The machinery is heard before it is seen.

A low, continuous hammering fills the enclosure, reinforced by the rock walls until its source is difficult to locate. Above it runs a pressurized hiss. From time to time a sheet-metal panel trembles against a loose fastening, producing a hard, bright rattle that lasts several seconds.

Behind the largest fern stand is a pump house, small enough to mistake for a boulder until the straight edge of its roof appears. Its concrete walls are streaked white below the bolts. A single wired-glass window admits daylight onto a flywheel, a bank of gauges, and the glossy black bodies of two pumps. One pump runs. The other has been dismantled, its open casing covered with a sack that has stiffened in the damp.

A pipe as wide as a person’s thigh leaves the building and follows the inner wall of the cup. Its insulation is wrapped in dull aluminum, dented where someone has stepped on it. At each joint, escaping warmth supports a particularly dense collar of growth. Small roots have entered the laps of the metal jacket. Farther along, the pipe turns downward and vanishes into a drilled shaft.

The installation serves something below. There is no village here, no larger station beyond the wall. There is only this small interception of the mountain’s heat, with its numbered valves, its maintenance ladder, and a cabinet of spare fuses mounted well above the wet ground.

A faded line on the cabinet door marks the maximum safe water level.

That line is surprisingly high.

Below it, the lowest shrubs all lean in the same direction. Dead fern stems lie combed through the living ones. In one sheltered corner, a strip of yellow packing tape has been woven into the moss. At long intervals the drains must choke, or a vent must open more strongly, and the whole cup becomes a warm pond. Then the water subsides, leaving the paths slick and the plants holding sediment in their folded leaves.

Today the pools are clear. Their bottoms are covered with tiny pale granules that shift only when a bubble rises beneath them.

There are few animals. A dark fly rests on the pump-house window. Something like a snail has left a silver track across the underside of a leaf, though it is difficult to find the creature itself. Once, a bird passes the notch without landing, carried upward so quickly that its shadow crosses the garden before you hear its call.

The constant noise makes these small absences easy to miss. It gives the impression of activity, but very little moves. Fern tips remain still. Steam hangs in the protected corners. The fly changes position by the width of its body.

Sun reaches the cup directly for several hours around noon. Then the leaves show their ordinary greens, rust brightens on the ladder, and every exposed bead of water shines. Beyond the lip, the snowfields glare. The boundary between climates is only a few steps wide. Beside the outermost pool, warm water soaks your boots while the wind chills your face.

Toward evening, the western wall cuts off the sunlight. Moisture begins to collect on the cooler metalwork and falls from the pipe brackets with slow, heavy taps. The pump continues at exactly the same speed.

By then the distant summits have disappeared into cloud. The notch holds only white air. The garden seems closer enclosed: wet stone, leaves, the little building, a light burning behind its wired glass. Somewhere beneath the path, a root presses into a crack. A thin mineral rim grows around the edge of a pool. Water reaches the precipice and follows the rock down.
