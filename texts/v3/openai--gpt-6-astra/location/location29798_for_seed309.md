The stair descends through wood for longer than the depth of the place seems to warrant. Its treads have been worn into shallow bowls, each holding a little dry chaff. Halfway down, the air becomes warm. Farther down it begins to move against your face in regular pushes, carrying the smell of cut pumpkin, old sawdust, and something faintly alcoholic.

At the bottom is a room large enough to contain a small town square.

Four immense stems hold up the ceiling. Between them there is considerable open space: a broad floor, a few low buildings, the dark mouth of a street that curves out of sight and, on investigation, returns to the square. Everything substantial is vegetable. The floor is a plate of intergrown roots. The buildings have grown thick skins of cork. High overhead, the ceiling is the underside of a root mat so old that its individual strands have merged into a single rippled surface. It has the softened contours of a cave repeatedly flooded and drained, though there has never been enough free water here to flood anything.

The room was grown beneath an orchard. The orchard is still there, taking sunlight, but the room has outlived several arrangements of trees.

It is dim enough that distances remain uncertain. Pale green strips run along the edges of the street, where luminous moss has been cultivated in narrow grooves. Their light makes the floor look cool. It is warm underfoot, almost the temperature of skin.

For the first few moments, the place seems sparsely inhabited.

Then your eyes begin to separate movement from texture.

The grain of one pillar is moving upward. Thousands of narrow beetles are climbing in the same direction, each carrying a crescent of pale fiber in its jaws. Beneath the nearest eave, what looked like a fringe of dried flowers is a row of bats, their bellies rising and falling out of time with one another. The street’s green borders are continually interrupted by the feet of small animals. A translucent centipede comes out of a joint in the floor, travels the width of a thumbnail, and disappears into the next joint. Something follows it from underneath, lifting the root surface slightly as it passes.

There are animals in nearly every available thickness. The open air is simply the part of the settlement they use least.

The noise takes longer to resolve.

A heavy, uneven knocking comes from the far side of the square. Above it hangs a shrill electrical whine. There are clicks like seedpods bursting, the scraping of claws inside walls, a wet rasp from somewhere beneath the street. Occasionally a whole section of ceiling starts rattling. Dust comes down, then stops.

The inhabitants call the room the Lower Mill.

Its machinery occupies three low buildings and most of the ground beneath them. Through a doorway, you can see a pair of wooden rollers turning slowly against one another. Their surfaces are ridged like molars. Bundles of pruned root and leaf-stem descend between them, becoming fiber on one side and a cloudy juice on the other. The rollers are alive. Their working surfaces wear away, heal, and are cut back to shape during the weekly stoppage.

The shafts pass through collars of hard seed tissue. Sap wells into the bearings a drop at a time. Farther inside, a pump contracts with the slow authority of a large heart.

Power comes down from the orchard in two forms: sugar in the living vessels, electricity in cables laid beside them. The cables are among the few things here that had to be brought from elsewhere. They have long since been overgrown. Where an inspection flap has been peeled back, a copper terminal gleams in its nest of white root hairs.

There is real industrial discipline to the place. The floor has marked freight lanes. Counterweighted gates close across the street before a load moves through. A row of horn switches controls the ventilators. Beside each switch, generations of hands have polished a dark oval in the wall.

But production depends on more than motors.

The mill’s sorting chamber contains a colony of beetles bred to distinguish seven grades of fiber. They work on sloping trays, dropping each piece through the opening appropriate to its thickness. Small lizards patrol the tray frames, taking mites that would otherwise clog the beetles’ joints. The lizards are kept out of the bearings by bands of bitter resin. Above the sorting chamber, swifts nest in a turning drum, entering and leaving through the hollow axle. Their constant departures keep a fine lint from settling in the passage.

Every useful arrangement supports several less convenient ones. Predators gather at inspection hatches. Larvae burrow into the switches. One building has a permanent, gentle lean because a nest inside its south wall is too valuable to disturb.

The few people who live below occupy lofts above the workshops. You notice their presence in small domestic interruptions: a shirt drying over a warm vent, three bowls set in a recess, a sleeping child behind a curtain woven so closely that even the smallest moths cannot get through. Nobody raises their voice over the machinery. They communicate across the square with shutters, hand signs, and short notes blown through hollow stems.

Water is carefully placed.

There is no pool in the square, no dripping roof. Most surfaces feel dry, though the joints between them remain dark. Moisture moves inside the plants, along channels too narrow to see. What escapes is caught. Condensation gathers on the ventilation roots near the stair, runs into leaf-shaped gutters, and is carried to a cistern under the press house.

Once in a while, a drop falls free.

Its impact produces an astonishing event. Antennae emerge from the floor. Little pale mouths open in the seams. A gathering forms around the wet patch, so quick and densely layered that the water seems to grow fur. Within a minute it is gone, divided among bodies. The gathering loosens. The floor resumes its appearance of bare wood.

After rain in the orchard, the whole room swells slightly. Doors become difficult. The machinery must be slowed until its clearances settle. On those days the residents shave curls from the thresholds and leave them in baskets for the mill.

This has happened for a very long time.

The stair’s hollow treads, the rounded doorways, the shallow freight ruts all preserve repeated movements. Old damage has grown smooth. A broken axle from an earlier press is enclosed within one pillar; you can follow its straight outline under the bark until it vanishes above reach. There are disused conduits full of nesting animals, and former rooms reduced by growth to cavities no larger than cupboards. One sealed doorway still bears a working bell. Something inside answers it with three faint taps, so the bell is left alone.

The room changes fastest during ventilation.

First, the electrical whine drops in pitch. The rollers pause with fiber hanging from their teeth. Across the square, freight gates settle into their sockets. People draw curtains over their lofts.

Then the four great stems open.

Long slits part in their bark, revealing wet folds inside. Air rushes down from the orchard, warm at first, then briefly fresh enough to carry the smell of leaves in daylight. The ceiling trembles. Bats wake and shift. Moths pour from recesses in such numbers that the moss strips appear to flicker, although their light is steady.

For several minutes the square is full of passage. There are wings, loose fibers, shed skins, airborne seeds. The current rolls them toward a broad outlet behind the press house. Insect hunters launch from the walls and hold almost motionless against the wind, picking bodies out of it.

The noise is tremendous.

Then the stems begin to close. The gale narrows to a whistle, then to four separate sighs. Chaff settles into the freight ruts. A resident comes down from a loft and sweeps the switchboard with a bundle of stiff leaves.

The mill starts again, one shaft at a time.

By then the place is easier to see. The apparent emptiness of the square is a maintained clearance, swept and grazed and patrolled. Its bare floor is continually trying to put out shoots. Along an undisturbed edge, several have already risen: small white hooks, tender in the green light. Before the next ventilation, something will eat them.
