The city stands on the worn top of a mountain range, although nothing near it looks like a mountain anymore. There are no peaks. The ground rises and falls in long, shallow swells, each too broad to be understood from where you stand. A street appears level until a distant warehouse disappears behind its middle. Beyond the last buildings, the plateau continues for several kilometers, then drops away into darkness. Warm air comes up from below. It carries no smell of rain or soil.

The altitude can be felt in the effort of walking. Ten steps up a loading ramp are enough to make you stop. The air is thin, warm, exceptionally dry; it draws moisture from the mouth without offering the ordinary relief of a breeze. Metal handrails feel almost the same temperature as skin.

There is very little light. Narrow amber slits beneath door lintels mark the buildings still in use. At long intervals, a lamp illuminates the foot of a staircase or a number stenciled on a wall. These lamps are hooded so heavily that their light never reaches the street. Looking across the city, you see a few low marks, some nearer and some impossibly far away, with no visible connection between them.

Only after your eyes adjust do the larger things appear: a factory roof obscuring several lamps; the pale side of a mineral embankment; the shallow, enormous bowl of a freight yard. Overhead, the sky has no stars. It is not clouded. It has the depthless appearance of the inside of a closed kiln.

The mountain beneath the city is older than its exposed stone ought to be. Its folds have been worn through, then worn through again. Bands that once ran vertically inside buried cliffs now cross the surface as low, rounded ribs. Some are black and dense, others cream-colored and soft enough to score with a thumbnail. Nothing stands high enough to cast much of a shadow. Even the hardest inclusions have become smooth islands in the surrounding rock.

In places, whole neighborhoods follow these bands. Houses and machine sheds stand on the dark stone; utility trenches occupy the softer seams. The result is a city of very long, slightly curving streets, separated by expanses of bare ground. A compressor station may have no other building within half a kilometer. Six apartment blocks face one another across a square large enough for a town, but the square contains only a weighing platform and three waist-high posts.

There are no gardens. No dead gardens, either: no beds, no borders, no sockets where trees were once expected to grow. The builders made no provision for roots.

Dust has gathered in the sheltered places, but less than one might expect. There is hardly any wind left capable of making it. What lies beneath the loading platforms is extraordinarily fine, pale gray, and uniform. A footprint remains sharp for years. On a few routes, generations of passage have compressed it into a dark strip no wider than a single person.

The buildings are industrial without being crowded. Most have thick walls of fused aggregate, rounded corners, and roofs that merge gradually into their sides. Large entrances have been narrowed by successive layers of masonry. Above one human-sized door, the outline of an older opening reaches three stories high. No sign explains what used to pass through it.

Pipes cross the streets on low trestles. They are broad enough to crawl inside, wrapped in a dull ceramic insulation that has cracked into plates. Their joints carry embossed arrows. Sometimes two parallel pipes have arrows pointing in opposite directions; sometimes all the arrows point toward a sealed wall.

Put a hand against one and there is a faint, even tremor.

The machinery still operates. It is simply difficult to catch it doing anything.

Behind the grilles of the pressure houses, flywheels turn slowly in near-darkness. Their polished rims show a moving thread of amber when a service lamp happens to be on. There are no exhaust plumes, no hammering lines, no furnaces opening onto white heat. The plants exchange pressure between deep chambers in the mountain. One chamber yields exactly what another accepts. A shaft turns a pump that feeds a governor that eases a load elsewhere. Far below the streets, a weight descends so gradually that a person could spend a lifetime beside it and remain uncertain.

The city has not quite stopped. It has become very good at continuing.

There are still benches beside the inspection doors, with shallow depressions worn into them. There are enamel cups in recessed cupboards and coat hooks polished at their tips. A few windows contain a warmer darkness than the streets outside. Once, crossing a wide apron of stone, you might see someone on a distant stair. They pause at a landing, rest one hand on the rail, and go inside. The encounter is too far away for a greeting.

Sound travels strangely here. With no leaves, water, insects, or traffic to fill the distances, small noises arrive intact but without any useful indication of scale. A latch closing across a freight yard sounds close to your ear. Your own boot striking a hollow utility cover seems to come from beneath the entire district.

Most of the time there is only the slight noise of breathing.

At intervals too long to anticipate, something inside the mountain makes a low, rounded click. It is not loud. You feel it through the soles before you hear it. The buildings give no response. Nobody comes to a window.

The few animals live near the warm pipe supports. They are flat, jointed things, each about the length of a finger, with matte shells the color of the masonry. You can watch one for several minutes without deciding whether it is alive. Then it lifts itself a fraction from the stone and moves sideways into a joint.

They leave narrow clean paths through the powdery mineral deposits around electrical insulators. Their mouths, if those are mouths, fit neatly over the ridges in the ceramic. There are never enough of them to call an infestation. Usually you find only the paths. In an abandoned switching house, a translucent shell lies under a cabinet, still holding the shape of the animal that left it. Nothing has disturbed it.

Toward the plateau’s edge, the streets widen and lose their curbs. Rails emerge from the paving, run through an empty transfer yard, and end at massive buffer blocks. Beyond them stands a row of winch buildings overlooking the drop. Their cables descend into the dark, straight and taut. No cage is visible. The machinery here is warmer than elsewhere, and the floor can be felt through the soles of your shoes.

This is where the height finally becomes real. A pebble pushed over the edge makes no returning sound. The rising air touches your face with the stored warmth of depths you cannot see.

Behind you, the city occupies its broad, worn summit: low roofs, isolated lamps, kilometers of nearly empty streets. There is no bright center and no obvious boundary between the working districts and the abandoned ones. Both have closed doors. Both have pipes that tremble under the hand.

Beside the nearest winch is a small desk bolted to the floor. A pencil lies in a groove cut for it. The open ledger records cable tension in columns of cramped figures.

The entries continue down the page.

Almost all the figures are the same.
