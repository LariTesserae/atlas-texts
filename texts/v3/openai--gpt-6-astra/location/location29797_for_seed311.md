Beneath the lowest landing of the flood stairs is a triangular space, scarcely larger than a cupboard. The door has swollen into its frame. Through the gap beneath it comes a steady white light and the noise of a much larger body of water.

Inside, everything is submerged.

The water is cool enough to ache against a knuckle. It fills the space to the underside of the stairs, including the narrow angles where air ought to have collected. There are no bubbles. A dropped bubble would be taken apart before it reached the ceiling: its gases sorted, dissolved, sent along different routes. The water is clear, but so crowded with transparent construction that you cannot see from one wall to another.

At first the structures resemble stacked glass slides. Then their depth becomes apparent. Each carries gutters, apertures, rows of little chambers; each is a district laid on its side. Between them are streets scarcely wider than a fingernail, their intersections marked by dull silver studs. Thin bridges join one layer to the next. Some go through apparently solid surfaces without making holes.

The whole settlement occupies the foot of the stairs. Its tallest structures stop below the third tread.

Its foundations are still forming.

Across the bottom lies a shallow basin of new white mineral, pleated where it has risen against the old floor. The ridges are sharp enough to cut skin. Nothing has had time to become a pebble. Fragments broken loose from the growing edges retain the exact shapes of what broke them: a five-sided socket, the underside of a pipe, a tiny flight of steps. The fragments move in slow files toward sorting slots beneath the buildings.

There is almost no open ground. Where a square of pale floor appears, machinery soon lowers something onto it, or a new rib of foundation pushes up from below. Even the spaces kept empty have uses. One is reserved for the swing of a valve. Another holds a column of water under such peculiar tension that nearby grit bends toward it, then slips around its surface.

Light arrives through six cloudy squares set into the far wall. Behind that wall should be several metres of earth. Nevertheless, the squares admit a plain, overcast daylight, with the slight yellow warmth of a window facing a courtyard. Once in a while a cloud passes across all six.

By that light, the place is unexpectedly ordinary in colour. White foundations. Grey brackets. Green oxidation around old fasteners. A blue stripe along a main conduit, neatly interrupted at every coupling. There are even shadows, precise and soft-edged, lying under the bridges.

The loudest sound comes from a spillway no longer than a hand.

Water falls through it although water surrounds it on every side. The falling sheet retains its surface, a silver fold drawn down between two ceramic cheeks. It strikes a submerged apron with a continuous pounding roar. Close to it, the noise seems to occupy the bones of the room. Farther away, behind a single transparent partition, it becomes the intimate rattle of rain against a window.

There are other sounds underneath: the snap of fresh mineral splitting along a planned seam; the low knocking of gates that cannot all be seen; a recurring scrape as a terrace moves sideways on its supports. Every few minutes, a deep note sounds from the floor. All the loose fragments stop. The water tightens against the walls. Then the note ends, and the fragments resume their progress, some in different directions.

No animal answers any of this. Nothing darts into cover. No tiny mouths work at the surfaces.

Life is confined to a few scraps of vegetation on the warmer side of a heat exchanger. Flat, dark-green blades emerge from slots in the white crust, each no longer than an eyelash. They lean into a current too slight to carry sediment. Their beds are carefully served: a capillary brings dissolved nutrients; a little comb passes through at intervals to clear mineral growth from their roots. Elsewhere, the walls are bare.

One blade has grown beyond its allotted slot. Around its tip, construction has paused. A bracket ends short of it. A transparent screen has acquired a shallow bulge. The machinery seems able to make room for this much wilderness.

The settlement has weather, although very little distance in which to have it.

Cold fronts arrive through the base of the door as flattened streams. They spread across the floor, turning the foundations briefly blue, then rise through the streets in ordered lanes. Warmer water descends from above. Where the two meet, a fine white precipitation develops: crystals so small they look like flour shaken into a bowl. The roofs steepen to shed it. Gutters swell. A neighbourhood of hinged panels closes with a rolling clatter.

Afterward, the crystals are gathered and pressed into new construction. A parapet appears where there was none before. A conduit thickens. The weather leaves the place slightly more built.

There is no central machine to watch, no obvious engine responsible for the transformations. Work passes from surface to surface. A wall clouds, sheds a skin, becomes clear again; the discarded skin folds itself into a channel. Two streets exchange levels while their bridges remain connected. For a moment a narrow passage reveals a view much deeper than the cupboard permits—ranks of small pale façades receding into cool daylight—then a gate slides across it, and the far wall is once again close enough to touch.

The old building above takes no notice. Its stair treads remain dry. Dust lies in the angle beside the landing. Only the bottom door is damp, and only at certain hours does the roar behind it become loud enough to hear from the hall.

Inside, beneath the steady borrowed daylight, a fresh ridge lifts from the basin floor. Water divides around its sharp crest. Before it has finished rising, three bridges have reached out to meet it.
