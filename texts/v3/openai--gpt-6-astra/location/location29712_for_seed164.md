The ground is pale and newly broken, though nothing here remembers the breaking.

It rises in slabs the size of provinces, tilted just enough that water gathers against their uphill edges. Between them are straight-sided clefts whose bottoms disappear into blue atmospheric haze. Their rims are sharp. A boot placed on an edge would meet an almost manufactured corner: a thin white lip, a face of greenish glass, then several miles of empty air. There is no scree below. Nothing has weathered loose.

At this height the cold seems to come from the open space as much as from the stone. The sky is an ordinary, luminous gray, brighter in one quarter, with enough light to distinguish the distant planes of the country. Shadows are soft and short. Across a gap, a cliff may look close enough to study until a bank of cloud passes in front of it and reveals that its smallest visible notch could contain a city.

There are cities. It takes time to learn where to look.

Most of the plateau is bare: long expanses of chalk-colored mineral, shallow water, occasional fields of dark vegetation. The cities occupy narrow strips along the fractures. From a distance they resemble the black seams in a cracked enamel bowl. Up close, each seam resolves into substations, terraced machine halls, bridges, cable galleries, and rows of tall, windowless buildings descending along the cliff. Their lowest structures hang beneath the land.

A city can extend for hundreds of miles and remain only three streets wide.

The streets have drainage slots down their centers. Their paving is dark ceramic, glazed by a permanent film of moisture. There are handrails, covered stairways, numbered entrances, recessed lamps burning a dull amber in the brighter daylight. Freight platforms stand at regular intervals. Here and there an enclosed waiting room contains benches facing a wall of cloudy glass.

Everything is proportioned for occupants. Nothing occupies it.

No wings cross the gaps. No small body disturbs the plants. There are no tracks in the soft beds beside the drains, no shells, no discarded bones. Even the old sheltered corners under the stairs contain only mineral dust and the fine, blunt roots of things growing down from above.

The noise is immense.

It reaches across the apparently empty country as a deep, continuous pressure, something felt in the teeth before it becomes a sound. Near the cities it separates into layers: the bass of generators, the broad rushing of water inside pipes, the high electrical note of equipment housed behind ribbed walls. Huge ducts open beneath the terraces. Their mouths are too wide to see into from any one position, and each emits a steady tone. Across a fracture, other mouths answer at slightly different pitches.

There is no rhythm to the machinery. No starting cycle, no impact, no warning bell. Even the freight lines move with a continuous frictionless hiss, pulling flat, unmarked carriers through enclosed tracks.

Walking changes the sound. The place itself does not. At one corner a bass note cancels and the world abruptly feels light; two steps farther on, it returns with enough force to tremble in the cavities of the chest. There are whole streets where two industrial voices hold a third, inaudible vibration between them. Water in the drainage channels stands in low, evenly spaced ridges there.

The water is everywhere close, though rarely abundant.

It sheets over the plateau in layers too thin to hide the stone. It beads on the underside of rails, darkens concrete around bolt heads, gathers in the cups of broad leaves. The upper surfaces of the great slabs are covered with shallow basins, each edged by a line of black moss. Some basins are no larger than a footprint. Others reach to the horizon, yet remain shallow enough that the pale bottom is visible beneath their gray water.

Along the fractures, springs emerge from horizontal bands of porous rock. Their discharge is constant. Thousands of little falls descend the cliff faces in clear, unbroken threads, joining below into hanging curtains of water. The falls vanish into cloud long before they could reach a bottom.

There is no spray beating back from below. The water simply leaves the visible world.

At the lip of one such fall, the vegetation is unexpectedly thick. Low shrubs make dense, interlocked mats, their branches pressed flat against the warmth leaking from the rock. Their leaves are broad, leathery, and dark enough to look almost blue. Between them grow translucent green blades with blunt ends, standing close together like the cut fibers of a cable. Moss swells over every sheltered ledge. Its surface holds so much water that pressure from a hand would release a clear pool.

The lushness has strict boundaries. A bed of plants may end along a line as clean as a shoreline, with naked mineral continuing beyond it for fifty miles.

The roots follow heat. Under the richest growth are buried conduits, natural fissures, or bands of stone that carry some of the city’s power outward into the plateau. From above, the vegetation would reveal the buried system: dark branching lines, wide green junctions, solitary round thickets over valves or wells. Occasionally a great patch of scrub flourishes in the middle of apparent nowhere. Beneath it, far down, an inaccessible machine is doing its work.

There are no flowers. The plants spread by contact, branch rooting into moss, moss holding the wet place in which another blade can stand. At their outer limits, growth and loss meet exactly. The last leaf remains the last leaf.

That is the peculiar condition of the country. Water moves, current flows, the freight carriers travel, but nothing accumulates.

The falls have not cut back their lips. The basins have no deep sediment. The white corners of the cliffs retain the same fine edges they had when the stone first separated. In the channels, dissolved mineral returns to the walls precisely where friction removes it. A scratch in the pavement fills from its bottom with pale crystals until the glaze is whole again. The process is very slow to watch and absolute in its result.

The factories participate in this balance.

Their largest halls contain banks of vertical cylinders that continue below the floor and above the roof. In the spaces between them run inspection walkways, glossy with condensation. Thick cables enter one side of each cylinder; pipes depart from the other. Gauges hold their needles against single marks. Behind heavy viewing windows, a white substance passes upward without turbulence.

It is possible to follow the outgoing pipes to the edge of the city. There they bend down the cliff and enter the rock.

The machines are feeding the plateau back into itself.

Other installations seem to maintain the gaps. Arrays of insulated pylons stand on opposite rims, facing one another across miles of air. Between their tips, the mist lies in long level bars. Beneath the pylons the rock is bare and unusually smooth, as though the cliff faces are bearing a pressure that never permits them to approach or retreat.

The land is held at the instant of its emergence: high, cold, wet, still carrying the clean geometry of whatever lifted it.

Beyond the last service road, that geometry becomes difficult to take in. The plateau continues through its own weather. Low cloud occupies the fractures; higher cloud rests against distant slopes. An entire storm system lies beneath the level of a bridge, its white upper surface illuminated through openings in the land. Farther out, there are isolated slabs with no city visible on them, only a dark wet margin and a faint thread of vegetation. The space between these presences is so large that looking across it becomes an effort. The eye keeps trying to find an intermediate object.

There is none.

You could stand beside a warm pipe, among wet leaves, with a thousand miles of bare country before you. Water would flow past your feet toward the edge. The machinery would fill the air with its vast sustained chord. On the far side of the cleft, rows of amber lamps would remain visible in the gray light, marking staircases, loading doors, the entrances to covered bridges.

One bridge ends halfway across.

Its unfinished end is square and clean, equipped with a railing and a drain. Water passes through the drain in a slender stream. Beyond the railing, the remaining distance is open sky.

Nothing about it suggests that work has stopped.
