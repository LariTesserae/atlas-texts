The first thing you notice is how far a sound can travel without finding anything to stop it.

A mill strikes somewhere beyond the northern rim of the city. Six heavy blows, then a scraping vibration that passes through the soles of your feet. Across the intervening miles, nothing moves except a few long strips of foliage lifting from the ground. There are no trees to break the view. The city lies low under a pale sky: broad roofs, shallow basins, avenues wide enough to have once accommodated processions of machines. Everything has the subdued green of plants kept alive through a very long winter.

You are standing on a leaf.

It is not a leaf in the ordinary sense, although the veins are visible. Each is wider than a road, and the spaces between them contain districts. Its edge lies beyond sight. Beneath it are older leaves, compressed into a plateau whose flanks descend through cloud. Along the southern escarpment, where the layers are exposed, you can distinguish the last several thousand surfaces: pale fibers, dark seams, the occasional thick rib curved like the hull of a buried ship. Farther down, the layers become too thin to separate. They look like grain in wood.

There is no exposed stone. What resembles gravel is a scatter of hard seed husks. What looks like dust is dry pith ground fine by wheels and wind. Even the gray material in the road cuttings is alive, though slowly enough that a cut can remain open for generations before its sides meet.

The plateau is very high and almost flat. Whatever mountains it once covered have lost their shapes beneath it. The land rises in long, nearly imperceptible swells, then descends into hollows so broad that walking into one feels less like going downhill than like watching the horizon lift. The wind has spent ages rounding every exposed surface. Roof corners are soft. Doorways have worn into ovals. There are ridges outside the city that may have been walls, or roots, or the windward edges of leaves laid down before the city existed.

Age here is visible less as ruin than as the absence of anything sharp.

***

The streets are alive, but they are not lush.

Their surface is a close, tough nap of tiny leaves, each no larger than a fingernail, laid almost flat against the wind. They darken beneath a passing shadow. In sunlight they turn their silver undersides upward, and a whole avenue can brighten gradually from one end to the other.

There is very little growing *above* the ground. A few isolated stalks rise over the roofs, bearing instruments or empty signal cages. Far apart on the open commons stand thick, hollow trunks with no branches. Their flared mouths face west. They are air intakes.

Between these sparse uprights lies an enormous amount of space.

The city has squares larger than its inhabited quarters. Residential blocks sit in low clusters at the margins, separated by acres of level green pavement. Long arcades contain mostly closed entrances. Here and there, a window has been trained open and a white curtain stirs behind its fleshy frame. A person crossing one of the squares remains visible for a quarter of an hour.

The buildings were grown in molds.

You can still see the seams: vertical lines along the walls where panels were clamped around swelling tissue, small circular scars where braces passed through. Older houses have shed their original facades several times. Beneath the eaves, layers of discarded bark curl outward, exposing the faded numbers of previous addresses.

Construction continues in a few places. Beyond a fence of stiff bracts, a municipal hall is thickening around its framework. Its walls are translucent near the top. Sunlight passes through them and falls red across the empty rooms. Workers have tied cords around the young window openings to keep them from healing shut.

The city’s maintenance consists largely of persuading things to remain useful.

Doors must be exercised. Staircases must be weighted while they grow. A room left unoccupied for too long becomes a knot.

***

There is machinery everywhere, though much of it is below the surface.

The mills draw on pressure rising through the plateau’s veins. Sap descends through one system of conduits and returns through another, driven by differences in temperature between the cold escarpments and the sunlit crown. Turbine houses straddle the largest channels. Inside them, laminated wooden rotors turn in dark chambers lined with slick, pale tissue.

They produce a deep, continuous pounding.

Near an operating mill, you feel each stroke behind the eyes. Window shutters tremble. Loose seed cases roll to the edges of tables. Conversations are conducted in the intervals between blows, and people who have lived here a long time can finish a sentence just before the next impact without seeming to hurry.

Elsewhere there is the clatter of freight wagons on hardened runners, the whine of saws cutting structural fiber, the sudden enormous report of a pressure valve opening. Steam rises only briefly. In the cool, dry air it disappears almost at once.

The factories make beams, woven membranes, pressure vessels, replacement teeth for gears. Their raw material arrives alive and generally leaves alive. Finished goods are stacked in bright yards so they can feed before shipment.

Some of the broad, apparently empty commons are production fields. Their pavement will be peeled up at the end of the season in strips thick enough to become roof panels. Fresh tissue already waits beneath it.

Nothing here is merely scenery.

Yet much of it is left alone for centuries.

***

Water appears in small, valuable circumstances.

At dawn, condensation gathers along the undersides of railings. The city’s drinking basins stand beneath enormous horizontal leaves, tilted by slow-growing joints to catch the morning beadfall. For an hour after sunrise, the basins tick quietly. Then the leaves dry, and the sound is lost beneath the mills.

There are damp recesses under bridges where fine white roots hang like uncombed hair. There are narrow channels carrying a finger’s depth of clear water through the factory districts. Along their banks grow softer plants: transparent fronds, little cushions of moss, a dark red film that withdraws into cracks when touched.

Rain comes a few times each season. It falls hard and cold, rattling on the roofs. The ground takes it so quickly that there are seldom puddles. For several days afterward, the city expands. Hinges stick. The avenues rise slightly along their centers. Surveyors walk the main routes with measuring rods, marking where freight clearances have narrowed.

The air smells of split stems and cold timber. Near the mills there is a sharper smell, almost sour, from sap heated under pressure.

No insects gather at the basins. No birds sit along the rooflines.

Once, far out on a production field, you may see a small gray animal traveling toward a drain. Its legs are difficult to count because it keeps placing them in the same few footprints. It pauses when the mill pauses. When the pounding resumes, it disappears beneath the pavement.

People set out little ramps at the drains, but no one seems to agree on what the animals eat.

***

By midday the light is clear and ordinary. Shadows have definite edges. You can see the distant intake towers and the pale service roads winding between them.

This is when the errors in the landscape are easiest to notice.

A freight wagon crosses an open square, and its shadow continues across after the wagon has stopped. A low building visible from the east has three stories; approached from the west, it has only one, though the same occupants can be seen at the windows. On the oldest avenues, walking downhill sometimes brings you back to a higher point.

These are not passing disturbances. They have addresses. They appear on maintenance charts.

At certain intersections, painted arrows specify which direction is currently continuous. A bell sounds before the municipal workers reverse them. Deliveries wait beneath shelters while the far end of a street is reassigned.

The trouble seems worst where too many old surfaces have been compressed together. The plateau remembers its previous arrangements imperfectly. A district can settle into the position it occupied when the supporting leaf was young and curled, even though that leaf has lain flat for ages.

There is a square near the western edge where footsteps arrive before the walker. You hear someone approaching across the open pavement, turn, and find it empty. Several minutes later a figure emerges from an arcade, following the sound’s route.

The benches there face away from the entrances.

Other places are less manageable. One factory continues to sound although its entire building was harvested long ago. On cold afternoons its missing roof sheds condensation into the grass. The city still supplies the site with pressure, because shutting off the line causes the noise to spread into occupied buildings.

Far below the escarpment, through a break in cloud, you can sometimes see another plateau. It carries the same city, smaller with distance, but its mills are silent and its squares are crowded.

The viewing platforms have been allowed to grow shut.

***

Toward evening the wind strengthens. Long seams in the ground open a little, releasing air that smells of deep, wet leaves. The factories increase their speed as the upper surface cools. Across the city, pressure horns call to one another: a low note from the southern works, two higher notes from the reservoir district, an answering bellow from beyond the horizon.

Lights come on inside the houses, warm behind thin walls. The open squares remain bright for a while under the sky.

From the rim, the city seems scarcely to rise above its surroundings. It is an arrangement of low swellings and pale incisions, a few upright stems, distant smoke-like breaths that vanish before they can drift. The great surface continues beyond it, worn smooth, alive down to depths no shaft has reached.

Under your feet, something tightens.

The nearest avenue shifts by the width of a finger. Far away, a row of shutters closes almost simultaneously. Then comes the long scraping sound of the plateau adjusting its weight on the leaves beneath it.

The mills keep working through it.
