The height is apparent first in what is missing. There are no insects around the warm stones, no distant calls, no soft mass of air holding the sounds of the ground together. A chip breaks from a ledge and makes one small, distinct click. Its fall gives back nothing.

Beyond the ledge, other summits stand at nearly the same level, separated by chasms too dark to measure. They occupy a broad region: a tilted tableland to the east, a chain of narrow towers, an open saddle strewn with slabs the size of houses. Farther away, their upper edges disappear into a ceiling of black airborne grit. There are no stars. Occasionally the grit thins enough to reveal another ridge, higher than the first, where there seemed to be only sky.

The stone is warm through the soles of the feet. In cracks it is hotter. A hand held above one of them feels a dry, sustained breath, carrying the smell of struck flint and something metallic. Nothing condenses on the cooler rims. White deposits fringe some openings, but they are brittle crystals, sharp enough to cut skin. They grow outward into the escaping gas.

This is a mountain range still acquiring its surfaces.

Most of the rock has never been exposed before. Broad faces stand clean and angular, without stains or weathered hollows. Where a wall has split, the matching halves can be seen across the gap: a knob on one side, its exact socket on the other. Some gaps are narrow enough to step across. Others have widened until it takes an effort to believe the two cliffs once touched.

There is almost no loose soil. What looks like a path is usually a shelf along a fracture. What looks like gravel is a recent scattering of shattered crystal, each piece retaining edges and points. Dust occurs in thin, strangely placed streaks—on the undersides of overhangs, in vertical seams, caught halfway up walls—where hot currents have carried it. The broad horizontal surfaces are swept bare.

Under that apparent bareness, the ground is continually shifting.

A long plate of stone rises at one end, so slowly at first that the movement registers only as a change in the distant outline. Then its far edge catches. The whole plate shudders, breaks into three unequal sections, and settles into a different arrangement. A passage closes. Another opens beside it. Deep within the new opening there is a dull red line, quickly obscured by falling fragments.

The sound arrives as a low concussion through the feet, with a brief rattle afterward. Up here, the thin air does little with it. A collapse across the valley can be immense and nearly silent: an entire buttress sliding down in a gray veil, its disappearance more startling than any noise it makes.

Between these events, the quiet is precise. A crystal ticks as it cools. Grit moves a few inches across a slab. Gas whistles through a narrow aperture until the aperture changes shape and the whistle stops.

The warmth comes unevenly. One terrace holds the lingering heat of rock lifted from depth; another, exposed longer, is cold enough to sting bare fingers. Hot seams thread between them. In the dark, their faint light reveals very little: the underside of a projecting shelf, a row of angular stones, one face of a column with the rest swallowed by blackness. There are places where the ground is visible only because it interrupts a glow farther below.

Across the saddle stand three low enclosures.

They are made from stacked stone, no higher than a crouching person, with narrow openings turned toward what may once have been the sheltered side. Their construction is simple but unmistakable. Flat pieces have been chosen for the tops of the walls. Small wedges hold the larger blocks steady. Inside one enclosure, several slabs form a raised sleeping place just long enough for a body.

Nobody is there.

The walls are older than the ledge beneath them. Their stones have dulled edges and pale crusts, unlike the fresh black material exposed around their foundations. They must have come upward with a larger piece of the mountain, surviving where everything nearby tore apart. One enclosure still stands square. Another has been stretched open by a crack running through its floor. Half of the third hangs over empty space.

There are no tools, vessels, ashes, or marks that explain who used them. A few small stones remain in a careful row beside an entrance. When the ledge trembles, two fall. The others stay where they were placed.

Below the shelters, in a recess protected from the abrasive currents, a dark film covers the rock. It is thin enough to resemble soot. At its edge, however, it divides into tiny branching lobes, each following a hairline source of heat. This is the only growth visible anywhere on the saddle. It has no stems and raises nothing into the air. In the places where its seam has cooled, it has contracted into gray flakes.

Fresh cracks offer it new territory, though most open too violently or become too hot. A patch may have spread for years across a handspan of sheltered stone, then be folded out of sight in a single night. There is no sign that anything feeds on it. Nothing crosses between the patches except drifting mineral dust.

Toward the towers, the terrain becomes less settled.

Thin fins rise out of the ground at steep angles, some translucent along their edges. They lean against one another in sparse groups, leaving wide, bare courts between them. Heat passes visibly through these courts as a distortion of the dim red seams beyond. The fins are remnants of a crust that has buckled and lifted; beneath them, the darker material is still yielding.

Here, movement can be watched without waiting. A fissure travels across the court in short advances. Each advance exposes a thread of light. One fin begins to tilt, pauses against its neighbor, then pushes both of them over. They fall so close that the fragments strike the ground with a hard, surprisingly ordinary clatter.

Afterward there is a new view through the towers.

It looks across a basin suspended among the summits, its floor broken into large, slowly turning plates. Their edges grind together. Some ride up; others descend into red openings and lose their corners. Dry fumes rise from the joins, then flatten beneath a crosscurrent and stream away into darkness. The whole basin is in motion, but there are long moments when nothing in it can be heard.

On the near rim, a fresh shelf projects over the drop. Its upper surface is perfectly clean. A faint heat lifts from it. No dust has yet settled there, and no fragment lies upon it.

Then a small stone rolls onto the shelf from above. It stops near the outer edge.

For a while, that is all the shelf holds.
