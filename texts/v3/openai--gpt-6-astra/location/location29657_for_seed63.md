The hollow sits just below the mountain’s summit, behind a lip of black stone. From outside, it looks like a shallow notch where a piece of the ridge has fallen away. Inside, it widens into a chamber large enough to hold a few houses, with a ceiling lost among descending ribs of rock. The entrance admits a thin blade of sky. Beyond it, other summits stand below eye level, their pale scree briefly visible when lightning moves inside the distant clouds.

No rain reaches this height. Even the storms pass underneath.

Heat comes up through the floor.

It is strongest in the central seams, where the rock has split into long, narrow slots. Nothing glows down there. Holding a hand above one feels like holding it near an open kiln, but the darkness remains complete. The air smells of scorched salt, sulfur, and the warm, bitter odor of crowded animals. Close to the entrance, the wind is cold enough to sting. A few paces inward, it becomes difficult to breathe without opening the mouth.

The stone between the seams has weathered into rounded humps. Over a long time, heating and cooling have peeled away its sharper edges, and the inhabitants have polished the rest. There is very little loose grit. Anything small enough to move is eventually carried into a crack, swallowed, or cemented to something else.

At first the floor appears pebbled.

Then a patch of it turns.

The smallest animals are the size of lentils: flat, dark-backed bodies fitted closely together, each with a pale fringe tucked beneath its shell. They cover the warm stone so completely that their boundaries are difficult to distinguish. When they move, they do so in shallow waves. One lifts, another passes beneath it, and a third settles into the space left behind. The floor can flow for several minutes without exposing any rock.

They graze on the thin crust that forms around the hot seams. It grows as a dull yellow film, almost invisible until a fresh strip catches the light from the entrance. The film is alive, although there is nothing about it that resembles a leaf or root. It takes its nourishment from what the mountain exhales. The little armored grazers scrape it away with mouthparts fine enough to leave parallel lines on a grain of salt.

Their moisture stays inside them. Their joints close like overlapping lids; their droppings are dry white flakes. When one dies, the others open it from underneath before its body can lose much to the air.

Above this moving pavement, the chamber is packed.

Animals cling to the walls in overlapping shelves. Others hang from the ceiling, their long bodies folded around the rock ribs, making the roof appear much lower than it is. Pale cords cross the gaps between pillars. Some are abandoned skins. Some are limbs. Some move so slowly that the distinction takes hours to establish.

The largest creatures are scarcely longer than a person’s forearm, but their numbers make size irrelevant. There is no empty recess deep enough to put a fist into. Every hollow holds an abdomen, a clutch of sealed eggs, a sleeping face with its sensory bristles pressed flat. Where two slabs meet, flexible bodies have inserted themselves into the join. Even the underside of a fallen chip may carry a little community, arranged from the heat-loving animals nearest the stone to the cooler-bodied hunters on the outside.

Most have no eyes. Those that do keep them shut except during the brief change of light at the entrance.

The chamber is remarkably quiet.

There are no territorial cries. A creature that announces its position to the whole hollow seldom keeps it. Courtship happens through pressure: a sequence of taps transmitted through stone, or one body leaning against another with a particular rhythm. Alarm travels faster. Hundreds of feet retract from a surface at once, leaving a sudden still patch that neighboring animals avoid.

What can be heard is slight and close: the faint rasp of feeding, the tick of a shell contracting, an occasional dry spill of discarded skin. The crowded walls absorb echoes. A stone dropped into the hollow seems to make a smaller sound than it should.

Three low structures stand along the cooler edge of the chamber. They are built from flat pieces of local rock, stacked without mortar and capped with broad slabs. None rises above the knee. Their entrances face away from the vents.

Whoever made them is gone.

One contains a layer of ash beneath generations of shells. Another has collapsed inward, leaving a triangular opening polished smooth by traffic. The third is still intact, though its roof slowly shifts as animals breed beneath it. Along its lintel are five shallow grooves, evenly spaced. Nothing else in the hollow is evenly spaced.

The structures have become refuges for a soft-bodied species that cannot tolerate the hottest hours. These animals resemble strips of thick, folded cloth. At rest they lie pressed together in the stone shelters, dull gray backs outward. When the floor cools, they extend jointless feet and unfold into the chamber.

They are predators, but they do not chase anything. They spread across the grazers and wait. Their undersides contain many small mouths, each able to open independently. Beneath a resting fold, a whole patch of the living floor may disappear.

Other hunters specialize in these folds. They descend from the ceiling on rigid, hooked limbs and lift one edge with extraordinary delicacy. The attack takes place underneath. From a distance, all that shows is a slow tightening, followed by the arrival of scavengers.

Activity follows the heat rather than the light.

Deep inside the mountain, something shifts at irregular intervals. A seam that has been merely warm becomes fierce. Another cools enough for the mineral film to return. The inhabitants begin moving before the change can be felt by a hand. Traffic thickens along certain ribs; the ceiling loosens into descending bodies; grazers stream sideways in bands.

During a strong pulse, the whole chamber rearranges itself.

Animals climb over the little stone buildings until their roofs vanish. Predators make room for prey because both need the same narrow zone of tolerable temperature. The pale cords spanning the pillars are drawn taut as their owners pull away from the floor. For a while, the hollow has distinct layers: small armored bodies below, folded flesh above them, suspended hunters higher still. Then the pulse passes, and the layers mingle.

Sometimes the mountain cracks.

The sound is low and brief, felt through the soles more than heard. Dust falls from a new opening. The animals beneath it scatter, and for a moment there is a bare patch—a little island of black stone in the crowded room.

That is where the first yellow film will form.

At the entrance, a few discarded shells collect behind the lip. They are bleached by the fierce daylight that never reaches most of the chamber. Wind lifts them one at a time and carries them out over the drop. They turn brightly once, then disappear into the enormous empty air below.

Inside, bodies close over the space they left.
