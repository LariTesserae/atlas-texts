The district fits inside a retaining wall. From the overhead pipe bridge you can see almost all of it: a rectangular basin of dark water, three low workshops, a pumping house, and the paved spaces between them. Beyond the wall are the backs of larger buildings. Their windowless brick faces close off the distance. There is no horizon, only a narrow band of pale, humid sky above the roofs.

Everything is low. The workshop floors stand a single step above the service lanes, and the lanes are scarcely higher than the water. Concrete walkways divide the basin into compartments. From above they look substantial; at water level they seem like thin ledges laid across something that could cover them without much effort. Each has a straight black tide mark along its side, although there is no tide here. The pumps keep the surface at the same height.

The heat is strongest beside the pumping house. You feel it on your face before you reach the open doorway: the damp warmth of the basin joined by the dry, dusty heat of motor housings. Inside, two large machines stand on square plinths. Their paint is a dull blue-green, darkened where hands and oily cloths have touched it. A third machine sits disconnected beneath a fitted cover. The running pair make a low, continuous roar that fills the room and spreads across the yard. A loose pane in the door adds a much smaller sound, a rapid glass chatter audible only when you stand beside it.

Nothing else needs to be loud to carry. Water falls through a slotted plate at the basin’s far end with a sound like heavy rain on a roof. Cooling fans beat behind wire guards. Somewhere inside the nearest workshop, a belt runs over a pulley with a soft, regular slap. The sounds overlap so thoroughly that the district seems to have one working voice, with individual parts becoming clear as you approach them.

The ground is new ground. It was made by driving sheet piles into the shallows, filling behind them, and pouring concrete over the fill. There are still crisp letters impressed beside lifting sockets, straight seams between pours, narrow ridges where wet cement squeezed beneath a shutter. A few pale stones lie on the bare gravel margins, their broken faces sharp enough to catch the light. There are no rounded banks, no old paths worn into the earth. Where the district meets the water, the edge is an engineered right angle.

Yet almost every surface is wet.

Condensation gathers beneath the colder supply pipes and drops onto the lane. The drops strike the same shallow hollows in the concrete, making separate, persistent tapping sounds. Drain channels carry thin streams along the workshop walls. Beneath a leaking flange, a mineral crust has formed around the bolts, white at its outer edge and the color of weak tea near the metal. The paving there is dark and slick. Farther away it is merely damp, with a sheen that takes the shape of whatever light hangs above it.

The lamps remain on beneath the awnings. They illuminate short lengths of pipe, a door handle, the yellow edge of a step. They do not reach far into the yard. Tall surrounding buildings and broad corrugated roofs keep most of the place in shade, but the shade offers little relief. The air is heavy enough that work clothes hang from shoulders rather than moving with the body.

There are few people visible. The machinery takes up less space than its noise suggests, leaving broad areas of empty paving between objects: a hose neatly coiled on a rack, a pallet of replacement filters, a small electric cart with its charging lead attached. Handrails mark routes that would otherwise be obvious. Numbers painted on walls identify valves, doors, and sections of the basin. The place is organized down to details that rarely require attention.

Vegetation survives chiefly where a seam allows it. A tuft of sedge grows beside a drain outlet. Several coarse grasses lean out from the gravel under a stair. On the far side of the basin, an elbow of soil supports a short stand of reeds, their lower stems crowded with old snail shells. There are no trees. The green that matters most is below the waterline: a close coating of algae on concrete, bright where a lamp reaches it, almost black elsewhere.

The basin is far more occupied than the yard.

Small fish hold beneath the walkways, their bodies appearing in brief silver angles when they turn. They gather where the falling water brings air down into the pool. Snails adhere to submerged bolts and the rough undersides of ledges. In the sheltered compartments, insects dimple the surface among scraps of floating husk. Midges hang in loose clouds beneath the lamps, and pale geckos wait above them on the walls, so still that they resemble small defects in the plaster.

Two gray herons keep to opposite ends of the basin. Each has a favored stretch of railing whitened beneath its feet. Neither pays much attention to the motors. The regular passage of workers disturbs them less than a floating object moving against the usual current.

There is little sense of weather here. Rain would increase the flow in the drains; sunlight would brighten the strip above the roofs. Neither reaches deeply into the district’s arrangement. The water is replenished, warmed, aerated, and drawn away at matched rates. Float switches preserve its level. Fans preserve the air’s heavy circulation. The wet places stay wet, and the dry patch beneath the spare pump stays dry.

Even the signs of use seem settled into their allotted positions: the oil stain within its painted maintenance square, the clean arc swept by a door, the algae ending exactly at the reach of the water.

Standing on the central walkway, you can feel the pumps through the soles of your shoes. The railing is warm and faintly moist. Below it, fish face into the current without advancing. There is motion everywhere you look closely enough, but across the whole enclosed yard nothing appears to be going anywhere.
