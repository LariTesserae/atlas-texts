The high country has been worn almost smooth. There are no young peaks, no sharp ridges catching the sun—only immense pale backs of stone, rounded and low against one another, ending at distances the eye mistakes for the horizon. Then a cloud moves below an edge, and the scale becomes clear. These are the last upper surfaces of mountains. Their valleys have deepened until they are mostly air.

From one stone back to the next may be half a day’s walk, or an uncrossable gulf. In the ordinary yellow light, both distances look much the same.

The rock is cream-colored where exposed, gray-green where shaded, and everywhere softly pitted. Loose grit is uncommon. Whatever could be broken off easily was broken off long ago. Even the largest hollows have polished lips, and the channels crossing the plateau are shallow, broad, almost gentle. They were made by rivers that no longer reach this height. A person can follow one for miles through warm grass and find, at its end, a smooth spillway pouring nothing into the sky.

There is very little weathering left to do here. Almost all the violence is happening in the air.

Wind rises through the spaces between the mountains with a continuous, enormous roar. It does not blow steadily across the ground. It wells up, doubles back, strikes from above. A patch of fern may lie flat to the east while another, twenty paces away, lashes westward. In the middle, tall stems spin upright in a small, furious circle. Loose leaves sometimes climb past the plateau without ever touching it, arriving from forests far below: dark, unfamiliar leaves as broad as sleeping mats, already shredded by their ascent.

The noise is too large to sort into separate sounds at first. After a few hours, distinctions appear. There is the deep furnace-note from the gulf to the south. There is a hard rattling where dry seed cases strike one another. There is the great soft crash of vegetation turning over in a gust, slope after slope, as though something enormous were running beneath it. At irregular intervals, the rock itself whistles.

It is full of holes.

Most are too narrow to admit a finger. Others open beneath grass into chambers large enough to shelter a few people. The oldest channels descend out of reach, intersecting, widening, connecting with the cliff faces far below. Air is driven through them by differences in heat and pressure. On sunny afternoons, a hole that has been drawing air inward all morning may begin to breathe warm vapor instead. Its whistle drops through several notes. The plants around it rise.

There are places where the ground gives a long, low moan before the wind changes.

The vegetation grows lavishly wherever these breaths reach the surface. Ferns stand shoulder-high in sheltered runnels. Thick-leaved creepers cover whole acres of stone in overlapping fans, glossy above and rust-colored underneath. Small trees spread sideways from roots wedged deep into old water channels; their trunks are scarcely taller than a person, but their branches extend for twenty or thirty paces. Some have rooted again at every point where a limb touches earth, so that one tree becomes a low wood with no obvious center.

Nothing grows tall for long.

The most luxuriant places are depressions no deeper than a house, where dark humus has collected and the wind passes overhead. Looking down into one, you see a dense, warm garden: braided stems, dangling roots, pale new leaves still furled like wet cloth. Looking across the country, you scarcely see the gardens at all. There is only the pale stone, the low vegetation, and a tremendous amount of empty sky.

Water seldom lies in the open. Rain comes in short, slanting bursts that may soak one ridge while leaving the next dry. Much of it is snatched away before it can collect. The plants live on what they can intercept: mist ascending from below, condensation inside curled leaves, moisture breathed out of the stone. Under the creeping growth, the ground is cool and damp enough to stain a knee. A few inches beyond its edge, the rock is dry and warm to the palm.

The pools are small and unexpectedly deep in color. They occupy smooth sockets shaded by roots, their surfaces dark amber from fallen leaves. Sometimes a gust finds a passage underneath one and fills it with bubbles. Sometimes a pool drains overnight, leaving a lacquer of pollen on the stone, and another fills several hundred paces away.

No one who lives here counts on a particular pool.

The warmth is surprising at this height. The sun is strong but not harsh, and the air coming up from the depths carries the stored heat of lower country. Even in cloud, it has a warm, mineral smell. There are cool hours near dawn when every leaf gathers droplets along its rim. By midmorning the drops have either been swallowed into the plant’s folded heart or shaken into the air.

During the strongest winds, entire mats of creeper lift from the ground.

They do not tear free. Their outer roots loosen, and their hooked inner roots hold. A green sheet rises, billows, falls, rises again. Beneath it, the pale underside flashes with dangling rootlets and the black pockets of soil they retain. These plants have grown this way for so long that some hollows are roofed by them. You can crawl underneath and sit in a dim chamber that inflates and collapses around you, smelling of bruised stems.

Occasionally, one breaks loose. It goes tumbling across the plateau, shedding dirt and insects, folds itself through a cleft, and disappears. Weeks later there may be a fresh green patch on a ledge that was bare before.

Animals are seldom seen. Small brown creatures live among the roots, gripping with all four feet and feeding when the air quiets. Their droppings and tiny, clipped stalks are easier to find than they are. Once or twice in a day, a broad-winged bird may rise out of a gulf. It hangs motionless for a moment, one wingtip trembling, then slides sideways behind the stone. Its call is inaudible.

The few human shelters are built below the surface.

A low rim of fitted stones, a covered smoke hole, three steps descending between roots: that may be all there is to see. Roofs are made of split branches weighted with slabs and covered by living creeper. Inside, baskets hang from pegs, stone-edged tools rest in wall niches, and water collects drop by drop in shallow clay bowls beneath a cool slab. The doorways face away from the nearest vents. There are no loose shutters.

Paths are marked with small stones placed in drilled cups. A stacked cairn would not last. Where a route approaches an edge, handholds have been polished into the rock by generations of fingers. The dangerous crossings have ropes braided from the long fibers peeled out of dead runners. These ropes are replaced often, even when they look sound.

People speak close to one another’s ears. Out on the open backs, they communicate by touching an arm, pointing, waiting. A dropped tool is caught with the foot before anyone tries to bend down for it. Nothing is set on a ledge simply because the air happens to be still.

There are brief lulls, but they are local. You may stand in perfect calm while a green hollow beside you is being thrashed almost white. In such a pocket, warmth settles on the face. A bead of water lengthens at a leaf tip. For a few seconds you can hear it fall.

Then the grass along a distant rim goes flat.

The next gust is visible crossing the plateau: leaves turning, trees pressing their branches to the stone, a loose veil of moisture rising from the gardens. It takes a surprisingly long time to arrive. There is time to kneel, put one hand through a root loop, and look out across the gaps.

Beyond them, the other rounded heights continue into the afternoon, their surfaces old and almost unchanged, their green coverings lifting and settling in the roar.
