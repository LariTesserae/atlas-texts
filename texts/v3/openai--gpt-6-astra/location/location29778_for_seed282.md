The hollow lies between two floors that the building’s plans show as touching.

It is scarcely large enough to crawl into. A corrugated ceiling hangs over a shallow basin, with pipes descending through it in close bundles, their insulated elbows almost meeting the floor. Beyond them, the space narrows into slots and folded recesses. Light travels poorly here. A green inspection lamp reveals one wet ridge, the undersides of three pipes, a little white growth on a bracket. Farther in, occasional amber pulses show that the hollow continues.

Everything is manufactured, including the ground.

Under a dusting of frost, the basin consists of overlapping sheets of grey ceramic no thicker than fingernails. They have been pushed upward from narrow dies concealed beneath the floor. Each arrives warm enough to darken the frost along its leading edge, advances a few millimetres, and cools. The next sheet lifts it slightly. In this way the hollow is acquiring hills.

The youngest rise stands beside a drain: a thumb-high arrangement of translucent scales, sharp enough to catch loose cloth. Its slopes have never carried running water. No grit has yet gathered at its foot. Elsewhere, older extrusions have begun to lean against one another, making short ridges and enclosed valleys whose floors still bear the parallel scoring of the machinery that produced them. These marks look like roads until one follows them to the sheer face of a new fold.

The cold enters through the ceiling. Broad refrigeration plates sit above the corrugations, hidden but close, and their outlines appear below as rectangles of heavier frost. Most surfaces are dry. Moisture gathers only where something interrupts the insulation: a bolt, a cable clamp, the thin metal throat of an inspection port. Around these points, clear ice spreads in tight, flattened lobes.

At intervals, a pipe warms.

The change begins with a small loss of whiteness. Then beads appear along its underside, trembling in place while the metal expands. For several minutes there is rain over a patch of ground no larger than a hand. Drops strike the ceramic with a bright, hollow ticking. Some run into the scored channels. Others freeze where they land and build little transparent pedestals.

One channel feeds a pool behind a raised seam. The pool is long and narrow, black at the centre, silver where it touches the walls. It has no stone beneath it, only a flexible membrane printed with serial numbers. The numbers swell and diminish when pressure changes below. Sometimes the whole pool rises without overflowing; its banks rise with it.

There is more movement here than the quiet first suggests.

A cable slowly tightens against a guide. A valve turns through a fraction of a revolution. Beneath the basin, feed screws advance material into the dies with a low effort that can be felt through a palm laid on the floor. Their work is staggered. One ridge grows while another rests. The hollow never quite shakes, but its surfaces are seldom all still at once.

Most sounds are small and separated by long pauses: the dry click of a thermal switch, a drop entering the pool, the delicate fracture of frost along an expanding joint. Once in a cycle, a relay engages somewhere beyond the wall. Its single heavy knock fills the space so completely that the silence afterward seems newly installed.

Vegetation survives on the warm side of two drainage wicks.

The wicks hang from a service coupling, pale woven strips stiffened by mineral deposits. Along their lower edges grows a narrow green nap. Close up, it resolves into short, crowded leaves, each folded along its length and rimmed with clear ice. There are no roots in the ceramic. Fine threads enter the weave and follow it upward into the coupling, where diluted salts arrive with the condensation.

During a warming interval, the leaves open almost flat. Their colour deepens. Tiny bubbles form in the water caught between them. When the pipe cools, they fold again, slowly enough that the movement is easy to miss.

A few fragments have established themselves farther down, on a porous filter tile. They make scattered green points in a broad chalky surface. Beyond that tile, the new ground offers them almost nothing: no retained moisture, no loose material, no sheltered thickness into which they can grow. A fragment dropped on a fresh ridge remains green for a while, then becomes part of its faint surface dust.

Nothing comes to eat it. There are no wings in the lamplight, no small bodies under the plates. The narrow passages stay occupied by cables, water and cold air.

At the far end, behind a mesh guard, the floor is still being made.

Here the ceramic emerges as a dull, flexible ribbon. It passes between rollers, receives a shallow corrugation, and slides into the hollow through a slit fringed with ice. Cooling makes it rigid. Continued pressure makes it buckle. The machine measures resistance rather than height, so it pushes hardest where the terrain is lowest and stops wherever the new sheet meets an older one firmly enough.

The resulting shapes are intricate and provisional. An arch formed yesterday now carries the edge of a rising plate. Beneath it is a chamber the size of a closed fist, lit amber whenever the roller sensor flashes. A bead of water hangs from its roof. On the next feed cycle the arch may lift, or split, or disappear beneath a smooth new slope.

There is little evidence of wear. Edges remain keen. Hollows contain shavings rather than sediment. Even the water is mostly occupied with finding its first routes, testing a seam, backing up behind an obstruction, slipping at last into an opening that did not exist during the previous thaw.

Near the entrance, a low ridge has begun to press against the underside of a pipe. Its highest scales are flattening under the insulation. Each advance lifts the pipe imperceptibly, and the old droplets beneath it gather toward a new lowest point.

Soon the rain will fall somewhere else.
