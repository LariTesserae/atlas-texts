The ground gives slightly, then holds. Under its thin film of warm water, it has the close grain of an unopened leaf.

There is no soil within reach. What feels like earth is a thickness of compressed stems, pale sheaths, and roots grown sideways through one another until their separate surfaces have disappeared. Lower down, the tissue becomes firmer. It carries the weight of the country. Farther down still, where a continent ought to have its stone, there are dark layers of living fiber under such compression that they ring if struck—though nothing here strikes them.

The land lies low. Its rises are broad and shallow, seldom taller than the vegetation standing on them. Water occupies the seams: long, narrow pools between slightly lifted plates of rootwood, wet hollows beneath hanging membranes, clear beads seated in the mouths of pores. Every surface is either damp or close enough to dampness that a touch brings moisture out of it. Yet there is little open marsh. The plants have taken most of the space water might otherwise fill, holding it inside their stems, under their skins, in innumerable flat reservoirs stacked beneath the ground.

It is warm in the way a covered place is warm. There is no hot direction, no cool side of a trunk. The water, air, and living ground have nearly the same temperature.

Darkness prevents any single view of the landscape. A hand can discover what an eye cannot: a smooth rib curving upward out of reach; a field of small, stiff blades all inclined at precisely the same angle; the yielding lip of a pool; a wall that turns out to be a leaf, enormously thick, descending from somewhere overhead. Distance must be learned by walking. A broad obstruction takes hours to go around. A passage between two growths continues for days, its floor almost level, its sides sometimes close enough to touch together and sometimes withdrawing beyond any possible reach.

The passages have no common orientation. They belong to the way the vegetation fits together.

Were light brought here, the first impression would be of freshness. Edges are clean. A split in the ground exposes ivory tissue without a stain. The tips of narrow fronds remain sharply folded, and the massive lobes of the terrain retain the soft creases of things newly released from a husk. Nothing is weathered into roundness. Nothing has accumulated a crust.

This freshness extends across distances that should have made it impossible. A ridge stretching beyond a horizon has the tender surface of a shoot. An entire basin seems to have opened only recently.

It has seemed so for a very long time.

The plants do not mature toward some later shape. Each keeps its present form by replacing itself from within. A frayed fiber is drawn back into a sheath. A dead cell is emptied and its contents passed inward before a patch of decay can form. Edges are maintained with such fidelity that the land possesses all the fine definition of new growth without visibly growing. Its wounds close to the shape they had before the wound.

A footprint would be a considerable event here. Even that would gradually lose its distinction, lifting from below until the original grain of the surface returned.

There are trunks, though few resemble trees. Some are low cylinders wider than small valleys, laid along the ground with their lower halves embedded in it. Others rise as closely spaced, flattened fins, making chambers of darkness within the greater darkness. Between them hang cablelike roots with wet, seamless skins. They enter the ground without a tangle. Their branching happens underneath.

Within these bodies, the landscape works.

Water passes through successive living filters. Dissolved metals are caught, sorted, carried into particular tissues. Thick membranes maintain differences of electrical potential across their faces. Whole stands of flattened growth are joined into circuits by underground roots, and currents move through them without a spark or a visible glow.

There are organs that compress gas, organs that separate salts, organs that manufacture tough translucent films one molecular layer at a time. In the deep tissue, long protein assemblies turn under load. The work is distributed among so many minute moving parts that no individual motion shakes the surface.

Its scale is industrial. Its forms have never been built.

One region prepares a wax that keeps the exposed skins impermeable. Another binds trace minerals into conductive strands. Elsewhere, across an expanse broad enough to possess its own atmosphere, a single kind of low, pleated vegetation refines the fluid that circulates through all the rest. The same operation repeats beneath every fold, with a consistency no workshop could sustain. There are no heaps of unused material, no slag banks, no loading grounds. Each output enters another tissue. What cannot be used locally travels through the deep roots until it reaches the organ that requires it.

Nothing has handles. Nothing has been assembled around a space left for an operator.

Occasionally a palm laid against a trunk can feel the faintest continuous tremor. It is too steady to become a pulse. A thin leaf held between two fingers may give a mild electrical taste to the mouth, though no current can be felt in the hand. Some pools have a mineral odor at their edges. Others smell faintly of cut stalks, except that nothing has been cut.

The energy comes from below, along living columns that descend farther than the shallow shape of the country suggests. They maintain contact with warm, chemically active depths. Here, the green parts of plants have become unnecessary. Much of the vegetation is black or colorless. Some of it is the muted white of flesh that has never been exposed.

Above the low plains, overlapping laminae spread across immense areas. They are supported at irregular intervals by thick ribs, leaving broad, low spaces underneath. Moisture gathers on their undersides. It rests there in level rows of beads, each bead held in a small depression. Only rarely does one become large enough to fall.

That fall can be heard.

There is the tiny separation overhead, then a soft tick in a pool, and the silence acquires a location for a moment.

No insects answer. No body moves through the water. There are no bite marks, burrows, droppings, discarded shells, or paths pressed into habit by feet. Hollow stems contain fluid or air or finer stems. Nothing nests in them.

The lack of animals makes even the softest parts of the place strangely complete. A translucent bladder can lie exposed at ground level, full of nutrient-rich liquid, with no defense beyond its thin wall. Tender tips fill the passages. There is no reason for thorns.

Nor is there much reason for flowers. Reproduction has become a dispersed function of the whole mass, carried out at its distant margins where living ground extends onto whatever lies beyond. Those margins are too far away to influence this interior. No seed arrives here on a wind.

There is scarcely a wind to carry one. Over the continent, the air has settled into broad, stable layers. The vegetation keeps the lower layer humid and warm; the great upper membranes restrain its movement. Beyond them lies a depth of cloud. No sunrise reaches the ground, and no nightfall alters it.

The place has weather in the sense that an ocean has pressure. Vast quantities of water hang above it. Vapor travels through its chambers and is taken back into its tissues. There are regions of permanent mist, regions where the air is clear, regions where every upright surface bears a film of condensation. Their boundaries remain in the same places. A bank of fog may occupy one shallow valley for longer than a forest elsewhere would live.

Walking into it, one would feel moisture collect on the face. Walking out hours later, one would emerge into the same warmth, beneath the same unseen breadth of leaves.

Everywhere, the immense quiet operation continues. A fresh seam lies open in the root-ground. Water stands level inside it, touching both of its pale sides. Far overhead, a bead holds to the underside of a leaf.

It is almost large enough to fall.
