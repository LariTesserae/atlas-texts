The city occupies a saddle between two mountains that have not finished becoming mountains.

Their summits are broad and blunt, still wearing the level beds of the seabed from which they rose. At the highest exposed edges, shells remain packed in the stone, their chambers full of black saltwater. Nothing has had time to round off. Fractures meet at sharp angles; terraces end in fresh, pale breaks. There is almost no soil. Beneath the buildings, the ground is blue-gray mineral, slick enough to reflect a light if anyone were to bring one.

Mostly, no one does.

The sky is an unbroken low ceiling, perceptible chiefly where it conceals the upper spans of the city. Far below, beyond the saddle, a colder darkness holds the valleys. Their distances are audible. A crack in the eastern massif returns first from the nearest apartment walls, then from the opposing slope, and finally, several seconds later, from something much farther down. On still nights the last echo seems to come from underneath the listener’s feet.

Water is everywhere, although very little of it appears to be going anywhere.

It stands in the joints of the paving. It bulges from seams in retaining walls and gathers in the cupped undersides of bridges. Broad, shallow reservoirs occupy roofs, landings, abandoned rooms. Their surfaces are covered by thick, almost transparent ice, beneath which bubbles remain trapped in long white files. Elsewhere the water is too saline to freeze. It lies black and heavy in channels between the streets, releasing a bitter mineral smell when the wind presses it into doorways.

The air is cold enough to hurt exposed teeth. Every handrail has a soft white underside of frost.

Yet inside the city’s walls, water remains liquid. You can hear it through the stone.

The oldest buildings are only a few generations old. They stand in close ranks on either side of the saddle: tall, narrow structures with rounded backs, facing inward toward sheltered passages. Their outer walls are ribbed to resist the weight of ice. Bridges join them at irregular heights. Below the bridges, more buildings fill the gaps, and below those run service galleries, pressure chambers, flooded foundations. In places, six or seven inhabited levels occupy the space between the open air and the actual ground.

Construction continues, but there are no cranes.

A new wall begins as a row of dull metal teeth driven into bedrock. Over years, each tooth draws dissolved minerals out of the water passing through it. The teeth thicken, meet, and become a continuous surface. Window openings are kept clear by small fields that can be felt as a resistance against the palm. An unfinished house resembles a collection of wet bones, with darkness already taking the shape of its rooms.

The same machinery holds some of the older streets level while the mountain rises beneath them. Elsewhere it allows a block to tilt by a fraction of a degree each season. Doorframes are adjustable. Staircases have spare treads folded beneath their landings. The city’s surveyors work with plumb lines sunk into wells, measuring against a depth that their instruments register but their boreholes have never reached.

All of this proceeds slowly. A corridor may take forty years to close. Residents know which floors will eventually become walls, and furnish accordingly.

What first seems like the roar of machinery is mostly animals.

They fill the sheltered surfaces of the city. They hang beneath bridges in packed, breathing sheets. They lie against the warm seams of doors. Their pale bodies occupy gutters, cable ducts, the shallow water over plazas. On a stairway, a person must sometimes wait while a mass of jointed backs shifts sufficiently to reveal the next step.

Most are nearly blind.

The commonest have six short limbs and broad, flattened heads, with whiskers that branch into stiff brushes. Their skin is the color of wet plaster, darkening to violet around the joints. They feed by pressing the brushes into mineral seepage and combing out the tiny animals living there. Those smaller animals eat films of chemical life nourished by the mountain’s newly exposed interior. Nothing grows upward toward the sky. The living wealth is at the cracks, where water brings unfamiliar compounds into contact with air.

The city’s exterior walls are warm by only a few degrees. That is enough.

Around a leaking pipe, bodies accumulate several layers deep. Those underneath scarcely move. Those on top make slow, determined adjustments, inserting a limb here, a narrow muzzle there, negotiating access to warmth. Their calls are disproportionately powerful: long, hollow notes amplified by sacs running the length of the chest. A whole façade may hum for an hour without any visible motion.

The sound enters the ribs before the ears.

Other creatures answer from beneath the ice. These are larger. Sometimes a reservoir’s white bubbles rearrange themselves, revealing a dark back traveling under the roof, or a mouth attached to the underside of the ice. The mouths are circular, fringed with fine scraping plates. Their feeding makes a rasp like furniture being dragged across an enormous room.

Higher up, on the city’s exposed crowns, live the animals that listen.

They perch shoulder to shoulder along parapets, wrapped in thick membranes that look like folded raincoats. Their heads are almost entirely ear. Every few minutes one opens its membranes and gives a blunt concussion of sound. The returning echoes tell it where the buildings end, where the flock has shifted, whether anything edible has risen through a ventilation shaft. A hundred others turn their heads to hear the same report.

Flight is rare and costly in the dense cold. When one finally leaves a roof, its departure is a ponderous event: membranes unfolding, feet releasing, a body dropping through the dark before its first deep wingbeat catches the air.

There are so many animals that the distinction between occupied and empty space becomes unreliable. An apparently smooth wall resolves under the hand into tightly fitted backs. A snowbank exhales. The low arch over a drainage channel is a creature resting between meals, its shell frost-bound to the masonry.

The inhabitants build around this abundance. Doors open inward so that resting animals cannot trap a household inside. Air intakes breathe in alternating pulses, discouraging nests without expelling the creatures that keep the ducts free of smaller parasites. Bridges have a pedestrian surface above and a roughened feeding surface below. Public shelters provide warm stone at floor level, leaving the benches comparatively clear.

The brightest lights are small amber indicators set beside entrances. Even these are hooded. A white lamp will draw the tiny swimmers out of a canal; the brush-faced animals follow them, and then the listening things begin to descend from the roofs. People navigate by textured walls, by the pitch of water in the drains, by the bass note assigned to each neighborhood. At crossings, those notes overlap in chords that can be recognized through the soles of thick boots.

Near the center of the saddle is an open basin wider than the surrounding blocks. No buildings stand in it. The city keeps this space clear for the water that the mountain releases as it compresses and warms at depth.

A low railing surrounds the basin. Beyond it, black water reaches almost to the rim. Thousands of pale animals float there with only their breathing folds exposed. From a balcony they would resemble close-set paving stones.

Once in several years, the water rises by the width of a finger.

The railing withdraws into its sockets. Drain mouths open throughout the adjoining district. Deep in the buildings, mineral teeth release their hold and engage again a little higher. The animals continue their long calls, though for a few hours the lowest voices disappear beneath the sound from the rock.

By morning, fresh water has spread over the lowest plaza and begun to freeze. A narrow strip of new stone stands above the basin’s former edge, clean and sharp, without a scratch on it. Before the ice has thickened enough to bear weight, small bodies are already gathering along its warmth.
