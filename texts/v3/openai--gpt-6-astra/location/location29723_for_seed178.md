The first thing you find is shelter for your face: the lee of a rock, a raised collar, the narrow dry interval beneath a branch. The wind carries water almost horizontally. It is too cold for the rain to feel like rain. It strikes as grit, gathers on eyelashes, runs under fastenings. Beyond the little shelter, a whole low country is moving.

There are trees, though at first they look like a rough surface laid over the ground. They grow sideways before they grow upward, their trunks buried in their own branches. Dark spruce and mountain pine cover the shallow ridges; alder crowds the hollows. The outermost needles wear clear sleeves of ice. Farther inside, beneath overlapping boughs, everything is green and dripping: moss as deep as a boot, fern fronds lying flat under the weight of water, little round leaves crowded into the shelter of roots. The vegetation has the richness of a place where growth is slow but very little is ever entirely cleared away. Each living surface carries another.

The storm opens brief views through it. A gust presses a stand of alder nearly flat, and for two seconds you can see across several miles of lowland: black ridges, pale channels, the dim silver of a broad inlet. Then the branches spring back and the country closes.

It is not quite night, or not only night. There is daylight somewhere above the cloud, but very little reaches the land. The sky has the dull opacity of wet slate. Water provides most of what can be seen, reflecting a brightness too weak to show on anything else. Streams appear before their banks do. Droplets draw the edges of branches. A waterfall on the far side of the valley hangs briefly in sight, gray against black, before wind tears its lower half into spray.

The ground is new in places.

Along the inlet, the sea has recently withdrawn from a succession of low shelves. There are mussel shells still fastened to stones above the highest tide. Between them, plants have taken hold in pockets of silt: thick grass, rushes, willow seedlings no taller than a hand. Inland, older wooded knolls stand among raw terraces of gravel. Frost has split the exposed rock into sharp plates. Channels have not yet settled into dependable courses. A stream may occupy one bed for a season, dam itself with gravel and uprooted brush, and break into the next hollow in a single wet afternoon.

Tonight several are doing it at once.

Brown water rushes through the alder roots. It piles up against fallen trunks, shudders, finds a weakness. Pebbles strike one another with a sound like crockery being shaken in a box. Occasionally a larger stone turns over, sending a deep knock through the bank beneath your feet. Small failures happen everywhere: a turf edge folds down, a root pulls free, a sheet of saturated moss slides from a rock and goes revolving into the current. The land is not being swept away all at once. It is being rearranged in a thousand close, violent operations.

Above everything is the noise.

The inlet booms where waves enter the caves beneath its newly exposed shore. The trees roar with a dense, tearing sound. Waterfalls hiss, streams hammer, sleet rattles on leaves and stone. Gusts strike so hard that the sound briefly loses its separate sources and becomes pressure in the ears and chest. You can see a bird calling from the top of a bent pine without hearing any part of its voice. To speak to someone a few steps away, you would have to turn their head toward yours.

Yet the place is full of animals.

Small birds work the sheltered undersides of branches, moving in quick, economical hops. They wait through each gust and resume as soon as the needles stop shaking. Under the forest roof, vole runs make dark tunnels through the moss. Deer have browsed the willow down to blunt stems; a group of them stands close together beneath a leaning rock face, their coats almost indistinguishable from the wet stone. Along the channels, otters use the slack water behind boulders. One surfaces in a patch of foam, shows a slick head and a silver fish, and disappears beneath the bank.

Where the river meets the inlet, seals gather outside the plume of muddy water. They keep turning to face the current. Fish displaced from upstream are arriving faster than they can find cover.

There are buildings, but you notice their effects before you notice their walls.

One stands on a low basalt rise near the river mouth. It is squat, windowless on the weather side, with a roof that follows the slope of the rock. Moss grows uninterrupted across the join. A warm amber slit beside the entrance is the only steady light in the valley.

Downstream of it, floodwater climbs over an invisible obstruction.

The raised water is no more than knee-high, a smooth, trembling ridge across the channel. Leaves and branches travel up its face, pass over the crest, and drop into an overflow cut leading away from the wooded ground. There is no gate beneath it. Instruments sunk into the banks alter the local pull of gravity across a strip of riverbed, enough to divide the flood without putting a solid structure in its path. The water still behaves like water. It froths, curls, catches debris. Only the slope it is obeying cannot be seen.

Most of the machinery is below ground. Its exposed parts are small and plain: dark ceramic caps among the roots, narrow inspection seams in rock, a handrail where someone occasionally needs to stand. Nothing pulses or announces itself. The system has been working through storms here for longer than the oldest pines have been alive.

Farther up the valley, three houses occupy separate ridges. Their foundations hold the underlying stone together, damping the tiny fractures that otherwise widen under frost. Their walls shed liquid water but retain a thin layer of air in which ice cannot form. Even so, the steps need clearing. Branches fall across the approaches. A broom leans beside one door, and split firewood is stacked beneath an overhang. The extraordinary engineering is reserved for the things ordinary maintenance cannot manage.

Between the houses runs a footpath that is mostly recognizable by what it avoids. It keeps to the ridges, crosses streams at their narrowest points, turns aside from the deepest moss. In places it disappears beneath standing water. Small footholds emerge when weight approaches, their upper surfaces roughening under a boot, then sink flush again after the crossing. Fallen needles collect over them. A visitor could walk through the valley and mistake most of its infrastructure for unusually considerate rock.

The forest offers no comparable consideration. Low branches catch at clothing. Water stored in the canopy falls in heavy, delayed showers after every gust. What looks like firm turf may conceal a gap between boulders, filled with black water cold enough to numb a hand within seconds. The young channels are especially dangerous: their banks can stand upright, grass still rooted on top, while the current hollows them from beneath.

There are pauses in the storm, but they are local. Under one stand of spruce, the air becomes still enough for mist to form. Droplets hang on a spiderweb deep inside the branches. A few meters away, exposed needles are thrashing hard enough to blur. Across the inlet, a curtain of sleet passes over a bare terrace while the nearer shore receives rain. The temperature sits close to a threshold, and every surface answers it differently.

By the river mouth, the tide begins to rise.

The change is almost invisible at first. Foam stops traveling seaward and collects along the margins. Then the brown current narrows, forced between banks of incoming salt water. Waves steepen over the bar. Their crests are blown backward into the dark.

Upstream, the diversion lifts its smooth ridge another few centimeters. Overflow spreads into the designated gravel basin, surrounding young willows and rattling through their stems. Deer leave the rock shelter and climb, reluctantly, into the trees. The otter has already gone.

For a moment the cloud thins. A little more light reaches the valley, enough to reveal how far the vegetation extends: forest over every sheltered rise, reeds along every channel, green mats clasping the raw stone wherever a root can hold. Even here, with ice forming on the windward branches and the ground shifting beneath the flood, growth occupies almost everything it can.

Then another gust comes across the inlet. The low trees bow in sequence from the shore inland. Water lifts from the pools in pale sheets, the amber doorway vanishes behind driven rain, and the valley is heard again more clearly than it is seen.
