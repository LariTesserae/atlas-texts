The water is warm enough that the colder currents feel like objects passing: a sleeve against the shoulder, a sheet drawn slowly over the back. There is no visible surface. Above the basin, far beyond the reach of any animal’s feelers, the limestone roof holds a few pockets of air. Most are no larger than a mouth. They fill and empty with the distant tide.

Down here, the plain is almost level. Fine gray sediment lies in its shallow depressions, interrupted by structures that rise a few body lengths, sometimes several times that, from the bottom. They stand close together. Between them run narrow passages, broader channels, little enclosed courts with soft floors. The settlement extends across much of the basin, thinning around warm springs and thickening along the slow routes by which fresh water enters.

Nothing in it has been cut or carried into place.

Its oldest foundations are colonies of tube-building worms. Generation after generation has grown on the abandoned tubes beneath, thickening the walls, branching outward, leaving hollows where the current must pass. Shelled animals have fastened themselves into the gaps. Sponges have spread over the resulting masses and bound loose fragments into their flesh. In some places these different builders make a low, continuous block; elsewhere they have produced freestanding towers, perforated from base to crown.

The proportions suggest a town more strongly than any individual shape does. There are dense neighborhoods and open reaches. There are channels that remain clear because everything living beside them depends on their flow. There are old, heavy structures around which the younger growth has had to accommodate itself.

No one sees the whole of it.

Most inhabitants have little use for sight. Pale crustaceans work the outer walls with their antennae extended. Small fish hover headfirst in openings, their movements so slight that the water hardly shifts around them. Long, soft animals pass through the interior galleries, feeling ahead with a rim of delicate fingers. A swimmer crossing a supposedly empty court would touch something almost at once: a suspended feeding thread, the trailing edge of a fin, a transparent bell contracting in the dark.

Vegetation is scarce. Near a distant fissure, where a trace of daylight occasionally penetrates through many turns of water, a few brown films cling to the stone. They do not spread into the basin. Most of what resembles foliage here is animal: fans held open to strain the current, feathered mouths that withdraw into tubes, branching bodies whose fine tips test the water.

Food comes down through cracks as a faint, irregular snowfall. It also rises from below.

The warm springs seep rather than gush. Their outlets are broad stains in the floor, soft patches where minerals have changed the mud to cream and yellow. Around them grow mats of bacteria, wrinkled into low folds. Grazers work slowly across these surfaces, leaving dark paths that close behind them over several days. The animals nearest the springs carry dense growths on their shells and limbs. Some spend much of their lives combing this growth into their mouths.

The water immediately above a seep is clear but unfriendly. Few animals cross its center. They gather at the margins, where the rising warmth mixes with cooler water brought from beyond the basin. These boundaries are crowded places. Feeding filaments overlap without quite touching. Crabs stand high on their legs to reach into a favorable layer. A shift of current can make an entire wall withdraw, thousands of soft crowns disappearing into their tubes within seconds.

Then the wall is only stone again.

The darkness is almost complete. An occasional disturbed animal produces a weak green point, too dim to reveal more than the curve of its own body. Sometimes a fish moves through a loose congregation of these creatures and leaves a short, scattered wake of lights. For an instant, fragments of the place appear at incompatible distances: the lip of an opening, a pale belly, a shell embedded in a wall. The lights fade before they can become a view.

Touch would find the buildings weathered. Their exposed edges are rounded by generations of grazing mouths and the slow passage of grit. Old tubes have merged into a material that resembles porous limestone. A few walls have collapsed, but their fallen portions remain in use. Burrowing animals enter them from below; smaller builders occupy the broken faces. Sediment gradually fills the lowest chambers.

In the oldest quarter, only the tops of the structures project above the mud. Here the lanes have become shallow grooves. Animals still follow them, partly because the current does and partly because the buried walls make firm edges beneath the soft floor. A broad, flat fish can settle over an entire intersection, covering it with its body while it waits.

Sound travels well, but little is offered to it. There is the occasional dry tick of a shell shifting under pressure. A grazing mouth rasps close by and stops. Farther off, a piece of weakened roof or wall settles with a dull knock that seems to come from everywhere. Long intervals contain only the low movement of water through constrictions, more readily felt in the ribs than heard.

The place is busiest when the tide turns.

Cool water begins entering through the lower channels. Flexible bodies bend in the new direction. Fine sediment lifts from the courts and runs along the ground in low clouds. The larger hunters leave their recesses, moving against the flow to meet what it carries. Smaller animals retreat into holes that fit them closely. Along the upper edges of the structures, feeding crowns reopen one after another.

At certain turns of the season, the water grows thick with larvae. They occupy every height above the bottom, drifting between the blocks and towers, settling, lifting again. Most are eaten. Some find a clean rim of shell, a protected crack, a place where the current is neither too strong nor too weak.

By the next tide, a few have fixed themselves there.

Their first structures are almost weightless. They add a pale grain to an old surface, then another. Around them, the basin continues its slow exchange of warm water and cool, the channels remain crowded, and the dark settlement grows along its worn edges.
