At first the plain looks like white stone. Then the light shifts, and what seemed to be grain in the stone resolves into joints: seams finer than hair, running through every visible surface. They cross the ground, climb the low swells, continue down into shallow basins. Some describe squares. Others belong to geometries so large that a day’s walking reveals only a slight departure from a straight line.

There is no bedrock beneath them. Digging would uncover another fitted surface, then another, all the way down through the load-bearing depths. The dust is manufactured too, although no one has manufactured any for a very long time. It comes from the slow wear of the plain.

The country is warm, dry, and almost level. Its highest features are broad backs of material rising a few dozen meters over distances of many kilometers. Once, perhaps, these were towers or retaining walls. Their interiors have settled outward into aprons of pale rubble; their rubble has softened into grit; the grit has lodged in the joints. What remains of a great vertical structure is sometimes only a change in the way the horizon shines.

Yet almost nothing is empty.

Underfoot, translucent plates overlap like tightly packed scales. Between them lie bronze filaments, small black apertures, beads of milky ceramic, thin folded membranes which withdraw when shaded. Even the apparently bare stretches are crowded with details. In some places the plates are the size of fingernails. In others a single continuous panel extends farther than sight, with its edge visible only as a line of accumulated dust. These different scales of construction meet without transition. A mechanism too small to see may occupy the corner of something built to carry a continent.

The sky is a bright, pearly yellow. There is no distinct sun. Light comes through high layers that have been stretched across the atmosphere, one above another, and repaired so often that their original pattern is gone. Their moving patches of opacity resemble weather seen through frosted glass. At midday the ground returns so much light that hollows are easier to see by their color than their shadows: cream depressions in a white expanse, faint apricot on the southern slopes, gray-green where old alloys lie exposed.

Standing here, one feels illuminated from beneath the chin.

The warmth is not severe. Much of it rises from below, and the air rarely becomes hotter than skin. But there is no relief in shade, only less brightness. A hand laid on the ground feels a gentle, persistent heat, like a machine left running in an adjoining room.

There are winds. They travel in wide, shallow rivers along routes established by buried heat exchangers. You may see one coming as a dull strip across the plain, carrying a layer of dust no higher than your knees. It reaches you with almost no sound. The grains are rounded beyond sharpness, and the surface they strike is fitted to absorb vibration. A breeze passes through your clothes, powders their seams, and goes on.

Farther out, two wind courses meet. There the dust rises into enormous standing veils, luminous on one side and brown on the other. They can remain in the same place for years. Their interiors are active with charge: particles climb, exchange electrons, sink, and climb again. Nothing flashes. The charge is harvested before it can become lightning.

Most of the landscape’s activity is like this—continuous, consequential, quiet enough to miss.

A basin several kilometers wide darkens over the course of an hour. Its plates have tilted a fraction of a degree to receive a different band of light. Elsewhere, a pale ridge contracts, opening a black slit along its crest. Warm gas emerges without a hiss. By evening, the slit has closed and the ridge is a little lower.

Across a salt-colored flat, small objects move steadily against the wind. They are not animals. Each is an exposed part of something much larger, traveling below the surface: a maintenance assembly carrying its sensory crown through a succession of openings that close behind it. From a distance they resemble solitary stones being dragged by invisible threads. They neither approach nor avoid one another. When two paths cross, one crown sinks, and later reappears beyond the other.

The systems still agree about many things. Weight must be distributed. Heat must not accumulate. Certain passages must remain clear.

They no longer agree about what the place is for.

Along one low escarpment, the ground is endlessly preparing a road. Fresh material wells up in a strip six meters wide, smooths itself, and hardens to a soft gray. A few hundred meters farther on, another system breaks the strip into hexagonal pieces and folds them underground. The road advances without ever getting longer. Its leading edge has worn a shallow circuit around the same basin, perhaps thousands of times.

In other places, repairs have become a kind of erosion. A cracked structure is reduced to grains; the grains are sorted; useful components are carried away; the remaining mass spreads and settles. Sharpness is expensive to maintain. Over immense intervals, everything has been made lower, broader, easier to support. The plain is ancient not because it has been abandoned, but because it has been tended toward this final, economical shape.

Life persists in the tolerances.

In the sheltered side of a joint, you sometimes find a narrow growth of dark green blades. Each blade is thick, waxy, scarcely taller than a match. Their roots follow threads of trapped vapor through the construction layers, taking what they need from imperfect seals. There are no streams, no pools, no damp ground. The nearest thing to dew is a faint clouding inside a transparent panel before its heating cycle begins.

The plants grow where that cycle is late.

Their patches are sparse but intricate. Old leaves do not fall; they collapse around the stem, making little insulating sleeves. Dust collects among them. Younger shoots emerge through the dust, and over centuries a green line becomes a low, black cushion. Some cushions have divided the same seam for so long that the machinery now routes around them. Others disappear overnight when a valve beneath them finally closes.

At the edges of these small gardens live translucent, six-legged animals. Most are no longer than a grain of rice. They spend the bright hours flattened beneath dead leaves, visible only by the pale contents of their bodies. When the overhead layers begin to dim, they climb out to scrape films from the ceramic. One may travel the length of a thumbnail, pause, and return. That is the largest animal movement you are likely to witness all day.

Occasionally there are tracks crossing open ground: two close rows of pinpricks, sheltered in the lee of a seam. They lead from one green patch toward another too distant to see.

The quiet makes distances difficult to believe. A shifting field the size of a city produces only a soft ticking, audible when you put your ear near the ground. A vast panel turns somewhere beyond the horizon, and you notice it as a change of pressure in your ears. Once in a while a loose flake skitters across the plates with a small, ordinary scraping sound. It is startlingly near.

Toward evening, the sky does not darken so much as become less white. Bands of amber pass through its high fabric. Long, low regions of the plain begin to glow on their own, releasing the light they stored during the day. The seams show first. Then the undersides of translucent plates. The ground becomes a crowded map of dim gold lines, with occasional dark interruptions where roots have entered.

From a low rise you can watch the change propagate for an hour, moving outward across hundreds of kilometers. Ahead of it the plain is dazzling and apparently still. Behind it, minute apertures open, membranes unfurl, the little grazers leave their shelters. Dust begins to slide toward the collection seams.

There is no audible signal. One district simply reaches its appointed temperature, then the next.

Under the warm soles of your feet, something immense adjusts its weight.
