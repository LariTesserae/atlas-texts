The place is behind a panel above the last service landing. You have to climb past the numbered floors, past the lift machinery, and turn sideways through an opening intended for a filter cassette. Beyond it, the building contains a small landscape: a pale basin, three sharp ridges, and a strip of level ground beneath a low ceiling. The whole of it would fit inside a freight elevator.

It is very high up. That fact is not visible from within. There is no window, no glimpse of a city or a slope falling away. Height declares itself in the pressure against the ears, in the cold of the entry frame, in the extraordinary amount of structure beneath this otherwise insignificant room.

Nothing here rests on earth.

The basin is cast magnesium ceramic. Its ridges are stiffening ribs, thin enough at their crests to look sharpened, rising where the casting needed strength. Between them, the floor has a shallow cellular texture, thousands of little six-sided depressions. At walking scale it would be difficult ground: abrupt shelves, narrow slots, slopes that end without rounding. At the scale of a hand, it is the inside of a manufactured component.

There is no dust softening it. The edges are as precise as they were when the mold withdrew.

The light is surprisingly ordinary. Two recessed strips illuminate the chamber with the clear white light of a well-kept workshop. They do not flicker. Under them, the ceramic is off-white, the exposed fasteners dull silver, the few insulated cables a faded green. Shadows are short and legible. You can see to the bottom of every open crevice. Nothing about the lighting suggests that this space is hidden or that no one has entered it for a very long time.

The sound does.

It is too loud to arrive as separate noises. Before your head is fully through the opening, it becomes a pressure across the face and breastbone. Deep in it are the vast, close rotations of compressors; above those, the cutting whine of something running at a speed no unaided eye could resolve. An even higher band makes the roots of the teeth feel exposed.

There is no clatter, no change of load, no irregular bearing announcing a future failure. Every machine beyond the walls is operating at its exact design condition. Their combined noise has settled into a permanent, crowded chord.

You could shout here and receive no evidence that you had done so.

Yet nothing in the chamber visibly vibrates. The ridges, the screw heads, the little pale growths on the floor all remain absolutely still. The basin hangs in a suspension that rejects the motions of the building while admitting the sound. This may be deliberate. Several features of the room suggest purposes that cannot be inferred from comfort or safety.

The cold is similarly complete. There is no frost because there is almost no water. Even the sheltered undersides of the ribs are dry. Metal has the flat, faintly blue appearance it acquires at temperatures that punish bare skin immediately. The air would take moisture from a breath before that breath had traveled far, and a visitor would be an unwelcome source of humidity.

Near the far wall, a few plants stand in the hexagonal hollows.

There are perhaps twenty of them, widely spaced. Most are no taller than a thumb. Each has a charcoal-colored base and two or three narrow blades, matte green along their faces, translucent at the edges. One has branched around a fastener, embracing it without quite touching. Another spreads flat across a shallow depression like a star pressed under glass.

They are not imitations. Their internal compartments, their selective membranes, their slow manufacture of new tissue belong to life. But the chemistry inside them is dry, and their roots terminate in conductive contacts laid beneath the ceramic. They were seeded here as part of the room’s electrical system. During commissioning they grew until each occupied the precise interval between two potentials.

Then growth ceased.

The tallest blade stopped less than a millimeter below an exposed conductor. It has remained there ever since, an unfinished bridge that the system requires to remain unfinished. No leaf falls. No shoot seeks a better patch of light. There is no dead vegetation among them, no litter, no hidden population consuming what they shed. Their economy has been reduced to maintenance so exact that its outward expression is stillness.

Between these scattered plants, most of the basin is empty.

That emptiness gives the ridges a peculiar consequence. A rise only a few centimeters high conceals an entire hollow. A cable passing through a sealed grommet becomes the sole landmark of a broad, bare reach. Beneath one overhang is a square of black material with a serial number printed so small it resembles a bed of seeds. Elsewhere, a bright bead of welding alloy sits fused to the floor. Nothing will carry it away. There is no season in which it will be covered.

The chamber has the freshness of something just made, without the expectation that it will weather. It has never acquired the minor damage by which a place becomes familiar. Even its sparse life has not blurred the distinction between the casting and what was added afterward.

If you crouched there long enough, the first impression of a mechanical cavity would give way to an apprehension of distances: the far side of the basin, the green blades beyond the second ridge, the low passage under the return duct where the white light ends. You would begin to know its few features individually. The hooked plant. The split rib. The empty socket with its copper interior.

All the while, the machinery would be deafening.

And when you backed out through the filter opening and replaced the panel, there would be no settling afterward, no startled creature returning, no cooling print of sunlight leaving the floor. The small high country would continue under its workshop lamps, sharp, dry, fully supplied, holding the same shape inside the same enormous sound.
