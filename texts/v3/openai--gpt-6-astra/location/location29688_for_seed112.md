The ground is pale wood, worn almost white by the wind.

It rises in long, shallow swells toward a horizon too distant to resolve. Grain runs through it in bands the width of roads. Here and there a darker seam opens, descends into green shade, and closes again miles farther on. The edges of these seams are rounded, polished, old. Loose fibers collect on their sheltered sides in drifts as fine as flour.

There is no soil beneath them. The wood continues downward.

At this altitude the sunlight has a hard, almost weightless brilliance. Anything raised above the plain casts a blue-black shadow. The air is cool enough to sting the inside of the nose, and so dry that a bitten lip begins to split before it begins to bleed. Nothing glistens. No cloud promises rain. What crosses the sun is pollen, lifted from somewhere beyond sight in immense yellow sheets.

The first impression is of emptiness: a bleached upland with a few distant uprights.

Then one of the uprights turns.

It is a leaf, perhaps twelve miles away, standing edge-on to the light. As it rotates, it becomes a broad green blade, taller than the cliffs below the plateau. There are others farther out, widely separated, each rising alone from a low mound of folded tissue. Their slow movements are independent. One presents its whole surface to the sun; another feathers itself against a gust; a third has closed into a narrow, trembling spear.

Between them stretches the living plain.

Its outer surface is dry and largely dead, a protective rind maintained by growth underneath. In the cracks, where the rind has drawn apart, fresh tissue shows a muted pistachio green. Close to these openings the ground gives slightly underfoot. A thin membrane can be seen contracting, resting, contracting again. It seals itself at the approach of a shadow.

All the water here is held inside bodies. The plants keep theirs behind layers of wax, cork, and densely packed fiber. Animals get what they need by eating living tissue or one another. Moisture spilled on the surface vanishes into the air so quickly that even wounds rarely leave a stain.

The sound arrives through the feet before it becomes intelligible to the ears.

A deep shudder travels along the grain. Then comes a rising mechanical roar, followed by several heavy reports in quick succession. The plain flexes. Dust leaps from every groove. Far away, a row of dark openings dilates along the base of a leaf, and air blasts from them hard enough to lay the loose fibers flat for half a mile.

There are thousands of these pumping systems beneath the plateau.

The great leaves cannot be supported, cooled, or supplied by the quiet circulation of an ordinary plant. Under each one lie chambers large enough to contain hills, lined with muscular vegetable tissue. They squeeze in sequence, driving gases through the continent’s hollow vascular trunks. Ring valves shut with enormous concussions. Bundles of contractile fiber tighten around shafts. In deeper tissues, stacked cellular membranes build electrical potentials and discharge them in coordinated pulses, pulling whole banks of pumps into rhythm.

Nothing has been assembled. Every valve grew in place.

Yet the country has the sound of heavy industry working beyond capacity: compressors labouring under load, a deep bearing grinding somewhere out of sight, banks of hammers starting and stopping, pressure escaping through a damaged seal. On certain stretches of the plain, two pumping districts fall briefly into synchronization. The roar becomes a single note. Small objects move across the ground. An animal caught standing in the wrong groove can lose its footing without any visible gust.

Then the districts slip out of phase, and the sound breaks apart again.

There is no hour when all of them rest.

The plateau makes its own weather. Sun warms the enormous leaf faces while their shaded backs chill the passing air. Exhaust from the vascular chambers climbs in dry columns. Cold air pours into the seams to replace it. Where these flows meet, the pollen turns in towering spirals, bright gold on one side and nearly black on the other.

A calm patch may last long enough for an animal to cross it. A moment later, wind comes down vertically and peels a strip of rind from the ground.

The strip lifts slowly at first, showing its green underside. It can be hundreds of yards long. As more of it comes free, it begins to lash. Other strips rise nearby. For several minutes the whole district flies apart in pale ribbons, each attached at one end, cracking above the plain with sounds too close and sharp to distinguish from pain.

Then a pressure valve opens elsewhere. The wind changes. Most of the ribbons settle.

Some are carried away.

This has happened so often that the exposed grain beneath them is already shaped to receive the next storm. The ridges have broad shoulders, the hollows smooth exits. There are no young, jagged mountains here. The continent’s relief has been worn into long-backed forms by ages of stripping, scouring, regrowth, and compression. Even its great fractures bend rather than run straight. Seen from above, the land would resemble timber that had spent centuries in a river, though no river has ever crossed it.

Animals inhabit the intervals.

Flat, six-legged grazers move along the lee of the grain ridges, scraping wax from the surface with mouths like planes. Their shells are white and finely ridged; at a distance they look like flakes that have not yet blown away. When the substrate begins its warning vibration, they spread their limbs and engage the hooks beneath their feet. Whole herds fasten themselves down. The blast passes over their backs with a thin, furious whistle.

In the seams live soft-bodied browsers with dark, heat-collecting skins. They emerge into sunlight in brief groups, feed on the tender green margins, and retreat before their protective oils can dry. Predators wait beside the openings, motionless for hours. Their hearing is poor. Sensitive pads along their jaws tell them how many footsteps are approaching, how heavy they are, and whether they hesitate.

Above the plain, long-winged animals use the exhaust columns to gain height. Their wings are narrow enough to fold into slots along their bodies when a vortex catches them. Sometimes hundreds circle one vent, scattered through a volume of air so large that they never seem to form a flock. A few descend with each pressure cycle to snatch insects lifted out of the depths.

There is much life, but little crowding. Food occurs along an edge, warmth on a particular face, shelter behind a ridge. Between such places lie miles of brilliant, apparently vacant surface. Crossing those miles is the principal work of many lives.

Communication is intimate. A grazer presses its forehead against another’s shell. Mates drum through the wood. Burrow colonies pass warnings along taut rootlets, one inhabitant touching another until the signal has travelled farther than any audible call could carry. The air is too loud to be useful.

Toward the plateau’s rim, the swells become steeper and the grain curves downward. The last great leaf stands far inland. Beyond it the living ground bends over an escarpment and exposes its thickness.

The cliff is a section through the organism: pale outer rind, green working layers, black ventilation galleries, thick amber columns carrying the sealed sap. Old channels have collapsed into rounded caverns. New ones press around them. In the depths, faint branching flashes mark a discharge travelling between pump districts.

Below lies another level of the same growth, and beyond that another, stepping down into a haze of suspended fiber. The lowest visible reaches are hundreds of miles away. Their leaves look like sparse dark bristles. Above them, pollen weather turns slowly in the sun.

At the rim, cold air rises from below with enough force to hold a shed fragment of bark motionless. It hangs there, spinning gently, while the cliff behind it trembles with the passage of a pressure pulse.

For a few seconds, the local pumps pause between strokes. The distant roar remains, but nearby one can hear small things: the ticking of cooling fibers, claws adjusting their grip, a dry seed rolling until it catches in a crack.

Deep underfoot, a valve begins to close.

The animals flatten themselves. The enormous leaf inland turns its edge toward the coming wind.
