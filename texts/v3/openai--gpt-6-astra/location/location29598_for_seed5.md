The entrance is a missing stone at the foot of a retaining wall. From the lane, it looks like a place where a cat might sleep: a low, pale opening, fringed with stiff leaves. You have to lie down to see through it.

Inside is a town no larger than a pantry.

It occupies the hollow between two foundations, below street level but above the water table. The ground is almost flat. What slope it has has been worn into a shallow central gutter, dry except for a bead of ice beneath each of three spouts. Houses crowd both sides. Beyond them are houses built over houses, little terraces resting on lintels, enclosed stairs climbing the backs of chimneys, passages that continue through the bottom floors of buildings because there was nowhere else to put a street. At the far end, an old root has been incorporated into a wall. Its exposed wood is polished where generations of shoulders have passed.

The largest doorway is about the height of your thumb.

Nothing here is made to resemble a larger town. It has its own proportions. Rooms are broad and low; roofs are steep only where a falling crumb of mortar might otherwise lodge. Most walls are built from flakes of the surrounding limestone, fitted with their naturally flat sides outward. The mortar contains white clay, crushed shell, and something fibrous. Here and there it has weathered away enough to reveal a strand of hair, laid through a joint like reinforcing rope.

The buildings are old. Their corners have lost the crispness of construction, and the lower steps are shallow dishes. Repairs are equally old. A broken lintel has been supported by a little fork of bone for so long that both pieces have acquired the same ivory color. There are blocked windows with windows built partly over them, gutters whose original destinations have disappeared, a narrow square reduced by successive additions to a place where two people can just pass sideways.

Yet the town does not feel ruined or improvised. Every obstruction has been accommodated. Every surface has found a use.

It is extraordinarily bright.

Above the hollow, a crack runs through the retaining wall and opens between two paving stones. Very little sky is visible, but daylight enters along a slant of white mineral and spreads across the opposite wall. The limestone returns it softly from every direction. Under the eaves, even the undersides of leaves are visible. Frost in an empty window makes that room look occupied by a small, steady lamp.

The cold has settled here as thoroughly as the buildings. It lives in the stone, in the packed earth beneath the paving, in the iron-colored pebbles used to weight doors shut. Nothing drips. Moisture arrives as a faint silver coating and remains where it forms until a leaf, a thread, or a porous strip of clay draws it away.

Water is precious without seeming scarce. The town is arranged to catch very little of it very well.

Each roof has a shallow groove along its lower edge. These lead to covered pots, some made from hollow seeds, others shaped by hand and fired unevenly brown. Wicks pass from the pots into planting beds. A bowl beneath the central spout has a lid with one hole in it. Around the hole, the ice has grown into a clear collar. Below it, the bowl is nearly full.

There is green everywhere.

It thickens the roofs and fills the spaces between stair treads. Cushions of moss bulge over courtyard walls, trimmed flat beside doors. Small evergreen leaves overlap like dark scales; beneath them lie older leaves, still sound, making a springy black floor. A creeper with pale stems travels through an entire row of houses. It enters one attic, emerges from another, and bears a cluster of waxy leaves in a room whose roof was removed specifically to accommodate it.

The plants grow slowly enough to be treated as permanent structures. A branch supports a balcony. A woven mat of roots holds the bank behind a bakery. There are places where living stems have been bent into railings and remain green on the outward side, worn smooth on the inward side by hands.

This is lushness without softness. The leaves are thick, the moss compact, the little trunks hard as horn. Nothing reaches far beyond shelter. Growth occupies the available space so exactly that a fallen leaf seems less like litter than a piece briefly detached from the whole.

Animals inhabit the remaining intervals.

A pale spider keeps a web under a bridge scarcely wider than a spoon. The web is short and dense, made for the tiny wingless insects that move along the masonry. Dark beetles shelter beneath overturned pots, their backs dusted with limestone. In a roof garden, a snail rests with its shell pressed against a warm-colored tile. Someone has built a low fence around the plants it prefers and left a gap leading to a bed of less valuable leaves.

Near the entrance, a wren feather is fixed upright between two posts. Its lower barbs have been combed apart. Whether it is a gate, a windbreak, or a warning about the creature that shed it is not immediately clear.

You begin to see the inhabitants only after looking for some time.

One stands beneath an awning, holding a shallow tray. Another is seated on an exterior stair, working a strip of bark against a stone blade. Their clothing has the muted colors of seed husks and old felt. Their movements are economical and very slow. A door closes with no audible click. A basket is lowered from an upper window, rests on the paving, and is lifted again with three pale roots inside.

There is no market noise, no hammering, no call from one house to another. Work that requires impact is done on thick pads of moss. Wheels, where they exist, turn on wooden pins greased with seed oil. Even the gutter has been lined with a soft, dark growth to keep falling water from striking stone.

For a while you think the silence is a custom. Then a cart passes in the lane above.

You feel it through your ribs before you hear it. Dust loosens from the entrance. Throughout the little town, hands pause where they are. The person with the tray lowers it to the ground.

The weight passes. Nothing has broken. Slowly, work resumes.

Farther in, beyond the little square, a stair descends to a door almost hidden by leaves. Its threshold is worn through at the center. Beside it stands a clay vessel holding a single green shoot, supported by a splinter and tied with several careful knots. The wall behind it is bright, dry, and deeply weathered.

There is no neglected quarter, no open plot waiting to be built upon. The town has reached the edges of its hollow. Its gardens have reached one another. What is left to do is mend a wick, sharpen a flake of stone, ease a root away from a hinge.

You withdraw without putting your hand inside. From the lane, standing again, you can see only the missing stone and the leaves at its edge. A little light lies on the floor just beyond them.
