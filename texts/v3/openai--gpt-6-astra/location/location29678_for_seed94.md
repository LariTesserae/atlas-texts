The high country begins above the clouds, but it is not dry.

Water stands in every hollow of the plateau. It lies black beneath floating leaves, clear over shelves of green stone, silver where the wind presses the sedges flat. The soil is shallow and almost always full. Walkable ground, if there were anyone to walk it, would be the long backs of rock between the pools: rounded, dark, slick with a growth too short to call moss and too dense to distinguish into separate plants.

Beyond these backs rise other plateaus, their edges softened by distance. Some carry forests. Some disappear inside weather. Far to the east, sunlight touches a broad white ridge that might be snow or the top of another cloud bank. There is no single summit. This is the upper surface of something continental, lifted long ago and worn until even its enormous cliffs have acquired the shapes of old stones.

The air is cold enough to redden skin. In the shade, last night’s ice remains beneath the overhanging lips of pools. Above it, leaves are unfolding.

They are thick leaves, waxed against the cold, dark on top and pale beneath. The plants crowd one another without quite excluding one another. Ferns grow from the forks of shrubs; low trees root in the accumulated dead fronds of ferns; moss covers the trees, and smaller flowering plants root in the moss. At ground level there is almost no bare surface. Even loose stones wear close-fitting green coats.

Where the slopes steepen, the vegetation thickens rather than thins. Water moving through the rock emerges in thousands of small seeps, keeping the cliff gardens wet through clear weather. Roots spread sideways along bedding planes. They enter cracks, swell there, divide, and emerge several body lengths away. Entire groves hang from the rounded brows of escarpments, their trunks leaning out into empty air, their crowns turned upward.

Below them is cloud. Occasionally it opens, revealing another green slope far beneath, then closes before the eye has found its foot.

For all this water, the country is quiet. There are few clean falls. Streams descend through roots and cushions of vegetation, broken into threads, gathered into shallow bowls, released again. Their sound seldom carries farther than their banks. At the great escarpments, rivers spread across the stone in films and descend behind curtains of hanging plants. A whole watershed can pass over a cliff with little more noise than rain on a coat.

The wind moves chiefly overhead. You can see it travelling through the upper foliage while the pools beneath remain still.

In the forests, the trees are short, massive, and closely spaced. Their trunks have a dark, fibrous rind. Most are hollow.

Inside each trunk, water rises through a succession of chambers. A chamber fills from below; pressure closes a flexible valve; the next chamber takes the load. Broad leaves near the crown turn slowly with the sun, feeding the tissues that maintain the pressure. At night the valves relax, and some of the stored water drains back through the roots.

This happens in ordinary plants too, in smaller and less visible ways. Here, the mechanism has become large enough to inspect. A split trunk exposes wet partitions, muscular collars, polished mineral surfaces where moving parts have rubbed for decades. Deep inside, a narrow spindle turns under the descending water. Its lower end runs in a cup of living cartilage.

A healthy grove is full of these slow rotations.

The trees use them to draw nutrient-rich silt through their roots and separate it into successive beds: coarse grains near the outside, finer particles within, dissolved metals passed into dark nodules along the trunk. Colonies of pale organisms occupy the warmest chambers. They digest the material the trees cannot use directly. In exchange, the trees keep their chambers supplied with moving water and air.

On cold mornings, faint vapour escapes through rows of pores in the bark.

There are no buildings, no roads, no discarded objects. Nothing here has been assembled from outside the life of the place. Yet beneath the forest floor, the roots form working networks of pressure lines and conductive fibres. Mineral-rich water carries weak currents between groves. A damaged stand can draw on the stored resources of its neighbours for a season or two. Where roots meet incompatible tissue, their junctions thicken into hard insulating knots.

The workings are mostly inaudible. Now and then a valve seats with a soft knock. A failing tree may develop a persistent tapping, heard for years before its crown begins to die.

Animals know these sounds.

Small furred creatures with broad feet sleep against healthy trunks, where the bark is a little warmer. They wake when the pressure cycle changes and climb to feed on the soft growth around the upper vents. Beetles shelter in the insulating knots. Their wing cases have the dull sheen of wet iron.

In the open marshes, herds of low, heavy-bodied grazers move through vegetation up to their shoulders. Their coats are long along the flanks and cropped close across the back by constant rubbing against branches. From a distance they resemble islands of brown sedge. Only the gradual relocation of a pale face reveals them.

They feed methodically, stripping one side of a leaf and leaving its tough central rib. Behind a herd, whole acres stand combed into a different texture.

Predators follow the firmer ground. They are slender, grey, and patient, with feet that spread under weight. Often they wait beside the narrow rock passages between pools. A hunt may begin with nothing more than one animal lifting its head. The nearby grazers stop chewing. Farther away, others continue to feed, unaware. Then several bodies move at once, water rises white around their legs, and the disturbance passes into the vegetation.

Within minutes, the pools have cleared.

Birds are abundant but rarely gather in noisy flocks. They travel singly beneath the low canopy, slipping between trunks, or stand on exposed rocks with their feathers lifted against the cold. Their calls are short and widely spaced. Across a marsh you may hear one note, then its answer from somewhere you cannot see.

By afternoon, the plateau makes its own weather. Moisture rises from the forest and catches against the colder air above. Clouds form low enough to erase the tops of trees. Rain begins in scattered districts: a darkening on one slope, a pale veil across another. Under sunlight, the nearest leaves retain their ordinary sharp colours while miles of country beyond them vanish.

The rain fills the upper pools. Their overflow travels through buried peat, enters the root systems, increases the pressure in the trunks. Hours later, far from where the storm began, dormant outlets open along a cliff. Water appears on previously dry stone. Hanging gardens lift and spread as their tissues fill.

These responses move across the high country slowly, outlasting the weather that caused them.

At evening the clouds sometimes sink. Then the full breadth of the place becomes visible: wet uplands beyond wet uplands, wooded basins, blunt ridges, lakes fitted closely into the worn land. Shadows fill the depressions without hiding the shine of water. There is still movement everywhere, but little of it is quick.

As the temperature falls, the trees begin returning their stored water to the ground. A few soft knocks pass through the nearest grove. Thin ice forms at the sheltered edge of a pool, enclosing the stems without bending them.

Under it, water continues downhill.
