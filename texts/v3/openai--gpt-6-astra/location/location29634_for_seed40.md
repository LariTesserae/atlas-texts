From the upper gantry, the district looks like a mountain basin that has been assembled but not yet occupied. White terraces descend toward a narrow central hollow. Beyond them rise ranks of cooling towers, retaining walls, and steep roofs with the same pale finish, so densely arranged that the gaps between buildings seem cut rather than left open. The farthest structures are blue with distance. Above those, there is more construction: an elevated neighborhood, perhaps, or the underside of one, its windows catching a light that does not reach the basin.

There is no exposed earth. Even the cliffs are made.

Their faces consist of stacked ceramic blocks, each about the size of a railway carriage, pinned together with dark metal staples. Fresh seams run down them. At the foot of a wall, what first appears to be scree resolves into broken insulators, white wedges, and heaps of unused bolt caps. The fragments have sharp corners. Nothing has had time to become gravel.

The air is cool enough to make a handrail unpleasant against bare fingers. It smells faintly of wet cement and hot electrical insulation, though the machinery is neither visibly hot nor loud. Sunlight—or something with the reach and color of sunlight—fills the district almost vertically. The bottoms of deep stairwells are bright. So are the interiors of abandoned cable housings. Under a bridge, you can see every rivet, every feather caught in the braces, but not what is illuminating them.

Shadows survive in a few protected places. They lie in cupboards and beneath loose floorplates, black and soft-edged, like deposits of a material the cleaners have missed.

Much of the basin is dry. Dust settles in the open channels, pale as flour, and the expansion joints hold narrow drifts of it. Moisture comes from elsewhere: the cold sides of pressure vessels, the mouths of vents, the undersides of walkways. A pipe can remain dry along its entire length except for one elbow that drips continuously into a shallow pan. The pan may be empty. You can watch the drops strike, see the rings spread, and still see the unbroken dust on its bottom.

The reliable water is marked with blue enamel.

Those channels carry a clear, slow runoff between factories and beneath the terraces. Most are no wider than a hand. At intervals they open into square reservoirs with ladders fixed to all four walls. The water stands a few inches deep, bright over green tile. Birds gather there, and small gray animals come down the ladders headfirst to drink.

There are animals everywhere once you begin looking at the joints.

Pale martins nest in the ventilation slots. Their tails protrude in rows from the shaded openings, though the slots themselves are too shallow to contain them. Long-legged white beetles browse the mineral crust around leaky valves. Something like a vole, with wide, rubbery feet, travels along the polished water pipes; whole families pause together at a coupling before crossing it. On the upper terraces, compact, goat-sized creatures with blunt faces graze on the fibrous packing that works loose between wall panels. They stand comfortably on ledges narrower than their bodies. Sometimes they lean into a wall and keep leaning until most of the animal is inside it, leaving only the hindquarters visible. Later they pull themselves free with a slow effort, chewing.

None of this produces much sound.

A hundred birds may lift from a tower without the usual rush of wings. Their flight is visible chiefly as a brief roughening of the white facade. There is an occasional footfall, a little claw ticking against enamel, a dry sneeze from inside a wall. Far below, a pump makes a single heavy stroke. The next comes so much later that you have stopped expecting it.

The factories are working.

You can tell by the belts, which advance an inch or two while you are watching, and by the gradual passage of rectangular shadows behind the inspection glass. Great flywheels turn with the deliberation of weather. A cable descends into a shaft all afternoon. On the opposing terrace, a press lowers through three stories toward a sheet of metal; by evening it has nearly made contact.

The district manufactures its own terrain.

At its eastern edge, wide extrusion mouths push out new paving. The material emerges warm and immaculate, already ruled into squares, already containing drains and sockets for street furniture. It advances perhaps the length of a thumb in a day. Railings extend from their housings to follow it. Farther back, a row of unfinished stairs rises toward a landing that has not yet been produced.

This is why so much of the place looks young. The oldest surfaces have a little pitting, a few softened edges, mineral fans beneath the seams. The newest retain the faint ridges of their molds. There are no ruins in the usual sense, only structures withdrawn from service and being slowly fed into larger structures. An entire warehouse on the southern slope has entered the wall behind it. Its doors are still accessible. Through them you can see shelves of washers and sealed lamps, all neatly arranged in a room that gets a little shorter each year.

Vegetation is scarce but persistent. Grass grows in the felt washers around standpipes. A low, dark plant spreads its round leaves over certain inspection covers, rooting through the stamped serial numbers. Here and there, a narrow bed of soil has been bolted to a balcony. The shrubs in these beds are wind-pruned on every side, small globes of dusty green carrying a surprising number of nests.

There is one orchard, high on a service terrace: twelve trees in twelve steel hoppers. Their trunks are thin, their bark silver, their branches trained along insulated wire. They bear hard, translucent fruit. On cool mornings, condensation beads inside the fruit rather than on the skin. The animals wait beneath the trees for a drop to form, descend, and fall through the unbroken rind.

Wind moves through the upper district, but it is difficult to predict where it will be felt. A ribbon on a railing may strain straight outward while the grass at its base remains motionless. Across a narrow alley, another ribbon points the opposite way. The densely layered structures hold pockets of different weather. You can pass from dry cold into mild, damp air by stepping across a drainage groove. In some stairwells, rain falls for a few minutes every afternoon. It comes from the next landing, not from the sky.

Height is similarly local.

Most staircases behave sensibly. A few return you to the same walkway several yards higher, with the railing now at your knees. Freight lifts are more dependable, although a lift marked DOWN may open onto a roof from which you can see the entire basin. The old maintenance notices advise passengers to judge elevation by breathing effort rather than by the view.

From that roof, the district extends for miles: white ridges crowded with machinery, tank farms arranged in tight circles, viaducts passing through the upper stories of workshops. Distance compresses and loosens as you look. A red access door on the far rim is suddenly close enough to show the scratches around its lock. Then it is a speck again.

There are roads, but almost no traffic. Their lanes are narrow and busy with animals. Small insulated carts move so slowly that nests have been built against their wheel guards. One cart carries a load of wet aggregate beneath a glass cover. Another transports a single upright door, closed in its frame. The latter stops at every intersection for a long time.

By what ought to be evening, the light has scarcely changed. Instead, the white surfaces acquire a faint warmth, and the animals begin moving toward their recesses. Condensation thickens beneath the bridges. A few windows turn dark, each one dimming the patch of pavement directly in front of it.

Then, across the basin, the press completes its stroke.

There is a soft click, no louder than a latch catching.

For several seconds, all the fine beads of water on the railings stand up into points. The birds remain where they are. Somewhere in the new paving, a seam opens cleanly, exposing the first blue enamel edge of a channel. A little water enters it and begins its slow descent toward the hollow.

By morning, the district will have somewhere else to go.
