Beneath the lowest step, where the stair’s weight rests on a buried stone, there is a hollow scarcely larger than a cupped hand.

It has no entrance from above. Water reaches it along the underside of the step, following a seam too narrow to admit a grain of sand. Air comes by another route, through the mortar and a succession of dry cracks. Whatever first hollowed the stone did so long before the stair was laid. Its interior is smooth in some places and deeply fretted in others, with the rounded, crowded contours of something that has endured a great deal of water without ever being washed clean.

There is a town inside.

From the single crack through which a little daylight enters, you can see only its upper roofs: overlapping chips of slate, supported on squat pillars of pale clay. Farther in, houses descend the walls in close terraces. Some have been cut directly into the stone; others are built against it from grit, shell fragments, and flakes struck from a dark, fine-grained pebble. Their doors are low. Their windows face sideways into alleys rather than outward over the hollow. There has never been enough room for a proper frontage.

At this scale, a single coarse grain in the parent rock becomes an obstacle around which three streets must bend.

The buildings are old enough that their separate materials have begun to resemble one another. Damp has darkened the clay. A thin mineral glaze covers the shell lintels. Roof edges, once sharp, have rounded into lips that hold water until the drops are almost as broad as the lanes below. Repairs remain visible under the glaze: a wall buttressed with a wedge, a cracked arch lashed together with a translucent filament, a doorway reduced to half its former width by successive layers of patching.

Nothing is made of wood.

The bridges are stone slivers. The ladders are notched splinters of bone. Gates turn in shallow sockets, or lift bodily from their grooves. In the lowest quarter, where the walls sweat continuously, little shutters of hammered copper have gone almost entirely black. Their makers used the metal sparingly. One shutter is plainly a flattened fastening, its original hole still visible near a corner.

The central space is occupied by water, though calling it a pool would make it sound too open. Pillars rise through it. Foundation walls divide it. Causeways cross and recross, leaving narrow chambers of black water under the houses. There is hardly anywhere that the surface extends unbroken for more than the width of a small fingernail.

Each compartment has its own meniscus. Water climbs the pillars and pulls itself up the corners, so that the streets appear to descend between banks of liquid held higher than their paving. In a few places, an alley runs through a clear, swollen arch of water. You could see the worn steps beneath it if the light were better.

The town’s oldest works are its drains.

They follow no convenient plan. A channel passes through the floor of a house, under a public stair, and across a little square in an open trough before disappearing into the wall. Another has been roofed with translucent flakes, each weighted at its corners. There are settling cups, overflow notches, and narrow stone tongues that persuade a rivulet to take one turn rather than another. All were shaped with small blows and patient rubbing. None needs adjustment now.

The entire place is loud.

A drop gathering at the highest seam strikes a hollow slab with a deep, wooden report. Another falls into a flooded shaft and produces a ringing note that hangs in the cavity. Water running beneath the lowest bridge makes a coarse, continuous rushing, much larger in sound than the thread of water responsible for it. The stone traps these noises and sends them back through the lanes. Under certain roofs, they become a drumming. In the doorways nearest the central pool, they overlap into a roar.

There is no square where conversation would be easy.

Along the walls are small polished recesses at the height where a hand might rest. Beside several doorways, the paving carries arrangements of shallow cup marks. Whatever the inhabitants used to tell one another, they seem to have relied on touch, on placement, on things left where they would be found. A row of three dark grains stands on one sill. Across the lane, a pale chip lies in a groove made precisely for it.

The cold is gentle but complete. It comes from the surrounding earth and the water in the stone. Every surface feels as though rain has only just stopped falling on it. Even the dry upper rooms retain that chill, and the few pockets of air beneath their roofs smell of wet flint and old mortar.

No roots enter the hollow. No green film softens the channels. Where a ledge looks furry in the dimness, closer inspection reveals a growth of colorless crystals, short and blunt from being repeatedly wetted. A cream-colored band around the central pool is mineral deposit, not scum. The town has the crowded texture of a place full of life, but nearly all that crowding is masonry.

There is one animal readily visible: a translucent, jointed creature tucked beneath the main bridge. Its body is thinner than a shaving of fingernail. Through it, the dark underside of the bridge remains faintly visible. It holds two forward limbs in the current, where minute particles catch against their hairs.

Sometimes it folds a limb to its mouth.

Its shelter is worn smooth, though the wear may be much older than the animal. There are similar hollows under other bridges, all empty. A white casing lies in one of them, caught by a ridge in the stone.

The light does not reach the bottom directly. It enters as a narrow grey blade, strikes the wet shoulder of a roof, and breaks into weaker reflections. The copper shutters catch almost none of it. The shell lintels hold it longer, giving certain doors a faint, milky outline. Deep in the town, illumination comes chiefly from the trembling undersides of water surfaces.

Yet very little seems to move.

The large drops fall in a settled sequence. The channels carry the same amounts. Every overflow returns to a lower basin, and each basin loses water through a fissure exactly as slowly as it receives it. The surrounding stone buffers the brief weather above: rain arrives here gradually, and drought withdraws by degrees too small to see. The hollow has had a long time to find its balance.

Even the signs of damage belong to that balance. A collapsed roof has become a diversion wall. Sediment has sealed an unwanted drain. The lowest stair ends in water at the seventh tread, whose rounded edge is coated with the same pale deposit as the pool.

Under the loudest falling drop stands a little roof on four heavy legs. Its sole purpose appears to be to bear the impact and divide the water. The roof is deeply cupped, nearly worn through at its center. Below it, protected from the blows, is a dry patch of paving no larger than a seed.

Someone took considerable trouble to keep that much ground dry.

It remains dry still.
