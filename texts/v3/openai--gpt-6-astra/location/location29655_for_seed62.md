The valley is brightest at its edges. There, newly broken stone rises above the trees in pale blades, each one holding the sun on a different face. At noon the upper slopes almost disappear in their own reflected light. Only the seams remain visible: blue lines, green lines, narrow cuts where plants have already entered the rock.

It is a high valley, broad enough to contain several distinct kinds of weather. A cloud can rest against the eastern wall while the western terraces lie clear and brilliant. Between them, the ground descends in uneven shelves toward a shallow central basin. There is no lake in the basin. From above, its level canopy might be mistaken for one, a dense surface of silver-green leaves interrupted by the roofs of three industrial buildings.

The mountains beyond are old. The valley floor is new.

It rose within living memory, lifting through the former soil in a sequence of slow fractures. Some of the old trees came up with it. They stand now on isolated platforms, their roots exposed along the sides like the rigging of stranded vessels. Other trees lean across the gaps, held upright by neighboring crowns. Beneath them, the fresh rock has edges sharp enough to cut a glove. Footpaths follow the fractures rather than crossing them; a short journey on the map can require an hour of patient turns.

Very little loose material has collected here. There are no broad fans of gravel, no deep banks of silt. Fallen leaves catch in cracks and remain there, darkening into thin seams of soil. In places a hundred kinds of growth occupy a cleft scarcely wider than a hand.

Everything grows close.

Low conifers spread horizontally before turning upward, making green tables beneath the taller trees. Ferns occupy the shaded spaces between their branches. Pale vines pass from trunk to trunk at knee height, then climb into the crowns and open thick, translucent leaves. Where sunlight reaches the ground, there are cushions of tiny, hard-leaved shrubs, so tightly packed that their individual outlines vanish. A foot placed on one would leave a mark for years.

The plants do not have the soft extravagance of a warm, rainy forest. Their abundance is built from small, durable parts. Leaves are waxed, furred, folded, doubled at the margins. Buds remain closed through long periods of clear weather. Roots travel extraordinary distances through the clean fractures in the stone.

Rain comes infrequently, usually as a brief, fine fall from a cloud that has caught on the rim. It darkens the uppermost foliage and is almost entirely intercepted before reaching the ground. For an hour afterward, drops hang in the valley at every height. The sunlight enters them sideways. Then they are gone.

Most moisture arrives overnight.

The pale rock cools quickly, and by morning its shaded surfaces are stippled with condensation. The plants have adapted to this arrangement with remarkable precision. Some spread flat roots over the stone, each root edged with a fringe finer than hair. Others lower tendrils into cracks where the night’s water gathers. A climbing plant with broad white leaf-backs turns itself inside out after sunset, presenting its coolest surfaces to the open sky. By midday it has reversed again.

There are pockets of standing water, but they are small and difficult to find. One lies inside a split boulder beside the northern path: a vertical pool, dark as polished iron, with its surface several feet below the opening. Another fills the socket beneath an uprooted tree. Neither overflows. Each has its own surrounding garden, lush within a few yards and abruptly absent beyond that reach.

The industrial buildings occupy the driest shelves.

They are long, low structures faced in white enamel panels, with glazed clerestories and roofs crowded by thin metal fins. Ferns press against their walls. In one place a tree has grown through the outside stair, and someone has unbolted two treads to accommodate it. Copper pipes run along the paths on short concrete supports, passing through thickets and emerging on the far side with their old identification numbers still visible.

Nothing smokes. There are no heaps of fuel or slag.

The machinery inside works on the valley’s continuing expansion. Deep shafts descend through the new stone, and within them anchored columns resist the almost imperceptible movement of the ground. The resulting pressure drives hydraulic systems. Accumulators the height of houses store it; narrow cables carry electricity from building to building and onward over the southern ridge.

A shaft may travel the width of a fingernail in a week. That is enough.

Through the windows, in all that light, you can see enormous wheels standing apparently motionless. Their rims carry little painted marks so the attendants can tell that they have turned. Pistons ease forward in cylinders wider than a person. Oil rises very slowly behind thick inspection glass. There are layers of cork beneath the machinery, flexible couplings in the pipes, felt seals around doors. Even the loose end of a chain is wrapped and tied.

The oldest warning plates concern vibration.

A dropped hammer can loosen a seam in the fresh rock several hundred yards away. The response is delayed and unreliable. Sometimes nothing happens. Sometimes, two days later, a pale ledge opens down its length and a section of woodland inclines by half a degree. The valley is still settling into the stresses of its emergence. Work here is careful, padded, conducted with tools laid out in individual cloth pockets.

Along the paths stand small shelters with spare soft-soled boots inside. Their benches are worn smooth. At the entrances to the works, brass bells have had their clappers removed; a lamp above each bell now serves the same purpose.

The quiet extends beyond the buildings.

Wind moves across the high rim, visible in distant grasses, but seldom reaches the basin. The interlocked foliage admits only a little air. A leaf turns. Another settles against it. Condensation occasionally falls from one level of greenery to the next with a sound so slight that its location is impossible to judge.

There are few animals. Pale snails gather beneath pipe brackets. Once in a while a small gray creature crosses an open slab, pausing at every seam before slipping into the vegetation. Its feet leave no visible trace. Birds pass overhead without descending. There is food here, and shelter, but the growing rock contains salts that most animals avoid; the plants hold those salts in their oldest leaves, which become stiff and pearly before they fall.

These leaves accumulate around the trunks in bright drifts. Even deep in the undergrowth, a little light seems to come upward from the ground.

By afternoon the whole valley has become almost painfully clear. You can distinguish the fittings on a distant roof, the separate tiers of a tree halfway up the opposite slope, the fine shadows cast by cables against white stone. Distance remains, but haze does not soften it. The air smells faintly of resin and cold metal, with a damp, enclosed odor rising from the deeper cracks.

Near the center of the basin, a surveying line crosses the path. Its two ends are fixed to neighboring rock shelves. The wire hangs low between them, weighted with a little ceramic marker. Behind it, the vegetation continues uninterrupted: stems passing over the wire, roots beneath, white flowers opening along a branch at shoulder height.

An attendant comes to measure the line every few weeks. There is a narrow board for standing on, a box for the instrument, and a pencil tied inside the lid.

The most recent measurement is written there.

Below it, enough room has been left for many more.
