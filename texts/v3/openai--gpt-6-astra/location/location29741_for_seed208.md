The country is low enough that water has trouble leaving it.

It lies in broad gray shelves beneath a sky the color of the underside of a shell. There are no peaks. The highest ground is a succession of long-backed rises, each perhaps the height of a house, worn so smooth that from a distance they resemble swells traveling beneath the land. Between them lie shallow basins, pale gravel flats, and channels with no obvious direction of flow. Rainwater moves one way across a shelf while, in a crack beneath it, darker water moves the other.

Everything is wet, but comparatively little is deeply submerged. Most pools would cover a foot, a knee. Their bottoms remain visible: cream-colored stone, black seams, occasional objects enlarged by the water into apparent significance. A rib. A pierced pebble. The jointed leg of something that has shed its casing.

The deeper holes are smaller and perfectly still. You notice them late.

There is almost no loose earth. Whatever soil this country might once have held has gone out through its cracks. Its ancient heights have followed more slowly. The remaining stone is not rugged enough to be called ruinous; even the ruins of its ruggedness have been worn away. Harder layers stand a finger’s breadth above softer ones. Fossils survive as shallow thumbprints. Here and there a strip of dark mineral crosses a whole basin, the last exposed edge of something that was once enormous underground.

Nothing grows upright. No grass interrupts the waterlines. No moss softens the stone. The few dark stains that might be mistaken for vegetation are either mineral deposits or animals resting so closely together that their separate bodies disappear.

At first the landscape seems empty because its inhabitants are nearly the same color as its surfaces.

Then a patch of gravel rises on six legs and walks sideways into a pool. A hundred little lids open along a submerged seam. What looked like a wet ledge draws itself inward, revealing a pale muscular foot beneath a shell as wide as a table. Farther off, something lifts its head above a basin rim, shakes water from a fringe of stiff whiskers, and lowers itself again.

The animals gather along the cracks. Warmth reaches the surface there—not enough to warm the air, but enough to keep certain narrow runs of water from becoming as cold as everything around them. The runs smell faintly of iron and struck stone. Along their undersides, unseen from above, slick colonies live on what the water brings up. Small rasping creatures graze these films. Larger creatures pry the grazers loose. Flat, slow-bodied scavengers pass beneath both, finding what settles.

A whole basin can look vacant while its lower surfaces are crowded with feeding mouths.

The most numerous animals carry low, ridged shells. They brace themselves against the current with hooked feet and pump water through chambers beneath their backs. Their young are almost transparent. In sheltered pools you can see them by the grit moving through their bodies.

Twice during the long gray day, these shell-bearers begin to call.

One starts near a warm fissure: a hard, hollow note, like a mallet striking a jar. Others answer farther down the channel. Within minutes the sound occupies the entire basin. It comes from beneath shallow water, from the undersides of shelves, from cracks too narrow to admit a hand. Each animal has several chambers, and each chamber sounds a slightly different pitch. Thousands together make a continuous, uneven roar.

But this is only the nearer part of the noise.

Under the plain, water is being driven through old cavities by a tide that cannot be seen from here. The chambers fill, trap air, and force it upward through the stone. Some vents whistle. Others produce a low, enormous bellow, sustained for so long that it ceases to seem like an event. Broad patches of shallow water tremble without breaking. Pebbles chatter in their hollows. At intervals, an underground passage closes with a concussion that can be felt through the soles before it reaches the ears.

The animals call over it, perhaps because of it. Their shell chambers match the pitches of particular vents. As the pressure changes belowground, the living chorus shifts to meet it.

On a still day the noise is worse. There is no wind to break it up, nothing soft to absorb it. Sound travels across bare stone and returns from the low rises. You can watch a distant animal open its jaws and hear nothing distinguishable from the general thunder. Speech at arm’s length is useless except in the brief lulls between pressure changes.

The people who cross this country carry flat pieces of bone. To attract someone’s attention, they strike a bone against the rock beside the other person’s foot.

Their shelters are scattered over several miles, never grouped into anything quite like a village. Each stands on a slight rise near a dependable vent or a basin that holds water through the falling tide. From a distance they are difficult to separate from the natural humps: low roofs of overlapping shell, walls of fitted stone, entrances turned sideways to the prevailing rain.

There is no timber. Doorframes are made from the curved jaws of large animals. Roof seams are packed with rendered fat, powdered shell, and strips of tough inner skin. A newly repaired seam looks almost black against the old silver surfaces.

Beside one shelter stands a rack assembled from bones lashed with tendon. Broad strips of flesh hang on it, not to dry—the air seldom permits that—but to drain before they are lowered into a brine pit. A shallow trough holds chipped stone tools. Most are scrapers and wedges. The wedges have polished handles where hands have worked them into the seams of living shells.

Few things here have sharp exposed edges for long. Tools are kept wrapped. Cutting is done in sheltered places. Windblown grit and the constant handling of wet mineral surfaces round away a useful edge surprisingly quickly, in a land that otherwise seems to change hardly at all.

The paths are not roads. They are strings of pale stones placed where footing remains safe. Some markers have a hole bored through them. Some are set upright in little sockets. The distinction matters: one marks a crossing, the other a place to wait.

A basin that is ankle-deep now may begin filling from below without any rain. The first warning is often the behavior of the shell-bearers. They release their holds and move toward the rim, all at once, turning a motionless gray surface into a slow outward crawl. Then bubbles appear. Then the water rises.

No one builds over a place where bubbles return after being brushed away.

Toward evening, though the light changes only from pewter to lead, larger animals come onto the shelves. They have heavy shoulders, seal-smooth hides, and blunt heads equipped with crushing plates. They move awkwardly on land, dragging their bellies between deliberate steps, but in the channels their bodies become unexpectedly quick. They hunt by pressing the sides of their faces to the water.

Behind them follow small scavengers on high, thin legs. These wait beyond reach while a shell is broken, then dart in to pick among the fragments. Their calls are shrill enough to emerge briefly from the underground roar.

Farther out, pale swimmers travel through the flooded corridors between basins. You see a back, then another, widely spaced. Their routes give the open country a scale the low terrain otherwise conceals. Something first noticed beside the nearest shelter may still be visible an hour later, crossing a distant sheet of water beneath another rounded rise.

Cold mist begins to collect on the windward faces of the stones. Droplets thicken along every lip and fall with a regularity that would sound delicate anywhere else. Inside the shelters, people place their palms against the floors to judge the state of the tide. Familiar vibrations pass through the stone: the rough tremor of a southern throat opening, the smoother pulse of a chamber filling farther west.

Eventually the pressure turns.

The great bellow falters. The shell-bearers stop in uneven groups. For a little while there is only the actual sound of water: draining, pouring, dripping into newly emptied spaces.

In that interval, the country seems much larger. You hear an animal breathing beyond the nearest wall. A tool being set down in another shelter. From somewhere across the flats comes the small, precise crack of a shell giving way.

Then a deep passage takes air, and the stone begins to sound again.
