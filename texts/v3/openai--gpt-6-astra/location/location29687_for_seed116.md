The place is inside a hinge, near the top of something very tall.

There is no view of the height. The chamber has no window, and the water filling it is too thick with minute life to carry an image far. But height makes itself known in other ways. A loose bubble races toward the ceiling, flattens there, and trembles. The structure sways. Once in a long while, a change in load travels up from so far below that its arrival feels less like a vibration than a new direction for gravity.

The chamber is about the size of a washbasin. It lies behind a bearing plate, beyond a narrow opening clogged with green threads. Everything in it was manufactured: the floor, the ceiling, the six shallow channels converging on the drain, even the pale grit caught in those channels. The grit is worn enamel. There has never been soil here.

Warm water enters through three holes in the left wall.

They were drilled as circles. Now they are soft, slanting mouths, each surrounded by a glossy lip. For ages, water carrying a little suspended metal has passed through them, removing an amount too small to see. Screw heads have dwindled into shallow coins. The ribs that once stiffened the floor are low, broad rises with no perceptible beginning or end. A maker’s stamp survives as a depression in which a snail can shelter.

The water has not eaten through the chamber. It has rounded away everything that interrupted its passage, and now there is little left for it to catch.

Across the middle is an open stretch of yellow-white enamel. Almost nothing grows there. Light spreads over it in a shifting, ordinary brightness, the sort that might fall across a kitchen sink in the afternoon. It comes from a service lamp on the other side of the ceiling, through a disc of thick glass clouded by scratches. A slowly turning obstruction somewhere above makes a broad shadow pass every few minutes.

When the shadow comes, the grazing animals stop moving. When it leaves, they resume.

There are only four rooted colonies.

One grows around the warm inlets, a dense, dark-green fringe of narrow leaves. Another occupies the groove beneath a bolt, its translucent stems tangled so tightly that it looks solid until a shrimp enters and the whole mass gives way. The largest rises from a broken seam in the floor. It has broad, blistered leaves, copper beneath and green above, held almost horizontal in the current. Tiny roots explore the seam and return along its edges, unable to find anywhere deeper to go. The fourth colony is a low moss on the ceiling, beautifully lush, hanging down into the water in small rounded cushions.

Between them there is a great deal of empty water.

The plants are not remnants of a forest. Their ancestors lived in filter cartridges. They were cultivated to bind dissolved metals, tolerate heat, and survive long intervals without maintenance. Their softness conceals a working toughness: the leaves have fine mineral hairs, and an old stem, broken open, contains a wire-dark thread. When a colony dies, its roots remain fastened to the enamel. New growth uses them as a trellis.

Much of the life here is smaller than a letter.

Snails polish the bare floor in overlapping paths. Transparent grazers stand on leaves and comb them with feathery forelegs. Flat, quick animals hide under the inlet lips, darting out to seize whatever the water brings. In the ceiling moss, pale eggs hang in clusters. Now and then one opens, and a creature with an enormous tail and nearly invisible body launches itself into the chamber’s open middle.

Most do not reach the opposite wall.

A single larger animal lives beneath the broad-leaved colony. It is the length of a thumbnail, with a blunt head and two dark spots set deep inside its transparent body. It can remain still through several passages of the lamp’s shadow. Then it folds almost in half and is somewhere else, leaving the leaf behind it rocking.

Nothing here makes much sound of its own.

The chamber is nevertheless loud.

The hinge carries the noise of engines through its metal: a continuous loaded growl, rapid clicking, the knock of something that has worked loose but still performs its task. Sound arrives through every surface at once. The little animals live in it as they live in warmth. A pump starting far below sends a hard shudder through the floor and shakes pale debris from the ceiling moss. A valve closing produces a report that would seem violent in air, but here it passes through bodies too small to offer much resistance.

There is also a slower sound, a long strained note as the hinge turns.

During that movement, the chamber changes.

The water does not simply flow harder. It leans. Loose grains slide out of familiar hollows; the broad leaves bend until their copper undersides face the light. The open middle becomes a crossing that cannot safely be attempted. Shrimp retreat into the bolt groove. The blunt-headed hunter presses itself against the floor, mouth opening and closing.

Then fresh water arrives, warmer than what was there before, carrying food, shed fibers, a little oil, and occasionally an unfamiliar animal.

These are the chamber’s seasons. They may happen three times in a day or not at all for weeks.

No inhabitant can leave by the inlets. Their outward current is too strong. The drain is wide enough, but beneath its rounded rim the water accelerates into a narrow throat. Things that enter do not return. Around it the enamel is almost perfectly clean, except for a ring of short green growth that flourishes in the passing nutrients. Young snails approach this ring very slowly. Some feed there for hours. Some lose their grip.

At the highest corner, above the reach of the swaying leaves, an air pocket rests against the glass. Its underside reflects the entire chamber upside down. Roots, mouths, worn ridges, moving specks: all are gathered there in a bright, trembling miniature.

The pocket grows between movements of the hinge. Small bubbles join it, each arrival briefly making a dimple. When the mechanism turns far enough, the pocket slides to a pinhole and disappears with a sharp metallic chirp. Water touches a patch of ceiling that has been dry for days. The moss nearest it opens.

Beyond the walls are shafts, housings, cables, an immense exposed framework. The hinge serves some portion of that framework which still turns to meet wind or light. Whatever the original purpose, the turning continues. Heat rises. Pumps work. Replacement water finds the old channels.

Inside, a grazer reaches the end of a leaf and feels into open water with its forelegs. The service lamp brightens as its shadow passes. A grain of enamel settles into the maker’s stamp, beside the sheltered snail.

The chamber sways gently over the great distance below.
