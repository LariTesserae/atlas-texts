The ground is warm enough that kneeling on it feels like kneeling where some enormous animal has just been lying. Water comes through the knees of your clothes slowly. There is no rain at present, only a fine condensation that gathers on every exposed surface, including the undersides of things.

There is no light.

Even after hours, no horizon separates itself from the sky. The extent of the country becomes apparent in other ways: the long delay before a sound returns, the slight change in air pressure beside a bank, the arrival of warmer water around your ankles. Somewhere far out on the plain, an engine completes a stroke. You feel it through the soles of your feet. The sound arrives later, small and upholstered by distance.

Underfoot is a newly established mixture of mud, broken shell, and mineral plates. The plates have edges sharp enough to cut leather, though their surfaces are soft and almost waxy. Some lie flat. Others stand upright in ranks, no higher than a shin, with narrow channels between them. Water fills these channels to the brim without quite overflowing. Where two plates touch, they sometimes continue into one another, so that a finger following an edge comes back along the underside of the same piece.

The land has risen recently. It is still rising, but so slowly that the only reliable evidence is found on the pump houses.

These are scattered across several miles of shallow basins and low terraces: square concrete buildings, corrugated sheds, a few riveted cylinders mounted on footings. Their doors were installed at ground level. Most now open onto a drop of six or eight inches. Inside, tools hang a little higher each year, although the nails remain at the same height above the floor. Maintenance crews have marked the changes in pencil on the doorframes. In some buildings there are several columns of measurements, each sensible on its own and impossible to reconcile with the others.

The machinery is still working.

A pump house can be found by the dry patch around it and the faint tremor in its handrail. Its motor turns with extraordinary patience. A belt takes nearly a minute to travel its own length; the piston descends so gradually that watching would not help even if there were light. Oil creeps through glass feed cups. Heavy current moves through cables buried in wet trenches, but there are no indicators, no sparks, no lit windows. The switches have raised markings meant to be read with a thumb.

Each pump draws something out of the saturated ground. Water certainly passes through the pipes. At the discharge ends, however, the flow contains hard little intervals through which a hand cannot move. They lodge against the banks and accumulate. Silt settles around them. Eventually another low shelf of land is ready to bear weight.

Much of the plain is made this way.

It is crowded country, despite its openness. Nothing tall closes it in, and there are few plants, yet almost every available surface is occupied.

Small creatures pack the wet faces of the mineral plates. They have soft bodies with a single hard ridge down the back, and they turn as a group when the pressure changes. Their movement makes a sound like a sleeve drawn over a table. Broad, jointed animals stand in the channels with their bodies just beneath the water and their knees above it. Fine worms inhabit the condensation on pipes. A palm laid against a warm tank comes away with several of them stretched between the fingers, each trying to keep contact with both surfaces.

Farther out, there are herds.

Their presence first registers as an interruption in the spaciousness: breath, a musky dampness, the delicate shift of weight through mud. They are large, low animals, with feet that spread widely and withdraw without suction. Hundreds may occupy a basin in almost complete silence. They feed on the thin living film that forms over standing water, taking it up in long, slow passes of the mouth. When they cross a channel, smaller animals cling beneath them and are carried to new surfaces.

A herd may remain in one place for months. Around each body, sediment settles in a shallow lee. By the time the animals move, they have left a field of low, precise hollows.

These hollows retain something of their occupants. Water lying in one stays warm. Step into another and the ground yields with the delayed responsiveness of muscle. Sometimes a whole basin of empty impressions turns, very slightly, toward the approach of a pump’s next stroke.

Vegetation is confined to places where the ground has stopped changing shape. There are wiry reeds beside old foundations, patches of leathery leaves on gravel bars, roots braided through abandoned cable trays. The reeds are widely spaced but connected underground. Touch one and, after a long pause, another touches the back of your hand from farther away than it ought to reach.

Their leaves are thick, without much distinction between upper and lower surfaces. They drink the air. At intervals they release drops large enough to hear falling.

That is one of the ordinary sounds here: a drop striking water, followed by the small rearrangement of animals around it.

There is also the settling of sheet metal as its temperature changes by a fraction of a degree. The soft knock of a floating shell against a pipe. Once in a long while, a bird calls from above the basins—a single low note, answered neither by another bird nor by an echo. Birds are plentiful, but mostly encountered asleep. They cluster along cables and the undersides of catwalks, hanging by feet and beaks, their warm bodies almost touching. Their wings are broad and thin. When a flock changes roost, the displaced air passes over the plain like a door opening in another room.

Travel follows the service lines.

A cable can be kept against the hip for miles, though sometimes it descends into water and must be followed by hand. Junction boxes provide places to rest. There are maintenance platforms with benches, tool lockers, drinking tanks fitted with cups on chains. The cups are invariably wet inside. Someone replenishes the tanks and replaces worn gaskets. On a bench, a pair of gloves may still hold the shape of recently withdrawn hands.

No settlement announces itself. Instead, human occupation is distributed in small provisions: a ladder where the bank becomes steep, a rope crossing, a rubber mat outside a door. At one station, meals are left in sealed tins inside a cabinet. At another, boots hang in graduated sizes from a rail. The boots are marked with the distances for which they are intended.

Those markings matter. Distance here is partly a property of what touches the ground.

Bare feet follow the shortest routes, though these are rarely safe. Heavy work boots take the long way around features too shallow to notice. A loaded cart can spend an entire shift crossing a terrace that a person crosses in twenty minutes. Once its load is removed, the cart stands close enough to the starting point to be reached without raising your voice.

The older walkways have iron strips bolted across their boards to make every crossing come out the same length. Loose bolts are carefully replaced.

Beyond the last maintained walkways, the basins broaden. Warm water lies at calf depth over smooth, uncolonized ground. Here the sharp mineral formations are fewer, and the animals gather along their edges rather than venturing into the open. The water has almost no current. Nevertheless, things placed in it eventually reach the center.

There are engines out there too, much larger than those in the sheds. Their foundations can be felt through the water: broad concrete rims, bolt heads the size of bowls, steps descending into warmth. No walls enclose them. Their moving parts pass so slowly that colonies grow on one face of a connecting rod while the opposite face remains bare.

You can stand beside one for a long time before realizing which part is moving.

Then the stroke completes. The water presses gently against both sides of your legs. Across the region, birds tighten their hold on the cables, the feeding herds pause, and a film of moisture appears on the driest tools in the most distant shed.

Afterward there is more ground.

Only a little: a fresh lip at the edge of a pool, a narrow ridge between two channels, enough room beside a foundation for a ladder that previously stood in water. Its edges are immaculate. It is warm and densely inhabited almost at once.

The next stroke is a long way off. In the interval, drops gather beneath the handrails. Animals ease themselves into the new crevices. Somewhere a worker runs a thumb over a gauge, makes a pencil mark, and waits for the reading to settle.
