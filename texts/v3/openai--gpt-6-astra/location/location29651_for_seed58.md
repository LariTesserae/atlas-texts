The neighborhood occupies a cut in the ground, shallow enough that the uppermost windows look directly into the roots of the street trees above. From below, those roots appear in the retaining wall as severed, pale circles. The excavation is recent. There are still tooth marks from the digging machines in the clay, and the corners of the exposed stone are sharp enough to cut a hand.

It is not a large place: three narrow lanes, a pump court, a stair at either end. A person could cross it in a few minutes if the lanes were clear. They never are. Pipes travel along the walls at shoulder height, dip under doorways, rise over loading hatches. Staircases have been fitted beneath other staircases. Between two buildings there is a third building so thin that its rooms must be entered one at a time from separate doors on the landing. Everything has been lowered into the cut and fastened wherever it would fit.

The heat collects here.

Above, a pale strip of daylight hangs between the roof edges. Down in the lanes, the light is amber, reflected from brick and dust-coated glass. Electric bulbs burn inside wire cages, even at noon. Some stand behind bowls of water intended to soften their glare; most of the bowls are empty, with a white tide mark around the inside. The air smells of hot mortar, machine oil, and the mineral smell released when water first touches dry earth.

Each wall offers a different temperature. The clay retaining wall is warm. The brick houses are warmer. The tiled backs of the workshops can scarcely be touched. These workshops have been built against a municipal heat conduit that passes under the district, and nearly every enterprise draws from it: a laundry, a drying room, a kiln for electrical ceramics, a little works that presses insulating sheets from pulp. The conduit was here first. Its great elbows occupy the lower floors of several houses. At one doorway, you have to step over a painted hump that is not a threshold but a buried pipe.

Nothing roars. The engines are small and enclosed, their belts kept tight, their housings wrapped in felt and foil. Power arrives through cables from somewhere beyond the cut. The loudest regular sound is a latch falling shut.

Under that quiet there are many lesser sounds. Water ticks inside a pipe too hot to hold. A cooling sheet of metal gives a soft, abrupt click. From behind a wall comes the slow scrape of a press completing its stroke. The laundry exhaust makes a breathy noise for six seconds, stops, and resumes several minutes later. Sounds carry clearly along the passages; someone putting down a cup can seem to be in the next room when they are two landings away.

The pump court is roughly square and almost entirely occupied by machinery.

Its central tank is sunk below the paving, covered by thick glass panels clouded along the edges. Looking down through a clear patch, you see very little water: a black surface far below, a ladder disappearing into it, one copper float that rises and falls by barely the width of a finger. Four pumps stand around the tank, each painted a different shade of the same old green. Only one runs at a time. The changeover is so gentle that it is visible first in the vibration of their handles.

Water enters the neighborhood reluctantly. There is an occasional seep through a seam in the clay. Rain reaches the lanes only when it falls almost straight; otherwise the buildings above intercept it. A storm may leave the whole cut dry except for one furious gutter emptying into a stone basin. Every roof, sill, and projecting ledge has a little channel cut into it. These lead to jars, then to troughs, then to the tank.

The most dependable moisture comes from a cold-water main suspended high across the court. It belongs to the upper city. In this heat it sweats steadily, and a narrow zinc cradle has been installed beneath it to catch the drops. The cradle is jointed in short sections, each tilted toward the next. A bead of water may take several minutes to work its way along the entire apparatus. Dust slows it. Old solder turns it aside. At the end it gathers enough weight to fall.

There are plants where those arrangements leak.

Not many, and none accidental for long. A ribbon of green lives in a cracked drain behind the laundry, trimmed close so it cannot obstruct the flow. Three pots of a tough, narrow-leaved herb stand on a landing below the sweating main. Their leaves are silver with mineral deposits except at the tips, where new growth is almost translucent. In the darkest corner of the court, a flat brown growth spreads over a piece of damp packing board. It is lifted each morning to let the board dry a little, then laid back in place. Too much water kills it as reliably as too little.

Elsewhere, vegetation survives as stored things: bundles of stalks in the paper works, seed wrapped in cloth, a wooden chair whose back has been polished until its grain shows clearly through the blackened finish. There is no green canopy below street level. The few leaves make small, sharply defined shadows beneath the bulbs.

No birds visit the opening. There are no insects around the lamps, no scratching behind the walls. A door can remain open onto a sack of meal all night. The drains contain sediment, fibers, a little slippery growth, but nothing that darts away when a cover is lifted. Cobwebs do not soften the corners. New corners stay bare.

The place is changing continuously, though often by movements too slow or too enclosed to notice.

Fresh mortar dries and pulls away from a doorframe. A brass expansion joint lengthens in the afternoon and pushes its indicator across a penciled mark. Wet sheets rise through a shaft on a chain lift and descend crisp a floor away. Beneath the paving, water is filtered through beds of crushed ceramic, sent through the laundry, settled, and used again in the pulp vats. Steam passes through wall coils, gives up some heat, and returns as a thin trickle. The last usable warmth goes into a cabinet where shoes are dried.

People move through this circulation without much conversation. They carry shallow trays rather than buckets, because the stairs are steep and the passages narrow. They pause at marked recesses to let one another pass. Many wear small squares of folded cloth at the neck; at a tap they wet the cloth, press it briefly against the skin, and wring the remainder into a collecting pan.

Certain surfaces must be kept clear. No packages may rest against a wall painted with a white horizontal stripe: those walls contain expansion plates. Nothing is hung from the cold main, though its brackets would support a considerable weight. The stairs to the upper street must never both be closed at once. These rules are written on enamel signs, alongside older instructions that have already been crossed out. The neighborhood has not existed long enough to discover all the ways it can fail.

At the western end, construction is still under way. Here the lane narrows to a timber platform above an unfinished chamber. The excavation floor lies only a few feet below, littered with angular chips. White survey strings stretch between nails. A stair has been cast that currently leads into solid earth.

This end smells different: less inhabited, more like broken rock. The exposed clay shows the pressure of vanished layers in delicate curved bands. It crumbles when dry, so it has been covered with mesh and sprayed with a thin sealant that shines in the dimness. Through the coating, beads of moisture are beginning to appear. Someone has put a cup under the largest one.

Late in the day, the strip of sky turns copper-colored. The light reaches one upper window, slides across a row of newly installed meters, and disappears. The workshops continue. A fresh sheet emerges from the press and is laid on a rack. A valve opens behind the laundry wall. The green pump nearest the stairs stops trembling, and the next one begins.

For a little while after sunset, the upper air cools enough to sink into the cut. It comes down the stairs in a narrow, perceptible stream. People shift stools toward it. The leaves on the landing stir, although nothing hanging higher moves at all.

By then the brickwork has stored a whole day’s heat. The cool air travels perhaps halfway along the lane before losing itself. Near the pump court, the paving remains hot through the soles of a shoe, and a newly washed patch dries from its edges inward. Under the zinc cradle, a drop grows, holds, and falls.
