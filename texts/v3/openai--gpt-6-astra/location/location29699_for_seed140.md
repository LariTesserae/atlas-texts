The hollow is small enough to cross in a few minutes, provided you know where the water lies. It occupies the worn crown of a mountain, far above the weather of the lowlands. There are no sharp peaks nearby. Time has taken those away. What remains is a broad stone shoulder, a shallow depression, and a lip you can recognize beneath your feet because the ground begins to fall outward instead of in.

Beyond that lip is a long drop into open air.

You cannot see it. You cannot see anything here, even after waiting for your eyes to adjust. There is no moon through the leaves, no pale fungus, no indicator shining on a piece of equipment. Hold a hand against your face and its presence is warmth, the smell of your skin, a slight change in how the air moves.

The darkness has a precise upper boundary, though there is no way to find it from the ground. A bird descending into the hollow passes out of daylight without entering shade first.

Underfoot, the mountain feels almost soft.

The stone is hard, but everything that might once have cut or snagged has been worn down. Bare feet encounter long, shallow grooves, rounded mineral seams, depressions that fit the heel. Water has polished some surfaces to a greasy smoothness. Others have the fine drag of unglazed pottery. Even the broken places are old: a split slab beside the pool has edges as blunt as the rim of a bowl.

There is very little loose grit. What could be carried away has mostly gone.

Water persists in the remaining imperfections. It fills a thumb-deep runnel, disappears under a cushion of roots, returns as a warm seep farther downslope. The central pool is scarcely wider than a room. Its bottom shelves so gradually that you can be ankle-deep before you hear the difference in your footsteps. Stand still and small animals begin touching your toes.

The water is warm, though not hot. There is no sulfur smell. Heat rises evenly from the bedrock, strongest just before the hollow’s slow rain begins.

Rain here is seldom a shower. Moisture gathers on the underside of leaves, swells along hanging roots, and eventually falls. For hours the place makes the sound of rain without anything arriving from the sky.

Most of the hollow is empty air.

Nothing grows tall in its center. There is no thicket to push through, no rank of trunks, no ruin occupying the open ground. You can extend both arms and turn without touching anything. Above you is enough unoccupied space to hold a large hall, though no roof can be reached or heard.

The lushness is gathered at the edges.

Plants lie over the encircling stone in deep, continuous folds. Their leaves are broad, supple, and heavy with water; some rest flat against the warm rock, while others curl outward into pockets large enough to put a hand inside. Vines run horizontally through the mass. Roots hang into every seep. There are mats with the yielding texture of moss, tightly packed rosettes, curtains of fine stems that part against your shoulder and settle back slowly.

A narrow strip of this growth can be several feet deep. Beneath the outer leaves, older leaves remain whole, then soften, then become a warm fibrous substance threaded with new roots. Almost nothing escapes the margin. Even fallen blossoms tend to lodge where they fall.

You learn the plants by their surfaces and smells: the cool waxy leaf that sheds water at a touch; the ridged one holding a row of droplets along its underside; the small thick leaves that release a peppery scent when bruised. One creeping plant smells faintly of ripe pears. Another leaves a bitter film on the fingers.

They grow without visible light. At their root tips are minute hard bodies, too regular to be seeds. These draw something from the heat moving through the mountain. A leaf uncovered and carried into daylight contracts along its midrib, slowly sealing itself shut.

There are three made things in the hollow, unless some of the stone is also made.

One is a low bench set into the driest part of the rim. Its surface is seamless and slightly yielding, though it feels like stone when struck. Water will not remain on it. A wet palm leaves a print that disappears from the outside inward.

Another is a ring fitted flush around the pool’s outlet. It has no moving parts that a finger can find. Silt stops just upstream of it, gathers into soft pellets, and is periodically nudged onto the bank. The water beyond runs clear.

The third is a slender post at the outward edge of the hollow. You find it by a low vibration through the soles of your feet. It is warm, perfectly straight, and too smooth to grip securely. Nothing is attached to its top. Nothing marks its surface.

The post has been there long enough for roots to reach it, die, and leave hollows that other roots have followed. Whatever it does requires no visible attention. Sometimes, while you rest a hand against it, the distant pressure in your ears eases.

For such an open place, the hollow is loud.

Most of the noise comes from the vegetation. Thick-bodied insects rasp beneath the leaves. Small amphibians call from pockets of collected water, each using its leaf chamber to enlarge its voice. Several have notes so low that their size is difficult to believe when one settles in your palm: a damp animal no longer than your thumb, throat pulsing against your skin.

There are clicking sounds, chewing sounds, the papery scrape of wings being folded. A heavier creature occasionally moves along the outer rim, placing each foot carefully. You hear its breath stop when it listens.

The pool adds a continuous sucking murmur where water enters a submerged crack. At intervals, an air bubble rises through it and breaks with a hollow report. The surrounding stone returns that sound more strongly than it returns speech.

Animals use the vacant center mostly as a passage. A wing brushes your sleeve and is gone. Something paddles across the pool, leaving a small wake against your ankles. Long pauses separate these encounters, but the margins are crowded with life. Every handful of dead leaf contains movement. Every flower visited by touch seems to have something already feeding inside it.

Nothing here benefits much from color. Courtship is conducted by drumming, scent, and vibrations sent through stems. Some insects carry combs on their forelegs and draw them across the ribs of leaves. Their partners answer several feet away through the connected roots.

Change is slow, despite the noise. Calls overlap until they seem almost stationary. Droplets take a long time to gather. A leaf may spend half a day lowering under the weight of water, spill its burden in three loud drops, and begin to rise again.

Even the warmth moves patiently. Sitting on the bench, you can feel it arrive through the soles of your shoes before the air becomes more humid. Later, the leaves begin dripping. Later still, the pool reaches the shallow notch beside your foot.

There is no dawn to organize these events. Hunger, temperature, and the filling of little reservoirs are sufficient. If you stay long enough, the first impression of featureless darkness gives way to a precise arrangement: the dry approach behind you, the pool a little left of center, the dense breathing border, the single post beside the drop.

Eventually you can walk from the bench to the water without holding out your hands. You stop at the right place before your feet get wet. Across the hollow, an animal is working steadily at a tough leaf stem. Water gathers overhead. The stone beneath you has kept the same gentle slope for longer than any of the living things can usefully remember.
