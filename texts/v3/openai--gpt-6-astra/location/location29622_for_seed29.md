Under a flake of rock near the summit, there is a hollow just large enough to put a hand into. You would not leave it there long.

The flake lies almost flush with the slope. Its upper face is gray, sun-whitened, indistinguishable from the thousands of angular fragments around it. Along its downhill edge, a black seam admits air. That is the entrance. The mountain outside is all exposure: thin atmosphere, bare ridges, wind strong enough to make standing a deliberate act. Inside the seam, the air is hot and close.

The hollow has only recently opened. A fracture lifted the flake a little, perhaps during the last season of small earthquakes, and exposed surfaces that weather has not yet softened. Every edge is sharp. The ceiling preserves the torn shape of the stone beneath it; projections above correspond to sockets below. In places they almost touch.

There is very little empty space. Splinters lean against splinters. Grains fallen from the ceiling lodge between larger grains, and finer dust fills their junctions. What looks, from the entrance, like a black floor is a succession of obstacles, with narrow passages descending between them. Farther in, yellow crystals crowd a warm crack. Some are no larger than pollen. Others have grown into brittle blades, stopping where they meet a neighbor or the roof.

Here the landscape is still acquiring its surfaces.

Gas rises through the lower fracture, too slowly to feel as a current. It leaves sulfur on the cooler stone. The newest deposits have a faint greasy sheen; older ones are opaque and pale. Nothing has rounded them. There are no channels, no washed hollows, no accumulation of smooth sediment. A loose grain remains where it falls unless an animal shifts it.

Only a thread of daylight enters beneath the flake. It reveals one sloping ledge, a scatter of ocher dust, and the bright tip of a crystal. Beyond that, the hollow is dark. The heat gives no light. It is simply present in the stone, increasing toward the back until the deepest crack is uninhabitable.

The animals occupy the margins of that heat.

Tiny mites move through the dust, numerous enough that a patch watched closely begins to separate into individual bodies. Pale springtails gather beneath an overhang near the entrance, where a trace of moisture sometimes persists on the cooler underside of the roof. A small dark beetle stands with its antennae inserted into a gap. There are shed skins caught among the mineral needles, empty egg cases, a dead fly brought in by the wind. Beneath the fly, almost its own color, something feeds.

No leaf has grown here. Food arrives in fragments: airborne spores, pollen carried far above the last vegetation, pieces of insects, occasionally an entire exhausted body. Thin microbial films occupy favorable patches of stone near the vent. Most are too sparse to see. The animals find them by passing over the same surfaces again and again.

A spider has made a few lines across one passage. They are almost invisible even in the entrance light. Dust gathers on them slowly. The spider waits in a recess where its legs fit against the walls, leaving just enough space for prey to pass.

The hollow is loud.

Wind presses against the outer seam with a coarse, sustained hiss. Sometimes its pitch changes as a gust strikes from farther north. Loose grit rattles briefly overhead. Under these sounds is a deeper vibration, regular and nearly continuous, conducted through the mountain.

Far down the slope, out of sight, a geothermal station draws heat from the same body of rock. Its turbines and pumps send a low mechanical tremor through foundations, bore casings, and bedrock. By the time it reaches this pocket, the separate machines have become difficult to distinguish. There is a heavy pulse, a finer whine inside it, and an occasional change of load that makes the flake hum.

Nothing manufactured has entered the hollow. There is no metal, no cut surface, no discarded thread. Nevertheless, an industrial rhythm passes through every grain.

The animals remain mostly still against it. When a pump changes speed, a beetle may pause with one foot raised. Then it continues along the edge of the sulfur, feeling ahead.

Change here is slow and crowded. A crystal thickens into a passage. Dust settles onto an egg case. A mite works beneath a fragment and loosens it by an amount too small to notice; days later, the fragment slips and closes one route while opening another. A minute alteration in the vent shifts the warm boundary outward, and the animals retreat toward the entrance. When it withdraws, they return.

At night the little strip of light disappears. The outer slope cools quickly, but the underside of the flake stays warm. Creatures shelter deeper beneath it, gathering along a narrow band where the cold from above and the heat from below are both tolerable. Beyond the entrance, the summit wind drives over bare stone. Within, feet touch crystal, antennae enter cracks, and fresh sulfur continues to collect on edges that have never known rain.
