The heat is in the ground before it is in the air. Set a hand on one of the dark stones along the water’s edge and it feels as though the sun has been shining on it for days, although morning has only just reached this part of the plateau. Water trembles in the stone’s little hollows. Somewhere underneath, a bubble rises through a narrow passage and lifts the surface without quite breaking it.

Beyond the plateau’s southern rim, clouds lie far below.

They fill the lower country completely, hiding the slopes, the foothills, whatever rivers receive the water falling from this height. Other summits emerge at a distance: pale, bare ridges with snow lodged in their shaded seams. Here, at nearly the same elevation, there are broad leaves wet enough to drip, fern thickets, and pools in which threads of green growth move slowly with the rising hot water.

The plateau is several valleys wide, though its valleys are shallow. They lie between rounded volcanic shoulders, their old black rock softened under rust-colored soil and a thick, yielding cover of roots. Nothing has the raw look of a recent eruption. The cliffs have lost their sharp corners. Gullies have found dependable courses. Even the mineral crust around the springs has been broken, buried, and grown over so often that it forms part of the soil.

Heat comes up through the whole basin, unevenly but persistently. A few places are too hot for anything but slick films of orange and cream. Most are merely hot enough to keep the rain warm, the roots active, and the night from cooling. Cold air crossing the outer ridges sinks into the basin and loses its chill among the leaves.

From a high shoulder, the landscape looks almost leisurely. Marshes occupy the lowest ground, linked by silver-brown channels. Low forest gathers around the cooler streams. Open stretches of sedge separate islands of dark foliage, and here and there a pale mineral terrace shows through, carrying water from one shallow basin into the next. Mist marks some of the springs. Others are invisible until a patch of open water catches the light.

Up close, there is little empty space.

Small plants grow on larger plants; roots fork around warm stones and vanish beneath floating mats. Moss hangs from branches in heavy pads rather than fine streamers. The trees are short, thick-trunked, and widely rooted, with leathery leaves that turn their narrow edges toward the noon sun. Where a trunk leans over a channel, it carries a second little forest along its upper side: ferns, seedlings, pale fungi, flowers no larger than a fingernail. Water falls from all of them at different intervals.

Some of that water is rain. Some has condensed overnight on the exposed crowns. Some has traveled upward through a tree and returned by a route too slow to see.

By midday the air above the marsh is hot enough to make breathing feel deliberate. It is still mountain air, thin despite the moisture. A short climb brings a pounding pulse. Large animals move with an economy that seems almost ceremonial, taking a few steps and stopping, turning their heads before committing their bodies to a new direction.

There are shaggy, narrow-hoofed grazers in the sedges. Their coats are sparse along the flanks and dense over the spine, useful when they leave the warm basin for the cold ridges. They feed where fresh runoff dilutes the springs, cropping young shoots from small raised islands. Behind them, dark birds turn over the exposed mud. Insects gather around the animals’ legs but seldom rise far; wind is always passing overhead, and the sheltered world near the ground is easier to inhabit.

The pools have their own boundaries. In one, clear water reveals small fish holding above a gravel bed. A few paces away, a milky pool is empty of visible life. The two overflow into the same channel, but only after the milky water has crossed a long, shallow shelf. Along that shelf, successive colors show where different organisms can live: white at the source, ocher below it, then olive, then the bright green of ordinary plants.

The lushness is precise. A hand’s breadth can separate a living root from a scalded one.

There is a continuous operation beneath all this growth. Water descends through fractured rock, warms at depth, expands, rises, and presses into branching passages. The passages narrow as minerals accumulate. Pressure builds. Somewhere a seal loosens, and a spring that has been almost still begins to pour. Elsewhere, the increased flow draws a pool down by the width of a leaf.

The basin runs through these changes all day. It has the scale and persistence of an industrial process, but little of the noise. Most of the work is done underground. A deep knock occasionally travels through the stone. Steam escapes with a soft, steady hiss from a cleft hidden in fern roots. One broad pool empties every few hours with a low swallowing sound, exposing slick steps of mineral before slowly filling again.

Animals notice these cycles. The grazers leave the pool’s edge before it drains. Birds arrive afterward to pick through the stranded weed.

Rain usually comes in the afternoon, when cloud rising along the outer cliffs catches on the plateau. First the distant ridges disappear. Then the light becomes flat and silver, still bright enough to show the veins in a leaf. The rain falls almost vertically in the sheltered hollows. It patters on foliage, taps the pools into overlapping circles, and darkens every exposed patch of soil.

For a while the geothermal mist and the weather become indistinguishable.

Water gathers quickly, but the old channels accept it. They deepen without wandering much. Brown floodwater slips beneath root bridges, spreads across the sedge flats, and clears again as it passes through them. At the rim, several streams fall out of sight. Their noise barely reaches the basin; the cliffs face away, and the cloud below takes the water.

Afterward, visibility returns in pieces. A black shoulder of rock appears across the marsh, then a stand of trees, then the bright outer edge of the plateau. Leaves shed their burden. The birds resume their quiet work.

By evening, the cold country beyond is visible again. Snow on the far summits turns blue. Here the pools keep their warmth, and pale insects settle on the undersides of leaves. Beneath a floating mat, something swims slowly enough to leave only a widening seam.

As the air cools, more of the basin begins to steam. Separate wisps rise from channels, bare stones, and small openings in the turf. They reveal how widely the heat is distributed, how much of the green ground is resting over water in motion. Then the wisps join, obscuring the lower trunks, while the treetops remain clear in the last ordinary light.
