Under the last stair of the summit ladder, behind a flap of blackened felt, there is a country no wider than a pair of cupped hands.

The ladder stands so high that clouds arrive beneath it with their bright undersides turned up. Above its final rung there is nowhere to climb, although the climbing continues: the uprights lengthen slightly whenever someone looks away. The little country is tucked into the joint where that rung enters the left upright. To find it, you have to kneel on the ladder with most of your body over the drop, lift the felt, and put your face against a breath of wet heat.

Inside, the air smells of steamed reeds, clay, and leaves bruised between fingers.

There is no stone that has not been placed. The lowest ground is a wad of bark cloth, pressed into the wooden joint and stitched through with sinew. Over this, somebody has laid flakes of fired clay, overlapping them like scales. Bundles of split rush form retaining walls. Pebbles of charcoal prop up the walls; knots secure the pebbles; the knots are themselves set in little sockets of wax. Even the dark substance around the roots is manufactured—powdered potsherd, rotten cord, chewed bark, ash wetted with spit. Dig anywhere and you come to a fastening.

There is very little room to dig.

The ground rises in crowded shelves toward the back of the cavity, each shelf higher and hotter than the one before. A green shelf overhangs a black shelf; beneath that is a red clay ledge just broad enough to hold a bead of water. A staircase of four seed husks climbs between them, but the fourth step is much farther from the third than it appears. Seen from the entrance, the difference is a fingernail’s thickness. Attempted from within, it is an ascent through thin air, with blood beating in the ears and the lower terraces disappearing under vapor.

Height has been packed here more tightly than matter.

The plants make use of it. Ferns no taller than eyelashes spread heavy, wet fronds across the ledges. A vine with translucent stems threads through the rush walls, sends a root down, then discovers that down has moved and sends another root sideways. Its leaves turn toward the seams in the felt. There are glossy cushions of liverwort, minute fans of sedge, pale shoots folded so closely together that one can open only by pushing another into the ground.

Their roots hold the construction together and prise it apart at the same time. A cord tightens. A clay scale tips. A whole terrace lifts slowly on the swelling back of a tuber. The neighboring plants lean into the new space before the movement has finished.

Nothing here has settled into its final shape.

Along the upper margin, a strip of wet clay is still acquiring the properties of a cliff. Thumbprints remain visible in it. Between two prints, a vertical face is deepening, not by losing material but by becoming more vertical: shadows gather in its shallow creases until there is room for a ravine. The clay does not crumble. It has no scree, no rounded corners, no old wounds. Every exposed edge carries the slight, damp shine of something just separated from a hand.

Water comes out of the bindings.

It darkens a knot, swells along one twisted fiber, and releases a heavy drop. The drop falls onto a leaf and stays there, trembling under the blows of more drops. Eventually the leaf bows, and a warm flood goes through three terraces at once. Roots submerge. Charcoal shines. The seed-husk staircase becomes a cataract.

Then the water draws back into the seams of the floor.

There are channels, but no channel has been cut by a stream. They have been scored with a bone point or pressed open with a reed. When a new channel appears, you may see the shallow groove forming ahead of the water, moving upslope a little faster than the flood. Occasionally it takes a wrong turn. Water piles against a wall until the wall is hurriedly given an opening.

The sound is enormous.

From outside, with the felt lowered, there is only a dull ticking in the ladder joint. Inside, each drop hits like a bucket overturned on a roof. Roots creak against their lashings. The damp clay makes a low, sustained grinding noise as its interior distances increase. Somewhere behind the highest ledge, a mallet is striking a peg with patient, irregular blows. No worker is visible. Sometimes the mallet pauses just long enough for a leaf to unfold, then begins again.

Beneath everything runs the hiss of heat entering wet material.

It is too hot to rest a fingertip on the upper terraces. Steam escapes from under the clay scales and collects on the cooler felt ceiling, where the droplets look almost black. There is no sun inside. Light comes through three needle holes and a slit beside the upright: narrow, tarnished shafts, thick with suspended moisture. A leaf passing through one of them glows yellow-green, showing every branch of its veins. Behind it, another leaf remains dark and wet.

The hottest place is a small unfinished basin at the back.

Its rim is a loop of rawhide pegged to six tiny posts. Within the loop, mud rises and subsides with a slow kneading motion. New surfaces appear in it: a slope, a flat, the suggestion of a ridge. Sometimes a ridge persists long enough for a seed to lodge on it. The seed splits almost immediately. Two pale leaves emerge, and the mud firms beneath their weight.

Sometimes the ridge folds away first, and the seed is returned to the edge.

This is where most of the country is being made. There is no spare space for it, so older portions are pushed into folds under neighboring shelves. They remain accessible through gaps that look too narrow to admit a root. Looking into one, you might see a wet plain extending behind a knot, its crowded vegetation shaken by rain. Move your head and the plain is gone; there is only a thread of sinew with a green stain along it.

The few animals know the folds better than any visitor.

A white, soft-bodied creature lives beneath the seed-husk steps. It has six feet and two blunt feelers, and moves with the caution of something carrying a full bowl. During floods it climbs onto the underside of a tread. There it hangs above water that is falling in both directions, its feelers tucked against its mouth. When the water recedes, it descends to feed on the green film around a peg.

Days may pass without its appearing. The plants continue their pushing and opening; the drops continue to thunder. Nothing waits for it.

Near the entrance, a strand of red cord hangs loose from a retaining wall. It is tempting to tuck it back, or to lift a displaced clay flake into line with its neighbors. But any adjustment travels. Pull the cord a little and a terrace tightens somewhere you cannot see. Press down a flake and hot water wells up around the fern roots. Even breathing into the cavity changes the weather: condensation gathers at once, the felt sags, and the upper basin starts to rise.

So you stay very still, your knees aching on the final rung, your face damp and hot while the cold summit wind pulls at your clothes.

Inside the joint, a new ledge is coming forward. It advances in small, sticky increments between two crowded walls. A root reaches across to touch it. Water shines along its edge before there is quite an edge to hold it.

From behind the felt comes another blow of the mallet, loud enough to feel in your teeth.
