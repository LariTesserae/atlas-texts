The hollow lies just below the mountain’s highest ridge, where the rock folds inward and makes a place no larger than a small grove. Above it are bare slopes, pale scree, the exposed backs of stones. None of that openness enters here. The hollow has its own low ceiling of interlocked branches, roots, and hanging vegetation. To stand upright in its center would be to put your head among wet leaves.

There is no path down. What looks like one is the dark back of a root, broad enough for a foot, disappearing beneath ferns.

The stone has been weathered past sharpness. Its ledges are blunt; its cracks have wide, softened mouths. Water has hollowed the floor into a succession of shallow basins, although so little of the floor is visible that their arrangement has to be understood from the vegetation. Fine rushes occupy the submerged shelves. Moss thickens where the water is only a skin. In the deeper cups, small black leaves lie flat against the surface, packed edge to edge, with no bare water between them.

The rock is a dark, greenish gray. Where it can be seen through the water, it looks almost soft. There are no loose chips, no fresh scars, no grit waiting to be carried away. Each stone sits deeply in the material around it, fitted by an immense history of pressure and weather. Even the smallest pebble has a hollow exactly its size.

Rain belongs to this place, but there is no rain falling.

Water occupies every available thickness. It fills the moss, beads the undersides of leaves, rests in the grooves of bark. Long clear drops hang from the ends of roots. They do not grow heavy enough to fall. Beneath a projecting slab, a thread of water extends uninterrupted from stone to pool: a narrow, transparent attachment, neither breaking nor visibly flowing. The pool below has no ring around the point where it enters.

The air is cold enough to make wet skin ache, but nothing is frozen. Leaves remain supple. The water is liquid. A faint mineral smell presses through the richer smells of soaked wood, liverwort, and old leaf mold. There is no wind to separate them.

Most of the light comes through a crooked opening far overhead. It is so weak that it does not illuminate surfaces so much as distinguish one darkness from another. A leaf can be known by its smoothness against the rough dark of moss. The underside of a branch is lost altogether. Tiny points on the rock turn out to be water rather than stars.

Only after the shape of the hollow becomes clear does its occupation become apparent.

The knobs along the nearest root are snails. Their shells are low and dark, mineral-encrusted, almost continuous with the bark. Between them sit smaller snails, and between those, mites as pale as grains of flour. Flat-bodied insects fill the seams. A beetle occupies the dry underside of nearly every curled leaf, though “dry” here means only that a drop does not cover it.

There are amphibians everywhere.

Little black ones rest in the moss with their limbs folded close. Larger, broad-headed ones lie half submerged beneath the floating leaves. Their backs bear the same irregular green patches as the stones. At the edge of one basin, five or six are fitted together so closely that the eye first reads them as a single ridged mass. Their eyes are open. Their throats are still.

Under the ledge, where the darkness is deepest, fur fills the crevices. Small mountain mammals lie pressed against one another, their noses tucked into the dense pile of neighboring bodies. Farther up, birds occupy the branch forks: short-tailed, thick-feathered birds with feet hidden beneath their breasts. A wing has been opened just enough to expose a narrow band of lighter feathers. It remains open.

Nothing startles. Nothing makes room.

There is so much animal life that concealment has become a property of abundance. A salamander is hidden by three others. A moth vanishes against the overlapping wings of moths. What appears to be a patch of finely textured bark resolves into legs, antennae, sealed wing cases. The hollow is not swarming. Swarming would imply somewhere left to go.

Its quiet is equally crowded. A low sound fills the space, barely above the pressure of silence: perhaps water within the mountain, perhaps the combined note of minute bodies. It has no pulse. There are no separate calls, no taps or wingbeats. Listening longer does not bring a distant answer.

The trees are old mountain shrubs grown thick at the base and unable to gain height. Their trunks turn sideways beneath the ridge, bend around boulders, and reappear as roots in places where there should have been branches. Ferns grow from their crotches. Moss covers the ferns’ older stems. Delicate translucent growths occupy the moss itself. There is no unclaimed surface, but neither is there any sign of something being crowded out. No leaf yellows in the shade of another. No seedling strains toward the opening.

At first the place might seem caught in an instant. Yet there is nothing here with the tension of interrupted movement. No falling animal, no splash held in the air, no branch bent against a passing gust. Everything rests in a posture it could keep.

The hollow has reached its condition.

The wood is wet without proceeding toward rot. An exposed root has been worn almost through, and retains precisely the thickness it needs. An insect rests beside a mouth that does not close upon it. There are eggs under leaves, but none are nearer hatching than the others. The water is full of small, complete bodies.

There are no cut branches, hearth stones, worked edges, or marks on the rock. Nothing has been arranged to shelter a visitor. A person could perhaps crouch on the one broad stone near the middle, though close inspection would reveal its surface already occupied by transparent worms laid in the shallow wet grooves.

From there, the ridge above would be invisible. So would the extent of the mountain. The whole world available to the eye would be a few feet of saturated growth, a little black water, roots crossing roots, and the immense number of creatures at rest within them.

On the lowest leaf hangs a clear drop. Inside it, upside down, is the small opening in the roof.

It is the brightest thing in the hollow.

It does not fall.
