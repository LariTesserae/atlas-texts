Near the summit, where the rest of the mountain is cold enough to keep old snow in its creases, there is a cleft that breathes warm air.

Its entrance is easy to miss: a crooked opening behind a fallen slab, barely wide enough for shoulders. Outside, the wind drags ice grains over pale rock. Inside, it stops touching you. The air is thin but warm, with a mineral taste and, farther in, a thick animal smell. A few steps down, the last light becomes a gray strip above your head. Then the passage widens.

The whole place could fit inside a small cathedral, though no single viewpoint reveals it. Stone interrupts everywhere. Thick fins descend from the roof; blunt pillars lean almost against them; shelves overlap along the walls. The floor is a succession of rounded humps with dark seams between. Some of those seams are ankle-deep. Others narrow for several yards before you hear a pebble strike.

Very little here has a fresh edge. Water once occupied more of the cleft, perhaps under pressure, perhaps when a glacier sealed its mouth. It left hollows inside hollows, broad scallops in the walls, pockets with smooth lips. Softer bands have worn back beneath harder ones, making shallow chambers stacked so closely that their ceilings are also the floors of the chambers above. The rock is dark brown where dry, nearly black where damp, and pale along certain seams where mineral deposits have thickened into soft-looking ridges.

The warmth comes through those seams. It is strongest near the floor, escaping from cracks too narrow to see into. Occasionally a little air rises against your hand, as gentle and definite as an animal exhaling.

There is water, but not much water to spare. Condensation beads on cooler overhangs and gathers until a drop becomes heavy enough to fall. Most drops land in old cups no larger than a footprint. A few cups overflow into narrow grooves, wetting the stone for a short distance before the moisture disappears beneath grit. Only at the lowest end is there a pool wide enough to lie beside, though too shallow to swim in. Its surface is black except when the dim light from the entrance catches a trembling edge.

The pool has a clean mineral bottom at its warm end. At the other end, where the water cools, it holds a fine accumulation of wings.

At first, sound gives the impression that the chamber is much larger than it is. There is a high, continuous chittering above, a dry rushing on either side, and a lower noise like someone slowly stirring gravel. Individual calls come out of that mass—sharp squeaks, clicks, a brief metallic rasp—and return from several directions. A drop striking the pool can sound surprisingly loud. A small stone dislodged on an upper shelf sets off a short clatter, followed by a change in the voices around it.

Then the darkness begins to separate into bodies.

Bats occupy the undersides of the stone fins, packed shoulder to shoulder in the warmer recesses. Their fur looks gray until they move, exposing chestnut backs and pale faces. They hang in clusters so deep that those nearest the rock are almost hidden. Every so often one works its way through the others, hooking a thumb over a neighbor, opening a wing just far enough to find another hold. The disturbance passes through the cluster in a series of small adjustments and subsides.

Higher up, where narrow fissures reach the open mountain, swiftlets cling to rough ledges. You hear their navigating clicks before you make out their pointed wings. They enter singly, rapidly, through openings that admit almost no light. Having landed, they become still enough to resemble flakes of the wall. Their few usable ledges are crowded with adults and young, and beneath each ledge the stone has acquired a pale, thickened apron.

The greatest number of animals is lower down.

Crickets fill the sheltered cracks. Their antennae extend from holes that seem too small to contain them, crossing and touching in the dark. Broad, dull beetles move over the floor with the unhurried certainty of creatures that rarely need to search far for food. Mites gather in the folds of fallen feathers. Small flies rest in bands around the moist rims of the water cups. On the warmest patches of stone, translucent young insects lie so close together that a passing shadow seems to make the surface contract.

Where the floor appears loosely pebbled, look longer. Some of the pebbles have legs.

There are predators enough to occupy every scale of this abundance. Spiders hang short webs across the mouths of cracks rather than across open space. Long-legged hunters wait beside the drinking places. Small dark lizards flatten themselves beneath warm flakes of stone, one eye level with the floor. They seldom chase. A cricket will eventually come within reach.

Almost everything eats something that has arrived from elsewhere. At dusk, the bats rise out through the entrance and the higher fissures to hunt over warmer slopes far below. The birds follow insects lifted by afternoon currents along the mountain face. They bring the lower world back in their stomachs, on their feet, caught in their feathers. Droppings, shed skin, eggshell, dead young: little is carried out again. The cleft has accumulated generations of this traffic in the protected spaces between its rocks.

Its sparse plants survive near the limits of the darkness. At the entrance, a few wiry stems grow sideways toward the light, their roots following a damp seam into warmth. A fern the size of an open hand occupies a pocket beneath the slab. Farther in, green dwindles to a dark stain around one slow seep. Elsewhere there are pale films, leathery growths beneath shelves, and threads that become visible only when moisture catches on them. Nothing rises into a grove. There is barely enough room for a leaf to unfold without being walked over.

Despite the noise, the place changes slowly. Most movement is local: a body turning, a beetle negotiating a stone, a wing shifting to cover a face. Warm air continues upward. Cooler air slips in along the floor. The small pool gains a little water during storms and loses it afterward. Its mineral rim grows too slowly to notice, except that old insect shells are embedded in it, their shapes gradually filling.

Night outside makes little difference to the light within, but it changes the pressure of bodies. The roost loosens. Wings begin to pass close to your ears. For a while the cleft is full of departures, each animal finding the same narrow ways between the pillars. Then the upper chamber grows quieter, and the sounds beneath the floor become audible again.

Above this pocket of warmth, the summit is bare and enormous. The wind moves over snowfields, broken ridges, long slopes with almost nothing alive upon them. Down here, within a few dozen paces, every sheltered inch has occupants. Even the spaces that seem empty are passages kept clear by use.

If you remain still beside the pool, something will eventually come to drink. It will pause at the rim, test the water, and settle its mouth to the surface. Behind it, from a crack you had taken for a line in the stone, another pair of antennae will appear.
