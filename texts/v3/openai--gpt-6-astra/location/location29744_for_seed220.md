The stairs descend only a little way before the daylight disappears. It is lost at a bend, behind a wall whose upper edge has been rounded by so many years of runoff that it looks upholstered. Below that bend the air is warm enough to feel used. It smells of wet mineral, old wiring, and the faint sweetness of roots growing somewhere out of reach.

The space at the bottom is not quite a square and not quite a chamber. You could cross it in two minutes, if you knew where the floor changed level. Its ceiling lies beneath several city streets; occasionally the weight of a vehicle passes overhead as a long, soft pressure in the ears. Nothing of the street itself is audible.

There is too much other noise.

A broad mechanical roar fills the hollow, punctuated by three hard knocks, a pause, and a dragging sound that lasts longer than it ought to. Elsewhere water seems to be falling in great quantities. It is not. Behind a grille, a stream no wider than a finger drops through a shaft whose proportions enlarge every splash. Near the stairs you hear a continuous metallic rattling, although the nearest moving part is on the far side of the chamber. The walls carry some sounds faithfully and swallow others. A footstep may vanish; a coat brushing a handrail can come back from overhead.

For a while there seems to be nothing to look at.

Then the eyes begin to find the small differences within the dark. The floor is paler than the air above it. A long bench interrupts that pallor. There are doorways, most of them sealed, and a balcony low enough to reach from the bench if its underside were not curved and slick. Farther along, a line of dull green points marks the lip of a drainage channel. They brighten in succession as something travels beneath them.

This was built as a public place. Its proportions still make allowances for crowds: stairs wide enough for people to pass in both directions without noticing one another, broad landings, shallow ramps turning along the walls. But nearly all the construction is pushed to the edges. Shops or offices occupy the thickness of the perimeter, their fronts closed by seamless shutters. Several generations of pipes, conduits, service galleries, and access doors are fitted above and behind them. A whole neighborhood’s worth of utilities surrounds an almost empty volume.

Only two things stand free of the walls: the bench, and a cylindrical post with its top missing.

The bench is dry. Its seat is warm.

Most of the floor is dry too, though not dusty. It has a close, dark surface like stone rubbed with oil. Long use has erased the joints between its original slabs, leaving shallow depressions that do not quite correspond to where anyone would walk now. Here and there the floor swells over buried foundations. The rises are gentle enough to be felt first through the ankles.

Water arrives locally. One ceiling seam beads for an hour, rains for ten minutes, then dries for most of a day. A patch beside a shutter stays damp in a perfect oval, even when the rest of the chamber has been dry for weeks. Beneath the balcony, a basin carved into the paving holds three fingers of clear water over copper-colored sediment. Its overflow has worn a channel no deeper than a thumbnail, wandering halfway across the square before finding a drain.

Everything has had time to establish its route.

The old runoff marks are pale and broad. The active ones are narrow, almost black. Around the drains, the paving has been eaten into overlapping crescents. Mineral crusts have formed, cracked under their own weight, and been worn smooth again. Even the exposed cables have a weathered look: thick, dull skins with deposits on their lower sides, hanging in the same settled curves as roots.

The machinery is less easy to date. Some of it has labels stamped in a recognizable script. Some has no visible means of being opened.

High in one wall, a row of black ceramic plates moves heat out of the inhabited floors above. Nothing glows. When the load increases, the space in front of the plates becomes difficult to focus on, and the roar deepens until it can be felt through the soles. Moisture disappears from the nearest handrail. A minute later it condenses on a colder wall across the chamber, with no perceptible draft between.

Below the plates is a delivery aperture scarcely wider than a book. Every so often it opens onto a brightly lit passage that appears much longer than the building could accommodate. A flat carrier slides out, crosses a short shelf, and enters a neighboring slot. The apertures close. The darkness seems more complete afterward.

People have left practical marks around these things. A strip of rough material warns the feet away from the heat-transfer wall. Three white notches indicate where it is safe to stand during a pressure release. Someone has scratched an arrow beside a service door, corrected its direction, and polished the correction through repeated touching.

There is little vegetation. Under the stair, where a tiny maintenance lamp remains on, a plant grows horizontally from a crack. It has six leathery leaves, each angled toward the lamp, and a root that follows the mortar into darkness. Its fallen leaves collect in a dry recess rather than decomposing. Farther in, roots from the surface have entered around a pipe collar. They make a pale tassel above the basin. Their tips are stained orange from the water.

The basin supports a few translucent, shrimp-like animals. They keep to the underside of its stone rim, moving into view only when vibrations from the machinery loosen something edible from the roots. Once in a while a small gray animal slips from behind a shutter to drink. It has broad feet and a thin tail and waits motionless through the loudest part of the cycle. Then it drinks quickly, with both ears folded back.

There must be others nearby. The rim is worn at that particular spot.

At irregular intervals, the whole chamber changes its breathing. The dragging sound stops. For perhaps five seconds there is only the enlarged trickle behind the grille and a soft ticking in the walls. The warm air begins to move toward the stairs. Loose root tips lean with it.

Then a deep report comes from under the floor.

The bench trembles. Water shivers out of the basin’s shallow end. Behind the sealed frontages, mechanisms answer one another down the length of the wall: a succession of impacts, quickening, then merging into the familiar roar. The green points along the drain run twice from end to end.

This is when the ceiling seam begins to rain. The drops fall heavily into one small patch, darkening the stone, feeding the worn channel. Everywhere else remains dry.

From the stairs, if you look back, the falling water is almost invisible. You can make out the maintenance lamp, the six leaves beneath it, and a slight shine where the basin meets the floor. The rest is a warm, noisy depth enclosed by streets, continuing its work with no one at the counters and almost nothing crossing the square.
