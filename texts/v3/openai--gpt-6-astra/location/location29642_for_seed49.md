At first the city seems to be on the other side of the water. Then a wake lifts, and you see windows beneath it.

The water is shallow, warm, and gray-green, spread through a built landscape so extensive that maps describe it in weather regions. There is no proper shore. Roads descend by degrees into canals; canals broaden into flooded interchange yards; yards rise again as kilometers of paved shoal, ankle-deep at noon and dry by evening. Buildings stand on foundations wider than the buildings themselves, sharing their lower walls with locks, culverts, public baths, freight sluices, and chambers whose purpose has been forgotten but whose doors still open on schedule. From almost any doorway, you can touch water without stepping outside.

Everything is occupied. A gap between two warehouses contains a repair market, six staircases, a breeding colony of pale waterbirds, and a pressure main large enough to carry a ship. Beneath the market are sleeping rooms. Beneath those, shops facing an older street, now enclosed and kept dry by pumps. Beneath the street, water moves through the hollow ribs of the foundation. Even the spaces inside walls have traffic: inspection animals, cables that change thickness with demand, small maintenance devices moving in files along warm ceramic rails.

Yet the city stays low. Most roofs are only a few stories above the water, and from them the world appears almost level: a packed expanse of pale masonry, wet metal, moving awnings, steam, and intersecting wakes. Taller structures are usually weather machinery. Their broad, perforated crowns turn slowly above the districts, combing charge out of storm fronts or drawing saturated air down into cooling wells. They are far enough apart to disappear in the haze, but close enough that their low notes overlap.

The noise is immediate and bodily.

Water pounds through gates below the pavement. Freight hulls knock against bumpers. Ventilation mouths the size of courtyards exhale across the canals, flattening the waves in long strips. Thousands of smaller sounds ride on these: shutters hammering, shellfish crackling in exposed drains, vendors calling through throat amplifiers, birds shrieking as a barge pushes them from a feeding ledge. Somewhere nearby, always, a slab is being lifted or a channel is being cleared. Conversations happen close to the ear. People pause unconsciously between syllables when the pressure bells sound.

The bells are not especially loud by local standards. You feel them through your shoes.

Much of the construction is old. Steps have shallow basins worn into them by feet and rain. Mooring rings have been pulled into ovals. Along the lower walls, the stone has softened into rounded shoulders, polished by suspended grit and by generations of animals rubbing against it at high water. New work follows these forms rather than replacing them. A fresh retaining wall arrives already curved to match the current; a bridge grows a thickened underside where the tide has begun to strike it.

The material looks like limestone until a damaged surface closes.

Repairs happen slowly enough to watch. A chipped corner beads with clear fluid, catches dust, and gathers a new skin overnight. A cracked arch can take months. While it heals, temporary ribs emerge from sockets in neighboring walls and brace it across the street. People hang laundry from them. Stalls migrate underneath. By the time the ribs withdraw, there may be petitions to keep them.

The city can rearrange its structure, but it must move its weight somewhere. Nothing simply vanishes. When a freight route is deepened, the displaced material passes through the foundations as a dense slurry and rises elsewhere as a terrace, a wall, or an inconvenient new hump in a familiar square. Notices are posted before major work, though the dates depend on pressure, salinity, and the behavior of districts hundreds of kilometers away.

This makes the ground restless without making it young. Its broad forms have persisted for ages: the same depressions collect water, the same long ridges carry the busiest streets. Within them, channels switch, partitions thicken, and alleys slowly change their width.

There are no trees. No vines soften the bridges, and no grass grows where masonry breaks. Roof gardens are arrangements of porous stone used to cool the rooms below. In the wetter districts, people cultivate sheets of cream-colored fungus in screened galleries, but from the street these look like folded cloth drying in the heat.

Life crowds the water instead.

Broad-backed animals graze the bacterial films on submerged walls, leaving clean arcs just below the surface. Their dark bodies collect around warm outfalls, packed flank to flank, rising occasionally to expose a blunt muzzle and a pair of wet breathing slits. Narrow fish shelter beneath them. Transparent scavengers work through the silt with dozens of jointed legs, nearly invisible until a passing shadow makes them contract.

The pale birds nest in masonry recesses above the ordinary flood line. They have short wings and large feet, better suited to hopping between pilings than to crossing open air. At a sluice opening, hundreds gather on handrails and cables. They know the machinery’s preliminaries: the change in vibration, the first sideways tug of foam. When the gate opens, they drop together into the churn.

Food begins in places where warm, mineral-rich water meets charged surfaces deep in the infrastructure. Dense microbial films grow there, harvested by animals and by the city’s inhabitants. Other colonies live in waste channels, filter galleries, and the wet undersides of heat exchangers. Nothing stays clean for long. The maintenance systems tolerate a precise thickness of living film, then pass a scraping pulse through the surface. For an hour afterward, the canals fill with feeding mouths.

By midday the heat settles low. The air smells of warm stone, salt, crowded bodies, cooking oil, and the metallic odor of recently opened conduits. Clothing clings. Condensation runs down shaded walls even when the paving in full sun is nearly too hot to touch. Light is ordinary and plentiful: white cloud, blue openings, the bright reflection off an unpainted roof. The strangeness is easier to overlook in this light. A load-bearing column subtly shifts its base while someone leans against it eating lunch.

There is almost no unobstructed distance at street level. Looking along a canal, you see stacked signs, bridges, ropes, vessels, balconies, suspended freight trays, and more doorways than you can count. The farthest visible object might be only two hundred meters away. Then a transport deck slides aside, and for a few seconds a straight water corridor opens toward the horizon. Beyond the local bustle lie district after district under separate rain showers. Lightning flickers above one. Another shines in sun. Far away, a bank of exhaust cloud is broad enough to cast an afternoon over a province of roofs.

Water circulates through the whole plain, but it is rarely still enough to behave like a lake. A vessel entering a lock sends a rise through side streets. Cooling operations draw a district down by half a meter. Rain falling beyond sight arrives beneath the pavement hours before the clouds themselves. Residents read stains and floating debris, but they also watch the animals. When the wall-grazers leave a canal, shopkeepers raise their lowest shelves.

Some streets flood every day. Their furniture is built accordingly. Tables latch to pillars. Market counters float within guide frames. Stair landings have mooring cleats beside the doorbells. Children learn where a familiar current becomes a dangerous one by putting their hands against grilles and feeling the pull.

Other floods are sudden enough to strip a room.

Then the city’s movements become visible all at once. Barriers heave out of the pavement, shedding old wrappers and sleeping crustaceans. Bridges lift with people still on them. A row of ground-floor shutters seals from one end to the other, each closure striking through the noise. Water hits a junction and rises into a standing ridge, brown on one side, silver on the other. Loose cargo spins in the trough. Birds scream overhead, unable to find a place to land.

A few minutes later the ridge collapses down a newly opened channel. People emerge to retrieve things. Rescue boats work against the remaining current. Someone begins hammering a bent door track straight. The market returns first on the upper landings, then on the stairs, following the falling water.

Toward evening, the upper air cools while the foundations continue to give off heat. Rain forms locally, sometimes beneath a larger clear sky. It falls hard over three or four districts, blurring roofs and filling gutters already full of traffic noise. The drops are warm at first. They strike the canals so densely that wakes lose their edges.

From a high maintenance platform, the rain reveals the size of the place. You can see showers advancing and dissolving across the built plain, their darker patches separated by gleaming expanses of habitation. There is no outer edge in view. The same low, crowded geometry continues until individual roofs disappear, then districts, then the distinction between structure and atmosphere.

Down below, lamps come on beneath awnings. They illuminate wet steps, baskets of shellfish, a bird asleep with its head buried under one wing. A repair crew waits for a wall to finish opening. People cross a footbridge carrying dinner in covered bowls, leaning into the hot wind from a ventilation shaft.

Under their feet, a gate begins to turn. The handrail trembles. Far along the canal, vessels shift against their ropes, one after another, before the change in water level can be seen.
