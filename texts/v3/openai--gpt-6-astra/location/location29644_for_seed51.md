The ground is low enough that rain has difficulty leaving.

It gathers first in the shallow prints between roots, then in long, grass-filled depressions, then in channels so broad and slow that their movement is visible only where a fallen leaf presses against a reed. Across the whole basin, water lies within a hand’s breadth of the surface. The apparently dry places tremble underfoot. A dark skin of soil rests on soaked peat, and beneath the peat are pale, rounded shelves of stone, worn into shapes like knuckles by an erosion that has had time to finish its work.

There are no sharp ridges on the horizon. Low wooded backs divide one wet reach from another. Beyond them, more reeds, more woodland, more expanses of dull pewter water continue into a distance crowded with rain.

The sky is close and dim. Its clouds have separate undersides: a low torn layer moving east, a smooth dark ceiling holding still, and, visible through occasional rents, a higher brightness without a discernible sun. Cool air collects beneath the trees. On open water the wind has enough force to flatten rushes in widening patches, but in the hollows even a breath seems to linger, condensing on fern tips and the fine hairs of unopened leaves.

Everything drips. Nothing drips alone.

The sound comes from too many distances at once: water knocking inside hollow roots, broad sheets of rain advancing through sedge, the coarse hiss of leaves rubbed together by wind. Under these is a deeper noise, continuous but uneven, like surf heard through several miles of forest. There is no sea nearby. The noise comes from the stone beneath the basin. At intervals it gathers into a rolling concussion, strong enough to make droplets jump from the undersides of branches.

Afterward, the channels run a little faster.

The trees grow in crowded islands. Their trunks are short, thickened at the base, and clothed in overlapping mosses whose greens are nearly black in the wet. Most lean. Some have rooted again where their lower branches touched the mud, forming groves that are single organisms spread around pools of their own making. Ferns occupy every raised patch. Long, translucent grasses grow out of the water, their blades faintly striped with silver. There are low shrubs bearing waxy leaves the color of old copper, and pale fungi that appear along the waterline after each prolonged vibration from below.

For all this growth, there is room to see a long way. The open channels are wide, the trees rarely tall. From a mound of roots, the basin unfolds in overlapping bands: black woodland, silver reeds, low mist, a lake, another wooded rise. Weather can be watched arriving from several miles away. It darkens one band after another.

Sometimes it arrives out of order.

A distant stand of reeds begins thrashing while everything between it and the approaching squall remains still. Rain stipples a pool for half a minute before any drops become visible above it. Then the missing weather catches up, filling the intervening air with water and wind. The discrepancy is usually small. Moss remains attached to bark; the roots hold; the reeds rise again. There are places along the margins of the larger lakes where the discrepancies have persisted long enough to shape the vegetation. Trees grow flattened on the side from which tomorrow’s wind most often comes.

The pale bedrock is exposed in a few places, mostly along the outer curves of channels. Water has polished it smooth without removing the fine structure inside it. Kneel close and the stone shows layers of minute branching cavities, repeated at successively smaller scales until the eye loses them. Each cavity contains a bead of water. Each bead holds a point of light, even under an overhang where almost no daylight reaches.

The points change together.

A disturbance travels through them faster than anything visible could travel through the rock: a cold blue alteration spreading across one exposed shelf, answering from another bank, flickering beneath the clear shallows half a mile away. Then the water level changes. A drowned root emerges. A pool rises around a fern without spilling over its rim. Farther out, a patch of lake becomes perfectly smooth while rain continues to strike it, each impact disappearing without a ring.

Whatever is happening here works below the scale of tools and above the scale of any single organism. It occurs in mineral pores, films on roots, suspended grains of silt. The basin’s water carries arrangements too small to see, able to pass through solid matter under conditions that recur with the weather. They exchange heat between separated pools. They relieve pressure beneath the peat. They alter, briefly and locally, which direction a surface slopes.

There is no building that contains the process, no exposed mechanism, no boundary between functioning material and ordinary ground. The stone has weathered while retaining it. Plants have grown through it and adapted to it. Whether it began as an intervention or has always belonged to the basin is no longer something the landscape makes easy to tell.

At the center lies a shallow lake filled with standing deadwood.

The trunks have been softened by centuries of wet seasons. Their tops are rounded, their bark long gone, their bases hidden in dark water. Between them hang curtains of a rootless plant, green-brown threads held just beneath the surface. The lake is loud. Small waves slap the timber; rain rasps over the open reaches; from below comes a periodic, cavernous rush.

When that rush begins, reflections loosen from the things they reflect.

A dead trunk’s image drifts sideways through the water, drawing a narrow wake. Clouds slide beneath the lake in a different direction from the clouds above. The reflected woodland advances toward the center until its dark boughs overlap the standing timber. Looking down from the bank, one sees a much more densely wooded shore than the one that exists around the lake.

During these intervals, the actual trees lose some of their contrast. Their edges grow pale. A branch against the sky becomes difficult to follow, though rain still strikes it and runs along its underside. The effect lasts a few breaths or most of an afternoon. Eventually the deep rushing diminishes, the reflections settle, and the far shore becomes solidly dark again.

Few animals are visible. Once in a long while a blunt-headed swimmer crosses a channel, showing two wet eyes and a ridge of fur before slipping beneath a root mass. Small burrowing creatures leave fresh heaps of mud on the higher islands. Their tracks tend to stop before exposed stone, even when crossing it would shorten the journey.

Birds visit, but do not gather. A single gray wader may stand for hours at a pool, lifting each foot slowly from the sucking bottom. When the blue changes begin inside the rock, it steps onto a root and waits.

The abundance here belongs chiefly to things that stay attached. Roots knit bank to bank beneath narrow channels. Liverworts grow in thick, glossy shelves. Algae form layered veils in pools, each veil occupying its own depth, its own temperature, sometimes its own rate of movement. Near the central lake, fallen leaves can be seen descending through several such layers, slowing in one, turning edgewise in another, coming almost to rest above the bottom. New leaves fall on top of them. The lowest remain green long after the trees that shed them have died.

By late afternoon, the clouds lower until the wooded rises are cut off at their shoulders. The light becomes greenish beneath the canopy and faintly violet over the open water. Cold gathers in the sleeves and behind the knees. Rain dwindles to a mist too fine to hear, but the basin remains loud.

Water is moving through the buried stone.

It presses upward in clear domes among the roots, runs in bright threads across moss, and lifts entire rafts of vegetation a fraction above their usual level. Across the lake, the dead trunks begin to hum. The sound is low and rough, each piece of timber giving it a slightly different weight. It passes through the soles of the feet before it seems to reach the ears.

Then, far out in the reeds, a long strip of water rises.

It lifts only a little, scarcely higher than the surrounding grass, and holds there with its mud-dark underside exposed. Fine roots trail from it. Rain dimples its upper surface. Beneath it, in the narrow sheltered space, the flattened sedges slowly straighten.

Along the whole length of the raised channel, small streams pour from the banks into the air and curve upward to join it. The movement continues for several minutes. Then the stone gives another deep report, the water lowers gently into its bed, and the reeds disappear beneath the current again.

Mist closes over the place. Farther off, beyond the next wooded rise, the same sound begins.
