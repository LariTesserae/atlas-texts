The first thing visible is a flight of steps, wider than a road, descending into green darkness. The upper treads are pale enough to catch the little light that comes through the water. Farther down, they become a rhythm of edges, then only an expectation.

Nothing walks on them. A current pours uphill.

It brings white grit, fragments of shell, and small transparent animals folded tight against themselves. Where the steps meet a landing, the water divides around a stone post worn almost spherical. One stream goes left through an arch. The other lifts abruptly, carrying its grit into the dim space overhead. For a moment the whole stair disappears behind a standing cloud. Then the cloud stretches thin, and the steps are there again, changed by a few grains.

This is the highest quarter of a drowned place. Its lower districts lie beyond the light.

Across several miles, broad terraces occupy the summit of a submerged ridge. There are courts, retaining walls, long raised ways, depressions bounded by parapets, and towers reduced to low, blunt stumps. The arrangement can still be followed, though almost nothing keeps its original outline. Corners have become shoulders. Openings have widened into saddles. On exposed walls, the joints are shallower than the channels made by generations of small animals passing over the stone.

From a distance it resembles an old limestone upland. Only when a sheet of sediment pulls away do the regular courses show.

There is no bedrock beneath the buildings. The ridge itself was laid.

At its broken margins, the place reveals layer after layer of fitted blocks: enormous pale ones below, smaller dark ones above, then pale again. A gulf opens along the eastern side, where part of the summit has fallen away. Looking into it, one sees terraces built upon terraces, walls buried inside walls, chambers packed solid with rubble before another floor was spread over them. Even the loose gravel in the hollows is the worn-down substance of construction. Every pebble was once part of something joined to something else.

No tools remain. There are no working gates, no lamps, no moving mechanisms. Whatever made the place is absent so completely that its work has become the only available geology.

The water is cold enough that movement never seems casual. Fish hold themselves rigid behind the lee of pillars, adjusting a fin by almost nothing. Crabs wedge into seams. Long, colorless worms withdraw at the change in pressure that precedes a surge. Above the terraces, the open water is full of suspended flecks, but there is no green growth on the stone, no trailing weed to make the current visible. The surfaces are bare, ivory, ash-gray, or black where a very thin living film has settled into sheltered pores.

Life gathers in the small differences between one exposure and another.

On the uphill face of a wall, there may be nothing larger than a pinhead. Behind it, an entire recess is occupied by pale anemones, each taking what the water fails to carry past. Mussels thicken the underside of a lintel. Brittle stars crowd a crack so closely that their arms resemble a bundle of fine roots, though there are no roots here. A flat fish rests in a dust-filled basin until the basin empties. Then it lifts with the dust, almost indistinguishable from it, and settles elsewhere.

The larger animals stay apart. A gray ray moves over a courtyard, following the relief of the paving at a constant hand’s breadth. Two broad-headed fish wait beneath opposite ends of a fallen arch. Once in a while a seal comes down from the brighter water above, turns among the columns with an ease the fish do not possess, and goes up again. Its passage makes the depth suddenly legible. There is air somewhere, far overhead, and weather.

The weather reaches this place as force.

Storms crossing the sea bend long, heavy waves over the summit. Below them, the water swings first one way and then the other. The old streets take that motion and sharpen it. A broad, slow displacement becomes a race between two walls; a race becomes a descending jet at the mouth of a stairwell. Water forced through a low doorway emerges fast enough to keep the paving beyond it clean while everything on either side lies under silt.

There is scarcely a moment when the entire landscape moves in the same direction.

At one end of a court, shell fragments creep east. At the other, a veil of sediment streams west. In the center, a vertical coil lifts a small crab off the bottom. It spreads its legs, tumbles twice, and catches a projection on the wall. Above it, fish have already turned to face a new current that has not yet reached the floor.

Much of this happens quietly.

There is a continuous low weight of sound, more felt than heard. Close by, grit makes a dry, intimate rasp over stone. Loose shells click in a hollow. Now and then a buried slab shifts with a deep knock that seems to come from everywhere at once. The water carries the sound far beyond the movement that made it.

The loudest thing may be a pebble rattling inside a hollow pillar. It rolls around the cavity with each reversal, tapping out an irregular sequence. For hours it can be heard along the adjoining passage. Then one surge lifts it through a worn opening, and the passage returns to its low hush.

The place is ancient, but it is not still. Its large forms have survived because they have become difficult to damage: squat, rounded, broadly supported, relieved of everything slender enough to break. The towers lost their upper stories long ago. The bridges that remain are almost solid embankments. Yet their surfaces are always being rearranged.

A channel can clear in a single turn of the water. A doorway can fill before the next.

There are rooms beneath the terraces in which pale sediment has stood undisturbed for an immense time. Their ceilings have worn thin from above. Occasionally a paving stone breaks through, and cold water enters a space it has not circulated through in ages. The first sign is a small white plume rising between the flags. Then the plume darkens. The surrounding floor begins to settle, each slab tilting toward the same point.

Animals arrive before the collapse is finished.

Little scavengers pick along the fresh edges. Fish turn repeatedly through the plume, taking whatever it brings out. For several days the new hollow may be the busiest place on the summit. Afterward, the flow shifts, rubble accumulates, and it becomes another shallow depression with rounded sides. From a distance there is no obvious wound.

Toward the western edge, the terraces grow broader and less enclosed. Here the last walls are worn down nearly level with the paving, and open water replaces the suggestion of streets. The summit ends in a long balustrade whose separate uprights have mostly merged with the base through their own fallen debris. Only three openings remain clear. Through them one can see the dark slope descending, its courses stepping away beyond sight.

The light is best here. It is a thin blue-gray illumination without warmth, enough to put a faint silver line along the back of a passing fish. When the surface far above clears, wavering brightness descends through the water in broad bands. A whole terrace appears, unexpectedly spacious. Hundreds of tiny shells shine from its joints. A shoal that seemed to be scattered debris turns together, and the open court briefly has a moving roof.

Then the light contracts.

Near the balustrade stands a low stone bowl, still fixed to its pedestal. Its lip has worn unevenly, and water enters through a notch on one side. Inside, a handful of white stones circles without stopping. They climb the outer curve, fall inward, separate, and climb again. The bowl has been hollowed deep by their motion. Some of the stones are almost perfectly round.

Beyond it, the current lifts a field of sediment off the paving. The old summit vanishes from west to east beneath its own dust. Fish withdraw into the gaps. The three openings in the balustrade become three darker patches, then disappear.

For a while there is only cold moving water, full of the place it is passing over.
