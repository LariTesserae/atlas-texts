The district occupies a shallow bowl in the city, scarcely deeper than the height of a doorway. You come down into it by six broad steps. At the top, the paving is almost dry; at the bottom, warm water covers the toes of your shoes.

There are only three streets here, arranged around a pumping court. They end at retaining walls, or pass beneath buildings through low, square tunnels. Everything is close enough to see, but the spaces between things are generous: a wide apron of flooded brick, a loading bay with nothing stored in it, a roofed platform facing an empty basin. The buildings stand apart, connected by pipes rather than continuous walls. Between them, the water holds their dark reflections with room to spare.

It is late afternoon, though the light has the depth and color of a storm at dusk. Smoke-colored glass roofs the court. Some panes have been replaced with sheet metal; others are so thickly coated in mineral scale that light arrives through them as a dull, greenish glow. High above, rain moves across the glass in bright sheets. Below, condensation gathers on the supporting ribs and falls in larger, slower drops.

The heat is immediate and intimate. It comes through the soles of your shoes. It rests inside your sleeves. A hand placed against a stone pier finds it warmer than the air.

Beneath the district runs a hot aquifer, and beneath that, something which gives the water its pressure. The pumps were built to manage the first. The old brickwork records several attempts to understand the second.

Every wall has a pale horizontal stripe about waist-high. Below it, bricks are black and glossy; above it, they are red, softened by decades of wet air. Iron doors have been cut into upper doorways. Windowsills serve as thresholds. There are staircases whose bottom flights have been removed, leaving landings suspended over the water, reached instead by narrow bridges from neighboring buildings. The district has been raised piecemeal without ever quite leaving its original floor.

The sound fills the court before any individual machine becomes visible.

A deep, continuous grinding comes from the pump house. Over it lies the hard chatter of a loose drive belt, the concussion of water entering a covered culvert, and a high electrical note that seems to collect beneath the glass roof. At intervals, a pressure valve opens. Its discharge overwhelms everything else, a white column striking a perforated plate until the whole platform shudders. Even when the valve closes, the ear keeps some of that brightness.

Conversation would have to take place close to someone’s head. There is nobody in the court just now.

Along the west wall, vegetation has taken possession of a strip no wider than a market stall. It grows with extravagant thickness inside that narrow allowance. Broad leaves overlap in slanting tiers. Ferns root in the mortar above them. Pale stems descend from a broken gutter, enter the floodwater, and become roots without any clear point of transition. The foliage is heavy enough to hide a disused staircase.

Elsewhere, individual plants occupy isolated pockets: a small tree in a cracked settling tank, a fan of leaves emerging from a drain, long green ribbons streaming behind a submerged grille. Their separation leaves the architecture legible. You can still see the whole width of the court and the low arch at its far end, where the outgoing water flashes copper beneath the bricks.

Nothing flits among the leaves. Nothing breaks the surface to breathe. There are no shells along the waterline, no nests in the rafters, no insects caught in the lamps. A sweet, fermented smell rises from fallen foliage, and beneath it the clean, chalky odor of hot stone. Decay proceeds in thick films and soft seams. An entire leaf can lose its substance while keeping its outline, becoming a transparent mesh that trembles in the current.

The pumps operate on a cycle longer than an hour and shorter than an afternoon. There are clocks in the control booth, but none has a minute hand. Each dial is divided into colored sectors, and the needles advance according to pressure.

As they approach the blue sector, water begins to rise through the court.

First it wells from the joints between paving bricks. Then the puddles join, and loose pieces of leaf turn slowly toward the drains. The machinery accelerates. A chain hanging beside the pump house tightens, lifting a row of iron shutters from their channels.

At the height of the cycle, the water rises farther than its basins should allow.

It retains the shape of them.

Above the rim of a square inspection well, a square column of dark water stands a handspan high, its corners softened but intact. Along an open gutter, the current runs several inches above the gutter walls. Where the pavement has sunk, a shallow pool lifts from the bricks, leaving a dim, dry space beneath it. Grit remains on the ground. The reflection of the roof rises with the water.

This is why the bridges have rails along their undersides. This is why the doors bear polished brass marks well above their locks.

For a few minutes, the district carries two distinct levels: the old floor, exposed in patches, and the elevated water following the routes it has worn over many years. The pumps pull at those routes. Suspended currents stretch toward intake mouths. Water bulges through grilles, gathering into clear ropes before the suction draws it in.

The plants respond before the pressure falls. Leaves rotate downward. Root curtains stiffen. In the settling tank, the little tree releases a scatter of fine yellow powder from its flowers; the powder lands on the raised surface and travels beneath a footbridge in a bright, coherent patch.

Then a heavy lever drops inside the pump house.

The water settles with a slap that runs from one end of the court to the other. Drains gulp. Shutters descend. A fresh brown trickle appears down the face of the retaining wall, following an old groove around the same projecting stone it has always avoided.

For a while, the machinery sounds almost quiet.

Heat continues to leave the floodwater. It clouds the lower windows and beads on the electrical conduits. Somewhere behind a wall, an automatic switch clicks repeatedly before catching. The lamps grow brighter, showing their filaments through amber glass.

At the steps, the water withdraws by one worn tread. Small ridges of mineral grit remain, parallel and precise. Each cycle leaves them a little higher, and each departure through the district scuffs them flat again.

Beyond the roof, rain keeps falling on the rest of the city. Here, under glass, the walls are beginning to sweat in preparation for the next rise.
