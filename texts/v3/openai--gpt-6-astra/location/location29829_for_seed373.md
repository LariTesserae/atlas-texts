The garden occupies a missing piece of the mountain.

From the ridge, it looks like a pale notch no wider than a village square: two walls of weathered stone, a floor too far below to see, and one narrow bridge crossing near the top. Beyond it, the high country continues in bare, rounded folds. There is no snow. Nothing shines with wetness. The cold has the clean, abrasive quality of a freshly broken mineral.

You hear the garden long before you see anything growing.

Its sound comes through the soles of your boots: a rapid hammering beneath a sustained bass note, interrupted by cracks so violent that your jaw closes involuntarily. On the last approach, loose grit dances uphill. A buckle left unsecured will beat itself against your coat until the metal wears through the cloth.

Then you reach the notch, and the noise opens around you.

Most of the garden is air.

The far wall stands perhaps sixty paces away, clearly visible in the ordinary afternoon light. Between you and it there is almost nothing: a few filaments, a scattering of winged things, one long green ribbon folding slowly through an emptiness large enough to hold a cathedral. You can look down for a long time without deciding where the floor begins. The walls retreat beneath their own ledges. Light reaches several pale shelves, then loses itself in a haze of airborne dust.

Everything alive has fastened itself to an edge.

Along the near wall, the vegetation is so lush that the stone disappears under overlapping fans, jointed stems, and broad sheets of translucent green. It grows out from the cliff in tiers. Some leaves are pleated like bellows; others hang in long, finely divided trains. Their colors deepen toward the roots: yellow-green at the tips, bottle-green in the folds, almost black where generations of growth have packed themselves into cracks.

There is no soil. Beneath the foliage lies a felt of silver rootlets, gripping individual mineral grains.

A broken leaf makes a dry snap. Its interior is layered, pearly, and faintly warm. No sap beads at the wound. The plants pass charge and fine mineral dust through their tissues; their flexible parts are made of something closer to living mica than flesh. They have been growing here longer than the bridge has existed, replacing themselves one wafer-thin layer at a time.

In quiet air, much of this growth would collapse.

There is almost never quiet air.

A gust rises from below and turns the entire near wall white. Thousands of leaves reverse at once, showing their reflective undersides. The long hanging trains lash upward. For a moment the garden seems to have been uprooted and thrown toward the sky, but every stem reaches its limit and holds.

The sound becomes unbearable.

Those broad leaves are sounding surfaces. The pleats open and close under pressure; the divided fronds comb the wind into tones. Air rushing through a stand of mature fans produces a roar with narrow, painfully distinct notes inside it. The opposite wall answers a fraction of a second later. Between them, the empty shaft fills with standing waves powerful enough to lift small stones.

You feel one pass across the bridge. Your chest tightens. Dust leaps from the parapet in a ruler-straight line, hangs there briefly, and falls.

The stone shows what this has done over time. Every exposed corner is rounded. The walls carry deep scallops, some small enough for a thumb, others large enough to shelter a house. Harder seams project as smooth ribs. There is no wreckage below each fresh gust, no impression that the mountain is newly being torn apart. The forms are old and settled. The garden has worn itself into a shape that can survive its weather.

Its inhabitants know the shape more precisely than anything with a map could.

At first, you mistake them for scraps of leaves. Then one turns against the wind.

It has six narrow wings surrounding a little dark body, and each wing bends independently. It climbs without beating them, slipping from one pressure surface to another. A dozen follow. They are everywhere once you learn to distinguish their movements: resting under fronds, clinging to root felt, crossing the open shaft in sudden, exact diagonals.

Most are smaller than a fingernail. Their abundance does not fill the space. A swarm can pass a few feet from your face and still seem like a handful of imperfections in the light.

They graze on the pale films that form along leaf edges. Others hunt the grazers. Long, jointed animals hang beneath the vegetation on paired hooks, unfolding nets of almost invisible hair when a favorable gust arrives. Flat white crawlers move against the stone in the sheltered scallops. In one recess, a colony of round-backed creatures has arranged itself in nested circles; the whole colony shifts a quarter turn whenever the bass note changes.

There are larger animals too, though you may wait an hour to recognize one.

What appears to be a strip of shadow beneath the far ledge detaches itself. It opens into a broad, thin body, perhaps the length of your arm, with a blunt head and a trailing fringe. It crosses half the gap in a single leisurely movement. Small flyers scatter ahead of it, each taking a different route through the invisible structure of the air.

The hunter misses them all. It folds, drops twenty feet, and catches something you never saw.

The bridge is the only obvious construction. Its deck is a narrow strip of dull ceramic, unsupported across the middle of the shaft. Close up, you can see that it is made of separate tiles with tiny gaps between them. Each tile remains exactly where it belongs while its neighbors tremble.

Along the parapet stand five squat posts. Three are bare. One has been engulfed by green fans. The fifth carries a smooth black socket, warm to the touch, with no visible controls.

When the wind becomes strongest, the gaps between the bridge tiles widen. The crossing grows several inches longer without either end moving.

Below the far end is a small shelter tucked into the cliff. Its doorway is low; its roof has the same rounded profile as the surrounding rock. Inside are two seats, a shallow shelf, and a square of transparent material set into the floor. Through it you can watch roots crawling over the shelter’s underside, their tips occasionally brightening with pinpoints of blue light.

The shelter does not exclude the sound. It takes the violence out of it.

Your teeth stop aching. You can hear your own breathing again. On the shelf, a fine layer of dust lies undisturbed even when the doorway is full of flying grit.

Whoever built this place understood the garden’s motion. Beneath the bridge and at intervals down the walls are small ceramic patches, easy to overlook among the pale minerals. They make no light and have no moving parts. Yet a falling leaf sometimes stops beside one, rotates, and departs sideways at the speed of a thrown stone. Around another, dust falls in a slow upward stream.

The patches appear to lend movement from one part of the shaft to another. A violent impact against the lower wall may become a gentle outward pressure beneath an overburdened branch. Somewhere far below, enormous forces are being exchanged through devices small enough to cover with a hand.

Whether the machinery sustains the garden or merely keeps the bridge intact is difficult to tell. Some patches are buried beneath centuries of root growth. Others have been deliberately kept clear by animals that scrape their surfaces with hard mouthparts.

Nothing here stays still long enough to serve as a view.

A bank of green growth opens, closes, and opens differently. Flyers pour out of a crevice, vanish beneath a ledge, return as a glittering diagonal. A brittle branch breaks loose and begins to fall; halfway down, a pressure wave catches it and flings it upward into daylight. Its leaves separate one by one. Before the bare stem leaves sight, tiny animals have landed on it.

For several seconds the pitch drops beyond hearing. The shaft seems suddenly silent, though the bridge shakes and your clothing pulls hard toward the gap.

In that interval, you notice a small plant growing inside the shelter doorway. It has only four leaves. Each is thickly ribbed, dark green, and no larger than your thumb. Sheltered from the great gusts, it moves in little jerks, harvesting the tremor of the wall.

A crawler emerges from beneath it, cleans a leaf with its front limbs, and retreats.

Then the next blast comes up from the unseen depths. The far wall flashes silver. The bridge lengthens under your feet. High overhead, a cloud of minute animals is thrown clear of the notch into the cold, bright afternoon, where they hang briefly above the bare mountain before steering themselves home.
