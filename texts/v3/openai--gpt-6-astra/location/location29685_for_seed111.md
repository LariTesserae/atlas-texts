The place is a split in the summit, narrow enough that its opposite walls almost meet overhead. From outside, if there were a higher place to look from, it would appear as a black notch in a pale, newly lifted mountain. Inside, it is large enough to hold weather.

The floor descends through three uneven chambers. None is quite a cave: each has a seam of open sky, though the seam bends with the rock and cannot always be found by looking straight up. At midday a little gray light reaches the upper chamber. Below that, light arrives reflected from wet surfaces, moving from one black slab to another until there is too little of it to show a hand. The lowest chamber remains dark even when the clouds outside are burning white.

Beyond the split, the mountain drops for a distance the eye cannot comfortably judge. There are other summits down there. Clouds obscure them most of the time, but occasionally a gap opens and reveals a ridge so far below that its snow looks like a pale deposit in a crack. The air on the outer lip is painfully thin. Within the chambers it seems easier to breathe, although there is no obvious reason it should be.

The stone has only recently broken.

Edges remain sharp enough to cut skin. Broad planes on one wall correspond to planes on the other, and a person standing between them could see how the mountain once fitted together. In places, crystals have been pulled apart rather than cleanly separated. Their broken fibers span the gap in white tangles, some no thicker than hair, others thick as wrists. Most have snapped under their own weight. A few still connect the walls.

These connections carry sound exceptionally well.

A small movement in the lowest chamber can be heard at the entrance as a deep, wooden knock. Water dropping onto a crystal strand makes a brief high note, followed several seconds later by a murmur from the stone. There is always that delay. The sound seems to go somewhere inside the mountain before returning.

Nothing here is rounded by long weathering. Even the gravel is fresh and angular, lying in heaps below fractures that show no staining. Yet among these heaps are hollows polished smooth, each about the size of a cupped hand. Something has occupied them for a very long time, or has occupied them very often.

At first the animals are mistaken for the materials of the place.

The soft-looking seams between slabs are bodies packed side by side. The pale specks on the ceiling are closed eyes. What appears to be a fringe of mineral growth beneath an overhang resolves, very slowly, into thousands of feet.

They do not scatter when disturbed. They tighten.

The most numerous are flat, six-limbed creatures with translucent backs and blunt heads tucked beneath their front plates. A large one would cover a palm. Most are smaller than a fingernail. They overlap in the fissures like scales, fitting around one another so closely that a whole wall may seem to flex when one of them changes position. Their blood, or whatever moves through their bodies, is dark violet. In the little available light it resembles another kind of shadow.

They feed on a gray film that grows where breath condenses on cold stone. There is not enough film to support them all, at least not by any familiar accounting. Some spend weeks with their mouths pressed to bare rock. Others cluster around freshly opened fractures, holding their heads inside as though waiting for something to emerge.

Larger animals live among them.

Long, pale grazers stand on ledges no wider than their narrow hooves. Their bodies are about the size of sheep, but their legs fold in an extra place, allowing them to lower themselves until their bellies lie flat against the rock. They have dense fur only along their spines. Elsewhere the skin is smooth and faintly blue, patterned with branching vessels.

Their faces are difficult to find. Both ends of the body taper, and both bear sensitive bristles. Only when one feeds does the distinction become clear: a small mouth opens sideways and removes a strip of lichen with a slow, precise movement.

There are perhaps thirty of these animals in the split. In such a confined place, that is a great many. Each has a ledge, a resting hollow, a route between chambers. They pass one another by compressing themselves into shapes that seem uncomfortable, then remain motionless until their ribs have returned to their usual spacing.

Their movements account for some of the deep knocking.

Above them hang colonies of furred animals, each no larger than a closed fist. They grip the crystal tangles and one another. Their clusters are so dense that the interior animals cannot be seen at all. Occasionally a limb works outward, finds another hold, and draws a whole portion of the colony a few inches to one side. The shift takes several minutes. It sounds like a heavy cloth being dragged across dry leaves.

The colonies breathe together, but not continuously. A long silence is followed by a swelling hiss that fills the chamber. Moisture appears on the opposite wall. Then the breathing stops again.

This is the principal weather of the lower rooms.

There is some rain from outside. It enters as drifting grains and small cold drops, blown through the summit seam. Most catches on the upper ledges. A little travels downward along the freshly exposed planes, collecting in shallow basins where blocks have shifted apart.

These pools are dark, still, and crowded.

Thin animals lie just beneath the surface, their transparent bodies crossing and recrossing like loose fibers in water. Small black swimmers gather at the edges. Each carries a bright bubble beneath its abdomen, so that when the water is disturbed the pool becomes briefly full of moving silver points.

One basin, no wider than a sleeping body, is warm at the bottom. A faint current rises from a fracture beneath it. The warmth is slight, enough to keep the water from becoming bitterly cold, and every submerged surface bears a close growth of rust-colored filaments.

The few green things in the split survive near this basin and beneath the upper opening. There are stiff cushions of moss, a scattering of lichen, and three rooted plants with thick leaves folded lengthwise. They grow almost horizontally out of the wall. Their roots enter cracks too narrow for the smallest visible animals.

Once in a long while, a leaf unfolds. Its underside is silver and densely haired. The grazers wait below, but do not eat it. Instead they lick the drops that gather at its tip.

Nothing grows tall. There is no soil deep enough to hide a finger. What looks like earth in the ledge hollows is a mixture of shed skin, pulverized crystal, old fur, and the dry casings of animals too numerous to distinguish. It holds moisture well. When compressed by a hoof, it gives off a smell of cold mushrooms and something faintly metallic.

The split is loud without being busy.

There are clicks from the crowded seams, the sustained rasp of feeding mouths, the breathing of the hanging colonies. Beneath these lies a low pressure of sound, almost below hearing, that strengthens toward the deepest chamber. It can be felt in teeth and kneecaps. Sometimes it ceases, and the smaller sounds become startlingly distinct: a drop falling, a claw losing its hold, a grazer grinding its jaws.

During these pauses every animal turns toward the same wall.

The wall is unremarkable: dark stone, a few pale veins, one long diagonal fracture. Nothing visibly moves there. After a while the low sound resumes, and the animals return to feeding or resting.

Near its base, several flat stones lie in a row. They have not been laid by people. The pale grazers move them.

A grazer will take a pebble into its sideways mouth, carry it with extraordinary care, and set it against one of the larger stones. It then presses its head to the arrangement and waits. Most pebbles are eventually removed. A few remain for years.

When the mountain’s low sound changes pitch, these little assemblages rattle. Different ledges have different rattles. Before a fresh crack opens, the grazer whose stones sound first begins to move away; the others follow, slowly, squeezing past one another along their established paths. There is no sudden flight. The warning usually lasts long enough.

New fractures occur perhaps once in a season. The process begins with a clean smell and a patch of warmth. Then a line appears across a slab that seemed whole. Over several days it widens enough to accept a claw.

Animals enter almost immediately.

By the time a fragment finally drops, its underside is already inhabited. The fall may be only a few feet, but in the enclosed chambers the impact rolls and returns until it resembles a distant storm. Dust settles on fur, moss, pools. For an hour or two the creatures hold still beneath it. Then mouths and feet begin to work, and the stone gradually recovers its darkness.

There are very few empty surfaces. Even so, there are places the animals leave bare: a narrow oval on the lowest floor, the underside of a projecting crystal, a shelf beside the warm pool. These vacancies remain exact. Dust accumulates in them. Water passes through them. A creature approaching one will feel ahead with a foot or bristle and turn aside without haste.

The bare shelf is the only place where a visitor might sit without displacing anything.

From there, after the eyes have had a long time to adjust, the darkness separates into layers. The nearest wall becomes visible first, then the slow passage of pale bodies above the water, then the hanging masses overhead. Farther up, a small piece of sky appears between two sharp edges. It is usually starless, filled with cloud.

A drop gathers on the silver underside of a leaf. It grows so slowly that it seems complete long before it falls. Below, a grazer lifts its head.

All around it, the mountain continues to open by imperceptible amounts, making room.
