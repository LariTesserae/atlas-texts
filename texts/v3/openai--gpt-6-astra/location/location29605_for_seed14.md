The bottom becomes visible only when you are almost touching it.

Until then there is cold water crowded with sediment: gray flakes, grains of black sand, scraps of pale shell turning edge-on and disappearing. A light reaches a short distance into this mixture and seems to thicken it. Beyond the beam, the basin continues for several miles, but nothing offers a view of that distance. Its openness is something you learn by traveling. You can follow the same low slope for hours without encountering an edge.

This is a drowned plain beneath a polar shelf. The sea above it is deep enough to extinguish daylight, shallow enough in places for the largest storms to trouble the bottom. Narrow passages between submerged banks admit powerful tides. Water enters from several directions at once, carrying different loads of silt, and spends itself in a broad depression whose outlines have been worn almost beyond recognition.

There were harder distinctions here once. Ridges, separate outcrops, a channel with actual sides. Ice passed over them; gravel passed over them; water has continued the work. What remains is a country of low backs and shallow hollows. The exposed stone is dark, rounded, and close-grained. Even its fractures have softened lips. In places a hand could find no purchase anywhere on a boulder larger than a house.

Most of the stone is buried.

The upper layer of the seabed is scarcely a surface at all. It is a slow suspension, denser than the water above it, through which an object sinks before meeting anything that will hold its weight. Beneath that lies soft mud; beneath the mud, compacted clay; beneath the clay, old gravel pressed into the irregularities of the rock. These layers sometimes slide over one another. A current peels away the loose upper skin and reveals a firmer floor, scored with small channels. On the next tide the channels fill. A whole acre can shift downslope without uncovering a single new stone.

The movement is nearly continuous.

At the entrance to the basin, the flow stays low and fast. It pours across a worn sill and spreads into overlapping currents, some sliding along the bottom, others lifting into the water above. Where they meet, sediment rises in thick folds. A clear interval opens, showing a sweep of gray ground and the pale underside of a shell; then a cloud rolls through and erases both. Close to the bed, sand travels in short, abrupt runs. Finer material never seems to land.

There is no spray, no breaking crest, nothing that gives the turbulence a familiar shape. A mass of water turns within another mass of water. Its arrival is visible first in the particles: their leisurely descent becomes a diagonal rush, then a sideways streaming so uniform that the bottom appears to move.

Despite this, the basin is quiet. There is no surf down here. Occasionally gravel ticks against exposed rock. A low tremor passes through the bed when a heavier bank of sediment collapses, too deep to be heard clearly, more a pressure than a noise. The loudest events may be taking place far overhead, where wind is driving waves across water you cannot see.

Life occupies small exceptions.

On the lee side of an outcrop, several ivory sponges cling close to the stone. They are coated with fine gray dust except around their openings. A brittle star has worked most of its body into a crevice, leaving two narrow arms extended into the flow. When the sediment thickens, the arms withdraw. Farther into the basin, there are stretches where the only evidence of animals is a sparse scattering of holes, each surrounded by the faintest raised rim.

A fish sometimes appears just above the mud: blunt-headed, mottled, its fins making small corrections. It holds position behind a rise no higher than a knee. When the current turns, the shelter fails. The fish moves a few body lengths, settles behind another rise, and is lost in the gray.

There are no rooted plants on the plain. Fragments of weed arrive from shallower ground, blackened and folded by their passage. One catches on a stone and hangs there for part of a tide, trembling along its whole length. By the next passage of clear water it has gone.

The cold is consistent rather than dramatic. There is no frost here, no exposed ice crackling in the air. Everything has already taken on the temperature of the sea. Decay is slow. A small bone can lie among the pebbles for a long time, becoming less distinct through abrasion rather than rot. Shells lose their ridges, then their hinges, and finally survive as curved white grains that collect in shallow depressions.

Nothing has been built. There are no straight edges waiting beneath the mud, no buried boundary that would explain the plain at a human scale. Its apparent paths are strips scoured by current. Its terraces are slips in the sediment. A line of stones that looks deliberately spaced resolves, close up, into the remnants of a harder seam.

The safest-looking ground is not necessarily stable. Pale mud can be firm enough to resist a probing hand; darker sand beside it may stream away at a touch. A hollow can offer shelter during one tide and become the throat of a current during the next. Only the broad rock backs remain dependable, and even they disappear beneath passing deposits.

Over days, the floor is restless. Over much longer spans, it scarcely changes. The same material is lifted, sorted, carried across the basin, and laid down again. Storms redistribute what earlier storms left behind. The plain has already lost most of what could readily be broken from it.

Near the center, the relief becomes so slight that you can stop recognizing it as terrain. There is just the pale reach of the bottom, visible a little at a time, and above it the dark water fully occupied by things too small to see separately. A current clears a space. For a few seconds, several yards of ancient, almost level ground lie exposed.

Then the silt comes over them again.
