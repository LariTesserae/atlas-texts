The district occupies a hollow beneath the old canal, no wider than a few adjoining courtyards. Its highest roofs almost touch the underside of a stone bridge. Below them, five or six levels of rooms descend into green darkness, connected by stairwells full of water. The lowest floor has disappeared beneath a soft deposit of lime, leaf matter, and the rust that falls from the machinery overhead.

There is water in everything. It fills the cupboards and the unbroken lamp globes. It occupies the thickness of rotten doors. In the narrow alleys, it has the faint yellow cast of steeped herbs; in the deeper shafts it is clear enough to reveal a railing several floors below, then seems to thicken suddenly into darkness. Only beneath certain cornices are there pockets of air, silver-bottomed and scarcely larger than a hand. They have remained there long enough for their edges to acquire mineral rims.

The water is warm. A little warmer beside the pipes, a little cooler where it comes through the canal masonry, but nowhere cold enough to discourage lingering. There is no current you can see at first. A strand of root hanging across a doorway may take several minutes to move from one side of the jamb to the other.

Construction has used almost every available space. Narrow brick houses stand against the cylindrical bodies of pressure tanks. Their upper balconies have been enclosed, then built over again. Service passages cut through kitchens. A flanged pipe the thickness of a tree passes through three walls without changing direction, and each household once gave it a different accommodation: a tiled recess, a painted arch, a cupboard with its back removed. At one end of the district, an entire staircase winds around a vertical pump shaft. The treads are worn into shallow bowls, each now holding its own dark bed of silt.

Everything was fitted closely even before the plants arrived.

They grow most thickly near the bridge, where daylight enters through a long fracture between stones. The light is weak and green, sufficient for broad, translucent leaves that lie almost flat against the water above them. Their stems descend through roof tiles into the rooms below. Farther down are finer growths: ribbons along banisters, mossy coatings on porous brick, pale branching plants anchored in the seams of ceramic insulators. Some leaves have adapted to the outlines of the things they cover. A row along a window frame is rectangular, each blade filling one missing pane.

There are places where the vegetation has become structural. Roots cross a collapsed landing in a tight mesh, holding together a mat of plaster and broken tile. A small tree, entirely submerged, grows sideways from the wall of a former washhouse. Its wood is the color of soaked rope. Its crown fills the washhouse and extends through both doors, so that the room can no longer be entered without parting branches. Among the leaves hang the ceramic handles of a switchboard, still attached to their wires.

The machinery continues to work.

Deep inside the largest building, a wheel turns once every few hours. It has no visible paddles. Its broad iron rim passes through a slot in the floor and reappears clean, while the exposed portion bears a dense, velvet growth. Nearby, a bank of glass cylinders alternately fills and empties with something clearer than the surrounding water. Small lead weights rise inside them. When a weight reaches the top, a contact closes and a dull amber lamp comes on in a different part of the district.

The lamps are few. One illuminates a flight of steps ending in a wall. Another hangs above a loading hatch furred with roots. A third glows inside a locked cage that contains nothing except a stool. Their wiring runs in orderly bundles along the ceilings, disappearing into junction boxes sealed with black resin. The light barely reaches beyond its fixtures; it makes the leaves nearby look heavy and brown.

There is almost no sound. The pumps work through slow differences of pressure, with massive components moving on water-lubricated bearings. Occasionally a valve seats with a single padded knock. It can be felt through a railing farther away than it can be heard. Loose objects have long since found resting places. The shutters no longer swing. Even the chains hang in deep coatings of growth that keep one link from touching the next.

Only in the central square is there open water, a space about the size of a small chapel. Buildings lean over it from every side. Their balconies nearly meet above a public fountain whose basin is still distinguishable beneath the silt.

The fountain runs underwater.

A thin column rises from its central nozzle, visible because it carries a dusting of white mineral grains. It ascends slowly, bends at the height of an upstairs window, and spreads into a level plume. The surrounding plants lean toward it. On the nearest walls, their roots have left clean tracks through the surface grime.

This is what the machinery maintains: a small circulation inside a larger stillness. Water is drawn down through the buried floors, warmed somewhere beneath them, filtered through beds of crushed stone, and returned to the square. The district consumes almost nothing. Its systems have slowed as passages narrowed and bearings wore, settling into a pace that the roots can tolerate. Gauges still register these changes. Behind clouded glass, a needle rests just above zero.

Animal life is scarce enough to be memorable. A colorless shrimp lives in the bowl of a wall lamp, coming out when the filament cools. There are a few flat snails on the clean sides of the fountain. Once, in a passage between two tanks, a fish appears: short, dark, with a blunt forehead. It remains motionless beside an inspection plate, then backs into a hole too narrow to seem capable of receiving it. For a long time afterward, the passage feels occupied.

The strangest detail takes longer to notice. The plants around certain doors have been pressed aside. Silt is thinner in the middle of the stairs. Some handrails are bare along their undersides, where a hand would curl to pull a body downward through water.

There are no fresh marks, exactly. No footprints could survive here. But in an upper room, beyond a curtain of fine roots, a workbench has been kept clear. Three spanners lie in ascending order of size. A replacement valve sits in an open wooden box, its brass surface darkened but free of growth.

Above the bench, an amber lamp waits for its part of the cycle. When it comes on, the room brightens by scarcely any measure. The tools acquire edges. Behind the wall, something heavy begins its almost imperceptible turn.
