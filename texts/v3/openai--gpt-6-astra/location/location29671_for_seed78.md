The ground is alive, but there is very little of it.

It runs in long, narrow reaches through the dark: ribs of intergrown wood, some wide enough to carry a migrating herd, others dwindling to a single root across a gulf. Between them are distances that swallow falling objects without returning a sound. The nearest slopes may be visible as a slight obstruction of the stars. Farther ones are known only by the weather arriving from them, carrying pollen, splinters, or the bitter smell of torn green tissue.

This is the upper growth of something continental. No one who lives here has seen the soil.

Underfoot, the surface resembles a young mountain ridge. There are sharp crests, abrupt saddles, and pale fractures that have not yet darkened. But the crests are seams where woody plates have pushed together. The saddles are places where one growing limb has bent beneath another. Along a fracture, tightly packed fibers gleam faintly with sap. A cut made in the walking surface reveals no stone, only layers of bark, insulating pith, and deep, tensioned grain.

Even the loose grit is vegetable: seed husks, brittle scales, frost-shattered knots. It crunches like gravel. In sheltered places it accumulates around the feet of small shrubs whose roots penetrate the living ridge. Their leaves are black, rolled into needles against the cold. Most are no taller than a hand. Each may have been there for centuries.

Beyond them, the air is almost empty.

A branch the breadth of a valley might extend for days of walking before dividing. Its next neighbor might lie a hundred miles away. There is nothing to fill the interval but darkness, a few traveling animals, and wind. The enormous system supports its inhabitants on a minute fraction of the space it occupies. Life is everywhere one can stand, and almost nowhere else.

The cold is dry enough to split a lip before it numbs it. Moisture arrives irregularly. Sometimes a low bank of cloud catches on the underside of a reach and stays there, combed into streamers by hanging roots. Frost builds on the windward fibers. When the branch flexes, it sheds white curtains into the abyss.

Sometimes the weather comes as rain, though up here it is usually a brief ticking of frozen beads. They lodge in bark furrows and persist for weeks. Animals learn the sound and come out from shelter, licking at seams before the water disappears into the plant.

There are ponds, but they occur singly and are guarded.

A pond forms where a broad fork cups upward and the living surface has sealed itself with wax. Its water is dark, shallow, and bitter with resin. Through its clear ice one can see white roots spread like fingers along the bottom. They withdraw slowly when the pond is full and swell when it is low. In the worst cold, the plant draws almost everything down, leaving an empty ice shell that breaks under the feet of thirsty visitors.

People test such places with poles.

Their settlements are small enough to miss even when passing close by: five or six shelters in the lee of a rising knot, each roofed with overlapping bark and tied down with braided root. Smoke leaves through narrow vents and is pulled flat at once. There is little spare fuel. A cooking fire burns dry rind shaved from designated places, never the soft inner growth. The ridges close small wounds well enough, but repeated cutting can start a split that runs farther than anyone intends.

A shelter floor is not fastened permanently. Its occupants wedge it level each season as the supporting wood swells and twists. Doorways change shape. A house left empty for a generation may be found with its back wall folded inward, its roof lifted intact on a new shoot.

The few old paths are marked with holes rather than cairns. Travelers feel for them with the hooked ends of walking sticks when the dark is complete. Three holes close together mean a division ahead. A long groove means lie down: a low crossing, an exposed crest, or a place where the wind comes upward.

There are stretches where everybody crawls.

The wind is the great noise here. It does not blow evenly across the growth. It gathers in the open volumes, meets an inclined branch, and rises with enough force to peel away dead bark in pieces larger than roofs. The fragments turn slowly, catch another current, and vanish. Some travel for days. A settlement may hear the slap and scrape of one passing overhead without ever seeing it.

Hollow stems make deeper sounds. Air enters breaks far below and travels through chambers lined with dry membranes. One reach drones. Another answers in a wavering note. At times the whole horizon seems to be blowing across the mouths of bottles.

Under that is the working of the wood: knocks, short reports, a drawn-out creaking that can continue all night. The load is always changing. Ice collects. Herds move. A lateral branch opens new leaves over several miles, taking the wind differently, and the strain passes through the surrounding growth.

The inhabitants distinguish these noises carefully. A crack that ends cleanly is usually harmless. A crack followed by a soft, repeated ticking means fibers are parting one after another. Then people take water, tools, children, and move toward the trunkward side of the nearest fork.

Sometimes they have hours.

The animals are numerous, though the distances keep them from seeming so. A traveler may walk three days seeing only claw marks, then reach a sheltered hollow crowded with bodies.

The common grazers have six low-set limbs and dense, dull pelts. They crop the winter buds along ridge margins, bracing four feet while the other two pull a twig within reach. Their hooves open slightly around a lip of bark. From a distance a herd looks like a patch of coarse fur moving along the skyline.

They keep to routes worn shallow by generations of passage, but the routes do not remain still. A feeding shelf tilts. A new spur grows into a gap. An old bridge dies back and becomes treacherously light. At the front of a herd, animals pause for a long time with their chins against the ground, listening or feeling.

Predators follow beneath them.

These have broad paws, hooked claws, and long bodies that can lie flat against a branch’s underside. They climb through hanging fibers with very little noise. Their hunting places are narrow crossings where a grazer must expose its feet over the edge. In still weather one sometimes hears claws testing the wood below the path: a delicate, exploratory tapping.

Small life occupies everything that is not exposed. Beetles shelter inside buds. Pale burrowers chew galleries in dead pith, producing a fine dust that drifts from holes like flour. Moths emerge during the brief thaws, large-winged and almost colorless. They fly downwind until they encounter another reach, sometimes crossing an emptiness no human party would attempt.

The plants depend on these crossings. Most blossoms open beneath the branches, protected from frost and grazers. They are heavy, fleshy cups with warm interiors. Their scent rises through cracks in the walking surface, unexpectedly sweet amid the resin and cold. Moths crawl inside to feed and remain there through the worst weather. When they leave, their bodies carry a pale powder that can be seen on a hand even in the weak starlight.

There is light below, occasionally.

Not a general glow, and not enough to make the depths familiar. Here and there a flowering chamber shows through thin tissue, amber as a covered coal. A feeding animal disturbs a colony of small luminous insects and a brief constellation loosens from the underside of a bough. Far away, a line of cold green points marks fresh damage where sap has begun to sour.

From a high crest these signs only enlarge the dark. A light appears so far beneath one’s feet that it is difficult to believe it belongs to the same structure.

Above, the sky is clearer than it is bright. Stars shine hard between moving bands of cloud. On some nights a dim wash along the horizon reveals the land’s larger shape: an enormous crown of sparse, ascending growth, with subsidiary crowns emerging from it. Much of what looks like mountain is a single limb seen end-on. Much of what looks like cloud is foliage, very far away.

The great leaves are rare at this height. They grow at the tips of long stalks, each a dark blade ridged with stiff veins. A mature one may cover the area of a lake. Most stand nearly edge-on to the wind and are invisible until they turn.

When one does turn, it changes the weather.

Air falls from its cold surface. Nearby cloud is drawn sideways. Snow stored in its pleats pours off in a white line, and a low detonation travels through the growth as its stalk takes the new load. People hear it inside their shelters and wait for the answering gust. The herd animals spread their feet. Moths fold themselves into whatever crevice they can find.

New leaves unfold quickly, over a few nights. Their edges are still soft, their surfaces slick and almost black. By morning—what the inhabitants call morning, measured by warming sap rather than by any dependable sunrise—the leaf has stiffened. Its weight has lowered a branch, raised another, tightened an old crossing, and opened a new fracture somewhere out of sight.

This gives the country its rawness. There are ancient organisms here, but few ancient surfaces. The edges are fresh. The slopes have not been patiently rounded. A precipice may still smell of sap. A gorge may have opened last winter between two limbs that once lay pressed together.

In quiet intervals, people repair the paths with short bridges woven from flexible shoots. They set the shoots while they are living, bending and binding them until they grow into a usable lattice. The work takes years. The oldest bridges have swollen around the original knots, preserving the shape of vanished cord inside their grain.

At the far ends of inhabited reaches, one sometimes finds a single post, a water cup beneath it, and a bundle of replacement lashings wrapped against the frost. Nothing more is built there. These are waiting places. Travelers stop to listen for a herd on the opposite branch, or for the change in wind that permits a difficult crossing, or for the party that should have returned before the last thaw.

Around the post the bark is polished by hands.

Beyond it, the reach continues into a space so large that its next bend cannot be seen. Cold air streams steadily past. Somewhere ahead, wood breaks with a sound like a door struck shut. The vibration comes through the soles a little later.

Then the wind shifts, and out of the dark comes the smell of newly opened flowers.
