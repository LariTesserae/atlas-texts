The city occupies the broad shoulders of a mountain whose summit wore away long before anyone built there. Its districts stand on separate shelves of pale stone, with shallow basins between them. From an upper road you can see six or seven districts at once: a scatter of apartment blocks, a pumping station with three square towers, freight yards, the stepped wall of a reservoir. Farther out, similar structures diminish across the plateau until buildings and exposed rock become difficult to distinguish.

Everything has room around it. Even near the center, a warehouse may face its neighbor across an acre of wet paving. Streets widen into turning grounds, spillways, spaces apparently reserved for something that never arrived. Yet the place is unmistakably a city. There are numbered junctions, overhead power lines, public clocks, loading platforms, stairways with handrails polished by generations. The emptiness has addresses.

Water lies close beneath almost every surface.

It beads along cracks in the roads and collects in the shallow depressions left by wheels. It wells through the floors of abandoned shops. Where the mountain has been cut for a railway, the exposed rock weeps in parallel dark stripes, each ending in a small mineral lip. Beyond the buildings, the plateau is stippled with pools no deeper than a hand. Their bottoms are cream, rust-red, or a bruised violet according to what is dissolved there. The water itself is usually clear.

There is little mud. Most loose earth washed away centuries ago. What remains is rock, aggregate, fired brick, concrete, and the hard laminated material deposited by warm water. The city has been acquiring this last substance for so long that some of its oldest walls are thicker at the bottom by several feet. Doorways have narrowed. Ground-floor windows have become low, deep-set openings with rounded sills. A few buildings have been entered through their second stories for longer than anyone remembers.

The air is warm, including on days when cloud hides the sun. Heat comes through the soles of your shoes. Rain cools your face but not your hands if you rest them on a parapet.

And everywhere there is the sound.

At first it seems like a train passing very close, though no train is visible and the noise does not recede. Beneath that is the enormous low note of water under pressure. Above it are shriller layers: steam crossing a valve, a metallic vibration traveling through a railing, the hiss of a million small leaks. The sound fills the open spaces as thoroughly as water fills the lower streets. Distance does little to diminish it. Across a basin, a building half a mile away can look perfectly still while its windows flash with vibration.

The source is distributed through the mountain. Hot water rises through deep seams, enters buried chambers, and is led from there into the city’s pressure works. Some chambers are natural. Others were enlarged by miners whose access shafts now stand beneath generations of masonry. The water drives turbines, lifts freight cages, heats the factories, and emerges eventually into the cooling basins. Each district uses a slightly different pressure. Their great pipes sustain different notes.

On certain streets the notes almost agree. Standing there produces an ache behind the teeth.

The oldest people can tell which district they are in with their eyes shut, and which gates have been opened uphill. They notice changes that visitors cannot hear at all. A shopkeeper may pause while weighing a parcel and look toward the eastern works. Several minutes later, a warning lamp will begin to glow above the street.

Very little moves quickly enough to account for all this noise.

The freight lifts ascend with a slowness that makes them look suspended. Broad turbines turn inside dim halls, their exposed shafts scarcely faster than a person walking. Water advances over the spillways in thick, nearly unbroken sheets. On a distant causeway, a maintenance carriage takes most of the morning to pass between two towers.

The pressure has been accumulating far underground for years before it reaches a valve. Operations are planned in seasons. Opening one route changes the level of a reservoir two districts away the following week. An adjustment made too abruptly can crack foundations laid by someone’s great-grandparents, so the brass handwheels are fitted with locks that permit only a fraction of a turn each day.

People live within these intervals.

Their buildings have heavy outer walls and a second, separate set of rooms inside, mounted on blocks of dark resilient material. Between the two structures is a narrow passage where buckets, tools, and folded ladders are stored. Inside the inner rooms, speech is possible. Outside, people write on slates or use economical gestures made close to the chest. Greetings involve touching the back of another person’s hand. A raised arm is reserved for danger because it must remain recognizable across a wet square.

Cafés advertise quiet rather than warmth. Their doors close in pairs. A good one has a floor that does not tremble beneath a full cup.

From these interiors, the city can look gentle. Laundry hangs under glazed balconies. Water brightens the worn steps. Cloud shadows travel over the far terraces, revealing the substantial distance between one neighborhood and the next. On clear afternoons the light is ordinary, almost domestic: pale blue sky, white highlights on wet stone, the yellow side of a building catching the sun. Nothing in the view prepares you for opening a door.

Vegetation survives in a few specific places. There are wiry sedges beside the cooler drains, small gray plants in pockets of windblown grit, and dark films that spread across the sheltered backs of spillways. The hot mineral water is too severe for most roots. Trees grow only where people have brought in soil and protected it from seepage. Each courtyard tree stands in a raised tank, its drainage inspected as carefully as the district machinery.

From the upper roads, these trees are landmarks. There is a district known for three of them, although only two remain alive.

Animals are uncommon. Small, translucent insects gather around freshwater taps. Flat snails graze on the dark growth beneath bridges, their shells stained the same colors as the pipes. Now and then a bird crosses the plateau without landing. Its open beak and beating wings are visible, but nothing it does contributes to the audible world.

Along the outer escarpment, where the cooling water finally leaves the city, there are more signs of life. Little tracks run through damp mineral dust. Something nests in the dry cavities behind the falls. You find pale eggshells on inspection ledges, though the keepers of the lower works disagree about what lays them.

The falls themselves descend in a series of broad steps toward a hazy lowland. They have worn the mountain into immense rounded folds. Old retaining walls continue these curves so closely that their builders seem to have worked by adding a few courses wherever the stone was about to give way. In places, masonry has dissolved and re-formed downstream. Bricks emerge from the white deposits with their corners softened, still bearing a maker’s stamp.

Farther into the city, there are bridges over channels that no longer carry water. These are not demolished. Their foundations transmit pressure between the banks, and removing even a small one can alter the pitch of a whole street. The city keeps a registry of structures that must remain standing for reasons no longer visible.

Its strangest places lie beneath these dry bridges.

Here, fine spray sometimes hangs in the air without falling. It forms a level band at shoulder height: a few inches of droplets trembling in place, fed by no leak anyone can find. You can put a hand through and bring it out wet. Move the hand lower, and it remains dry. The bands are marked on maintenance maps, since they shift when the pressure system is changed. A stairway may acquire a permanently wet seventh step. An old passage may become usable again after forty years.

The residents treat this as another property of the mountain. There are ways to build around it.

Toward evening, workers leave the scattered halls and walk home along elevated paths. At each crossing, they wait for lamps rather than bells. Their clothes darken in the suspended damp beneath an aqueduct. Behind them, the works continue at almost the same rate.

The plateau loses its color gradually. Pools become patches of sky among the buildings. Lighted windows reveal small rooms within the vast construction: a person peeling fruit, someone repairing a sleeve, a child asleep against the inner wall.

Outside, warm water rises through the rock. It travels along channels rubbed smooth by centuries, pauses in broad basins, and spills over measured edges. The city shudders around it. On the lip of an unused fountain, a fresh bead of mineral is forming, too slowly for anyone watching to see.
