The district occupies a shallow hollow between two rows of old factory buildings. It is small enough that, from the central pumping house, you can see nearly all its limits: a brick retaining wall furred with green, the backs of three warehouses, a covered passage disappearing beneath a boiler hall. There is no distant view. Windows, pipes, leaves, and the undersides of walkways fill the space where a horizon might have been.

Water stands a few inches below the streets.

You see it through the seams in the paving, where each stone has a dark border, and through the iron inspection grilles set outside the doors. Below the grilles, a second version of the district trembles in black water: inverted railings, lamps drawn into long yellow threads, roots hanging down among the reflections. Some of the streets are still dry enough to cross. Others have become narrow canals with pavements along their sides, although the paving continues beneath them, complete with curbstones and pale crossing marks. A cart track enters one pool and emerges several yards farther on. Its rails are polished brown by the water.

Nothing here has collapsed recently. The walls lean by amounts long accommodated. Where one warehouse has settled, its door was cut shorter; where the street sank, another step was added. Rain has rounded the brickwork until the mortar stands forward in places, making the walls look woven. Drain outlets have worn little basins beneath themselves. The basins overflow into channels, and the channels have deepened just enough to carry what comes down them. Even the damage has a practiced route.

It is warm in the hollow, especially close to the ground. Heat seeps from buried mains and collects under the low roof of leaves and connecting bridges. The upper windows are cool and filmed with condensation; the basement windows sweat on the inside. There is a smell of wet clay, machine oil, and green stems bruised against warm metal.

Vegetation occupies the district by taking advantage of its fittings.

Ferns grow in the cups of disconnected pipe elbows. Broad, dark leaves spread from the gutters and rest against the glass of clerestory windows. A vine with thick, jointed stems follows the overhead cable trays, descending wherever a cable descends, so that the junction boxes wear hanging skirts of roots. Between two buildings, a planted strip only a yard wide has become a grove. Its trunks are slender and pale, pressed into oval sections where they have grown against the railings. Their foliage fills the alley from wall to wall. Beneath it, brick steps disappear into a soft accumulation of leaves.

The plants are not swallowing the machinery. There are places where they stop with remarkable precision. Moss fills a pump casing’s bolt recesses but leaves the moving shaft clear. Roots run along the foundations of the engine beds, thickening in the warmth, yet none passes beneath the feet that carry the greatest vibration. Every so often a leaf touches a drive belt and acquires a clean, dark edge. The same plant produces its next leaves an inch farther away.

The pumping house is the district’s largest room. Its doors have been removed, and its floor lies level with the street, so the interior feels like another courtyard, only roofed. Four pumps stand there on green-painted plinths. The paint has blistered into tiny islands; bare iron shows between them, black where it is oiled and orange where it is not. Broad belts rise into the dimness. Above them, a line shaft turns behind a mesh guard crowded with fern fronds.

The machinery is loud enough to make distance difficult to judge. A deep, continuous turning fills the hollow. Over it come the slower sounds: a valve seating with a heavy knock, water dragging air around a bend, the loose clatter of a guard panel. A pipe against the eastern wall gives a resonant note whenever pressure rises. The note lasts so long that you cease to hear it as an event. Then it stops, leaving an unexpected space in the noise.

Despite this, little seems to move.

The pumps turn at an unhurried rate. Their flywheels are so broad, and the light so poor, that you must watch a spoke pass a fixed point to be sure. Water climbs the glass gauges by the width of a fingernail, holds, and sinks again. A drop gathers at a flange for several minutes before falling. Out in the court, the surface of a flooded loading bay advances across a brick, darkening it from one end to the other.

There is no splash that cannot be traced to a drain. No wings pass beneath the bridges. The standing water has neither larvae nor the quick punctuation of small mouths breaking its surface. Leaves remain whole until age or machinery damages them. On a sheltered wall, pale flowers open against a hot-water pipe. Their pollen collects on the sill below in a fine, undisturbed stripe.

The district has no residents now, though it retains the close arrangements of a place where many people once worked within earshot of one another. A narrow stair climbs over a compressor room to a row of offices. There are hooks beneath the office windows, shelves beside the doors, a tiled recess for washing. A lunchroom projects over the canal on iron brackets. Through its fogged glass you can make out tables and a large-leaved plant growing from the floor around a table leg. The plant has lifted one side of the table gradually enough that the cups on it have merely slid together.

Electricity remains. Small lamps burn under enamel shades, casting dull pools of amber over doorways and gauge panels. Most are on even during the day. Above the hollow, the sky is a soft gray strip divided by bridges and laundry-like lengths of insulated cable. Sunlight occasionally reaches an upper wall, but by the time it arrives at street level it has passed through glass, steam, and several layers of foliage. Down here, a new leaf can be brighter than the sky.

At the lowest corner stands a square settling basin enclosed by a waist-high parapet. Water enters through three mouths in the brickwork, each flowing at a different depth. The basin’s surface is almost opaque, green-black under a powdering of mineral dust. Long leaves lie flat on it, attached to plants rooted in the submerged steps. Beneath them, machinery can be seen when the lamps catch it: a wheel, a chain, the pale face of a dial.

The dial is still working.

Its needle moves very slowly, although its casing is full of water. Other gauges throughout the district agree with it. Those in the pumping house, those screwed to the warm pipes, even the little instrument behind the lunchroom counter all rise and fall together. They have different scales. Some are marked in units, some only with red and white arcs. One has no marks at all. Yet they reach their highest points at the same hour.

That is when the walls begin to weep.

Moisture appears first around old repairs: a replaced brick, a plugged doorway, the outline of a pipe removed long ago. It thickens into beads and runs down the masonry. For a while, every building contributes water to the streets. The pumps deepen their note. The leaves beneath the gutters tilt under the increased flow.

Then the gauges fall, and the walls dry from the top down.

The cycle is too slow to feel like a malfunction. There are drain troughs beneath the wettest walls, and brass drip edges above the electrical cabinets. The oldest stonework carries the same tide marks as the newest concrete. Whatever brings the water out was understood well enough to build around.

In the covered passage beneath the boiler hall, the sound of the pumps is felt through the soles before it is heard. The ceiling is low, ribbed with pipes, and beaded with warm droplets. At its far end there is a locked gate. Beyond the gate, a short flight of steps rises toward a much drier street. Dust lies on the uppermost tread.

From that threshold, looking back, the hollow seems deeper than it did from within. The lamps repeat in the water. Bridges cross and recross between the buildings. Leaves conceal the feet of stairways and crowd the mouths of alleys. Somewhere under all of it, a valve opens with a long metallic groan, and the water along one curb begins to travel.

It moves no faster than a person could follow with a fingertip. A loose piece of fern stem turns beside a drain, rests briefly against the iron, then slips through. The gauge in the pumping house lowers a fraction. Warm water continues to run down a wall that has been rounded smooth by years of the same small flow.
