The ground falls so gently toward the pumping plain that water takes days to discover the slope. It lies first in shallow sheets, black except where a distant maintenance lamp draws a thin yellow line across it. Then it gathers along seams. The seams lead to gutters broad enough to walk in, the gutters to open conduits, and the conduits toward a depression several kilometers wide, where the old intake towers stand in the mist.

There is no exposed earth. Under the water is ceramic aggregate, under that a mesh of dull metal, under that more chambers, foundations, and load-bearing cavities. Even the low ridges on the horizon are structures. Their rounded profiles come from an immense duration of abrasion: rain, vibration, chemical seepage, the slow passage of maintenance machinery. Whatever angles they once had have been rubbed away. A stairway on one ridge has become a smooth incline with a faint change of color at each former step.

The whole region has reached a kind of architectural old age in which buildings resemble terrain without ceasing to function as buildings.

It is cold enough that a hand placed on a railing soon aches. Not freezing. The water must remain mobile, and somewhere beneath the plain heat is still being spent to ensure that it does. Warmth reaches the surface unevenly. Around certain inspection covers, vapor rises in low, restless fans; a meter away, condensation beads on a pipe with the clarity and chill of melted ice. Much of what looks like weather is the exchange between these systems. Mist thickens when the deep machinery opens its exhaust paths. Rain begins beneath overhead galleries long before it reaches the spaces between them.

There may be a sky above the upper works. From the plain it is difficult to tell. High in the darkness, long horizontal masses disappear into cloud, and occasional lights reveal their undersides: riveted plates, cable bundles, a hanging ladder too long to keep still. The light never reaches far enough to disclose what holds them up.

The sound is tremendous.

At first it seems to come from the intake towers, whose slotted walls breathe out a continuous roar. But standing behind a wall does not reduce it much. The deck beneath your feet is sounding too. So are the conduits, the hollow ridges, the wet rails. A low mechanical note passes through the soles and settles behind the teeth. Over it lies the white crash of moving water, and above that a harsh metallic flutter, as if a sheet of steel kilometers long were being shaken.

There are rhythms inside it. Every few minutes, a bank of valves closes somewhere out of sight. The impact crosses the plain as a succession of dull blows, arriving first through the floor, then through the air. Loose water jumps in its puddles. Drops fall from cables all at once. A little later, the towers take a deeper breath and the whole landscape seems to lean into its work.

A person could shout here without hearing the beginning or end of the shout. Communication, if anyone still crosses this district, is by lamps held close to the body or by a hand on an arm. The few surviving signs use raised symbols large enough to read through gloves. Most have been worn down to soft, ambiguous shapes.

The plain is open despite being wholly made. Between the towers are long reaches of flooded deck, empty basins, low service embankments. A solitary gantry stands so far from anything it might once have lifted that it looks less like equipment than a unit of distance. Beyond it are three circular settling fields, each bounded by a rim barely higher than the water. Farther still, a cluster of squat turbine housings makes a darker band against the dark.

Nothing here offers the quick reassurance of a human-sized room. The accessible doors are scarce, and many of the things that resemble doors are expansion joints or pressure plates. A low rectangular shelter might contain a stairwell. It might instead be the exposed crown of a machine whose body occupies the entire ground beneath you.

Its materials have aged differently. The ceramic paving has become satin-smooth, interrupted by islands of rough aggregate where its skin has failed. Bronze fittings are black and rounded. Iron persists in thick, laminated ribs, shedding flakes that collect in channels like brittle leaves. In protected recesses, old paint survives: a strip of cream, a blue circle, a number printed over another number. Out on the exposed surfaces, even corrosion has been worn into broad, stable shapes.

The water carries the evidence of all this slowly downhill. Pale mineral paste settles at the edges of pools. Metallic dust forms dark fans below drains. Some gutters have acquired banks made from the machinery’s own wear, and these banks support the sparse vegetation.

It grows low. Narrow gray blades emerge from the slurry in bundles no wider than a fist. A leathery green film clings to the warmer discharge lips, repeatedly submerged and exposed. Beneath one leaking flange is a mat of dark moss, dense in the center and failing at its cold edges. The plants do not spread across every available surface. They are restricted to little coincidences of warmth, water, and accumulated material. Most of the plain has only two of the three.

There are no birds on the railings, no insects over the pools, no small disturbances moving against the current. A long filament trembles underwater and proves to be a root. Something scrapes within a culvert and the scraping repeats with mechanical precision. The channels carry loose bolts, mineral clots, strips of gasket, but no shells or bones.

The living patches alter the place nevertheless. Roots enter a joint and hold its grit together. A green film narrows the mouth of a drain. Water backs up by a few millimeters, spills into another seam, begins polishing a surface that has been dry for centuries. In a system this old, such small obstructions have had time to redirect whole watersheds.

Maintenance continues.

On the far side of a settling field, a low machine advances along an embedded track. It has no cab and no lights facing outward. Its passage is visible as a moving bar of illumination beneath its chassis, where brushes and grinding heads touch the rim. It takes much of an hour to pass a single support. Behind it, the old surface is clean, wet, and slightly lower.

Other machines are almost indistinguishable from the structures they tend. A section of railing folds inward when a pressure front arrives, then lifts again after the water has passed. A gantry moves several centimeters during the night. Deep inside a ridge, a cutter follows a drainage route whose original lining has long since vanished, maintaining the route through successive layers of construction.

Repair here often means removal. Projecting edges are ground down before they can fracture. Uneven decks are planed to the lowest safe level. Choked channels are reopened along whatever course the water now favors. Over enormous spans of time, maintenance has helped make the smooth, low country it was meant to preserve.

There are places where this history can be read in a single exposed section. Along the wall of a deep overflow trench, cable ducts lie above a band of old flooring; beneath the flooring is a sealed window; beneath the window, another duct. The trench cuts through what must once have been several separate interiors. Its walls have been glazed against leakage, and water moves along their foot with the heavy, even speed of a freight belt.

At intervals, the current reverses.

The first sign is not in the trench but in the shallow pools nearby. Their surfaces draw tight, dim reflections stretching toward the drains. Then the gutters empty with startling speed. The exposed deck shines for a few seconds. Far below, something enormous changes load, adding a descending tone to the roar.

Water returns through different openings, clear at first, then cloudy with pale sediment. It rises without splashing until the plain is wet again. The gray plants bend the other way. A maintenance unit pauses with its grinding heads raised.

These reversals divide the place more reliably than day and night. Some surfaces are safe during the long outward draw and dangerous during the return. Certain causeways remain above water except for the few minutes following the third valve sequence. The physical rules are consistent, but their signals are buried in deafening noise and darkness. Old route markers stand on the higher seams, their bases polished into necks by repeated flooding.

Near the center of the depression is a broad, shallow basin without towers or visible machinery. Water enters it from all directions. No outlet can be seen. Its surface remains almost perfectly level while the surrounding channels surge and drain.

Crossing toward it, the vibration changes. The coarse flutter drops away. What remains is a pressure so deep that it is felt in the chest rather than heard. The water has polished the basin’s rim to a continuous black curve. Along that curve, spaced farther apart than seems useful, are small white lamps.

Most are still lit.

Their reflections extend inward across the basin, thin and unwavering despite the flow. In their light the bottom is visible for a little distance: smooth plates, overlapping, descending by gradual degrees. There is no silt here and no vegetation. Whatever settles is drawn between the plates.

From the rim, the plain behind you is a scattered arrangement of lights and low silhouettes, active over distances that are hard to judge. A valve impact lifts silver droplets from an unseen channel. The maintenance machine continues its slow circuit. Condensation drifts beneath the high works.

After several minutes, a joint opens in the basin floor. It is close enough to see clearly, a narrow dark separation between two plates. Water enters it without a whirlpool. The neighboring plates settle by a fraction, and a pale line appears along an edge that has not been exposed for a very long time.

Then the joint closes. Water covers the pale line. The lamps continue burning, and the great sound goes on.
