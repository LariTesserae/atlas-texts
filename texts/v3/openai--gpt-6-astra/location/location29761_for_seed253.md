The water begins far below the place where the mountains used to end.

Their summits are still down there, under several valleys’ depth of black water. Above them, held on columns of living wood too broad to take in at once, lies a second country: a high, submerged shelf of terraces, shallow basins, and long passages open at either end. There is no rock in it. What feels like ground is compressed root, pale beneath its skin of algae. The low ridges are bundles of stems. The cliffs are the exposed ends of growth laid down so long ago that its separate strands have fused.

Everything here has been made, though nothing was made with tools.

The first foundations were woven by animals scarcely larger than a hand. They pulled trailing roots into loops, fastened the loops with secretions, and laid their eggs inside. The roots continued growing. Other animals enlarged the shelters, joined one to another, raised their young on the undersides. Eventually the work outlived any single nest. Whole slopes accumulated from those small decisions: keep this opening clear, close that one, carry a flexible stem here.

The builders are still present. Most of what they do now is maintenance.

***

It is cold enough that a body loses warmth slowly and without relief. Not the cutting cold of ice water; something more patient. The water has the temperature of a deep spring, and it keeps that temperature inside the smallest hollow as well as across the broad avenues.

There is very little light. Looking upward, you may find a grayness that could be the surface, although it is much too far away to show waves. The highest branches disappear into it. At this elevation, so far above the buried earth, there ought to be exposure: wind, white glare, the smallness of things seen from a summit. Instead there is the weight of water overhead, and the sense that you have arrived at a great height inside a closed room.

The distances are considerable. Across one basin stands a single column, broad at its foot and narrowing into darkness. Beyond it, after a long interval, another. A terrace may extend for the length of a town without bearing anything taller than a low mat of leaves.

The place is wholly vegetable, but it is not crowded with trunks. Its living mass has gone into floors, ceilings, supports. Between these are enormous clear volumes of water.

Along the terrace edges, leaves lie flat and overlap like the scales of a fish. They are dark olive where there is enough light to distinguish color, black elsewhere. Some are broader than a person. Their margins are worn smooth by generations of grazing mouths. Underneath, fine rootlets hang in short, evenly cropped fur. Small animals move through this fur continuously, making it lift and settle without changing its outline.

Farther from the dim upper water, the plants have almost no leaves. They grow thick skins and fleshy seams. Their nourishment arrives in the current, in the droppings of animals, in material caught and digested by the pale films living inside their tissues. Very little is lost. A loose fragment does not drift far before something finds it.

***

The architecture has no sharp edges left.

There are doorways, though that is only what their proportions suggest. Their sills have been rubbed into shallow saddles. There are chambers with roofs so smoothly hollowed that they resemble the interiors of old seed cases. Long depressions cross the floors where currents have passed for ages, wearing the living surface away at almost exactly the rate it replaces itself.

In places, an older pattern shows through: a row of arches half closed by growth, a ribbed wall beneath a newer skin. But even these remnants are rounded. Nothing looks recently broken. Nothing looks unfinished.

On the outer rim, the shelf thins into broad, blunt lobes. You can swim beneath one and look out into water without a visible boundary. The supporting columns descend past sight. They are not vertical all the way down; some lean, some merge, some send immense braces sideways into the dark. Their arrangement makes the height palpable. This is a country with a bottom, but the bottom is elsewhere.

The outer faces are polished by a current too steady to see.

You hear it.

A deep, continuous roar travels through the structure. Water is being drawn through thousands of narrow passages in the rootwork, and each passage contributes its own rough note. Close to a wall, the sound gains texture: a throaty rushing, a faint knocking, the scrape of hard mouths against wood. In the open basins these merge into something like heavy rain heard from beneath a thick roof.

There is no answering silence. Even a sheltered chamber has the low vibration of the whole shelf passing through its floor.

***

Animals are abundant, though you seldom see many at once.

Long fish hold themselves under the terrace lips, their pale bellies turned toward the living ceiling. Flat grazers move over the dark leaves. Transparent shrimp occupy cracks by the thousands, visible chiefly when their eyes catch the little light available. Burrowing animals push pellets out of holes in the floor; smaller animals arrive to take the pellets away.

Occasionally a broad, slow swimmer crosses an open basin. Its fins barely move. It follows a route between columns so established that the vegetation along the route has grown clear of it.

The little builders work inside the walls. They have soft bodies, gripping feet, and blunt jaws capable of bending young roots without severing them. When a seam loosens, they gather there. When an opening narrows, they graze its edge. Their nests occupy only a fraction of the spaces they preserve. Water must pass through the rest.

There are eggs, hungry mouths, deaths in the root fur. Yet from a distance the place holds absolutely still.

A leaf is eaten and replaced. A passage is scraped and regrown. The same amount of water moves through the same openings, carrying the same muted load of food. No storm reaches this depth. No season alters the temperature. Even the loudness is constant enough that, after a while, a small nearby scrape becomes more noticeable than the encompassing roar.

You could return to a basin after a lifetime and find the lone column standing at the same distance, its foot surrounded by the same shallow depression. The animals resting beneath the ledge would be descendants of the ones you remembered. The ledge itself would contain almost none of its former substance.

Your hand would still fit the worn hollow on its edge.
