The first impression is of an enormous green floor lifted into the light.

It occupies a broad basin high in the mountains, several miles across, with low shoulders rising around it and a gap at the far end through which nothing is visible but brightness. The lower valleys are under cloud. Up here, the sun has cleared the weather, and every wet surface answers it: grass blades, puddles, the zinc roof of a distant shed. Even the undersides of the clouds are luminous. You have to narrow your eyes to see the ground.

There are almost no trees. A few willows stand in sheltered folds, scarcely taller than a person’s knee, their branches growing sideways through one another. The openness is immense, but there is very little bare earth. Moss, sedge, rush, and close-packed alpine herbs cover the basin continuously. They climb the low rises and run down into the channels. In the shallows they continue underwater, paler and more loosely arranged, waving so slightly that you have to watch for a while to see them move.

From a distance the vegetation looks like a single surface. Underfoot it becomes complicated.

There are firm cushions with tiny red stems, slick lanes of flattened grass, bulges of pale moss that retain the shape of a boot for several seconds. Water shines in the print, then the stems begin to lift. Some places carry your weight without yielding. Others settle under both feet together, gently enough to be mistaken for dizziness. A few narrow pools have no visible banks at all: the living carpet simply thins until the dark water shows through.

This is young ground. Along the newest margins, roots have knitted themselves across what was open water a few decades ago. Sediment catches in them. Dead stems accumulate beneath the living ones, making the first soft thickness of peat. The basin is acquiring a floor from above.

Its channels are still shallow and undecided. They divide around a tussock, rejoin, spread into a sheet no deeper than a fingernail. There are no cut ravines, no exposed ribs of weathered stone. Where a stream meets a dense mat of roots, it usually goes around. Sometimes it goes underneath and reappears twenty yards away as a patch of trembling brightness. Water is everywhere near the surface, seldom in a hurry.

The air is cold enough that wet fingers soon become clumsy. In shade, the grass still holds the morning’s chill. Out in the sun, your coat warms across the shoulders while your boots remain cold. There is a clean, faintly sour smell from the saturated plants, stronger wherever the ground has been pressed.

And over all of it is a roar.

At first it is hard to locate. It seems to belong to the basin itself, like wind heard from inside a building, except that the rushes are nearly still. You can see their seed heads standing upright. You can see a scrap of mist hanging against the southern slope without changing shape.

Then, near the bright gap, you make out the works.

A concrete intake house sits low against the ground, its walls weathered white, its roof secured with rows of bolts. Beside it are two sluice wheels, an electrical cabinet, and a short gantry carrying a chain hoist. There is no settlement around them. A maintenance track approaches in two dark strips, disappears beneath grass, and becomes visible again where its sleepers cross a wet hollow.

Water collected from the basin passes through a screened channel and drops beyond the rim. The generating station is much farther down, invisible beneath the clouds. Here there is only the intake, the overflow, and the beginning of a pressure pipe large enough to walk through.

The overflow makes most of the noise. A broad, steady volume of water pours over a concrete lip into a shaft below the edge. You cannot see the falling water from the meadow. You see only the level approach to it, a smooth dark sheet with bits of sedge drifting toward the screen.

The sound is astonishingly large for so little visible movement.

Close to the intake, speech has to be shouted directly into an ear. Farther out, words remain audible but lose their edges. Across the whole basin the roar occupies the air, roughened now and then by a deeper vibration from the pipe or the metallic shaking of the trash rack. A hand laid on the gantry rail feels a continuous fine tremor. The machinery runs unattended for long stretches. Its paint is intact beneath the fittings and faded everywhere exposed to light.

There are small accommodations for people: a grated walkway, a handrail around a square opening, a hut with one window and a stovepipe. Under the hut, moss grows between the concrete piers. A pair of heavy rubber gloves hangs inside the window, fingers downward. Everything human is concentrated into a few hard, practical objects, separated by acres of wet green.

The animals are easy to miss. Once, a pipit rises from the sedges and flies low across the basin, its call entirely lost. A few dark insects gather on the warm side of a marker post. In a clear pool there are tiny movements among submerged stems. Most of the visible activity belongs to water and light.

As the afternoon passes, almost nothing changes position. The shadow of the intake house moves across the rushes. Pools that were white become transparent, revealing last year’s leaves beneath this year’s roots. A cloud touches the outer slope and leaves a fresh brightness on the grass. The overflow continues at the same pitch.

Near sunset, the green floor takes on more relief. Small ridges emerge from what looked level at noon: the backs of old root mats, the lips of pools slowly closing, patches of firmer ground around which the water has begun to choose a route. Beyond them lie darker, wetter stretches where the vegetation has only just gained a hold.

There is no clear line between the meadow and what the meadow will become. At its edges, loose stems lie out across the water, still floating, already putting down roots.
