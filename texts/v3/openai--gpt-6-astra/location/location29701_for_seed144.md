The cloud layer lies below the road.

Through breaks in it you can see another country: dark foothills, a river taking three days to turn a corner, the pale geometry of fields. Up here the road crosses a basin of yellow stone so high that the sun seems less like weather than a condition of the material. It is hot before dawn. By midmorning, a buckle left against bare rock can burn a hand.

The heat comes from underneath as well. In places the road has been lifted into shallow blisters, and someone has drilled each blister and fitted it with a short iron pipe. These pipes do not smoke continuously. They breathe out a little wet air, pause, and breathe again. Around their mouths the dust is dark.

The basin is several miles across, enclosed on three sides by ridges whose crests stand nearly level with one another. It might once have held a lake. Broad benches follow the walls, and the lowest ground is filled with overlapping mineral fans, each one ribbed and scalloped like something poured slowly from a great height. Their edges have rounded with age. There are no fresh, dramatic wounds in the landscape; even the landslides have settled into recognizable neighborhoods of stone.

From a distance it looks bare. Walking into it corrects that impression.

Every surface is occupied. Flakes of mica lie tilted against one another. Pebbles have collected in the lee of larger pebbles. Brown insect cases fill cracks scarcely wider than a knife cut. Under the lip of each mineral shelf are nests, abandoned nests, shed skins, the white stains of droppings, little felted plugs of hair. There is almost no vegetation to soften this accumulation. A few thorn cushions grow where the terraces meet, and black-green cords of algae mark the routes water used last night.

You cannot put a palm down without disturbing something, though you may have to wait to see what moves.

The commonest animals are the pale, many-legged ones that live beneath the loose crust. When your shadow reaches them, they flatten. When it passes, they resume carrying things: dead flies, salt grains, fragments of a brown membrane that grows inside the steam cracks. They work in separate lanes without making permanent trails. Above them, little blunt-headed lizards sit with their feet lifted alternately from the hot stone. Their bellies are the color of tarnished silver. They rarely run far. There is usually prey within reach.

Higher on the walls are thousands of narrow nesting holes. Small birds move between them and the basin floor in low, quick flights, picking insects from the damp margins. They make almost no calls. You notice their numbers when a shadow crosses the ground and half a cliff face detaches into the air.

The thing casting the shadow may be an eagle, or one of the broad-winged scavengers that circle at ridge height, scarcely moving a feather. It may also be the cable bucket.

There are only four buildings in the basin. All are low, with roofs of corrugated metal weighted by stones, and all stand close to natural vents. The largest contains a turbine. Its exhaust passes through banks of dark pipes set beneath the eaves, where some of the steam becomes water again. Most of the works is outdoors: valves in stone-lined pits, insulated wires on short ceramic posts, a narrow-gauge track that disappears under mineral deposits and emerges farther on.

The turbine turns slowly enough that its individual beats can be felt through the floor. Outside, twenty paces away, it is almost inaudible.

Power goes to the cable line and the pumps. The buckets travel across the basin on wires difficult to see in the hard light. Some carry sacks of mineral salts; some carry replacement pipe, food, bundles of empty canvas. Most are empty. They advance with the patience of objects drifting on water, until one passes a support wheel and gives a brief, dry clack.

The line continues over the western rim. Nothing here shows how far down it goes.

Water appears in small, inconvenient places. A hand-deep pool occupies a hollow halfway up a cliff. The shaded side of a boulder sweats while its sunny side is too hot to touch. At one terrace there is a stream six inches wide, perfectly clear, which runs for forty yards and disappears beneath a crust of orange stone. It has been doing this long enough to carve a channel smooth as a handled tool.

The pools have sharply different lives. Some are sterile and beautiful, with white rims and blue centers. Others hold dense populations of dark larvae, transparent shrimp, and flat snails that graze the warm film on the bottom. The birds know which pools to visit. So do the little dust-colored mammals that come out beneath the pipe racks, standing briefly upright before crossing open ground.

Around a leaking pump flange, their footprints overlap until the mud has the texture of woven cloth.

By afternoon the air begins to move uphill. Cloud presses against the outer ridges, spills through the lowest gaps, and stretches into the basin in long, thinning fingers. For a few minutes the ground vanishes beyond fifty yards. Droplets gather on wire, feather, thorn, and metal. The cable drips along its whole visible length.

Then the hot stone eats the fog.

It clears first around the vents, then over the broad mineral fans. Dampness remains in seams and under shelves. Animals emerge to drink from surfaces rather than pools: lizards licking the undersides of rails, beetles lifting themselves on their forelegs beneath a collecting drop. The thorn cushions darken and loosen slightly. Their leaves are so small that this change in posture is easier to see than the leaves themselves.

Occasionally the cloud rises fast enough to bring rain. It falls heavily for perhaps six minutes. Water races down every rib in the terraces, carrying dust, husks, and loose salt into the basin floor. The empty channels become a single branching system. Pools overflow into pools they have not touched for weeks. Creatures that seemed confined to one hollow spread into the next.

Afterward there is a smell of wet iron and something like boiled grain.

The old workers’ paths avoid certain patches of flat ground. These are not especially hot, and they look sound. But after rain they ring faintly underfoot. Beneath them, water has entered chambers in the rock, and the steam is finding its way out through passages too narrow to see. A cup set down there will tremble toward the lowest edge of its saucer. Loose washers arrange themselves in little groups.

At the pump house, tools hang from hooks. Nothing small is left on a table overnight.

Evening brings long shadows without much relief. The ridges go copper-colored, then dull purple. Below the basin the cloud sea turns luminous, lighting the undersides of the birds as they return to their holes. The last buckets continue moving overhead.

This is when the larger animals descend: lean, high-shouldered grazers with short horns and broad, split feet. There is little for them to crop, but they know every thorn cushion and every ledge where a flush of growth follows damp weather. They also lick the terraces. A herd can spend an hour moving across apparently empty stone, each animal selecting its own narrow seam of salts.

Their passage makes less noise than expected. A hoof clicks once; a loose pebble travels a few feet. Then there is only the faint movement of breath and bodies.

At night the basin remains warm. Its crowded surfaces continue their work beyond sight. Larvae rise through the pools. Moths settle on wet pipe joints. Mineral skins thicken by amounts too small to measure from one morning to the next. Under the road, pressure lifts another shallow dome.

Through the open door of the turbine house, a single electric bulb lights a chair, a ledger, and a dark crescent where a cup has stood. Outside, condensation forms on the bucket cable and begins its slow journey downward, gathering at each low point until it is heavy enough to fall.
