The garden lies one floor below the street, in the bottom of a block that has been built around until almost no sky remains. You enter by a concrete stair between two warehouses. At the top there are traffic noises, pale daylight, a strip of ordinary pavement. By the sixth step the handrail is wet. By the twelfth, leaves are brushing both shoulders, and the sound from below has become too large to speak over comfortably.

It is the pumping machinery. Two pumps work in a chamber behind the far wall, drawing drainage water into the district main. You cannot see them from the stairs, but their vibration travels through the rail and the soles of your shoes. There is a low, continuous roar, a finer metallic rattle, and the hollow rush of water entering a pipe. Every few minutes one of these sounds changes pitch. Nothing seems to move in response.

The space is no larger than a small public hall. Three sides are brick, rising to the backs of buildings; the fourth is a retaining wall pierced by the pump-room doors. Overhead, a greenhouse roof spans the gap. Its wired glass is stained, patched with translucent plastic, and shaded by the higher floors around it. Light comes down greenish and diffuse. Even at noon, the undersides of the planting benches are dark.

This was built as a municipal propagation house, close to the boilers so that waste heat could keep plants through winter. The boilers have been replaced, but insulated hot-water pipes still cross the enclosure. Their jackets are split in places. Ferns gather beneath those places, where condensation drops regularly from the seams.

The old arrangement survives inside the growth: four parallel benches, a central aisle, shallow drainage channels, a raised platform for potting. Almost none of it presents a bare surface. Long troughs hold aspidistra and begonias. Vines climb the bench legs, cross the mesh tops, and descend into neighboring beds. Moss fills the joints of the platform so completely that its separate slabs are visible only where their corners tilt. Along the back wall, roots have grown through the drain holes of pots and into the accumulated leaf mold beneath. Those pots can no longer be lifted. Some have split into curved fragments that remain caught in the roots.

There is no clean division between a plant and the place that supports it. A mound beneath a spreading fern turns out to be a stack of old coir bales, still held together at one end by black twine. What looks like a bank of soil conceals the lower half of a wheeled trolley. Seedlings grow in its rusted tray. On the shaded side of a pipe, a narrow stripe of moss follows a persistent leak for several yards.

The central aisle is kept open, though only just. Someone cuts back the stems at shoulder height and scrapes the walking surface often enough to expose a wavering strip of concrete. The cut ends are visible among the leaves, neat and dark. Farther in, you have to turn sideways past a clump of broad foliage that has grown around a stopcock. Its red handle remains accessible through a small, deliberately maintained opening.

Water is everywhere without forming much open water. It rests in leaf axils, spreads in transparent sheets over the retaining wall, hangs beneath the bench frames. The floor has a slight fall toward the pump room. Along that fall, runoff moves through a shallow channel under a skin of duckweed until it reaches a grated sump. There the floating green ends abruptly. The bars are cleared daily, leaving a dark rectangle in which water trembles with the vibration of the machinery.

After rain, the walls continue to drain for hours. Fine threads emerge from mortar joints well above head height. Some follow old mineral tracks; others disappear behind the climbing stems and return lower down as drops from leaf tips. The masonry has endured this for decades. Its edges are rounded, its lower courses darkened almost black. White deposits have built up beneath a cracked overflow socket, spreading over the brick in overlapping crusts.

The warmth is steady rather than sunny. It comes from pipes, motors, wet walls that never quite cool. The air smells of damp terracotta, warm iron, and the sweet, dense decay under the benches. Near the pump-room doors there is also the faint oily smell of serviced bearings. A ventilation fan runs behind a louver high in the wall, but its pull barely reaches the floor. A few leaves near the opening incline toward it. Elsewhere the foliage is still.

There are no calls from birds, no scurrying under the benches, no insects ticking against the glass. The few conspicuous movements are drops falling and the slow displacement of duckweed above the drain. A yellow leaf lies on the surface there, its stem caught against a root. It turns a little whenever the pump load changes, then settles back.

The enclosure has the density of a much larger settlement. Pipes cross above pipes. Electrical conduit skirts the doorframes. A galvanized access stair rises behind a curtain of vines to a landing where several building services meet. Windows from storerooms and workshops look into the roof space. At one of them, stacks of cardboard boxes press against the glass; at another, a fluorescent tube burns all day. Their pale rectangles are mostly hidden from below.

Yet the plants make the boundaries difficult to judge. A gap between two benches seems to lead farther into the block, until your eyes adjust and find a wall only an arm’s length away. Tall leaves conceal the stair you came down. The roof is visible in disconnected pieces, each framed by overlapping stems. You are always close to brick, machinery, or another person’s workplace, but you can rarely see more than a few feet clearly.

The place changes slowly. Roots thicken inside joints. A bracket sinks beneath moss. Each season, the cleared aisle shifts by an inch or two as some plants are cut back and others are allowed to remain. Maintenance keeps the pumps working, the doors opening, the water moving downhill. Beyond those necessities, growth occupies what it can.

At the far end, beside the warmest pipe, an old enamel sign is still fixed to the wall. Most of it is hidden behind a fern. The exposed corner says KEEP CLEAR. Beneath it, the narrow channel runs freely toward the sump, its concrete bed polished smooth by years of water.
