The district lies beneath a square that no longer has a stair down to it.

It is not deep underground. The roots of the square’s trees come through its ceiling, and in a few places the underside of the paving is visible: pale slabs resting on old beams, their joints plugged with soil. But the district has its own roofs, its own streets and gutters, its own public clock. These occupy a cavity scarcely larger than a market hall. From the right landing you can see almost the whole place, though you cannot see any route through it. Roofs overlap balconies; balconies carry water tanks; the tanks have been boxed in to make rooms. Between two buildings there may be a lane, or only a slit through which someone has threaded a belt drive.

The heat is strongest at ankle level.

It rises through the new black stone on which everything stands. This stone came up slowly, within living memory, lifting a warehouse floor and then the warehouse itself, opening the shallow chamber beneath the square. Its surface still looks unfinished. There are sharp folds like cloth pinched between fingers, small glassy blisters, ridges that will cut a sandal. In the lanes people have laid perforated iron plates over it. Elsewhere they have poured lime into the creases, enough to level a doorstep but not enough to cover the dark material entirely.

A strip of exposed ground runs behind the bathhouse. It is only three paces wide, yet feels like the district’s wilderness: brittle shelves of rock, a little slope of splinters, warm hollows packed with fern. No one crosses it. There is nothing on the far side except a wall, and the stone is still making room for itself.

Most of the buildings are brick, with lower courses that have been replaced several times. The old bricks are plum-colored and soft at the edges. The new ones are yellow, hard, and oddly clean. Where the rising ground has cracked a wall, the repair follows the crack rather than concealing it. Bright seams climb across dark masonry, turn beneath windows, fork around lintels. Some houses look as though they have been carefully reassembled after falling, though they have never fallen. Once every few years a door needs shortening.

Everything is close enough to touch something else. Even the plants seem to have had to negotiate for space.

Broad, thin leaves spread beneath the ceiling, held almost flat to catch the light leaking through the square. Their stems descend in bundles through holes cut in awnings. Ferns fill the cool sides of cisterns. A climbing vine with dull red leaf-backs has crossed three houses and entered the clock tower through its missing face. The district’s gardeners do not work in beds so much as in seams: a thumb-width of soil behind a pipe, a brick removed beneath a sill, the open mouth of a disused chimney. From these small allowances comes a surprising weight of green.

The leaves nearest the warm stone are narrow and waxy. Higher up, where the air is less hot, they grow soft and enormous. In one passage a single leaf makes the whole roof. It has been supported with three lengths of wire, and its drip-tip hangs over a glazed jar.

Water is collected by such exact arrangements. There is no running stream, and very little water stands uncovered. A forgotten cup can be empty before evening. Yet nearly every surface bears some evidence of damp: a dark margin under a tank, mineral whiskers on a tap, a green stain where a pipe sweats.

Rain in the square reaches the district late. First the ceiling cools. Then a few roots darken. Hours afterward, water begins to fall from places that look solid. The inhabitants know which paving joint feeds which gutter, which wall will weep, which balcony must have its laundry brought in. Narrow copper channels guide the seepage into covered tanks. Overflow goes to the plants. Only after a long rain does water reach the street, where it settles briefly in the sharp depressions of the black rock, trembling with heat.

The smell then is thick and mineral, with a sweetness from bruised stems. For a while the place seems greener without becoming any brighter.

Its light comes from above in small, unreliable portions and from lamps fixed low on the walls. The lamps have cloudy amber globes. Their wiring runs beside the water pipes, protected by ceramic sleeves wherever the two must cross. There is enough illumination to recognize a neighbor, read a dial, see whether a leaf is healthy. The upper corners of rooms remain obscure.

Behind the laundry is the engine house. Its door stands open because there is no other way to lose the heat. Inside, two broad pistons move at a pace slow enough to follow with the eye. They draw warmth from the stone and pass it into a network of shafts, pumps, and heavy accumulators. The machine’s flywheel nearly fills the room. Someone has painted a white stripe on its rim; you can watch that stripe descend, disappear behind the floor grating, and return.

This is the district’s main industry: maintaining a small, continuous motion.

Power travels through wall shafts to ventilators, a grain mill, a workshop press, the laundry rollers. None works quickly. The press takes most of a minute to close. The mill produces flour in a soft, steady thread. A repairer may spend an afternoon sitting beside a turning spindle, waiting for a surface to become true. Work is arranged around the available force, and there is more patience than surplus.

For all its machinery, the place is quiet. The engine rests on thick pads of woven root fiber. Belts are dressed and tightened before they begin to slap. Loose metal is considered a fault to be attended to, not a background condition. What you hear is the faint roll of a bearing, a drop entering a covered tank, a brush sweeping grit toward a drain. Voices seldom need to rise. Across the central court, someone setting a glass on a table can be heard through the foliage.

The court itself is almost entirely occupied by a tree.

Its trunk emerges between the iron paving plates, broadens around a public pump, and separates into several leaning stems. It is not one of the trees growing in the square. Its crown remains below the ceiling. The leaves are dark green, leathery, each with a shallow cup at its base that holds a little moisture. Beneath it are six chairs, a locked cabinet of tools, and a circular basin without water. The basin is full of pale roots.

People sit there in the hottest hours. The chairs have narrow backs and slatted seats. Conversation comes in short exchanges with long intervals, because everyone already knows the visible news: which wall has moved, whose tank is low, where a new shoot has blocked a shutter.

There are few animals. A small gray snail sometimes appears inside the cool neck of a jar. Once or twice a week, a moth travels between the lamps with a labored, almost pedestrian flight. There are no birds beneath the square, and no resident dogs. Fruit must be pollinated with a brush. Fallen leaves remain where they land until somebody gathers them.

The plants, however, do not entirely wait for people.

A vine tied away from a vent will stay there, but its next shoot will grow toward the vent’s moving air. Fine roots find the cooler pipes and sleeve them without entering the joints. The great court tree has never broken the public pump; instead it has grown around the handle’s full arc, leaving an opening shaped exactly for its use.

Less easily explained are the empty spaces that appear in the foliage before repairs. Two days before a ceiling beam gives its first audible crack, the leaves beneath it begin to turn edge-on. A week before a wall needs shoring, its creepers loosen their hold. People take advantage of this. Props are stored within easy reach. No one calls the plants prophetic. There is a word used locally that means something closer to *feeling where the weight will go*.

The human construction follows these small withdrawals. A new bracket is fixed where the vine has left room. A pipe is rerouted through a gap that opened last month. The district has no master plan, but it is full of straight cuts, numbered valves, careful soldering. Its strangeness has not made its inhabitants careless.

At the far end, beyond the workshops, a narrow ramp ascends under the square’s perimeter wall. This is the only entrance. Deliveries come down on a flat trolley with a hand brake. The ramp’s upper door is kept nearly shut, leaving a blade of daylight so bright that it looks solid. Warm air passes out at the top of the gap; cooler air enters along the floor. On difficult days people stand near it for a few minutes with their shoes off.

From there, looking back, the whole settlement seems folded into its own green shadow. You see a tank, a lit window, the curve of the flywheel through two open doorways. A woman on a balcony eases a leaf away from her drying shirts. Farther down, a man has placed his palm against a fresh seam in the street and is waiting to decide whether it is warmer than yesterday.

Nothing appears to be happening quickly enough to watch.

Then the white stripe returns on the wheel. A drop falls into the jar. Somewhere beneath the paving, too slowly to make a sound, the new ground presses upward, and the district holds its place by yielding a little at a time.
