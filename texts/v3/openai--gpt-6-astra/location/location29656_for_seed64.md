The garden occupies a missing tooth in the summit machinery.

From the outside, there is almost nothing to see: a dark notch beneath a cast-iron mounting plate, high on the underside of a cliff. The cliff stands above the useful atmosphere. Far below, the cloudless air acquires color, then opacity, then the appearance of a solid blue floor. No ground is visible. The mounting plate belongs to a machine whose other parts are gone, except for three bolts as thick as tree trunks and a length of toothed rail disappearing into the rock.

Between one bolt and its socket is the garden. You could cover its entrance with your hand.

Inside, it is crowded enough to be a country.

The terrain begins with a lip of dull metal, rounded by an abrasion that ended long ago. Beyond it, the floor drops steeply into a crooked chamber scarcely longer than a forearm. Mineral shelves project from both walls. Some are natural; others are the remains of screw threads, their regular spacing worn down into an imperfect succession of terraces. At the bottom, a basin of powdered graphite holds no liquid and has no drain. Its surface is packed hard, bearing the precise, shallow impressions of leaves that are still resting on it.

There is no water here. Not frozen in the walls, not concealed beneath the floor, not drawn upward through roots. The pale crusts are not salt left by evaporation. Nothing has ever needed to dry.

Nevertheless, nearly every available surface is green.

The largest plants stand two finger-widths tall. They have thick, overlapping leaves, dark as bottle glass where the light enters and nearly black deeper in the chamber. Their margins are finely serrated, but the teeth bend inward, each leaf protecting a narrow interior full of smaller leaves. Other plants lie flat against the metal in round, densely pleated rosettes. Some resemble moss until you look closely and find that every filament is a jointed column, with tiny paired blades pressed tightly to its sides.

There is a low undergrowth of cream-colored scales. Beneath that, a finer undergrowth, violet and almost granular. Beneath that, roots.

The roots occupy everything the leaves do not.

They pass through pits in the iron, around grains of stone, into the hairline space between a washer and the mounting plate. At several points they have laid themselves across empty space in broad, flat straps. Nothing hangs loosely. Nothing has room to trail. Even the darkest recess contains a structure fitted to it: a wedge-shaped bulb, a fan of fibrous tissue, a pale cushion bearing hundreds of unopened cups.

The plants are neither brittle nor succulent. Their substance has the yielding firmness of cold electrical insulation. A broken edge—there is one, near the entrance—shows green tissue threaded with dull silver. Their chemistry belongs to this place: carbon, mineral oils bound so tightly they cannot flow, chains of compounds that would be useless in an ordinary leaf. Water would spoil them.

They do not reach toward the light. The ones under the deepest overhang are as elaborate as those beside the entrance.

Light arrives as a narrow, pewter-colored rectangle. It reveals little at once. First the outer leaves, then the shine along a root, then a tiny yellow insulator almost buried under vegetation. The chamber has no glowing flowers, no luminous veins to make it conveniently visible. Its abundance remains difficult to inspect. Looking farther in means losing sight of what is nearer.

The cold is similarly unobtrusive. There is no frost to announce it. The iron is simply cold enough that skin would adhere to it, while the plants retain their slight, stubborn flexibility.

Three manufactured objects remain inside the garden.

One is the yellow insulator, no larger than a lentil, with a brass contact at its center. One is a perforated strip that crosses the lower basin like a footbridge, though it was never intended to carry feet. The third is a square ceramic housing set into the rear wall. Two green stems emerge through its cable aperture. A row of stamped numbers survives on one side, each character filled with black root tissue.

Together they suggest an old monitoring device, something that measured strain in the mounting bolt. They are the only indications that this intimate, congested hollow belonged to an industrial structure rather than being a cavity in a bone.

The machine is gone. Its sound is not.

Inside the socket there is a loud, level note, too low to seem proportional to the space. A harder tone lies across it. Behind both is a broad metallic pressure, like the interior of a factory heard through a wall, except that no individual operation can be separated from the whole. No strike. No bearing catching. No belt slapping at an interval.

The sound never swells or diminishes.

It appears to reside in the iron rather than pass through it. A leaf touching the wall contains the same note. So does the ceramic housing. So does the packed graphite in the basin. There is no quieter corner. The smallest sheltered space is as thoroughly occupied by sound as by roots.

Nothing moves to produce it.

That is the difficult fact of the garden. The leaves are not motionless because the air is still. They are motionless in the more complete way that the shape of a crystal is motionless. Their condition has no next stage. The pale cups are not waiting to open. The broken leaf is not healing or decaying. The roots have not stopped at obstacles; their ends are simply where roots end.

Even the old wear belongs to the present arrangement. The rounded threads, the polished entrance lip, the graphite compressed beneath the leaves: all are settled forms, with no remaining tendency to become smoother, deeper, finer.

There are no animals to disturb this completeness. No mites in the leaf folds, no eggs under the scales, no web spanning the entrance. What looks briefly like a small leg is a forked root. What resembles a shell is a seed case, still attached to its stem and full.

The garden is lush without growing. Its sound is loud without an event. It occupies a little darkness at an almost inconceivable height, held between the rock and the last hardware of a vanished machine.

At the entrance, one broad leaf fits exactly beneath the iron lip. Its upper surface is dim green. Its underside is silver-gray. Between its edge and the metal is a gap just wide enough to look through, and beyond that gap the blue depth falls away without a foothold.
