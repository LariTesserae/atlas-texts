The place is high enough that the sky, seen through its few openings, has very little color. It is a dull violet-gray, almost the color of the fired clay surrounding it. There is no view of a horizon. The openings are narrow and set deep in the masonry, and beyond them there is only that thin, dim light.

Everything within reach has been made.

The floor is brick. Beneath the brick are older bricks, visible where a shallow hollow has worn through the upper courses. The walls are brick, the ceiling is brick, and the immense supports that leave so little room to walk are assembled from wedge-shaped blocks pressed into molds by hand. Even the grit in the corners is powdered construction. There is no exposed rock, no buried hillside entering the foundations, no seam where the work of building gives way to something that was already here.

The whole place is perhaps sixty paces across. It has the crowded interior of a storehouse, although nothing is stored in it except more structure: piers, buttresses, low arches, thick-walled shafts, shelves carrying the feet of other arches. Passageways squeeze between them. Some admit a person sideways. Others are no wider than a hand, but have carefully laid floors and little vaulted roofs, as though the builders considered these spaces just as important as the larger ones.

The sound fills all of them.

It is a continuous, crushing chord, too low in part to hear and too high in part to separate from the sensation of pressure inside the head. Near a wall it enters through the jaw. In the narrow passages it seems to occupy the chest before there is room to take a breath. There are several distinct notes, but no melody, no pulse, no moment in which one rises over another. A voice would disappear here. A shout would be something felt in the throat and never received by the ears.

The building is an instrument, though it has no strings, metal tongues, or moving parts. Its thick walls contain ducts. Their mouths can be found close to the floor: dark rectangular slots with rounded lips, each opening shaped and reshaped by abrasion so old that it resembles deliberate polishing. Air passes through them at a steady pressure. Somewhere below, beyond any accessible stair, the ducts gather into larger chambers; somewhere above, they divide again.

There is no gusting. A pinch of dust held at a vent would draw out into an unwavering line.

The builders needed only clay, cord, shaped wooden gauges, and the patience to lay one course upon another. They made the curves by letting each row project a little farther than the last. They made narrow throats by rubbing the fired surfaces with grit. On sheltered bricks, impressions of fingers remain beneath the dim crust of age. Some are small. Some have dragged sideways when the wet block was lifted. The marks recur in groups, then stop, then appear again higher in the wall.

Nothing identifies the people who left them.

They are gone so completely that their absence no longer feels like an event.

There are no roots splitting the joints. No nests occupy the ducts. No web crosses even the smallest passage. The warmth of the masonry supports nothing visible. It is the dry, enveloping warmth of a kiln long after firing, except that it never diminishes. Lay a palm against any surface and the same temperature meets it: floor, pier, ceiling brick reached from a ledge. There are no cool recesses.

Water survives here only as an implication of manufacture. Once, clay must have been mixed. Once, these surfaces yielded under thumbs. Now the material is hard and faintly porous, shading from brown-red to a pale, exhausted ocher. A few blocks have black centers exposed by wear. Others contain crushed fragments of still older brick, so that the building is partly made of buildings whose shapes cannot be recovered.

Age has taken away almost every edge it can reach.

The thresholds are broad depressions. The corners of the piers have become long, unequal curves. At one junction, two neighboring supports have worn back just enough to create a passage that was never designed, its sides smooth at shoulder height. Elsewhere an old doorway has been filled with close-set blocks, and the filling has aged until its outline is scarcely distinguishable from the wall.

Yet nothing is falling now. No flakes detach. No fresh dust gathers beneath the arches. Whatever processes enlarged the openings and rounded the stairs have arrived at a final arrangement. The remaining surfaces are those the air can pass without further altering.

Near the center is a small chamber, less crowded than the rest. It might be called a courtyard, except that it has a roof, or a hall, except that there is nowhere to stand without nearly touching one of its six massive piers. Between them lies a raised slab made from many bricks ground into a single shallow curve. It bears no carving. Above it, a shaft admits a little more of the violet light.

Here the separate notes become easier to distinguish. Each pier contains a different part of the chord. The slab carries the deepest one. Its surface does not visibly tremble, but teeth brought together would feel it.

From this chamber, the surrounding passages resemble channels left in an otherwise solid mass. Their turns are visible for only a few feet. Beyond each turn there is another warm wall, another close arch, another opening full of sound.

A narrow stair leads upward to a final slit in the outer masonry. Its steps have been worn almost into a ramp. Through the slit, looking down, one can see the outside of the structure descending out of sight: courses of handmade brick, immense battered shoulders of buttress-work, tiny dark vents. There is no ground below them within view. The building continues into the dimness, supporting this small, crowded summit at an unguessable height.

No sound arrives from outside that can be distinguished from the chord.

No change of light marks the passing of an hour.

The little summit remains warm, occupied to its limits by masonry and sound, with just enough space left between its ancient supports for someone to enter, turn sideways, and go a few paces farther.
