The place is shallow enough that, from the entrance, you can see almost all of its upper leaves. They lie in overlapping tiers beneath a pale ceiling: broad copper-green blades, fans of cream-colored needles, thick folded leaves the color of the inside of a fig. Nothing rises much higher than a person. Yet you cannot see the opposite wall. There are too many small trunks, screens, hanging roots, things built in front of other things.

Warmth comes through the soles of your feet.

The floor is made of fired clay, though very little of it remains exposed. It has been laid in low, uneven terraces, each perhaps a handspan above the next, with paths that narrow where the planting has crowded over their edges. The highest ground is a mound near the center, no taller than a table. The lowest is a long trough filled with black flakes. Everything is bounded by a wall of thumb-sized bricks. At first the enclosure seems scarcely larger than a courtyard; walking its perimeter takes much longer than crossing it ought to.

There is no soil beneath the garden. Lift a loose paving piece and you find another layer of worked clay, then a lattice of wooden strips, then tightly packed disks of a pale, chalky material, each impressed with a fingerprint. Farther down, if you keep lifting, the layers become smaller without becoming finer. Little beams support little vaults. Stairs descend through spaces that would not admit a finger. There is no bedrock to reach.

The garden has been made all the way through.

Its vegetation grows without water. There are no damp places, no coolness hidden under the leaves, no scent of rot. Even the deepest shade is warm and powder-dry. The plants draw something from the difference between the heated floor and the slightly cooler air. Their roots are braided cords, buried in beds of crushed pottery. Their stems have the faint ridges left by a tool turning against soft material. At the base of every trunk is a seam.

Some were assembled from carved pieces. Others were coiled upward by hand, like pots. Once planted, they continued.

A sapling beside the entrance has only three leaves, all carefully cut from thin, translucent horn. Its newest leaf is still emerging, soft at the folded edge, and bears no knife marks. A larger tree farther in has put out branches through the holes originally bored for tying it together. The old lashings are embedded in the trunk. Growth has preserved the knots and drawn them inward.

The plants are close-set and various. Low cushions of silver bristles fill the spaces between paving stones. Flat vines spread along the walls, each leaf fitting precisely into the gap between two others. There are yellow stalks with heavy, dark seedheads; shrubs whose foliage hangs in little pleated packets; a stand of broad-leaved plants that turn their undersides outward very slowly over the course of the afternoon. These undersides are red, and for an hour the entire rear quarter of the garden seems warmer than the rest.

Little shade is wasted. Under the shrubs grow smaller shrubs, and beneath those, a minute branching cover like green stitching. Even the undersides of the terraces carry hanging growth. You must kneel to see it: pale fronds extending downward into spaces where no one could have planted them without first taking the floor apart.

The garden is young. Its bricks still have sharp corners. The fired surfaces have scarcely begun to pit, and there are fresh splinters on the handrails. Chips left from construction remain beside the things they were cut from. A discarded shaving near a tree will sometimes straighten over several days, set one end against the floor, and begin to resemble a very slender tree itself.

There are animals everywhere, but few movements draw the eye.

Six-legged creatures the size of walnuts sit in rows on the warm terrace edges. Their bodies are made of overlapping flakes, ocher and dull blue, with tiny pegs for eyes. They spend most of the day facing the masonry. When one moves, the others make room with an almost imperceptible adjustment.

Long, jointed grazers cling underneath leaves. Their mouths work steadily against the hard green surfaces, leaving pale tracks. The leaves thicken around these tracks rather than healing them away. Older foliage is densely patterned with generations of feeding.

Near the central mound, soft-bodied animals sleep in little hollows lined with powdered charcoal. They look like small pouches sewn from suede, except that their seams open and close as they breathe. Sometimes a narrow face appears at one end. Sometimes it appears at the other. They eat the discarded skins of roots.

There are flying things, too, although they seldom fly. Most hang beneath branches with their wings folded into rectangles. Their wing membranes are thin enough to show the weave of the fabric from which their ancestors were cut. One may detach, cross a path in a short, level glide, and settle without a sound.

You can stand still for a long time before understanding how crowded the garden is. A knot in a railing uncurls. A row of beads above a doorway turns out to be a resting family. What seemed to be a leaf’s serrated edge is a procession of tiny bodies, each holding the next by its hind feet.

None of them drink.

There are no basins, drains, gutters, or vessels for carrying liquid. The tools left in the work alcove are wedges, awls, stone burnishers, a wooden mallet, and several lengths of abrasive cord. Beneath the bench stands a shallow tray of dry clay. Press it in your fingers and it binds, not through moisture but through continued pressure. Release it too soon and it falls apart. Hold it long enough and it keeps both the shape and a little of your warmth.

This is how much of the place was made.

The work alcove is empty when you first pass it. Later, from across the garden, you may see someone seated there, shoulders bent, pressing a small object between both palms. They are obscured by foliage. The mallet never sounds. When you return, there is a newly formed animal on the bench, motionless beside a little heap of offcuts. It may take weeks to begin moving.

Other changes take longer. The wall is being extended, one brick at a time, though its outer boundary does not move. Each new section makes more room inside. A narrow bed appears between two established beds. A path acquires a bend. Plants which were once neighbors remain leaning toward one another across the new distance.

The fresh sections are easy to recognize. Their mortar lines are clean, and the creatures avoid them until the bricks have been touched sufficiently often.

Light lies evenly across the garden. It has no obvious source. The pale ceiling is built from overlapping panels, thin enough that you might expect daylight behind them, but an unfastened corner reveals only the dark underside of another floor. Shadows nevertheless fall in a consistent direction. At midday they shorten. In the evening, which comes very gradually, they lengthen toward the entrance.

Sometimes a shadow reaches a wall and passes beneath it.

There are places where you can put your hand through a shadow and feel the other side of a leaf growing elsewhere in the enclosure. Garden animals use these passages more readily than the paths. You may watch a little jointed grazer crawl behind the shadow of a post and emerge high in the foliage, still moving at the same laborious pace.

The makers accommodate this. Small rungs have been fitted along certain shadows. Elsewhere, clay plugs stop openings that led somewhere inconvenient.

Nothing happens quickly enough to feel like a disturbance. A seedpod opens over several afternoons. A wall settles by the thickness of a fingernail. One of the sleeping pouch animals wakes, spends an hour easing itself out of its hollow, and finds that a root has grown across the way. It rests its face against the root until the root begins to lift.

Mostly the sound is the faint rasp of feeding, an occasional dry tick from a cooling joint. Close to the floor, you can hear a subdued friction, like someone rubbing a thumb across unglazed pottery. It comes from below the lowest accessible layer.

At the entrance there is a place to sit. The seat has been polished by use, though the steps beside it still show the fresh strokes of the adze. From here the garden seems complete: crowded, warm, quietly occupied.

Then you notice a small square of bare floor between your feet.

Its edges have been scored. Four holes have been made for posts. In its center, lying on its side, is a green folded thing no larger than a grain of barley. Someone has already given it roots.
