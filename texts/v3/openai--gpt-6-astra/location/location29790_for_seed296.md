From the town, the horizon looks low. It is only at the western edge, where the paving ends, that you discover how high you are.

There is no slope beyond the last houses. The ground simply stops. Far below, cloud lies in a white, tightly packed mass, its upper surface furrowed by winds that never reach the town. Occasionally a furrow opens and shows a second bank of cloud beneath it. Nobody agrees on how many such layers there are. Survey chains lowered over the edge come back wet, then frosted, then wet again, without having touched anything.

Up here the country is almost level.

It was worn level before it was lifted. Its hills are the last remnants of hills, long-backed and shallow, with exposed stone rounded like the heels of old hands. There are no sharp peaks, no loose scree. Even the immense precipice has a softened lip. Across the plateau, narrow grooves in the bedrock lead toward rivers that no longer exist. The town’s main street follows one of them; after rain, water still knows which side of the street to take.

The stone is gray-green when dry and nearly black when damp. It contains pale seams, sometimes as fine as hairs, sometimes wide enough to build a staircase from. Builders once quarried these seams. Now they mostly dismantle older buildings, trimming and turning each block so that an unworn face looks outward. Some stones have served in a reservoir wall, a mill, a house, and a retaining terrace. Their hidden surfaces carry incompatible arrangements of bolt holes.

The town fills a broad depression between three low rises. From above it would look like something gathered by a rake: long, close lines of roofs, growing beds, tram tracks, drainage channels, and narrow courtyards, all following the grain of the rock. There is little vacant ground. Where a building has fallen, its cellar becomes a garden before anyone has finished clearing the upper rooms.

Vegetation is thick but seldom tall. The trees spread sideways under the wind, their trunks buried in their own branches. A single dark-leaved tree may shade six gardens and pass through the broken windows of two houses. Ferns occupy the gaps beneath it. There are leathery rosettes in roof gutters, copper-colored moss on the north walls, and mats of small round leaves so closely fitted together that rain stands on them like mercury on cloth.

Much of this growth looks black until you turn a leaf over. The undersides are wine-red, pale violet, sometimes a startling clean yellow. In the dimness, the plants spend very little on display.

The light comes chiefly from the sky to the east. Overhead hangs a nearly permanent lid of low cloud, but along that eastern margin there is usually a narrow brightness. Windows face it. Greenhouses lean toward it. On still afternoons, whole walls of leaves slowly change their angle, and the town seems to turn its attention in one direction.

Water is not abundant enough to waste. There are no lakes, only storage pools tucked beneath streets and small quantities of running water doing several jobs in succession. A channel leaving a nursery turns a wheel in a repair shop, cools a bearing in the weaving works, washes grit from a public stair, and finally enters a bed of dense, filtering reeds.

The rain arrives in brief, local passages. You can watch it cross the town: first a roof darkens, then a row of terraces, then a strip of pale roadway disappears. A courtyard may receive a minute of hard rain while the courtyard beside it stays dry.

More dependable is the condensation.

Each evening, cold moves out of the stone. Iron railings bead first. Then the pipes, the roof slates, the old glass insulators on the tram wires. Above the town stand racks of closely spaced metal plates, each angled to drain into a narrow trough. They resemble enormous books left partly open. Their undersides are furred with moss that workers comb away with wooden scrapers.

At dusk the collecting yards begin to tick.

It is a small sound, multiplied: droplets striking gutters, loose metal contracting, pawls advancing in the meters. Beneath it comes the low labor of pumps. The factories run most heavily at night, when there is enough collected water to cool the engines. They make cable, pump housings, woven insulation, replacement glass. Warm air leaves their roofs through broad slatted vents. Gardens planted around these outlets remain green through the coldest weeks.

There is always work in progress, though very little of it is loud. A freight tram passes with its wheels wrapped in replaceable bands of compressed fiber. A line of shutters closes across a drying shed. Workers move along the collector racks, tipping stubborn water into the troughs. In one street the paving has been lifted so that roots can be guided beneath it; in another, roots are being cut away from a pipe they have bent almost shut.

The quiet is partly custom and partly the air. At this height, a shout loses its body quickly. Bells sound thin. People signal across the works with lamps or by raising colored boards, and a person can be quite close before you hear their footsteps.

Animals are harder to find than their traces. There are tiny pale snails under the collector troughs, their shells almost transparent. Something narrow and furred uses the warm ducts beneath the bakery, leaving five-toed prints in spilled flour. Once or twice a week a long-winged bird rises from below the western edge. It crosses the town without flapping, examines the roofs, and returns to the clouds. People look up from their work to watch it. They do not call it common merely because it keeps coming back.

The plants provide most of the visible movement. Tendrils find new wires. Fern tips unroll in the sheltered heat behind engine houses. After a wet night, little stalks push through the moss on the old retaining walls and release a gray dust when the morning tram passes. Fallen leaves are pulled beneath the living mats so quickly that the paths rarely need sweeping.

There are also movements for which the town has no settled explanation.

At intervals, moisture rises through the ancient river grooves even when the storage pools are low and there has been no rain. It comes without pressure: a darkening of stone, then a continuous film, then water deep enough to reflect the sky. The flow takes the old direction, ignoring drains cut across it. For an hour or two, streets become shallow streams. Doorsteps have removable bridges for these occasions.

The water is colder than anything around it. It carries no silt, only occasional grains of rounded white sand.

The pump engineers keep records. Gardeners bring buckets. Children float scraps of wood toward the vanished river mouth, which is now somewhere beyond the precipice.

By morning the grooves are usually dry again.

Walk out past the collecting yards and the buildings gradually lower into the ground. Workshops become sheds; sheds become stone chambers with planted roofs; then there are only garden walls and the rounded backs of the old hills. From the last rise you can see the whole settlement, dense and dark in its hollow, with faint steam caught among the roofs.

Beyond it, the plateau continues for several miles: green seams in bare stone, abandoned channels, small sheltered thickets, the occasional square of an old foundation. Nothing on that worn surface appears capable of explaining the drop at its edge.

Evening reaches the hollow before it reaches you. Lamps appear beneath the leaves. The eastern strip of sky narrows. Somewhere among the terraces a machine starts, sending a brief shiver through the water in the gutters, and the first drops begin to fall from the metal racks.
