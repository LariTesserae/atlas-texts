The water begins at the threshold, standing upright in the doorway without spilling.

A little of it bulges past the brass frame. When the light changes, this lip of water becomes visible as a thick lens, rounding the pale tiles of the room beyond. There is no shore, no shallow entry. One step takes you from a dry service landing into thirty meters of warm, clear water.

The whole place hangs above the mountains.

Through the chamber’s lower windows, beneath the mesh floors and the tangled roots, you can see cloud moving across distant ridges. Sometimes a summit emerges, black and small, then disappears again. The chamber is attached to a mast whose base is lost below that weather. Outside, the air is cold enough to frost the inspection rails. Inside, the water is the temperature of a greenhouse late in the afternoon.

It occupies a space about the size of a narrow cathedral, though its interior is too crowded to offer a cathedral’s long view. Porcelain terraces project from the walls. Stairways double back beneath them. Great square ducts descend through beds of vegetation, and transparent pipes cross the stairways at knee height, filled with water slightly greener than the water around them. Wherever there might have been an open shaft, someone has fitted another shelf, another root basket, another bank of pale ceramic fins.

There is no earth. Under every thickness of green is a manufactured surface.

The highest terraces are surfaced in cream-colored plates, each folded into little ridges to hold nutrient gel. Below them are slopes made from triangular glass blocks. The blocks are new enough that their edges remain exact; light breaks against them in narrow, stationary rainbows. Even the apparent boulders wedged between the lower stairs are cast objects, hollow iron forms covered in a rough mineral enamel. Their lifting eyes have not been removed. Roots pass through them.

Nothing has had time to become rounded. A leaf may cover a corner, but underneath it the corner is still sharp.

The vegetation grows with an almost architectural fullness. Broad, translucent leaves overlap across the terraces like sheets laid out to dry. Fine red stems fill the railings. Pale climbing plants have found every cable and followed it, making bundles so thick that the original cable is visible only where it enters a junction box. In places, several species occupy the same bracket: a cushion of dark moss, a fan of ribbon leaves, a web of white roots pressing outward through the moss.

All of it is submerged. The leaves hold themselves in positions that would be impossible in air—extended horizontally for meters, or rising straight from their stems in soft, unsupported columns. Some are thin enough to reveal the entire branching system inside them. Others have a waxy, blue-black surface on which the window light rests without penetrating.

There are no fish among the stems. Nothing grazes the moss or nibbles the roots. No small body darts away when a leaf shifts. The abundance has no accompanying bustle.

Old growth is collected mechanically. Beneath each terrace, toothed combs advance along tracks so slowly that their motion can only be established by returning hours later. They draw fallen leaves into narrow slots. Behind the wall, the leaves are reduced to a brown liquor and fed back into the planting plates. Occasionally a severed ribbon of green lies across a comb and remains there all afternoon, its free end describing a very small arc.

The machinery is everywhere, but almost nothing sounds.

Motors sit inside thick housings embedded in the chamber walls. Their shafts turn through oil-filled seals. A governor wheel beside the entrance takes several days to complete a revolution; there is a white index mark on its rim, and a row of dates penciled on the casing. The chamber’s pressure is regulated by broad metal diaphragms that flex less than the thickness of a leaf. You might hear one settle: a low, brief note, felt along the teeth rather than heard clearly.

Most of the time there is only the soft contact of foliage against foliage.

Daylight enters from above and through three high windows facing the open sky. It is ordinary daylight, bright enough to distinguish the reddish stains around the bolt heads and the yellow margins of a sick leaf. Clouds passing outside alter it gently. The water carries it down through every gap, so that even the lowest level is not dark. There, illumination arrives broken into rectangular patches by the stairs overhead.

The lowest level is also where the water is youngest.

Behind a grille, rows of small nozzles manufacture it one clear bead at a time. Each bead remains attached for a while, swelling with extraordinary patience, then releases into the surrounding water. Nothing distinguishes the new water except its slight warmth and the way nearby roots incline toward it.

The plant beds supply something the nozzles require. This is stated on several enamel signs, although the substance is named only by a factory code. The plants cannot live without the manufactured water; the water cannot be made without the plants. Storage tanks occupy the wall cavities, sufficient to keep either side alive while the other is repaired.

Beside the tanks are spare doorframes.

They are the most carefully maintained objects in the chamber. Each has its own rack, its own serial number, its own padded clamps. A frame holds the water where a wall cannot: along an opening, across a missing pane, around the arm of a maintenance worker. Cracks in the masonry are harmless until they acquire a complete outline. An enclosed shape is an invitation for the water to stand somewhere new.

For this reason, none of the railings form closed loops. Their corners stop a finger’s width short of joining. The root baskets have one side left unfastened. Workers inspect climbing stems before they can circle a pipe and graft back into themselves.

High in the warm chamber, beyond the last accessible stair, a vine has done exactly that.

Its ring is no wider than a dinner plate. Inside it, a second body of water hangs at a slight angle to the first. You can recognize the difference by the bend in a stem seen through it, and by a trapped bubble that has spent months moving toward a different idea of up.

Below, the combs continue their slow passage. The windows brighten. A fresh leaf unfolds against a numbered valve, covering the number one digit at a time.
