The upland begins above the clouds, but there is no light above them.

A second weather lies higher still: a depth of suspended mineral dust that takes the sun out of the sky. At midday there may be a faint copper thinning overhead, enough to distinguish a ridge from the darkness behind it. Most of the time the country is known by its nearer surfaces—the dull sheen on a stone, the pale underside of an animal turning, the brief blue thread of a discharge passing between two standing rocks.

The ground is warm. Even on the highest divides, where the air is thin enough to make flight difficult, warmth comes through the soles of the feet. It rises unevenly. A patch of flat gravel can be almost hot; a few steps away, a shallow hollow holds cool air and smells of damp clay.

There are thousands of miles of these uplands.

They are not mountains in the young, jagged sense. The ranges have worn down into enormous shoulders, separated by troughs so broad that the far side is usually lost to distance rather than weather. Rock ribs lean through the soil at low angles. Escarpments descend in rounded stages, their old fractures packed with black sediment. Here and there a surviving summit stands above the general level, blunt and solitary, with a polished windward face.

Very little is loose. What could fall has mostly fallen. What remains moves slowly, in flakes, in grains, in long compressions that buckle a slope by the width of a hand over a season.

Beneath this settled surface, the land is still working.

Hot brine travels through deep beds of porous ironstone. In some places it descends far enough to boil, and its return drives a circulation through chambers hundreds of miles apart. Mineral valves form where the passages narrow: flexible deposits that swell when heated, close a throat, cool, and reopen it. Their cycles overlap. Pressure accumulates under one province while another empties. Electrical charge follows the brine through conductive seams.

The whole plateau runs like a buried industrial district, though nothing here was built.

There are pumping grounds, condenser slopes, regions of steady low-voltage current. There are basins where dissolved metals precipitate by the ton, settling into fine black beds beneath films of water. A pressure front can take months to cross the upland, announcing itself first as warmth, then as a taste of copper in the air, then as the opening of vents that have been shut for years.

But at the surface it is mostly quiet.

A vent may breathe without a hiss. Warm air rises from a crack wide enough to put a hand into, carrying no visible steam until it reaches colder air well overhead. Occasionally, from somewhere under a hillside, comes a single heavy knock. It has too much depth to seem nearby. Loose pebbles tremble, stop, and retain their places.

Water is scarce enough that its signs are more common than water itself. There are pale mineral lips around dry depressions, dark tracks down the shaded sides of boulders, leaves arranged to catch condensation from their own stems. Rain falls in narrow districts. A pressure change beneath the ground can feed a column of damp air that climbs, cools, and rains back on the same twenty miles for half a night. Beyond its edge, the dust remains undisturbed.

The rain is warm and tastes faintly metallic.

For several days afterward, shallow water occupies the rock pans. Small animals arrive from distances that seem disproportionate to their size. They emerge from cracks, from beneath stone plates, from tunnels whose mouths were invisible while dry. Some are no longer than a fingernail. Others unfold jointed legs until they stand knee-high above the puddles.

There is much more life here than the first view suggests.

The large emptiness between shrubs is crossed continually, but not conspicuously. Broad-footed grazers move in dispersed herds, each animal keeping tens of yards from the next. Their coats resemble the ground closely enough that a whole slope can begin walking before the eye understands it was occupied. They feed on brittle stems, on the pale growth beneath lifted stones, and on mineral crusts softened by recent damp.

They also gather above the conductive seams.

There they lower the bare pads beneath their throats to the earth and remain still for hours. The current does not feed them by itself; it sustains the colonies living in the chambers of their stomachs, helping them make use of food too poor for an ordinary grazing animal. Along a good seam, several hundred may be resting in a line that extends beyond sight.

Their young sleep between their forelegs. Predators patrol the outskirts.

Nothing roars. A cry would travel a long way in this open country, and most creatures have reasons not to advertise their position. The common sounds are a soft dragging of hide over gravel, the clicking of mouthparts, an occasional blunt exhalation. Courtship happens through vibrations passed into the ground. At the right hour, a palm laid flat on a warm slab can feel a whole assembly that cannot be heard.

Vegetation grows where the underground cycles are dependable. Low trees, widely spaced, carry thick, nearly colorless leaves around dark trunks. Their crowns are narrow, saving moisture, and their roots reach much farther sideways than the branches do. Some live partly by dim light. Others live in partnership with organisms that consume what the deep water brings up.

A grove may consist of fifteen trees spread across a mile.

Under each is a distinct small climate: cooler stone, a little retained humidity, fallen leaves worked into something approaching soil. Pale burrowers keep the root zones open. Beetles graze the bark. Flat-bodied hunters wait where the canopy’s condensation lands, each beneath a drip it has learned to expect.

Between groves there are stretches where even the crust of low growth disappears. The surface becomes smooth, dark, and faintly greasy with mineral dust. These are the long crossings. Migrating animals move through them at an unhurried pace, guided by temperature, slope, and the electrical grain of the ground. Sometimes several migrations intersect without mingling. Small creatures pass beneath the bellies of large ones. A predator follows one column for days, then leaves it for another.

Above them, broad-winged animals ride the invisible heat.

Their wings scarcely beat. In the darkness they are noticed when they interrupt a patch of slightly lighter sky, or when one banks close enough for the warm air to carry down its musky smell. They descend at the temporary pools, fold themselves into surprisingly compact shapes, and drink with their faces submerged.

The deepest valleys contain cloud rather than rivers. From the upland edge, this lower weather appears as a slow, pale movement far below. Once in a while it rises high enough to spill over a saddle. Fog creeps across the warm ground, beads on every exposed surface, and vanishes before it has traveled far. For an hour afterward the sparse shrubs glitter whenever the rocks discharge.

There is no single season for the whole country. The distances are too great, and the buried systems keep different time. One region is cooling while another grows feverishly warm. A basin fills; a vent field falls dormant; animals abandon a familiar route because the current beneath it has shifted east.

On a quiet slope, the first sign of such a change may be almost nothing: several grazers lifting their heads at once.

Then, over the next few nights, more arrive. They pause, test the ground, and continue toward a ridge that has stood empty for generations. Far beneath it, a passage is opening. The stone is not yet damp. The trees have not responded. But already the small animals are coming out, and the dark air above the ridge is beginning to rise.
