The city occupies a shelf of high ground just below the mountain’s split crown. From its outermost wall, you can see the whole basin: pale terraces, three wooded gullies, an exposed ridge with a stair cut into its side, and, beyond these, the blue edges of other heights. Nothing is softened by distance until those far mountains. Nearer stone has the clean corners of something recently broken.

The ground seems to have risen almost intact. Beds of dark rock stand upright through the pale soil, bringing their buried stripes into daylight. In places, a crack crosses a courtyard, passes beneath a wall, and continues on the other side with its edges still matched. The builders have spanned these openings rather than filled them. Short bridges of peeled timber cross gaps narrow enough to step over. Wider fissures have stone lintels, worn smooth by the mason’s abrasive rather than by feet.

It is a city, unmistakably, but one with considerable room inside it.

There are streets broad enough for two carts, retaining walls, public stairs, drainage channels, long storehouses divided into bays. There are districts separated by stretches of open ground. A line of houses follows one contour for half a mile; above it, a solitary workshop stands in a paved square. The next inhabited terrace begins beyond a slope of grass and exposed bedrock. From a distance, the city looks less like a concentration of buildings than like an agreement between scattered buildings about where the streets should go.

Its materials remain close to their origins. Walls are stacked from flat stone, with the irregular ends facing inward. Door frames are heavy wood. Roofs are bark beneath turf, or overlapping stone slabs held down with smaller stones. Copper appears sparingly: a rivet, a hinge pin, the thin green lip of a basin. Most things are secured by pegs, lashings, weight, or careful fit.

Yet the construction is exact. A channel no wider than a thumb runs along the uphill edge of every lane. At each junction it enters a settling bowl carved in the paving. Staircases have shallow side grooves for dragging loads. Storage rooms are raised above the ground on mushroom-shaped stone supports. The public cistern has a broad roof and four separate approaches, so that drawing water need not obstruct the street. Simple tools have made a complicated place.

The light is clear and ordinary. It comes from a sun held well above the western ridge, bright enough to show the fibers in a rope and the green beneath a leaf. The air is cool. Snow occupies a few deep cuts near the mountain crown, but the city itself is not frozen. Its shaded walls are damp to the touch.

Moisture has gathered selectively. Most of the basin is dry underfoot, a mixture of fine grit, short turf, and slabs that ring faintly under a heel. Then a staircase turns between two walls and enters a luxuriant seam of green. Ferns fill the joint between paving and masonry. Broad leaves spread beneath a public bench. Moss hangs over the lip of a trough in thick, blunt tongues.

The gullies contain the fullest growth: close-set little trees with pale trunks, glossy shrubs, low beds of flowers almost concealed by leaves. Their roots occupy every pocket of soil between the new stone faces. Higher up, vegetation becomes scattered but remains vigorous. An isolated bush can be so dense that no branch is visible inside it. A cushion plant on bare rock holds hundreds of tiny flowers, each open.

There is no struggling growth here. There is only growth, at the extent it has reached.

This becomes noticeable first in the water.

Under the cistern roof, a bead hangs from the wooden delivery spout. It is swollen almost beyond attachment. The paving beneath it is dry. Farther down the street, a basin holds a shallow sheet of water with a corrugated surface, as though a breeze has passed across it; the ridges keep their exact shape. At the edge of one gully, a spring extends a clear, curved finger over a rock. Through that finger, the moss behind it appears enlarged.

No drop falls. No wet patch spreads.

The city is not awaiting a thaw or the end of a spell. There is no strain in its stillness, no stored movement pressing to be released. The water has this shape in the same complete way that the stone has its shape.

So do the animals.

They are abundant once the eye begins finding them. Small horned grazers occupy the grassy ground between districts. One stands with its muzzle deep in a flowering shrub. Another has a forefoot lifted above a slab, its weight distributed with exquisite confidence among the other three. Lizards lie along warm wall tops. There are beetles beneath translucent leaves, burrows with bright eyes at their mouths, nests tucked under the eaves of houses.

A flock of dark birds occupies the air above the lower square. They are spaced irregularly, some with wings extended and others folded close, each feather sharply visible. Beneath them, their shadows lie across the paving, the roof of a stall, the shoulder of a person carrying a basket.

The inhabitants are sparse enough that a street can be followed for a long distance without encountering one. Their clothes are wool, felt, and soft hide, fastened with bone toggles. A mason kneels beside a partly fitted threshold. Two people stand opposite each other at a grain counter, with a shallow measure resting between their hands. On a terrace overlooking the basin, a child leans against a grazer whose thick neck fur has parted beneath the child’s fingers.

None looks vacant. Attention is everywhere: in the angle of a head, the close regard given to a stone joint, the slight tightening around an eye facing the sun. But attention does not arrive at a conclusion. Hunger does not increase. The lifted foot does not become a step.

The unfinished work belongs here as securely as the completed buildings. A half-woven panel hangs on a loom under an awning. Its loose threads extend downward without tangling. Beside a new retaining wall, baskets of aggregate stand in a neat row. There is no future in which that row will shorten, and no need for one. Even incompleteness has its settled place.

It is mostly quiet. In the roofed market there is a low, continuous tone, too soft to identify as a voice, audible only because the surrounding silence is so broad. Near the trees, something like the finest part of insect song occupies the air. Neither sound pulses or changes pitch. There are no calls and answers, no footsteps returning from an alley, no drip in the cistern’s hollow chamber.

From the highest public stair, the city’s arrangement becomes clear. Its open spaces are not gaps left for expansion. They are part of its construction: a meadow between warehouses, bare rock within a precinct wall, an entire unbuilt slope framed by two converging streets. The houses face these spaces. Benches face them. Narrow windows have been placed to admit views of a single tree or the pale side of the mountain.

On one such bench lies a folded cloak. Beside it is a cup, half full. The cup’s small shadow reaches precisely to a joint in the stone seat.

Beyond it, the basin remains wide, green in its sheltered seams and bright across its naked ridges. The young cliffs retain every sharp projection. The roofs retain their turf. Over the lower square, each bird has all the sky it needs.
