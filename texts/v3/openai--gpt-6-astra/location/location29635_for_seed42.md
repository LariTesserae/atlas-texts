The town occupies a drowned saddle between two mountain peaks. Above it, through six or seven fathoms of clear water, the peaks appear as dark interruptions in the sky. Clouds pass behind them. At noon there is enough light to see the grain of a wooden latch, though anything red has become brown, and the distances have the green-blue color of old window glass.

You can swim across the town in a few minutes. Crossing it on foot, with a stone belt and a hand on the guide ropes, takes considerably longer.

There are three streets, a square, and a steep passage that used to lead down to the lower pastures. That passage now ends at the lip of the saddle, where the lake becomes too deep for rooted plants. Its last steps hang out over blue water. Nothing marks the edge except a handrail, renewed so many times that it has the thickness and irregularity of an old vine.

Almost everything else is growing.

The original roofs survive beneath layers of pondweed. Willow roots have entered the walls, followed the joints, and thickened around the stones until a house can lose half its masonry without changing shape. Along the eaves, aquatic moss makes soft ledges deep enough to bury an arm. Rootlets descend through former chimneys. The square is paved with a close, springy turf of short water grass, clipped by snails and the blunt mouths of fish.

Only where feet repeatedly settle does the town’s old material show: a pale threshold, the corner of a stair, a strip of slate worn smooth beside a doorway. These exposed places are rounded. There is nothing recently broken here.

Despite the vegetation, the town feels spacious. The larger plants stand well apart. Each has claimed a house or a section of street, sending its leaves upward into a separate crown near the surface. Between their trunks lie clear lanes, broad enough for two swimmers to pass without touching. The plants spend most of their substance on the floor, the walls, the undersides of bridges. What looks at first like empty water is a room lined on every side with life.

The town is still used by the people who live on its floating roofs.

Their homes are low reed shelters fastened to rafts of living root, directly above the houses of their grandparents’ grandparents. Each shelter has a central opening in its floor. A ladder goes down through it into the submerged building below. From beneath, these openings are squares of bright, shaking light, sometimes crossed by a bare foot or the dark oval of a basket.

The people have bone awls, stone weights, a few iron knives kept carefully greased. They mend the underwater town with split branches and braided sedge. Loose walls are not rebuilt stone by stone; roots are guided around them, tied into place, and left to thicken. A damaged bridge may take several seasons to repair, during which everyone uses another street.

Some rooms contain air under their steep roofs. Divers surface there to rest, their heads entering a close darkness that smells of wet timber and crushed leaves. The air is brought down in inverted clay jars and released beneath the beams. These chambers are small and practical: somewhere to catch a breath, speak a few words, hang a lamp while working on a knot. Below each one, a rope bears a sequence of wooden toggles so that a swimmer can find the opening by touch.

The water is cold enough that work is brief. Hands lose their accuracy first. People descend in pairs, accomplish one task, and climb back toward the warmer surface. A morning’s labor might leave only a new binding around a root or a basket of snails hanging beneath a raft.

But the lake is never quiet.

Beyond the saddle, out of sight, water falls through a long cleft in the mountain. Its roar enters the basin through the rock and fills the town from below. In the square it is a broad, continuous thunder. Inside a house it changes pitch, gathering in the corners, humming through a surviving lintel. Put a palm against a stone wall and the sound becomes a faint pressure in the wrist.

Closer noises are distinct against it: a pole striking a mooring, the hollow knock of a jar, gravel rattling from a basket. Fish feeding on the walls make dry little scraping sounds. When someone walks overhead, the raft gives a deep wooden groan.

Nothing visible seems to account for so much noise. The leaves barely move. A strand of weed released from a hand may take several minutes to drift across a doorway.

Animals occupy the town at every scale. Shoals of small silver fish pause beneath the bridges, facing a current too slight to feel. Larger, dark fish rest in the upper stories, emerging slowly when a diver disturbs the silt. Crayfish inhabit the drains. Freshwater sponges cover the shaded faces of beams, and tiny translucent creatures rise from them whenever a sleeve brushes past.

Along the old market wall, the snails are so numerous that their grazing leaves pale trails through the green. The people harvest only alternate sections of wall. The untouched sections seed the cleared ones again.

An otter sometimes comes through the town at speed, turning corners with an ease no swimmer can imitate. For a moment the streets scatter into motion. Then the fish return to their stations, and the stirred particles settle into the moss.

At the lower end of the square stands a tree that was already old when the saddle flooded. Its crown is gone. Its trunk has become a hollow column, sheathed in roots from the living raft above it. A stair winds around it, with broad landings at the doors of three houses. Nobody can now say which parts are tree, which are construction, and which were added merely to support something else.

Near its base, someone has cut a shallow niche and placed a smooth white stone inside. Divers touch it as they pass. The stone stays clean.

Late in the day, sunlight enters between the peaks and reaches the steep passage at the town’s edge. For a little while, each step is visible beneath its short green covering. Beyond the handrail there is only depth.

Up in the roofs, a basket is lowered. It descends very slowly, turning on its rope, while fish gather beneath it to investigate the crumbs falling through the weave.
