The garden occupies a hollow near the summit, no wider than a village square. Above its broken rim there is only a brown sky and the undersides of clouds that never open. The country below cannot be seen. Sometimes a pale scrap blows up from it—a wing case, a flake of stone, something too worn to identify—and goes circling in the hollow for days.

It is warm here, even in the shade. Put a hand against the ground and the warmth comes slowly through the skin, steady as the heat of an animal asleep under a blanket. The air smells of crushed seed, hot chalk, and the sharp resin released when a branch breaks. There is no scent of damp earth. There is no damp earth.

Most of the hollow is bare.

Three great masses of vegetation stand apart from one another on the mineral floor, each lush enough to conceal a house. Between them lie broad, empty lanes of cream-colored rock, swept almost clean. From the rim, the arrangement looks deliberate: three deep thickets, three open passages, a low stone structure against the northern wall. Down among the plants, nothing feels arranged. Everything is reaching, opening, folding, climbing over something else.

Leaves hang in long overlapping scales, dull purple on their upper surfaces and pale gold underneath. Some are as broad as a sleeping mat. Others divide into such fine filaments that whole branches look furred. Thick creepers braid themselves around porous trunks. Fans of stiff growth flare from the roots, and beneath the fans are smaller plants with translucent, inflated leaves. Through those leaves you can see grains moving, rising against gravity in narrow streams.

None of this growth drinks.

The roots enter seams in the stone and pry out a soft yellow mineral. They grind it, draw it upward, and distribute it through their tissues as a warm, mobile dust. The leaves are chambers rather than blades, packed with thin partitions. A cut stem does not bleed. It exhales, shrivels a little, and seals its wound with a bead of amber-colored wax.

There are places where the exposed roots have polished the rock through centuries of small movements. The stone is grooved and rounded, not freshly shattered. Old hollows hold the black husks of roots that exhausted one seam and withdrew into another. The garden has shifted around the basin many times, but slowly enough that the worn paths of its former positions remain legible.

Its upper parts are much less patient.

A gust enters over the western lip and all three thickets turn gold at once. Leaves flip, creepers tighten, huge fans snap shut. The sound is like a hundred heavy rugs being beaten. Then the gust strikes the opposite wall, folds back on itself, and returns at ground level. Dust runs through the open lanes. Seed cases tear loose. Something shrieks inside a crown of purple foliage, and fifty other things answer.

The hollow is rarely quiet enough to hear a footstep.

There are the dry detonations of ripe pods, the wooden knocking of hollow limbs, the continuous rasp of animals feeding beneath bark. There are low notes made by air passing through trunks, and higher ones from the creatures that live in them. A single gust can make the basin sound crowded with horns. When the wind briefly drops, the smaller noises become astonishingly clear: claws ticking on leaves, jaws crushing mineral, a busy crackle from thousands of jointed bodies moving through fallen husks.

The animals are everywhere, although at first they resemble parts of the plants.

What looks like a fringe of dead leaves along a branch lifts its heads together. Each animal has a flattened body, six gripping feet, and a long ribbed throat it inflates to call. Their cries are harsh and metallic. They are defending the branch from others of their own kind, which arrive continually, carried sideways through the air on stiff membranes.

On the ground, pale burrowers travel beneath the loose drifts of husks. Their backs briefly raise moving ridges. They eat the wax shed from wounded stems and are followed by narrow, many-legged hunters that run with their bodies held high, scarcely touching the surface. Larger creatures wait in the bare lanes: squat, chalk-colored animals with knuckled forelimbs and closed eyes. They appear asleep until a seed case rolls within reach. Then they strike hard enough to knock fragments against the basin wall.

Some of the seed cases escape by opening.

They split into three or four curled vanes, catch the next gust, and wheel upward. Inside each is a dark kernel and a small animal, tucked together in a nest of fibers. The animal gnaws its way free only after landing. It carries the kernel into a crack for shelter; the kernel begins to root around it. Many of the garden’s trunks have an occupied chamber at their center. On warm evenings, soft tapping comes from inside the oldest wood.

The empty lanes are not empty because nothing can grow there. They are where the wind does its work.

Air pours into the hollow from several directions, although the clouds above seem barely to move. It races along the stone, collides, rises, falls. Some currents recur so faithfully that the plants have grown around them, leaving narrow tunnels through their foliage. Others wander. A calm patch may cross the basin like a large invisible body, announced by leaves drooping one after another. Behind it comes a blow violent enough to lift a mat of creepers off the ground.

You learn to watch the small things. Filaments stiffening on a leaf mean a downward current is coming. The chalk-colored seed eaters flatten themselves before a crosswind. If the trunk horns all fall silent together, get low: the next movement of air will come from beneath.

There are fissures in the floor. Most are too narrow to admit a finger, but they descend into the heated mountain, and at intervals the mountain exhales.

The first sign is an orange powder trembling out of the cracks. Then the entire hollow lifts into motion. Fallen leaves rise edgewise. Animals cling to roots, tuck into crevices, or deliberately let go. A column of husks, seeds, winged bodies, and torn vegetation climbs toward the sky, turning slowly at its center and furiously at its edges. For a few breaths the garden extends upward almost as far as it spreads across the ground.

This is when the tallest plants flower.

Their crowns open in the rising dust. Pale structures unfold from between the leaves, long and branching, so thin that they are scarcely visible until pollen settles on them. The flowers remain open only while the warm updraft holds them apart. Creatures scramble across them in frantic numbers. Some feed. Some lay eggs. Some are trapped when the air weakens and the branches fold again.

Afterward, pieces come down for a long time.

The little building against the northern wall has no roof in the usual sense. Its upper stones overlap inward, leaving a narrow slot that points away from the strongest wind. It is built of rounded blocks gathered from the hollow, fitted with smaller flakes and bound here and there by old root cord. Inside are two stone benches, a shallow hearth full of white ash, and a rack of wooden tools weighted at their handles so they cannot blow away.

There is a hooked pole for drawing branches down. There are stone wedges polished by use, baskets woven so closely that they hold dust, and a mallet with a head of dense black rootwood. Above the benches hang folded garments made from the inner skins of leaves. They are light, tough, and faintly translucent. In a gust they rustle even inside the shelter.

Whoever tends this place works by cutting and tying, not by planting rows. Short lengths of cord hold young creepers away from the wind lanes. A split branch has been braced with a shaped piece of bone. Near one thicket, three stones mark a root seam that must not be disturbed. The marks are old enough to have sunk slightly into the worn surface, but the cord on the branch is new.

There are no vessels for water. The largest pots have narrow necks and fitted stone lids; they contain yellow mineral meal, wax curls, and seeds that tick softly against the pottery when the air changes. A drinking bowl would be an incomprehensible object here. A bowl is for catching what falls from a pod before it scatters.

Beyond the shelter, a few steps have been cut into the basin wall. They lead to a notch in the rim, where the noise becomes almost unbearable. From there you can see how little ground the garden has: a shallow pocket in an immense shoulder of rock, the three leafy crowns crouched within it, the tiny shelter dark against the stone. The outer slope drops steeply into a dim atmosphere full of drifting grit. No distant green answers this green.

Yet the hollow does not feel precarious to its inhabitants. Their pathways fit its turbulence. Their nests occupy the places where two violent currents cancel. The oldest trunk is hollowed into a shape the wind cannot easily break, and the smallest animals know when to release their grip.

As the sky darkens, the foliage lowers and spreads. Broad leaves overlap the smaller growths. Golden undersides disappear. The garden becomes three nearly black presences with wide spaces between them, and the warm rock gives back the faint light it has kept.

The noise changes rather than diminishes. Day calls stop; the buried animals begin scraping. Hard seed shells roll in circles along the empty lanes. Somewhere inside the northern thicket a limb rubs against another limb with a deep, irregular booming.

Then orange dust starts to tremble in a fissure beside the steps. In the dimness, one crown opens a little before the others.
