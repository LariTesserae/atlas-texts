The town occupies a shallow depression, no wider than a few minutes’ walk, though crossing it is not always possible in a morning. From the rim you can see almost the whole place: pale streets, an open square, three low terraces of dwellings, and the broad, empty chamber above them where a roof ought to be.

Everything solid is vegetable.

The streets are exposed runners, flattened by their own growth into long, slightly yielding bands. The houses are hollow knots. Their doorways have the thick, rounded lips of old pruning wounds, although nothing here has ever been cut. Steps have grown where a body would need steps; shallow gutters follow the edges of the lanes; roots arch over intersections with the regularity of bridges. There is a civic order to it. Approaching the square, the street broadens. Beside every doorway there is a recess deep enough to shelter someone from rain.

No one built these accommodations. They are part of the plant’s anatomy, as orderly as the chambers of a heart.

Nor does much appear to live in them.

The spaces are extraordinarily bare. No undergrowth crowds the lanes. No canopy fills the overhead light. Across the square, a single hanging tendril seems almost indecently conspicuous. You can stand in the entrance of a dwelling and see through four more dwellings, their empty rooms connected by oval openings, all the way to a patch of warm sky. A few pale leaves lie flat against a wall, so closely fitted that they resemble repairs.

The vegetation has not filled the air. It has taken over everything on either side of it.

Underfoot, there is no soil. A split in the pavement reveals older pavement, then a soft dark layer, then another curve of living wood. The town has been growing through its own remains for so long that its lowest streets are compressed into grain. Here and there a door survives as a narrow black line in a wall. Rain has rounded the exposed surfaces; passing feet, though you rarely see them, have polished the thresholds to the color of green olives. Even the newest growth comes out weathered, bearing the grooves of water that has not yet run over it.

The air is warm. Moisture gathers selectively.

One stretch of street will remain powder-dry through an afternoon of rain, while a sheltered room nearby sweats from every surface. Droplets collect on the ceiling and hang until they are the size of plums. Then, with no warning, they run sideways into a seam. There are small pools in the square, each in its own smooth basin, none more than an arm’s length across. Their water is clear, faintly amber, and very still despite the continuous vibration of the ground.

It is difficult to notice the stillness at first because of the noise.

The town roars.

Not with wind, and not with any single voice. A dry, tremendous rattling pours out of the walls. Beneath it comes a bass concussion, several times a second, felt in the breastbone and in the roots of the teeth. There is a shrill rolling whistle overhead. Something clicks so rapidly that its separate strokes become a hard, shining sheet of sound. At intervals, a bellow passes beneath the square, and all the water in the basins rises into low transparent domes.

You cannot hear your footsteps. You cannot hear yourself call.

Then you begin to see the animals.

A hairline in a doorway opens into a row of mouths. What looked like a scar across a bridge is a procession of small, flat-backed creatures moving flank to flank, their bodies exactly matching the bark. Under the lip of a gutter, hundreds of translucent legs paddle through a film of condensation. Moths, almost without thickness, cling edge-on to the walls. The shadows beneath the steps contain soft animals folded into shapes too narrow for their apparent size.

Their numbers are immense, but they occupy the margins. The center of every room remains empty.

They feed, mate, hunt, and fight along the surfaces, or just inside them. Predators travel beneath the green skin of the street as visible swellings. A swelling pauses under a cluster of tiny grazers; the pavement dimples; several grazers disappear. The survivors continue scraping. Higher up, a long-jawed animal works its way along a cornice, pulling strips of sweet tissue from the wall. The wound closes behind it, but slowly enough that smaller creatures follow to drink.

Much of the roaring comes from this life pressed flat around the hollows. Some of it comes from elsewhere.

A beetle-sized creature rubs two plates together beside your hand, and its call arrives from the far terrace with the force of collapsing timber. A throat bulges beneath a windowsill. The answering note comes out of a pool. Distances do not quite hold here: adjacent surfaces may be far apart, while two empty rooms on opposite sides of town may share the same interior.

You discover this by looking through a doorway and seeing the back of your own head.

Turn around, and there is only the square.

Wait, and the figure in the doorway turns a little later.

These misalignments are ordinary features of the place. Animals use them. A small white hunter springs into an empty alcove and emerges from a drain across the square, already chewing. Flying creatures rarely cross the open air directly. They enter folds beneath the eaves and reappear where the light is more favorable. Once in a while, a whole flock goes through the wrong opening and spills out of the same doorway for several minutes, though only a dozen birds entered.

The town accommodates this without visible strain. Its hollow spaces are older than their current boundaries.

Along the lowest terrace is a lane that periodically becomes a watercourse. There is no river upstream. First the paving darkens. Then beads of moisture rise through it, join, and begin flowing toward the square. Blind silver animals come with the water, unfolding from dry crevices where they have waited compressed and motionless. They crowd the shallow current, rasping at the street, pursuing one another around its raised seams.

For perhaps an hour, this is the busiest part of town. Water slaps against thresholds. Broad leaves unroll from the gutters and hold themselves over the flow. Something large moves underneath, forcing the entire lane upward in a traveling hump.

Then the current thins.

The silver animals flatten. Their fins tuck away. They slip between layers of the pavement as the last water drains into the roots. By evening, the lane is dry enough to hold dust, except that the dust is pollen and shifts away from your feet.

Nothing here is abandoned, although nearly everything has the spaciousness of abandonment.

The empty rooms are actively maintained. Small browsing animals keep the walls smooth. Leaves turn their growing edges away from doorways. When a tendril extends too far into a chamber, it begins to vibrate, darkens, and withdraws. No branch is allowed to bridge the square. The plant repeatedly attempts it, sending out exploratory green fingers after rain, but each attempt stops at the same invisible boundary.

This restraint gives the place its strange grandeur. It is a little settlement with the interior volume of something much larger. The square feels less like an opening among buildings than a carefully preserved breath.

Late in the day, the terraces begin to shift.

The movement is slow enough to miss until a doorway no longer faces where it faced before. A bridge lowers its crown. Steps become shallower. The basins in the square tilt toward one another, but their water stays level with a horizon you cannot see. Across the upper rim, warm light illuminates a membrane that was invisible all afternoon: a nearly transparent sheet stretched high above the town, pulsing outward.

The open chamber has a roof after all.

As it expands, the air cools slightly. Condensation stipples the membrane, revealing its vast fine veins. Every animal along the walls begins to call at once. The roar becomes so complete that it seems to remove the distinction between sound and touch. Doorways tremble. Water lifts from the basins in branching threads.

For a moment, the whole town is preparing to say something.

Then the membrane relaxes. A brief warm rain falls through the empty air. The streets drink most of it before it can run. Animals emerge along every seam, shining and ravenous.

The square remains unoccupied.
