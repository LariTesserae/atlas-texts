The city is beneath a plain of black stone, though almost no stone is visible from inside it. Everything facing the open air is vegetable: the pale floor, the ribbed undersides of bridges, the long vault overhead. Even the dust consists of tiny scales shed by growing surfaces. It gathers in the lee of buildings, curls when disturbed, and eventually fastens itself somewhere to begin again.

There is no water running through this place. No gutters, no wells, no dark seepage at the foot of a wall. The air is cool enough to sting a bare nose, and so dry that cloth crackles when unfolded. What circulates inside the plants is a clear, oily fluid with a resinous smell. A broken stem does not bleed freely. Its contents draw back from the wound, and the edges press together with a faint adhesive click.

From the southern galleries, the city appears to occupy the bottom of an enormous, shallow bowl. You can see several kilometers across it. Broad avenues divide low districts of ivory and gray-green, with occasional towers rising like the unopened flower stalks of some immense crop. Between districts lie empty expanses, their surfaces faintly corrugated, as if someone has pulled a comb through soft wax. Nothing grows out of these expanses because they are already growing. The ground itself thickens, stretches, and lays down the foundations of neighborhoods that have not yet arrived.

The tallest buildings stand well apart. Each has room for its full shadow.

Overhead, far above the tower tips, the ceiling produces an even white light. It is lined with small, overlapping leaves whose undersides shine when fed by currents moving through their veins. The effect is closer to an overcast afternoon than to a cave. There are brighter patches where new tissue is thin, and darker bands where the vault has doubled itself for strength. These bands travel slowly over the city as the roof grows. A square that has enjoyed steady light for years may enter a long, mild shade, and its inhabitants move their seed racks to another side of the street.

There is no visible sun to look toward. At the appointed hour, the ceiling dims by districts.

***

The streets are not paved. They are trained.

Their surface is a continuous mat of short, hard fibers, all bent in the direction of travel. Walking with the grain feels almost frictionless; walking against it produces a dry rasp underfoot. Freight lanes have deeper grain and raised edges. At junctions, workers use heated rollers to persuade the fibers into new alignments. The rollers move slowly, carried on six or eight articulated legs so that they do not mark the portions already finished.

A new intersection smells of toasted seeds.

Most industrial work takes place inside the thicker buildings, where their living walls absorb vibration. A factory may announce itself only by a slight warmth in the pavement and a row of ventilation pods opening along its roof. Through those openings comes a breath of pale dust, then nothing. Inside, insulated shafts turn. Presses mold structural rind. Banks of narrow vessels cultivate conducting fibers, sorting them by the speed at which a signal travels through them.

The largest works grows bridge joints.

Its yard contains dozens of enormous, unfinished hinges, rooted upright in evenly spaced beds. Each is a forked piece of pale wood with a dark elastic membrane between the branches. They flex a little as they mature. Workers pass among them with measuring hoops, testing resistance, trimming side buds, encouraging one hinge to widen and another to stiffen. Beyond the yard, completed joints hang in the cool air to become dormant.

Machines here are not all alive. There are ceramic bearings, metal cutters, narrow steel rails. Such materials are precious partly because they stay the same size.

You notice the industrial order before you notice the people: numbered growth beds, freight signals, inspection strips, access doors kept clear of buds. Then a tram slips across an elevated causeway, its motor barely audible, carrying a dozen passengers through what looked like an uninhabited district. Far below it, three maintenance workers stand beside a seam in the ground, waiting for it to finish opening.

Much of the city is waiting in this attentive way.

***

The bowl is still being made.

Along its northern edge, the floor rises into low, blunt folds. Their surfaces are taut and unmarked. They have never carried a road or held an animal’s footprint. Underneath them, the subterranean vegetation pushes against the surrounding rock, loosening it grain by grain with fine root tips and electrical discharges too weak to see. Mineral powder is drawn inward, sorted, incorporated. The cavern expands without the crash of excavation.

Sometimes a whole slope advances during the night.

Nothing here looks ruined. There are few rounded corners, few worn hollows, no scree accumulating under ancient cliffs. Even the steep faces have the provisional look of something just taken from a mold. Where a structure is no longer wanted, it is not left to decay. Its useful compounds withdraw along the veins; its hollow shell folds inward and is pressed into the floor. For a season there will be a broad depression, slightly warmer than the neighboring street. Then that, too, levels out.

The city’s oldest district has disappeared three times without changing its address.

At the frontier, survey posts lean as new ground lifts them. Temporary walkways end above pale ridges not yet firm enough to bear weight. You can stand at a railing and watch a landscape acquire its first slope. It happens slowly enough that the eye doubts it, but a suspended plumb line shows the change.

Beneath your boots there is a fine, intermittent trembling, like the tension in a bow being adjusted.

***

Animals are easier to find if you sit still.

Small six-legged grazers emerge from grooves in the street and crop the loose fibers without harming the mat beneath. Their backs resemble split seed cases. They move in loose groups, stopping together whenever a shadow crosses them. Long-legged predators wait beside warm service conduits, almost indistinguishable from the discarded braces used to support young walls.

There are gliders in the upper air. They launch themselves from roof vents and ride the broad, slow convection currents above the factories, crossing entire neighborhoods without a wingbeat. Their bodies are light and nearly flat. Against the luminous ceiling, you see the darker knots of their organs through amber membranes.

Their calls are short wooden notes, widely spaced.

At times the ceiling releases pollen. It falls as a nearly invisible gray veil, and the gliders gather beneath it, turning on their edges to feed. The street animals climb doorframes and stand with their mouthparts raised. Filters slide across factory vents. Pedestrians pull soft masks from their collars and continue walking.

By evening, every horizontal ledge has acquired a fine silver line.

Most of this abundance remains inside the substrate. You hear it occasionally: a localized ticking in a wall, a scratch moving beneath a bench. Transparent inspection panels reveal busy channels just below the street, crowded with pale scavengers and beetlelike carriers hauling pellets toward deeper chambers. The great open spaces above them remain quiet. Life is dense in the thickness of things.

***

There are rules one learns quickly.

Do not leave untreated wood on the ground overnight. Do not block a warm seam, however inconveniently it crosses a room. Cuttings belong in the collection slots, never in household bins. A closed door that has begun to bud must be reported before the buds open.

Every neighborhood maintains several empty lots. These are not future building sites but places into which the surrounding structures can release excess growth. In a vigorous season, a lot may fill with a dense, low tangle of folded leaf and soft column. Harvest crews arrive before it hardens. They work with electrically heated wire, lifting long sections onto freight sledges while the ground draws tight behind them.

The crews scarcely speak. Their instruments indicate pressure, temperature, and the direction of the next surge. A good foreman can also read these things in the angle of an unfurled leaf.

Near the center of the city is a square kept entirely bare: a wide green-gray surface between the transport exchange and the civic nursery. Bare, here, means that no second layer is allowed to rise from the first. Children slide small wheeled toys across it. Grazers patrol its edges. On cool afternoons, people sit along the warm base of the exchange and watch freight trains emerge from the eastern tunnels.

Across the square, a new tower is being raised. Its lowest rooms are occupied already. Higher up, the windows are shallow impressions in a smooth sheath. The top is still soft, supported by a ring of temporary braces, and turns a few degrees over the course of the day as its tissues lengthen.

When the ceiling begins to dim, workers switch on amber lamps around that unfinished crown. The braces cast narrow shadows over the occupied windows below. Inside one of those windows, someone moves a chair away from a wall that has grown perceptibly since morning.
