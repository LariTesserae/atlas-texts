The rain reaches the plateau from below.

It climbs the escarpments as a warm spray, forced upward by winds that have crossed the lowland seas. At the rim it passes through hanging roots and the worn mouths of drainage tunnels, then spreads inland close to the ground. A person standing there feels rain under the chin, inside the sleeves, against the undersides of the hands. Farther inland, where the wind loses force, it becomes ordinary rain again. There is no clear boundary between the two conditions. Trees near the edge grow thick bark on both sides of their branches.

There is no light.

This is not a night that will finish. Above the plateau, above the whole broad back of the continent, lies an atmospheric depth through which light does not arrive. The cloud is warm at its lower surface and increasingly cold overhead. Occasionally something immense turns within it, and the pressure changes enough to make every pool tremble, but there is no lightning. Eyes are of little use here. Even the animals that retain them keep them covered beneath skin or translucent scales.

The country is known through contact, smell, and the behavior of sound.

Underfoot, the plateau is a succession of broad stone swells, shallow basins, and valleys worn almost smooth by an age of water. There are few sharp summits. Ridges that elsewhere might be knife-edged have become long, blunt shoulders, their rock buried in a greasy skin of clay and roots. A stream may spend a hundred miles descending so gently that its direction can only be established by floating something in it. Then it finds an old fracture and vanishes into a depth from which the sound returns much later, altered and accompanied.

There are basins large enough to hold separate weather. One fills with hot, still fog while the next is swept by a wind that flattens the marsh stems. Between them stand low divides, scarcely higher than the trees. Crossing one can mean stepping from a downpour into air crowded with dry wings.

Much of the vegetation feeds on what the rain carries.

The principal trees have no leaves in the familiar sense. Their crowns are masses of soft, ribbed vanes that comb the damp air for mineral dust and the oily spores lifted from the seas below. Beneath them, black mats spread over stone, digesting the rock slowly enough that individual colonies outlast rivers. Other growths draw warmth from the ground. Their roots descend through cracks toward the deep hot water, and their trunks are slightly warmer than a hand. In the still basins they grow close together, joined by ropes of tissue thick enough to trip a large animal.

Nothing stays dry for long. Seed casings swell against bark. Fleshy shelves overlap on fallen trunks. Strands of climbing growth trail from one crown into another and continue down into the pools, where a different set of small creatures grazes them. A dead tree does not leave an opening so much as change the traffic through a particular volume of darkness.

The abundance is easiest to understand by stopping.

First there is the rain, and beneath it the running water. Then the chewing becomes distinguishable: a steady rasp from the root mats, clicks from inside hollow stems, the soft, deliberate tearing of something stripping a trunk. Broad-backed animals travel the flooded hollows in groups. They test the bottom with jointed forelimbs before committing their weight, and communicate by striking the water with the flat of the throat. Their calls pass through adjoining pools and enter the ground.

Smaller animals listen from the banks. Some are waiting for the herds to loosen food. Some are waiting for a calf to hesitate.

The flying creatures are numerous but careful. They navigate by short bursts of sound, and their calls compete with rain, machinery, one another. Along the busiest valleys they have settled into distinct bands of pitch. Above a river, the air can seem like an enormous room full of squeaking hinges, with a deeper pulse beneath it from wings too large to beat quickly.

Predators exploit the gaps. One long-bodied hunter moves only when a nearby waterfall surges. Another lies against the warm trunks and answers the contact calls of young animals with almost perfect accuracy. Around its habitual trees, the herds have learned to touch noses before separating.

There are structures, though a traveler might cross several days of marsh and stone without encountering one.

They stand on the old divides: isolated engine houses, pumping stations, relay towers with no lamps. Their foundations are sunk into the soundest rock, and their upper walls are rounded by abrasion. Ceramic insulators are furred with damp growth. Heavy doors open onto floors scored for drainage. Cables leave through sealed apertures and descend into trenches that have long since become streams.

The industry is mostly underground.

Far beneath the root layer, turbines occupy chambers cut along the continent’s wet fractures. They take power from the descending water and use some of it to compress air, which is sent back through pipes to the scattered settlements. The rest drives machinery whose work arrives at the surface as vibration: rolling mills, boring heads, crushers, the slow reciprocation of pumps large enough to alter the level of a lake.

A single station may serve a territory wider than a small country. Its operation can be felt long before its walls are reached. Metal laid against the teeth gives a faint tremor. A shallow pool develops parallel wrinkles. At regular intervals, something under the ground draws a breath, and water sinks an inch around the roots before rising again.

The living landscape has accommodated these pulses. Certain filter colonies open only when the pumps reverse. Burrowing animals nest beside warm return pipes. Great mats of vegetation have accumulated over leaking joints, fed by minerals from depths no root could reach. When a line is shut down for repair, the change sets whole valleys moving.

Maintenance crews travel without lamps, following tensioned guide cables between stations. Their boots have broad, divided soles for gripping submerged stone. On their belts they carry probes, listening cups, and small hammers. They stop often to strike the ground. The echo tells them whether a culvert is clear, whether a foundation has shifted, whether the apparent bank ahead is stone or a raft of roots.

They also listen for a particular silence.

The plateau sometimes lifts.

Not much: a hand’s breadth across several thousand miles, usually over the course of an hour. The old surfaces bear it well. Their fractures close, water presses upward through the marshes, and the rivers pause. In the underground stations the crews disengage the largest shafts before the load reaches them. Above, the herds climb onto the broad ridges and stand shoulder to shoulder.

For a short time the waterfalls cease.

Without them, sounds usually buried in the country become audible: water working inside stone; roots pulling against one another; the far, uneven knocking of unsecured machinery. From the deepest basins comes a low vocal sound, taken up in other basins so distant that no single listener hears both.

Then the continent settles. The channels refill. Waterfalls resume with a force that tears loose old banks and carries whole rafts of vegetation into the gorges. The animals descend after them, feeding on what has been exposed.

Most of the country remains where it was. The long ridges are rounder by an amount no one can measure. A pool has become a channel; a channel has acquired a warm island of tangled roots. At an isolated station, a crew clears mud from the intake screens and puts the turbines back under load.

The vibration travels outward through the wet stone. Along its path, the feeding vanes open.
