The town lies barely above the water, on a broad shelf of pale stone inside a black volcanic bay. From the far side it is difficult to distinguish the houses from the shelf itself. Both are low, thick-walled, and crowded with irregular openings; both have wet feet. Behind them, the ground rises just enough to become a bank of broken lava, then disappears into warm cloud.

There are no trees on the skyline. No grass occupies the cracks. Even the shallow water is clear of weeds. The colors belong to other things: white lime, charcoal rock, brown shell, the dull pink of certain crabs. Where a wall has split, its interior is almost as clean as its face.

The land looks newly made. Lava preserves the folds in which it cooled, including thin, hollow blisters that would break beneath a careless heel. Some stretches resemble heavy cloth dropped on the ground and hardened before its creases could relax. Elsewhere, black slabs stand at slightly different levels, with narrow seams between them. There is little loose earth. Nothing has yet been rounded into a hill or sorted into a beach.

Yet there is no sense of an eruption approaching, or of the country settling. The ground is quiet beneath the noise. Its rawness has become its lasting condition.

Warm springs emerge along the seam between the lava and the white shelf. They do not burst or bubble violently. Water simply appears in sufficient quantity to fill channels, wash steps, and pass beneath houses. At the spring mouths, the stone is smooth and faintly yellow. Farther out, it becomes porous, full of tiny cavities lined with chalk. A hand placed in one of the channels can remain there comfortably. A hand placed on the dry rock beside it feels much the same temperature.

The whole lower town is arranged around this water.

Streets are narrow raised paths with drainage on either side. At the crossings, flat stones bridge the channels; some are scarcely wider than a foot. Doorways open a hand’s breadth above the flow. Within the houses, floors slope toward small holes in the walls, and sleeping places are platforms of fitted stone covered with hides. There is very little wood. What reaches the bay is saved for handles, pegs, floats, and the ribs of boats. Roofs are made of overlapping slabs where the walls can support them, stretched skins where they cannot.

From above, the settlement would look almost continuous: roof against roof, drying ledge against parapet, little square courts sunk among thick masonry. But at walking height it opens repeatedly. A passage too narrow for two people leads onto a fish-cleaning terrace. A dark doorway turns out to be a stair descending to a landing. Between two houses, the entire bay appears, flat and gray beyond a hundred close black backs of resting seals.

There are animals everywhere.

Seals occupy the lowest ledges in such numbers that people take long-established detours around them. The old bulls lie across steps like dropped sacks, their whiskers wet, their breathing audible at close range even through the general uproar. Smaller animals crowd the warm outflows. Crabs work along the margins, testing the undersides of shells and the soft material caught against stones. In deeper water, fish gather in compact schools beneath each discharge, turning together so that an otherwise dark channel briefly fills with silver.

Birds cover the roof edges.

They are not a distant movement in the atmosphere. They stand within reach, hooked claws gripping the stone, pale eyes following every bucket. Their droppings whiten the upper town. Their feathers lodge in drains. They fight over scraps in the lanes and nest in any wall cavity too awkward for a person to reach. People fit doors with heavy swinging flaps, not for privacy but to keep the birds outside.

The noise is enormous.

Water contributes a continuous low rushing beneath the paving. Over it come the seals’ guttural calls, the sharp clatter of birds landing on stone, the shrieking of crowded nesting ledges, and the blows of people splitting shell or dressing blocks. In the covered passages, these sounds overlap until individual sources become impossible to locate. A hammer struck behind one wall seems to answer from another. The bay catches the cries and returns them.

Conversation happens close to the ear. Across a court, people use gestures. A raised palm means wait; two fingers pointed downward mean the landing is occupied; a flat hand moved sideways sends someone toward another passage. Children learn to watch faces before they learn to make themselves heard.

The light is sufficient but never generous. A low marine cloud rests against the higher ground, fed from below by the warm wet basin. Through it, the sun is a broad pale area rather than a visible disc. Under the projecting roofs, everything becomes brown and charcoal. The brightest things are a freshly opened shell, a wet shoulder, the white edge of a step. Looking out from an interior, the bay seems luminous. Standing beside the bay, it seems dim.

There is no cool refuge in the shadows. They are warm, damp spaces smelling of salt, hides, mineral water, and fish.

Beyond the inhabited shelf, the same arrangement continues for miles: low black points enclosing shallow basins, white mineral aprons around spring mouths, bird colonies on every inaccessible rise. The town occupies only the most convenient convergence of channels and landings. Farther around the bay are smaller clusters of houses, recognizable by their straight wall tops and the lines of shell floats marking traps.

The traps are woven from split hide and narrow lengths of salvaged wood. Hooks are bone. Cutting edges are chipped from the dark glass found inland. The people know how to lever a slab, dress its bearing surfaces, and give a wall enough inward slope to carry a roof. Their construction is heavy and close-fitting. There is little decoration except rows of selected shells pressed into wet lime around doors, where fingertips have polished them.

Without plants, the life of the bay begins in less visible places. Thin bacterial films coat submerged stone near the springs. Small grazers rasp at them; worms inhabit the mineral pores. Beyond the harbor, cold seawater meets the warm seepage along the bottom, supporting beds of shellfish so dense that their living edges overlap. The fish arrive there to feed. The seals follow the fish. The town takes a portion of all of it.

This abundance does not soften the place. There is no leafy distance, no soil scent, no shelter made by something growing overhead. Shelter has to be fitted together by hand.

For all its noise, the bay has an extraordinary steadiness. The springs keep their level. The sea’s movement reaches the inner shelf as a slight pressure in the channels rather than a breaking wave. The same ledges remain wet, the same upper steps remain dry. There is no flood season to empty the houses, no dry season to expose a different shore.

Small acts occupy this fixed arrangement. A person lifts a trap. A bird takes the place another bird has vacated. Water threads through a wall opening polished smooth around its lower edge. Someone resets a loose paving stone with a shell chip underneath.

Seen from the lava bank, none of this alters the town’s outline. Its roofs remain packed against the pale water; its dark passages hold their warmth. The newly formed rock waits without becoming weathered, and the enormous animal sound lies over it all, as constant as the cloud.
