At eye level, the country looks almost level. A low, crowded growth extends to a pale horizon: thorn scrub, fleshy gray leaves, yellow grass, and thousands of blunt earthen towers. There are no mountains to measure the distance against. What looks like a ridge nearby proves to be the raised edge of a colony; what looks like a distant settlement is another reach of the same country, continuing beyond sight.

Underfoot, very little is flat.

The ground is crowded with collars of fresh soil around burrow mouths, narrow runways beneath the grass, roots half uncovered by excavation, and clay walls built against the bases of shrubs. Some towers are no higher than a knee. Others rise above the vegetation, thick at the bottom and divided into rounded buttresses, their shaded sides nearly vertical. Between them stand older structures whose outer chambers have collapsed and filled with windblown seed. Plants grow out of their roofs. Lizards occupy the cracks. New construction climbs over the old.

There is no road through this place, but there are thoroughfares everywhere.

Most are too small to notice until you crouch. A passage the width of a finger runs beneath a thorn branch, bordered by grains of earth carried up one at a time. Farther along, it becomes a covered gallery, a brittle tube with a dark interior. Larger paths slip between the towers, polished by padded feet. They fork around rooted obstacles and rejoin beyond them. At some junctions the soil has been worn into a shallow bowl by generations of animals pausing, turning, sniffing, choosing a direction.

Almost all the traffic is hidden.

In the ordinary white light of late morning, the surface seems quiet enough to hear a seed case twitch open. A beetle crosses a patch of bare ground with its shadow directly underneath it. Something small moves through the grass without shaking the tops. From within a mound comes a faint dry ticking, audible only with an ear close to the clay.

The country is warm rather than fiercely hot. Sunlight heats exposed soil quickly, but there is so much cover that cool strips persist throughout the day. Every thorn bush holds a complicated shadow. The deep galleries remain cooler still.

There is no open water. No glint between stems, no damp smell, no dark ribbon of a stream. A broad white hollow may suggest a lake from a distance, but walking toward it reveals a hard salt crust scattered with wind-rolled husks. The plants at its edge stop abruptly, each rooted just outside the soil it cannot tolerate.

Yet elsewhere the growth is thick enough to catch at the legs.

Spiny shrubs interlock over mats of small, waxy leaves. Succulents crowd the sunny sides of the mounds, their thick blades bearing old tooth marks sealed over with pale scars. Tough vines thread the thorns. Beneath them are bulbs, swollen roots, and short-lived herbs that spend most of the year as seeds. Green is present everywhere, though seldom the soft green of watered gardens. Here it is blue-green, olive, silver, or a deep gloss visible only when a leaf turns.

Rain comes rarely. When it does, the soil takes it almost at once. The towers shed it down their flanks into root-thick pockets; the white hollows briefly soften at the surface; buried seeds open in such numbers that the ground changes color within days. Long afterward, when the surface has dried back to powder, moisture remains below reach of the sun.

That buried reserve supports the whole crowded place. Many animals never drink. They live on leaves, roots, seeds, or the bodies of other animals, and spend the brightest hours where their breath will not dry them out.

The land itself is young. Much of the surface is fine sediment laid down recently enough that drainage has scarcely organized it. There are no deep gullies, few stones rounded by long travel, no weathered cliffs. Wind still brings new material from the great dry basin beyond the horizon. It arrives as a faint haze, settles on leaves and mound tops, and gathers behind every obstacle.

A fallen branch begins a rise. Grass catches against it, then dust catches in the grass. Burrowing animals lift deeper clay into the new deposit. Termites bind loose grains into walls. Roots hold the resulting lump in place. Across enormous distances, these small accumulations are making relief where almost none existed.

The soil colors reveal the work. The plain is pale buff, but fresh additions to the towers are reddish or dark brown, drawn from layers below. Some have wet-looking seams where repairs were made overnight, though by noon even these have dried. Here and there a mound has a sharp new crest, still free of windblown chaff. Beside it, an abandoned wall has become a bed of tiny flowering plants.

Each large structure gathers a neighborhood.

A thorn shrub establishes itself where runoff and excavated minerals collect. Its fallen pods attract rodents. Their tunnels provide shelter for lizards and small snakes. A ground bird scratches a hollow beneath the outer branches. Ants sort the litter around the entrance to their nest, leaving crescents of discarded husk. Beetles shelter under those husks. Predators pass along the shaded routes joining one such pocket to the next.

The same few square meters may be occupied at a dozen depths.

There are eggs in the upper soil, larvae among the roots, sealed chambers of stored seed, and passages descending beyond the reach of ordinary digging. Above them, insects feed inside stems. A spider waits where two thorn tips nearly meet. Even bare-looking ground carries tracks so fine that they disappear when the light shifts.

Late in the day, longer shadows connect the isolated patches of shade. Movement spreads with them.

Small rodents emerge and sit briefly at their entrances, forefeet tucked against their chests. Birds step out from beneath bushes to work along the mound bases. A narrow snake follows a runway, stopping with its head just inside each crossing. Termite columns extend beneath freshly made covers; where a gallery has broken, pale bodies hurry across the exposed inch while darker-headed defenders hold the edges.

None of this produces much noise. There is the soft rasp of grass against a shell, a brief rush beneath leaves, the tap of a dropped seed. A bird gives one low call and receives an answer from farther away than expected.

The wind is the largest sound, and even that is mostly a continuous brushing. Across so much heated ground it develops its own daily habits. Small whirlwinds lift dust in the salt hollows. Broader currents travel over the towers and leave their burden in the thicker vegetation. On some afternoons the far country fades into a brownish veil while the nearest leaves remain sharply lit.

Toward evening, flowers that looked like dry knots loosen and open. Their scent stays close to the ground. Moths begin visiting them before the sky has lost its blue.

From above, the region would resolve into a dense stippling of roofs, crowns, hollows, and narrow lanes, interrupted by pale salt flats. Up close, it is harder to separate one structure from another. A root passes through a wall. A burrow undercuts a path. The soil from that burrow becomes part of the next mound.

At the edge of a fresh excavation, a few grains slide down the slope. A worker appears with another load, places it, and retreats. Beside the entrance, a seedling has opened two thick leaves into the last warm light.
