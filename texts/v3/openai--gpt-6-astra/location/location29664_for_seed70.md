The water lies high on the mountains, above the weather.

From the lower valleys it looks like a long, blue-green ridge, softer in outline than the black peaks that carry it. Clouds gather beneath its shores. At certain hours their upper surfaces brighten with sunlight that has passed through the lake, so that the country below receives a wavering green afternoon even under a white sky.

Up here, almost everything is submerged.

There are a few exposed summits: narrow blades of volcanic stone, still rough enough to catch and hold a fallen leaf. Between them, the water extends for miles across a high, recently opened basin. Its warmth comes from below. Deep fissures release heat without much bubbling, and the temperature changes so gradually from one slope to another that a creature crossing the basin might never notice it had entered warmer water.

The lake is clear, but it is difficult to see far through it. There is too much growing in the way.

Forests occupy the submerged ridges. Their trunks are pale, thick at the roots, and almost without branches for the first several yards. Then they divide into broad, loose crowns of translucent leaves. These are not drowned trees. Their leaves hang open in the water, each one a little thicker at the margin, their undersides covered in fine hairs that hold silver beads of gas. Occasionally a bead becomes large enough to escape. It rises through the foliage, stopping beneath one leaf and then another, and may take most of a day to reach the surface.

The trees grow close together. Their crowns overlap, but the leaves do not tangle. Where two meet, one slowly turns edgewise, making room.

Below them, shorter plants fill nearly every patch of sediment. There are dark green rosettes with stiff, serrated blades; soft branching growths the color of apricots; low beds of round leaves that lift slightly in the warmth coming through the rock. Long ribbons rise from crevices and lie along the undersides of the tree crowns. Some are rooted twenty or thirty yards below the light they have reached.

A slope can be crowded enough to look solid until a change in the sun reveals the spaces inside it: narrow blue passages, small chambers between roots, a whole second hillside visible through a break in the leaves.

The stone is young.

It has not yet been rounded into the usual shapes of a lake bed. Black shelves end abruptly over deep water. Broken faces show blue-gray interiors beneath a thin brown skin. Here and there, a slab has split so recently that no root has entered the crack, though plants already lean across it from both sides. Much of the basin floor consists of tilted plates, their edges sharp and their lower ends lost beneath fine, pale sediment.

There is very little loose gravel. The mountain has broken faster than the water can wear it down.

On the steeper walls, vegetation grows in descending layers. Roots grip the lips of fractures; leaves spread outward; finer growth hangs below them. Seen across one of the deeper channels, these walls resemble slopes of dense woodland seen through unusually clear air. Only the occasional drifting particle gives the water away.

Near midday, sunlight reaches surprisingly far down. It lays broad patches of gold across the upper terraces and picks out individual leaves in the middle depths. Below that, the colors simplify. Orange becomes brown, red becomes black, and the pale trunks persist longest, standing in dim rows along the submerged contours.

There is no dark central abyss. Even the deepest part of the basin remains faintly green.

At the outer rim, the lake behaves differently.

Its shore does not always follow the stone. In places the water extends beyond a broken cliff, holding a rounded edge over the open air. Through it, the lower mountains appear stretched and trembling. The clouds beneath can be seen from underwater, moving with a speed that seems almost violent compared with the stillness among the roots.

Nothing solid supports this overhanging margin. The water simply keeps the shape of the basin that used to be there.

Along these edges grow thick fringes of small, dark leaves. Their stems reach from the last stone ledges into the unsupported water. Roots hang down, stopping just short of the boundary. A few cross it and emerge into the air, where their wet tips whiten in the sun.

The margin yields to pressure, but slowly. A leaf pushed against it makes a shallow outward dimple that remains for hours after the leaf has withdrawn. In some places there are larger depressions whose causes are no longer present.

Water does leave the lake. Now and then a drop gathers beneath an overhang and falls toward the clouds. For a long time afterward, a little hollow remains where it detached.

The basin loses so little this way that its level is marked not by a bare shoreline but by changes in the plants. Near the surface, broad leaves spread flat and become opaque. Their upper skins are waxy and warm to the touch. From above, entire sheltered bays appear to be meadows, interrupted by black peaks and narrow lanes of open water.

Most of the animals live beneath these floating leaves, and even there they are scarce.

A translucent snail may occupy the same stem for weeks, leaving a clean strip through its coating of green. Small, jointed creatures shelter inside the folded bases of the ribbon plants. Once in a while, a flat-bodied fish emerges from the forest, crosses an open channel with three or four strokes, and disappears into another stand of pale trunks. The leaves continue moving after it is gone.

There are no shoals, no busy flickering among the branches. Much of the growth bears no sign of having been eaten.

The quiet is not complete, but sounds are widely separated. A small click comes from cooling stone. A root shifts in a fracture. Somewhere out of sight, a gas pocket slips free with a soft, hollow note. These noises travel far through the warm water, arriving without a clear direction.

At the surface there is sometimes rain. Seen from below, it dimples the bright ceiling and briefly fills the upper few inches with silver needles. The disturbance seldom reaches the forest. Beneath a crown of broad leaves, the water remains almost still.

Change is easiest to notice at a broken ledge near the eastern rim. There, a single fresh slab lies tipped against the slope, its upper face bare except for three rooted shoots. Fine sediment is collecting in its lower corner. One shoot has begun to turn toward the light; another is sending a root into the dark gap beneath the stone.

Beyond it, ridge after submerged ridge extends across the basin, dense with growth. Sunlight shifts over them as the clouds pass far below. The newest leaves are still unfolding, slowly enough that their pale, creased surfaces seem to be part of the permanent landscape.
