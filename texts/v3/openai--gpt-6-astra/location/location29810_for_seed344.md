Behind the pumping house, where two concrete walls almost meet, there is a piece of ground no larger than a room. You reach it sideways, past a disconnected pipe whose open end is stuffed with a mineral deposit the color of old teeth. Beyond the pipe the space widens. There is sky overhead, a pale rectangle of it, and enough daylight to see everything without a lamp.

The ground has risen here.

Not much: a hand’s breadth in places, less elsewhere. Hot water coming up through the old paving has laid down a new surface, covering the square edges of the slabs with cream-colored stone. The newest deposits are white and soft enough to mark with a fingernail. Farther from the seep they have hardened into little shelves, thin-lipped and surprisingly sharp. They resemble terraces seen from very high up, except that a whole basin will fit inside your palm. Each holds clear water. Each spills over at one particular notch.

There is very little movement to watch. A basin fills until its meniscus bulges, releases a thread of water, then fills again. The threads are so fine that the stone beneath them seems merely shinier than the rest. It takes several minutes to establish which way the water is going.

The noise makes this patience difficult.

On the other side of the wall, a pump runs continuously. Its low note occupies the enclosure like a heavy object. Above that is the rougher sound of water being forced through a narrowed pipe, and above that a thin metallic vibration from a loose fitting somewhere you cannot see. The three sounds do not quite settle together. At intervals they produce a shuddering beat that seems to pass through your teeth. Nothing in the little court visibly responds except the water nearest the wall, where a pattern of tiny parallel ridges remains almost stationary on the surface.

You would have to raise your voice to speak here. There is no one to speak to.

The heat is close and practical. It comes from the wall, the paving, the exposed run of pipe at ankle height. Standing still does not help. The damp air touches skin without cooling it, and the shallow pools are warmer than a hand. Only at the entrance, where outside air slips through the gap, do the underside of an iron bracket and a shaded strip of metal carry drops of condensation. These grow slowly, trembling under the machinery’s vibration, then fall into a round hollow they have not yet had time to deepen.

The place is too new to be worn.

Along the left wall, a seam in the paving has opened by perhaps two fingers’ width. Its edges are dark, angular, clean. One slab has lifted without breaking, tilting a rusted washer into the corner of a puddle. Another has fractured into three pieces that still fit together perfectly. There is no grit in the cracks yet. Water rises between them without bubbling.

Someone once attempted to contain the seep. A short curb crosses half the enclosure, then stops. Behind it are two bolts, a length of flat steel, and the lower portion of a gauge fixed to the wall. The gauge has lost its face. Its needle points into open air.

The curb now makes a waterfall scarcely thicker than a sheet of paper. Beneath it, fresh mineral has built a small white apron, hiding the concrete’s coarse aggregate. Water is gradually incorporating the repair into the thing it was meant to prevent.

Vegetation occupies only the margins. Three narrow shoots grow from a joint near the entrance, where rainwater sometimes reaches them before mixing with the hot seep. Their leaves are stiff, dusty at the tips, yellow near the paving. A little farther in, there is a patch of flat green growth no larger than a spread hand. It follows a cooler trickle from the base of the outside wall and ends abruptly where that trickle enters a warm pool. The boundary is precise enough to look cut.

Elsewhere there is bare mineral, bare water, bare concrete. No insects skim the basins. Nothing has nested inside the open pipe. The few fallen leaves that find their way in remain whole, darkening underwater until a pale crust begins to fasten their edges down.

By noon, sunlight reaches the center of the enclosure. It illuminates the bottom of the main pool: a shallow depression about the size of a washbasin, with one submerged screw and a brown stain beneath it. The water is exceptionally clear. Viewed from the doorway, the screw seems to lie on dry stone, until a drop falls somewhere nearby and the straight shank bends.

For an hour the fresh terraces shine. Their wet lips cast minute shadows. You can see that they are not all white: some have a diluted peach color, some are gray where dust has settled, and one narrow band is translucent amber. These are records of slight differences in the water, though there is no convenient way to read them. A change somewhere behind the wall, a different intake, a cleaning cycle, a valve left fractionally open—each might have put down its own layer here.

Then the sun passes behind the pumping house. Ordinary shade returns. The enclosure does not cool appreciably.

Rain adds a second, temporary arrangement to the ground. It falls cold enough to trouble the clear pools with faint veils, fills the spaces between the mineral shelves, and runs toward the doorway. For a while the whole surface becomes connected. The little terraces disappear beneath one sheet of water, their sharp rims visible only as pale lines below it.

Afterward, the separate basins emerge again. The warm seep resumes its quieter routes.

The pump keeps running. Its sound is loud enough to suggest that a great deal ought to be happening, but the unfinished curb, the three shoots, the tilted slab all remain where they are. Under the shallow water, stone gathers on the screw a fraction at a time. Its slot is still visible. The next time someone comes through the gap, it may not be.
