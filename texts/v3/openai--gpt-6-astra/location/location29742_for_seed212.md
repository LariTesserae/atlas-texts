The plain is low enough that, from its highest ridges, you can see the shine of heat all the way to the horizon. Everything between is alive. The ridges are folded bark. The pale expanses below them are leaves laid edge to edge, fused into a continuous surface, and what looks at first like exposed earth in the cracks is a finer, reddish tissue, closing slowly over itself.

There is nowhere to stand that does not grow.

Underfoot, the surface has the firmness of an unripe pear. It gives a little, then presses back. At intervals it splits along straight lines, opening seams of brilliant green before the sides swell shut. These seams run for hundreds of paces. They intersect at broad, flattened nodes from which thick stalks rise in clusters, dividing and rejoining overhead. From a distance, the arrangement has the unmistakable proportions of a city: avenues, crowded blocks, open courts, tall structures gathered toward several centers. Up close, there is no joint that was fastened, no surface that was cut. Every arch has grown into the support opposite it. Every stair is a sequence of buds arrested at different sizes.

The city is still coming up.

That is the first thing the sound makes clear.

A deep, continuous cracking passes through the ground, overlaid with the gunshot reports of husks splitting. Great sheets of leaf membrane snap taut between columns. Tendrils rasp against ridged stems with the volume of something being dragged across an entire square. Beneath these separate sounds is a roar so full that distance becomes difficult to judge. A branch opening beside your shoulder can disappear within it; a root tearing free on the other side of the plain arrives through your feet.

There are no calls. No wings, no insect drone, no small feet in the litter. All the noise belongs to growth.

And growth is not gentle here. It comes in fronts.

Across a district of tightly pleated towers, a dark band develops beneath the surfaces. For a moment the towers lean in different directions, as if considering their own weight. Then the band rises. Every pleat unfolds at once. The towers gain height, their neighbors are pushed aside, and a passage that was open a moment ago becomes a narrow green cleft. Somewhere inside, a chamber ruptures. A white tongue of new tissue surges from it, braces against another building, and begins to harden in the sunlight.

The sound hits a moment later.

It is a warm, bright country. The sky is ordinary blue, and the sun is strong enough to bleach the highest leaf edges nearly yellow. There is little of the dimness one might expect from so much vegetation. Broad blades turn throughout the day, passing sunlight down between themselves. Whole facades become luminous. A court can be shaded one instant and flooded with hard light the next as a roof rolls back into a thick green scroll.

In those sudden exposures, every surface shows its youth. The corners are sharp. The bark has not yet weathered into flakes. Pale creases remain where structures have unfolded, and new buttresses carry the soft indentations of the chambers in which they formed. Nothing has stood long enough to look ruined. What falls is immediately under pressure from what replaces it.

Water is scarce outside the flesh of the place.

There are no streams along the broad avenues, no ponds in the courts. The air smells hot and green, with an astringent edge like bruised stalks. Dust consists of pollen, dry pith, and fine golden scraps scraped loose where branches grind together. It drifts low over the living pavement until a field of minute hairs stands up and catches it.

Moisture appears in protected places: a cool seam under a folded root, droplets along the underside of an overhang, a thumbnail-sized pool deep inside a cup-shaped leaf. At dawn, condensation gathers on the highest spines. Each droplet is drawn down a groove and disappears through a pore. The structures below contract slightly as it passes into them. Sometimes this produces a descending sequence of hollow knocks, roof to wall to foundation, audible even through the larger roar.

Rain, when it comes, barely reaches the ground. The city opens upward. All the green mouths that seemed decorative widen, all the roof leaves deepen into bowls. A brief shower can hold an entire district still. For those few minutes the cracking stops, and the small, widely separated impacts of water seem shockingly loud.

Then the bowls seal. The stored water moves inward. Growth resumes with enough violence to fling old leaves across several streets.

From the ridge, the larger organization becomes visible. Dense knots of uprights stand around wide, low basins. Ribbons of flexible stems connect one knot to another, their interwoven tops making raised causeways. Farther out are paler districts of inflated chambers, and beyond those a rough margin where the plain is still forcing itself into architecture. There, green wedges emerge from flat tissue with their edges already straight. They push apart, opening lanes between them, while fibrous nets stretch across the gaps.

Nothing appears to inhabit these arrangements.

The openings have no dependable relation to bodies. Some resemble doorways until you approach and see that they are an inch deep. Others, scarcely wider than a slit in bark, contain a visible succession of spacious rooms. A small cavity can carry the roar of a whole distant quarter. Looking into it, you see sunlight falling through structures that cannot fit inside the stem.

Such discrepancies are easiest to notice at midday, when the light is clearest.

An avenue may run straight between two rows of columns and arrive at the back of the place where it began. Its length remains visible behind you. A causeway casts its shadow across a court that is actually above it. In the eastern districts, young branches sometimes fork before reaching their point of emergence; you can follow either fork down to the same patch of unbroken bark.

These things have consequences. Where two streets cross without occupying the same height, growth accumulates in thick collars. Where a chamber becomes larger inside than outside, its walls turn pale and sweat away some of their scarce moisture. The plain must feed its impossibilities. Around the most extravagant structures, neighboring plants are small, tough, and tightly shut.

The place has learned to economize.

Old avenues are absorbed from the edges inward. Towers hollow themselves to support new floors. A bridge that has ceased to lead anywhere softens into a suspended root, then lowers its tip until it finds a surface worth entering. Nothing is simply abandoned. Even the papery skins shed by swelling trunks remain attached by threads, fluttering and scraping until something reels them back.

There are many kinds of plant, although their boundaries are hard to follow. A carpet of tiny succulent lobes thickens into a wall of bark; the bark opens into fernlike fronds; the tips of the fronds meet and form a single glossy fruit the size of a square. Along its underside, translucent roots reach into the crowded air. They draw no visible food from it, yet swell steadily.

The fruit ripens over the course of an afternoon.

Its color moves from green to amber in irregular patches. The neighboring structures bend away. A ring of seams opens in the ground beneath it, and the surrounding roar takes on a strained, higher note. At last the fruit parts along five lines.

Inside is another district.

It unfolds downward first: columns finding purchase, courts flattening, stairlike shelves pulling apart. Then its upper surfaces turn toward the sun. The empty rind hangs above it for a while, a huge limp canopy, before shrinking into the new roofs. By evening, the district is as crowded as those around it. Its avenues already have the worn-looking polish of leaves that have never been walked on.

Heat lingers in the low ground after sunset. Pale undersides turn outward, and the architecture loosens fractionally as if preparing to breathe. The roar changes register. There is less splitting now, more friction: long, overlapping groans as whole quarters lean together, rubbing their skins smooth.

In the darkened wells between them, the first droplets form.

For a little while the newest growth becomes almost motionless. Sharp green tips stand against the remaining daylight. Below, deep in the continuous plant flesh of the plain, something takes up the water. A pressure travels outward. It reaches one foundation, then another.

All across the low horizon, the roofs begin to rise.
