The first thing you see is the underside of a garden.

It projects over the arrival platform on ribs of pale concrete, forty or fifty meters above your head. Ferns crowd its drainage slots. Between them hang thick ropes of roots, dark at the top and silver where the sun reaches them. A drop gathers on one root tip, grows heavy, and falls through the bright air. Long before it reaches the platform, it has become too small to see.

Beyond the garden’s edge there is no ground. There is a lower bank of cloud, and through a break in it, another bank much farther down.

The district occupies the summit of a structure so large that its load-bearing members have become the local geography. Great buttresses rise out of the clouds and broaden into terraces. Between them lie inhabited hollows, reservoir courts, cultivated shelves, and narrow chasms crossed by bridges. From one end of the district to the other is perhaps an afternoon’s walk, if you know which lifts still carry passengers. You can see most of it from the arrival platform, but never all at once. A planted wall obscures a row of houses; beyond the houses a glazed roof flashes white; behind that, another immense terrace lifts a line of trees into the sky.

Nothing here rests on earth.

Under the paths are vaults. Under the vaults are workshops, cisterns, counterweights, and deeper vaults. Even the dark material in which the trees grow was assembled: shredded bark, kiln grit, compost, flakes of worn insulating brick. A fallen leaf may spend years passing through beds and gutters before it is caught, ground, and carried uphill again.

The place has been doing this for a long time.

The paving is a patchwork of cream ceramic and reddish clinker, worn into shallow bowls around doors. Stair treads sag in the middle. Old parapets have lost their sharp corners beneath generations of hands, trailing stems, and maintenance brushes. Here and there an obsolete pipe has been cut flush with a wall, leaving a green-ringed hole that now houses a small bird. Some repairs are recent and square. Others have weathered so completely into their surroundings that only the pattern of bolts reveals them.

In the broadest basin, the roofs descend in steps toward a long, narrow reservoir. From above, it resembles a river laid between retaining walls. It bends because the chambers beneath it require the weight to be distributed that way. Along its inner curve, balconies almost touch the water; along its outer curve, there is a steep bank of planted lattice, dense with glossy leaves.

The water is low today. A pale band shows above it, then a darker band of small mussels attached to the ceramic lining. In the shallows, fish turn beneath the shadow of a footbridge. Their backs are the same brown as wet bolts.

There are no broad marshes, no soaking expanses of soil. Moisture occurs in favored places. The underside of a cold-water main is always wet. The western face of a ventilation tower catches cloud in the late afternoon. Below the laundries, a sequence of filtering beds supports enormous soft leaves with holes bitten through them. Elsewhere, the path is warm and dry enough to raise a little powder underfoot.

Yet the vegetation is lavish. It has been given every useful surface.

Trees grow in deep boxes recessed into the main beams, their trunks low and thick, their crowns spread sideways along tension cables. Vines cover stair enclosures and cross alleys on wires. Succulents fill the hot outer ledges. In sheltered courts, banana leaves open above waist-high thickets of ginger and flowering shrubs. Ferns occupy the seams beneath them. Moss marks the exact routes taken by water too slight to notice.

There are places where you pass through three kinds of shade in as many steps: the broken shadow of a trellis, the green shade beneath a vine, then the cool gray shadow of a massive wall. Beyond each is sunlight so bright that the paving seems to retain it.

At this height the sun is hard and remarkably clear. Small objects cast precise shadows. The far side of the district looks close enough to touch until a person steps onto one of its bridges and reveals the distance.

The warmth comes partly from that light and partly from below. Engines, pumping halls, and workshops occupy the enclosed depths, and their waste heat rises through tiled flues. On cooler mornings, lizards gather along the warm joints in the pavement. The public seats nearest the boiler chimneys are always occupied.

High screens of ribbed glass break the worst of the wind. They stand along the exposed edges in overlapping ranks, so tall that birds use their frames as flight paths. Some panes are clear, some milky with age, some replaced by yellowish resin. Through them the outside sky appears in slightly displaced strips. An open maintenance gate admits a thin, cold current that runs across the floor and disappears into the warm air of a court.

Near that gate you can feel what the district is keeping out.

Most of the time, however, the air barely moves. Heat gathers against south-facing walls. Leaves turn slowly on their stems. Laundry hangs with a heavy, almost sculptural stillness between the upper apartments.

The sound is continuous.

Under everything lies the low pressure of the fans: not quite a note, more a vibration in your chest when you stand against a railing. Pumps knock in distant chambers. Belt drives make a soft, rapid slapping behind grilles. A freight carriage travels along the rim of the reservoir, its steel wheels announcing each joint in the track. You hear it long before it arrives and for a long time after it has passed.

Closer at hand there are birds everywhere. Small green ones work through the flowering vines, scattering petals onto the steps. Swifts nest in the upper ventilation mouths, choosing ducts that have been permanently bypassed. Broad-winged scavengers sit on the warm housings of transformers with their feet tucked into their feathers. They watch the compost carts.

Insects occupy the finer spaces: bees in the herb beds, beetles beneath loose ceramic edging, mosquitoes in a forgotten bucket on a roof. Geckos emerge around the lamps at dusk. A family of small, blunt-faced mammals lives behind a disused elevator door, coming out at quiet hours to forage along the fruit terraces. The door has been labeled for repair so many times that its surface is almost covered with dates.

Human movement fits into this abundance without dominating it. A worker crosses a bridge carrying a coil of hose. Two people sit outside a repair shop, stripping insulation from wire. On an upper path, a little electric tractor draws a train of bins full of pruned branches. It moves at walking speed, and people step aside well before it reaches them.

The machinery is powerful, but little of it is hurried. Reservoir gates open by fractions. A great ventilation shutter takes several minutes to turn. The irrigation travels through the district in a cycle so long that one neighborhood is drying while another is just beginning to glitter. On watering days, the first sign is often a change in sound: leaves ticking under drops, a gutter beginning to speak behind a wall.

Actual rain comes less often. Sometimes cloud rises around the structure and the far terraces vanish. The air cools. Glass beads over. For an hour or two the district collects water from every exposed edge, directing it through channels rubbed smooth by use. Then the cloud sinks again, leaving the gardens damp and the upper rails already drying in the sun.

At the outermost lookout, the parapet is broad enough to sit on. Its capstones are warm. Behind you are layers of foliage, pale walls, awnings, chimneys, bridges, windows, and the measured labor of the pumps. Ahead is an expanse of brilliant air.

Looking down through the safety mesh, you can follow a buttress for several hundred meters. Gardens become sparse along it, then give way to isolated tufts in service joints. Farther down, only the inspection rails continue. The buttress enters cloud without narrowing.

A bird drops from the parapet, folds its wings, and is gone.

Beside your hand, a tiny fern has rooted in the mortar around an old bolt. Its newest frond is still curled. Someone has painted the bolt recently, carefully working around the stem.
