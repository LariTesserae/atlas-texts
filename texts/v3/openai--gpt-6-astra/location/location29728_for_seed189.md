The hollow lies just beneath the ground, inside a root thick enough to contain a sleeping fox. There is no entrance from above. A split in the root’s underside opens into it, but the soil presses so closely around the split that finding the place would require following a breath of warm air through several feet of darkness.

Inside, it is bright.

Light comes through the living walls in broad, pale bands, the color of sun passing through a peeled grape. There are shadows, though no single direction accounts for them. A raised seam on the floor casts one shadow toward you and another into the wall. When the light strengthens, the second shadow becomes visible deep inside the tissue, like a vein.

The hollow is almost entirely empty. Its breadth is only a handspan, its length perhaps three, but there is nothing across most of that distance: no hanging roots, no tangle, no soil, no little forest of shoots. The emptiness feels extravagant. Everything that grows here has grown outward, leaving this one volume unoccupied.

Floor, walls, and ceiling are plant.

Underfoot—or under a fingertip, which is the more appropriate way to explore it—the floor is firm and faintly yielding. Thin layers of fiber run lengthwise beneath a transparent skin. Along the margins, these fibers rise into ridges, each ridge carrying an edge as clean as a newly unfolded leaf. The hollow is young. It has opened by separation rather than wear: two surfaces that once touched have pulled apart, leaving tiny broken connections between them. Some still stretch from ceiling to floor, too fine to see except when they twitch.

The first noise you hear is one of these connections breaking.

It is a sharp report, startlingly large for the space. Then comes a rolling, woody thunder from beyond the walls. The root is growing. Farther out, it shoulders soil aside, tightens against a stone, releases, tightens again. Each movement reaches the hollow amplified. There is the squeal of wet fiber sliding over wet fiber, the deep clack of a seam closing, and, at intervals, a rushing sound that seems to require a river.

There is no river. A bead of sap is moving through a vessel narrower than a hair.

The root lends everything a voice too large for its body.

It is warm enough here that moisture rarely lasts on exposed surfaces. The floor is mostly dry, with a satin drag to it. Dampness gathers at three small depressions where the living skin thins. In one, a shallow lens of water holds a green reflection. In another, moisture appears only after a prolonged crackling in the ceiling. The third looks empty until touched; then a drop rises around the point of contact, drawn out of the floor.

Water is not abundant enough to lie idle. Each wet place has a sharply defined margin. Beyond it, the tissues are pale and tough. Within it, minute transparent lobes lift and flatten, drinking. A drop falling from the ceiling may vanish before its ripples finish.

Above the hollow, somewhere beyond the root and the soil, leaves are exposed to ordinary weather. Their responses arrive below in a different order.

Rain first announces itself as brightness. The walls turn almost white, and their concealed vessels become dark branching marks. Later comes the sound: thousands of soft impacts, compressed into a roar. Only much later does the air cool enough for condensation.

Sometimes the rain never arrives at all. The root holds the brightness and the noise of a shower that passed over another part of the plant.

There are no reliable days here. At one end of the hollow, the wall may glow with noon while the other shows the low amber of evening. These regions drift. Where they meet, a narrow strip of tissue curls away from itself, exposing a fresh green underside. The strip grows rapidly in the difference between the two lights, then settles flat when they agree.

Most of the hollow’s activity is like this: quick, local, and difficult to catch twice.

A blister rises on the floor, holds for a few breaths, and subsides. A crease travels the length of the ceiling. One of the three pools shifts sideways without spilling, carried by the rearrangement of cells beneath it. The empty center remains clear while its boundaries labor.

Occasionally, something crosses it.

The animal is pale, jointed, and no longer than a grass seed. It spends most of its life tucked into a seam near the lower opening. When it emerges, it proceeds cautiously, testing the floor with two long forelegs. Its body makes a tiny dry tapping, almost lost beneath the root’s enormous creaks.

It carries a stiff splinter of dead fiber.

At the nearest damp depression, it places the splinter across the rim and presses down with its body. The thin skin of the floor dimples. Moisture gathers underneath, then wells over one end. The animal drinks. When finished, it pulls its splinter free and takes it back into the seam.

There are faint impressions around that pool from many such visits, though none remain for long. The floor heals continuously. Even the animal’s preferred crossing route is erased between journeys.

The hollow is less consistent about distance.

Viewed from the lower split, its far end is plainly close. The curved wall, a little raised knot, and a whitish fold beneath the knot are all easy to distinguish. Yet a loose fiber dropped from the ceiling can fall for several seconds before reaching the floor. Its descent passes through a depth that was not visible beforehand.

The little animal knows where to step. It keeps to the margins, where surfaces meet and distances stay attached to them. It never leaps across an open interval. Once, a flake peeled from the ceiling drifts into the center and grows smaller as it falls, dwindling until it is a pale point. Then it lands beside the pool at its original size.

Its impact shakes water up the wall.

Afterward, the hollow contracts. The ceiling lowers by the width of a vein. The far end draws nearer. For a while, the space is precisely as small as it looks, and the sounds are merely loud.

Then a fresh seam opens behind the raised knot.

It opens slowly, revealing two moist green faces joined by shining threads. Warm air pours from the slit. The threads stretch. Within the new space, light is already present, though no surface has yet turned toward it.

The animal retreats with its splinter.

Around the seam, the wall swells and pales. A few droplets appear, briefly abundant, trembling in the thunder of growth. One falls upward and adheres to the ceiling. Another runs into the opening.

The hollow becomes a little larger.

Outside, the soil has not moved.
