The first thing visible is the light on the upper walls. Long before the streets receive any sun, the city has a pale rim: parapets, watch platforms, the edges of stone roofs, all bright against a sky so clear that the blue seems dark. Below them, the houses remain the color of cold ash.

The city occupies a high country of ridges and enclosed basins. There is no single mountain above it. Mountains stand in every direction, some close enough to show individual seams of rock, others reduced to white slivers along the horizon. Between them stretches a plateau so broad that several kinds of weather can be seen at once. Snow trails beneath a cloud in the east. A nearer basin is full of motionless haze. Far to the south, sunlight lies on bare slopes for what might be a hundred miles.

Most of the ground is dry.

Snow survives in joints, under stairs, on the shaded side of boundary walls. On exposed surfaces it is often worn away without melting. The wind carries it close to the ground in thin, brief streams that disappear among the foundations. What remains has the hardness and bluish translucence of old glass. People cut footholds into it with the same small iron picks they use to loosen building stone.

The rock is young in its exposure. It breaks into slabs with clean corners. Cliffs retain the shape of the fractures that opened them, and the scree below has not yet acquired the rounded, settled look of an old hillside. A fallen block may be as large as a house and sharp enough to split a rope drawn across it. Builders make use of these falls. Some streets run between boulders whose upper halves have been squared into walls, while their buried weight serves as a foundation.

From a distance, it is difficult to tell where construction ends. A cliff face becomes a retaining wall; the wall supports a terrace; the terrace carries a row of houses, and behind the houses the natural rock rises again. Much of the city has been made by filling gaps.

Its oldest districts are particularly dense. Each building leans against another through broad masonry shoulders. Passages turn tightly to keep out the wind. Stairs pass beneath rooms, emerge into little courts, then continue over the roofs of lower houses. There are places where someone walking uphill crosses three households without entering any of them.

Wood is precious. A substantial roof beam may have traveled for weeks from a lower valley. Most ceilings are stone slabs laid across narrow rooms, or short poles supporting brush, clay, and a heavy covering of gravel. Doors are small. Windows are smaller, shuttered with boards where people can afford them and layered felt where they cannot.

At midday the south-facing walls become warm enough to touch without pain. Old people sit there on folded wool, drawing their feet back whenever the moving shadow reaches them.

The city is immense, but its immensity reveals itself slowly. From one height, the visible roofs seem to end at a ridge. On reaching that ridge, another inhabited basin opens below: more terraces, more yards, another pale road running between walls. Beyond it are further settlements joined so closely by animal enclosures, storage compounds, shrines, and watch stations that their separate names matter more than their boundaries.

Travelers measure the crossing in nights.

There are broad routes through this accumulation of buildings, though none were made for wheels. Their steps are shallow and uneven, fitted to the tread of pack animals. At intervals the road widens around a trough or a weighing court. Stone posts are polished where generations of lead ropes have passed around them. Along the busiest stretches, the outer corners of houses have been rounded by loads of brushwood and baskets brushing against the masonry.

Even here, the sound is restrained. Hooves knock briefly on stone. A wooden latch falls into place. Someone shakes frost from a blanket on a roof, producing a dry flutter. Thick walls contain voices, and the streets break the wind into separate drafts. From a high terrace, the movement of hundreds of people below may arrive as little more than a faint, irregular murmur.

Water has its own architecture.

Above each district lies a protected catchment: a shallow hollow, a shaded gully, a bank where winter drifts reliably gather. Low walls keep animals out. Narrow channels carry meltwater beneath fitted slabs, away from sunlight and drying wind. Where a spring emerges, the buildings crowd around it with particular care, leaving an enclosed stair down to a dark basin in the rock.

The water there is almost black. A small crust of ice forms at the edges and is broken each morning. Wet footprints extend a few paces from the steps, then stop.

Some districts have no spring. Their cisterns hold the yield of occasional snowfalls and the brief summer thaw. Every roof is graded toward a stone spout. After a rare shower, people go out to clear grit from the channels while the dampness is still spreading across the walls. For an hour the whole place smells mineral and earthy. By afternoon, only the deepest joints remain dark.

Vegetation gathers where that moisture lingers. Tough little grasses grow below leaking troughs. Low cushions of flowering plants occupy cracks too narrow for grazing mouths. There are gray shrubs with stems scarcely taller than a hand, their roots extending much farther than anything visible above ground. On sheltered slopes, enclosed plots hold a thin crop of barley during the short growing season. Elsewhere, food arrives up the passes in sacks.

The animals are more conspicuous than the plants.

Small birds occupy holes in the masonry, emerging into the sunny courts in quick, scattered flights. They pick grain from dung and disappear beneath roof edges whenever a shadow crosses. Ravens wait on storage towers. In the upper air, broad-winged scavengers circle so high that their motion is almost imperceptible.

Within the city are long-haired cattle, goats, sheep, pack animals with frost on their whiskers, and heavy-coated dogs asleep against shut gates. Many houses shelter livestock on their lowest floor. Warmth rises through the ceiling, along with the smell of wool and hay. At dawn, doors open and animals step into the lanes in little groups, each group joining another until the road out becomes a slow, densely packed procession.

Beyond the last walls, herders spread them across enormous grazing grounds. The forage is poor, so the herds move over distances that would elsewhere separate whole provinces. Seen from a watch platform, they resemble dark sediment collecting in the folds of the land.

Wild grazers keep to steeper slopes. Their pale rumps flash among the rocks, then vanish when they stand still. Small burrowing mammals maintain settlements of their own in the gravel terraces, with cropped patches around each entrance and narrow tracks connecting the holes. Foxes work these places at dusk. On some mornings a line of larger predator tracks appears beside the outer wall, following it for a while before turning toward the hills.

There is life everywhere, but much of it is conserving warmth. Birds sit puffed into spheres. Dogs curl over their noses. Herds shelter shoulder to shoulder below an escarpment, hardly moving through the coldest part of the day.

The people build with similar patience. A retaining wall advances a few yards in a summer. Stone is carried by hand, levered into place, and packed with smaller fragments. A new stair may serve one family for years before the houses above it are finished. Damaged structures are seldom cleared entirely; sound portions remain, and later rooms grow around them.

At the city’s margins, half-built walls enclose bare ground. Some will become courtyards. Others already protect a patch of soil from the wind, accumulating dust grain by grain.

Toward evening, the inhabited basins fall into shadow in succession. Smoke rises from dung fires and settles into shallow layers between the roofs. The highest paths remain bright after the streets below have turned blue. For a little while, people still moving along them appear distinctly against the rock: a herder, two laden animals, someone carrying a door on their back.

Then the sunlight withdraws to the distant mountains.

Gates close across the livestock courts. Felt is pressed into the gaps around shutters. In the spring chambers, the surface of the water begins to stiffen at its edges. Above the quiet roofs, the last birds turn toward openings in the stone, choosing their way into the countless small shelters of the city.
