The heat remains after the light has gone. It comes out of the ground and the thicker tree trunks, out of the banks of dead leaves lodged between roots. There is almost no breeze. Even where the forest opens, the air seems to have been standing there all day.

This is low country, a broad coastal flat crossed by the remains of rivers. Their old channels persist as shallow, winding depressions, sometimes only a difference in the height of the undergrowth. Between them are long backs of clay and gravel, too low to be called hills. The land has had time to lose its sharpness. Banks have slumped into gentle inclines; stones have settled deep into soil; fallen trees soften for years before their outlines disappear.

In daylight, the forest would show several kinds of green, interrupted by patches of pale, dry earth. Now it is almost entirely black. A narrow opening overhead admits a few stars. Elsewhere, the leaves are visible only when something shifts them and one darkness moves against another.

Water is present chiefly through its effects.

A strip of taller trees follows a buried channel. Ferns crowd the underside of a bank where moisture seeps out slowly enough to vanish before it can gather into a trickle. Under a stone, the soil is dark and cool to the touch; an arm’s length away it has baked into small, hard plates. There has been no substantial rain for weeks. The vegetation lives on what the clay retains and what the deeper roots can reach, drawing down stores laid away during the wet season.

Here and there, the old riverbed holds a pool.

These pools are small, steep-sided, and difficult to see. Their surfaces lie below roots and fallen leaves, with no sky reflected in them. One has shrunk to a patch of water scarcely wider than the crown of a tree. A shelf of drying mud marks its former edge. Tracks overlap on that shelf until individual prints become almost unreadable: split hooves, narrow toes, round pads, the fine stitching of birds.

At the remaining water, everything arrives by turns.

A small mammal drinks with its body stretched backward, ready to leave. After it goes, a frog eases out from beneath a root. Beetles occupy the muddy lip. Mosquito larvae hang just under the surface and drop when a shadow passes, though there is so little light that the shadow must be slight indeed.

Away from the pools, the forest is no less crowded.

The crowding is mostly small. Every curled leaf provides a chamber. Every fissure in the bark has something fitted into it. Termites work inside fallen timber whose outer surface still looks sound. Ants travel along the undersides of branches, disappearing at knots and emerging farther on. Caterpillars feed at the margins of leaves, removing the forest in portions too small to notice. Beneath loose bark, geckos wait close enough to one another that their hunting spaces scarcely leave a gap.

A hand placed anywhere would interrupt something.

The larger animals occupy this abundance without making themselves obvious. Birds sleep in the dense middles of shrubs, their feathers barely distinguishable from the leaves. Bats hang in a hollow limb above the dry channel; occasionally one loosens its grip and slips out without touching the others. A snake lies along a branch with its head beyond the last fork. Farther off, something heavy stands under a fruiting tree, chewing slowly. Its feet remain in the same places for a long time.

For all this life, the prevailing impression is quiet.

There are insects calling, but they do not form a single continuous roar. One gives a short, dry pulse, then stops. Another answers from well away. Between them are the faint ticks of feeding, a leaf scraping bark, the soft contact of an animal’s belly with litter. A fruit falls somewhere out of sight. The impact is small and final, followed by a brief disturbance beneath the tree.

Most movement takes place within cover. There is little reason to cross open ground quickly unless something has gone wrong.

The vegetation is thickest on the low banks, where shrubs press into climbing plants and the climbing plants descend again in ropes of foliage. Old leaves hang beneath new ones. Seedlings grow in the decayed forks of fallen trunks. Even the dead wood carries a layered population of fungi, insects, roots, and dust. Nothing here stays vacant for long, but occupation is often invisible from more than a few inches away.

Beyond this immediate tangle, the same arrangement extends for miles: wooded rises, shallow channels, dense pockets of greener growth, occasional bare flats where salt and heat keep the trees back. The forest is not a wall around one clearing. It is a whole region of overlapping cover, with its own routes and interruptions. Animals can travel great distances through it while rarely stepping into anything that would look, to a person, like a path.

There are no cut stumps, no fences, no discarded containers caught in the roots. Nothing has been assembled here.

Yet the night contains a sound that does not belong to the forest. When the air settles completely, a low mechanical note reaches across the flats from industrial ground beyond them. At first it resembles pressure in the ears. Then a second tone becomes distinguishable inside it, steady and heavy, held too evenly to be wind.

The source is hidden beyond the trees. Its sound arrives stripped of clatter and detail, reduced to the continuous running of distant machinery. Sometimes it fades for several minutes, although nothing nearby has moved. Sometimes it can be felt more clearly beside an opening in the canopy. There is no corresponding light, only that faint evidence of another landscape continuing through the night.

Close at hand, a beetle turns a fragment of leaf.

The air cools by very little. On a few exposed leaves, moisture begins to gather along the edges. It will not be enough to wet the ground. Before dawn, small mouths will find some of it; the rest will disappear soon after sunrise.

The pools lower almost imperceptibly. Roots continue their slow withdrawal from the clay. Inside a fallen trunk, termites enlarge a hollow without altering the shape above it. The forest holds its position through the hot darkness, densely occupied at every height, while thousands of separate appetites make changes too small to see.
