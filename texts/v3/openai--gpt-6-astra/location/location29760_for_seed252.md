The hollow lies just under the surface, inside a blister of pale volcanic stone. It is no larger than the space beneath a fallen table. From above, there is almost nothing to notice: a fresh crack, a little whiteness along its edges, heat lifting from it into already hot air.

Inside, the light is fierce.

Sun enters through the crack at an angle and strikes a sloping face of calcite. From there it spreads across the hollow, reflected between wet, nearly white walls. Even the undersides of the ledges are illuminated. A thin fold of stone cuts across the opening, but its shadow is filled with light from below. Only a few pinholes in the ceiling remain dark.

The floor has not had time to become level. It consists of three tilted plates, a scatter of chips, and a shallow basin where the plates meet. Some fragments still fit the scars above them. Their edges are sharp enough to split a drop of water: a bead arriving at a broken corner separates, hesitates, then runs down both sides.

There is water everywhere, though scarcely enough anywhere to cover a fingertip. It glazes the walls, gathers beneath the ceiling, fills the narrow joints in the stone. The basin is clear and very still in its center. Around its margin, tiny bubbles rise through white sediment. Each bubble lifts a little cone, escapes, and leaves the grains to settle into a slightly different slope.

The hollow opened recently. A movement in the rock pulled apart a sealed seam and admitted air to water that had been under pressure. Since then, everything has been adjusting. Dissolved minerals come out of solution. Thin flakes loosen as the exposed stone heats and cools. The first crusts grow over the first fractures.

There is no rounded pebble here. No bank of old mud. Nothing has traveled far.

Heat enters through the rear wall, where a deeper fissure brings up water too hot to touch. Its arrival is almost soundless. A clear swelling appears between two crystals, holds its shape for a moment, then slumps into a rivulet. Close to the source the rock is bare and faintly translucent. Farther down, evaporation has left a white border as fine as salt on the lip of a glass.

The air smells of hot stone, with a slight metallic sharpness. It is humid enough that the cooler ceiling continually sweats. Drops grow there for several minutes before falling. Most strike the sloping plates and spread without a splash. Occasionally one reaches the basin with a small, distinct tick.

That is the usual sound of the place: one tick, then a long interval in which water moves without being heard.

On the hottest wall, the brightness does not come entirely from the sun.

A colorless film occupies the boundaries between mineral grains. It is too thin to make the surface look coated. Within it, molecular assemblies pass heat and electrical charge through a sequence of precisely fitted reactions, releasing part of the energy as visible light. They also move calcium, repair their own active surfaces, and close damaged pathways before the hot brine can dissolve them.

A laboratory could recognize the operations without being able to reproduce the whole process. Here, they happen in a patch smaller than a hand.

There are no instruments, housings, or buried machines. The working material is the film itself, reproducing at its edges and taking apart its failed components. Whether its distant ancestors developed these capacities or received them no longer makes a visible difference. It lives where hot water meets cooler stone. It stops where that narrow exchange stops.

By daylight, its contribution is easily missed. It merely makes the rear wall seem unusually clear, unusually exposed. When a cloud crosses the crack overhead, the front of the hollow dims and the hot seam stays white.

Nothing leafy grows around it. There are no roots in the ceiling, no moss in the wet corners, no green stain at the waterline. Most of the hollow is unoccupied mineral surface. The film occurs in four small patches, widely separated, with bare rock between them. A fifth patch has recently begun on a fallen chip. Its presence is betrayed by a wet-looking edge that persists after the surrounding stone dries.

At the basin’s coolest end, a pale mite stands on six points of contact, holding its front legs above the water. It is smaller than a grain of coarse sand. For a long time it does not move. Then it advances along the margin, testing the surface ahead, feeding where a trace of film has washed down and caught against a crystal.

Its body is almost transparent. Against the white floor, it becomes visible chiefly when it casts a shadow.

There may be another beneath a ledge. There may not. The distances between these small lives are considerable at their scale.

As the afternoon heats the surface above, less water condenses overhead. The hanging drops shrink. The basin withdraws by the width of a hair, exposing a fresh strip of shining sediment. At the same time, flow from the deep fissure increases. The rear rivulet thickens and finds a new route around a chip that fell that morning.

For several minutes, two channels run side by side. Then the older one dries.

Its abandonment leaves a delicate ridge, sharp and white, scarcely higher than dust. The new channel begins laying down its own margins immediately.

Toward evening, the beam through the opening climbs the opposite wall. Individual crystals flare as it passes. The ceiling cools first; droplets gather again, larger now, and one falls onto the mite’s feeding place. The animal retreats beneath the lip of a plate.

The basin rises. Warm water reaches the abandoned channel and softens its new edge.

After sunset, the crack becomes a dark line overhead. The mineral film still lights the hollow: white at the hot seam, weaker across the basin, sufficient to show a single ceiling drop growing heavier. In its curved underside, the whole small chamber is reflected upside down.

Below it, another bubble works its way through the sediment.
