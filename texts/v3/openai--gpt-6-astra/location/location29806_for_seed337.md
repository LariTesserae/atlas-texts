The town stands lower than the fields. From the road, you see its roofs before you see any walls: pale reed, dark reed, boards weighted with lumps of clay. Beyond them, the opposite rim of the hollow is nearly level with your feet. People are moving down there, carrying baskets between houses so tightly set that their shoulders disappear and reappear behind corners.

Until recently, this was the shallow end of a lake.

The water was led away through a cut in the southern bank. Some remains in a central pool, some in the drainage trenches, and some has yet to leave the ground. But most of the basin is exposed now, a broad, almost level floor several paces below the surrounding country. Its clay is blue-gray where freshly opened and the color of flour where it has dried. There has not been time for a proper soil to form. No turf holds it. No weathered stones lie loose upon it. A dropped seed lands either on a hard skin or in something soft enough to close over it.

The first streets were marked with cord while the lakebed was still wet.

They are narrow, straight, and crowded. Each has a shallow gutter down one side and a raised walkway down the other, made from brushwood under packed gravel brought in from beyond the basin. The gravel is valuable. Where a handful has spilled, someone has swept it back. At the street crossings, split logs bridge the gutters; their upper faces are already polished by bare feet, although the undersides still smell of sap.

Nothing here has had much time to become old, but almost everything has already been used hard.

The buildings are close because only so much ground has been made firm enough to bear them. A few stand on rafts of crossed timber. Most rest on beds of bundled reeds, topped with rubble and thick clay plinths. Their walls rise in different stages: low courses under damp cloth, chest-high partitions with doorways but no lintels, complete houses pressed against roofless rooms. You can look down a passage and see a finished sleeping alcove, a woman smoothing a wall with her palm, and, farther on, the stake that marks where the next house will begin.

There is hardly an unclaimed patch of level ground. Between dwellings are brick stacks, water jars, drying mats, bundles of poles. Under the projecting roofs hang baskets and spare lengths of cord. Even the open spaces are occupied by future things: a rectangle reserved for a storehouse, a circle of pegs around a well, a lane kept clear because it must eventually carry the overflow from three streets.

From above, the town looks packed and settled. Within it, you notice how much is still being decided.

The daylight is ordinary and plentiful. This is no cavern, only a settlement sunk into the land, open to a broad cool sky. Clouds pass over the whole basin at once. On clear mornings the eastern bank keeps the lower houses in shadow for an extra hour, while sunlight catches the roof ridges and the white bellies of jars laid out to dry. By midday, the clay reflects enough light that people working at the bricks narrow their eyes.

The air stays cool near the ground. Put a hand into an unfinished foundation trench and the chill is immediate. Moisture beads on the shaded side of water jars. A folded cloth left overnight on a low bench will be damp underneath, though no rain has fallen.

Rain comes in brief, fine showers. When it does, the town changes its manner without much commotion. Mats are lifted over unfinished walls. Brickmakers turn their newest bricks on edge beneath shelters. Children retrieve bowls from the lanes. At each crossing, someone removes the little wad of straw that has caught against a bridge support.

Then the gutters begin to run.

Their water is milky at first, carrying clay from the fresh surfaces, and clear a little later. It passes through the town in a linked system of cuts no wider than an arm, gathering toward the southern outlet. There, a wooden gate holds back water when the river beyond the bank is high. When the river falls, two people lift the gate with a lever, and the basin drains again. A small shelter stands beside it. Someone is always within sight of the water marks.

The town’s quiet is partly the quiet of its materials. Clay receives a footstep without ringing. Reed screens soften voices. Loads travel in baskets or on low sledges, not wheeled carts. There is hammering, but it comes from one place at a time: a wooden peg driven into a roof joint, a stone mason fitting the sill of the sluice. Most work makes smaller sounds. A cord pulled through reeds. A blade scraping a wet board. Bricks lifted from their molds with a faint release of suction.

From a raised walkway, you can watch a whole neighborhood working and hear only the nearest pair of hands.

At the northern edge, where the bank curves inward, a strip of the former shore survives. A few willows stand there, their roots exposed by the lower water. People have wrapped some roots in clay and reed litter to keep them from drying. The trees carry small, cold-colored leaves. Beneath them is the only irregular shade in the basin.

Elsewhere, vegetation has scarcely begun. A green seam follows a leaking channel. Rushes persist around the central pool, cut short wherever they interfere with access. On heaps of drying lake mud, seedlings appear and vanish under the next basketful. There are no gardens yet, only a few shallow trays outside doorways, planted with onions and bitter leaves in soil carried down from the fields.

Animals are uncommon. A wagtail works along a gutter, advancing a few steps whenever a person approaches. Small snails cling beneath the surviving reeds. Once or twice a day, a dog comes down the entrance ramp, noses around the food stalls, and leaves with clay packed between its toes. The pool holds a few fish stranded by the draining; their movements show as brief disturbances under the dull surface.

The largest open area is not a square but a drying ground. Hundreds of fresh bricks lie there in rows, each with the same shallow thumb mark from being turned out of its mold. Walking beside them, you see slight differences in color and firmness. Yesterday’s batch is dark. Last week’s is pale enough to stack. Farther away, under reed covers, bricks wait for walls whose foundations have not yet been laid.

Beyond the drying ground, the basin continues into an expanse of damp clay where surveying cords tremble in the breeze. Narrow test trenches expose the clean layers beneath. There are stakes for another street, another drain, another row of houses. The work advances outward from the crowded center, but not evenly. A soft patch delays one district. A successful drain opens another. People learn the shape of the place by loading it, cutting it, waiting beside it.

Toward evening, cool air settles into the hollow. The upper fields remain sunlit after the lanes have dimmed. Cooking smoke rises almost straight, then spreads above the roofs at the height of the surrounding ground.

The last workers cover the bricks. Water continues to gather in the trenches. Beneath the new houses, the reed beds compress by amounts too small to see.

At one doorway, a man crouches to shave a little clay from the bottom of a door that fitted yesterday.
