The land barely rises out of its own moisture. Across the whole low basin, the difference between a patch of standing water and a patch of forest floor is often no more than the thickness of a root. There are no banks to step down, no clear shorelines. Water darkens the soil until the soil becomes water, with leaves lying flat across the uncertainty.

Heat occupies everything. It is in the mud, in the undersides of leaves, in the small spaces between the scales of an animal. Even the water seeping from the ground is warm. Nothing offers that brief coolness a wet place ought to offer. A drop gathered on a hanging root touches the pool below without changing its temperature.

Overhead, broad leaves interlock beneath a low, pale roof of vapor. The sun is somewhere beyond both layers. Its light reaches the basin as a dull green illumination, sufficient to show the nearest trunks but not to separate the deeper ones. Distance accumulates in overlapping silhouettes. Through occasional gaps, the forest can be seen continuing across wide stretches of reedbed and shallow water, out to a low margin where the canopy and the mist become indistinguishable.

The ground looks new.

Its exposed patches are smooth and dark, without the grit and mixed fragments of old soil. Here and there, pale mineral matter bulges through it in rounded lobes, soft-looking but firm under the weight of roots. None of these outcrops has a broken face. None has been cut into channels. Water beads on them, joins itself into a thin film, and slips away without leaving a mark.

There is no loose stone. Nothing seems to have fallen from anything else.

Along the open reaches, the pale material forms shallow shelves just beneath the water. Their edges are swollen, almost unfinished. They suggest a surface that has recently emerged and has not yet acquired weather. But the great plants growing across them are fully established, and the same roots that enter the shelves disappear deep inside them. It is difficult to tell whether the land rose around the forest or the forest held the land together while it formed.

Vegetation uses every available support. Thick, low trees spread sideways, their trunks hidden by sheaths of climbing stems. Leaves gather in tiers: small glossy ones pressed against bark, long divided ones extending into the gloom, enormous undivided blades held level over the pools. The edges of these blades almost touch. Between them hang curtains of fine roots, each carrying a silver line of water.

There are flowers, but most are tucked close to the ground. They are fleshy and dark, plum-colored cups opening from the bases of stalks. Their smell is stronger than their color: ripe fruit, warm sap, something faintly animal. Insects crowd their interiors so densely that the flowers appear furred.

The whole place is occupied.

Small bodies cover the wet trunks. Some have the color and dullness of bark; others are translucent, showing a thread of green or brown inside. Jointed animals brace themselves beneath leaves, each at the center of a little clearing in the smaller life. Snails lie along stems in overlapping ranks. Broad-backed beetles feed shoulder to shoulder on swollen fruit, while tiny flies stand on the beetles.

At water level, the density is greater still. Fine legs stipple the surface. Flat larvae fit themselves under submerged leaves. Fish hold between the roots, their fins working just enough to keep their mouths pointed into the slow seepage. Some are no longer than a fingernail. Others are heavy, blunt shapes that can be mistaken for pieces of the bottom until an eye becomes visible.

Larger animals are present everywhere without offering much of themselves to sight. A muscular tail lies across a mineral shelf. A rounded flank fills the space behind a curtain of creepers. Above it, gripping feet press into a branch too deeply shaded to reveal the body they belong to. There is scarcely an empty hollow large enough to receive a hand.

Yet the abundance does not produce a feeling of pursuit or commotion. Most creatures seem settled into exact positions. Their mouths work. Their throats pulse. Their limbs adjust against the steady pressure of neighboring bodies. The basin is busy at a scale too small to disturb its outlines.

Its sound is correspondingly full. A coarse insect rasp extends through the trees, with lower, hollow calls sounding inside it. Close by, there is the dry clicking of mouthparts, the soft tearing of plant tissue, the faint percussion of animals touching leaves. Beneath all of this lies a continuous watery murmur, although no stream can be found.

The calls overlap so thoroughly that beginnings and endings disappear. A listener might wait for a pause and gradually realize there is no pause to wait for.

Nor is there a convincing sense of weather approaching. The vapor remains at the same height. The leaves carry the same weight of moisture. No gust turns their pale undersides outward. The dimness neither deepens nor lifts.

This constancy extends beyond the air. The waterline stays fixed against the roots. The mineral shelves remain smooth. Fallen leaves lie just beneath the surface, softened to the point where their veins show clearly, but they do not seem to pass beyond that condition. Beside them, new leaves remain tightly folded at the tips of shoots, glossy with the effort of opening.

There is movement everywhere, but little evidence that it adds up to change.

Farther out, where the trees thin into a broad, sedged flat, the basin becomes visible as a whole: an immense shallow hollow, densely alive, with pale ground showing through warm water in scattered patches. Low islands carry clusters of broad-leaved plants. Animals fill the narrow spaces between their stems. Mist rests against the foliage without flowing over it.

Nothing here has acquired the shape of ruin, or even wear. The roots are not exposed by retreating earth; the water has not learned a channel; the shelves have not lost their rounded edges. The place holds the freshness of land just made, though no part of it appears to be waiting for what comes next.

Under the leaves, in the warm green half-light, another throat takes up a call before the last one finishes.
