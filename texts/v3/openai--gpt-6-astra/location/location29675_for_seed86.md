The ground opens through a long break in the limestone, scarcely wider than a streambed from above. Thorn scrub grows along its lips. Below them, daylight catches a few pale ledges, then seems to run out before it reaches anything that could be called a floor.

But there is a floor, and a country of sorts beneath it.

The first slope descends through angular rubble into a chamber broad enough to lose its opposite wall. Beyond that chamber are others, joined not by narrow passages but by low, immense arches. Ridges divide the underground basin. Dry channels pass between them. There are shelves as wide as fields, hollows filled with fine sediment, long aprons of fallen stone. From some positions, one opening frames another and another, each a less distinct gray, until distance becomes indistinguishable from the darkness of the rock.

The ceiling is not everywhere visible. Where it is, it resembles the underside of a mountain rather than a roof: great pendent masses, seams, slanting beds of limestone. Here and there, a fracture admits a blade of weak daylight. These separate openings give the basin its scattered landmarks. A white slope. A green stain. One bright edge on a block otherwise entirely black.

Most of the stone underfoot has sharp corners.

The cavern itself is old, but its exposed surfaces are comparatively fresh. Broad plates have detached along bedding planes, revealing pale interiors that have not yet acquired the brown skin of the older walls. Some lie flat; others lean together with spaces beneath them. A boot placed carelessly would slide on the smaller fragments, but the large blocks are settled deep in their own weight. There is no continuing rumble of collapse. The broken country has come to rest.

Water occupies very little of it.

A damp seam darkens one wall for a hundred paces, then ends. Condensation beads beneath an overhang without quite gathering into drops. In a sheltered depression lies a pool no larger than an animal’s body, clear enough that its surface is visible only where it reflects an opening overhead. Its rim is marked by a thin mineral line. Elsewhere, the channels are dry, their beds filled with pale grit and little chips of stone. They retain the shape of drainage without the sound of running water.

The air is cool and close to the same temperature everywhere. Near the deepest hollows it rests against the face like air from a cellar. Beneath the larger openings there is a faint current, too weak to stir anything heavier than a fern tip.

There are only a few ferns.

They occupy the precise places where light and moisture overlap: a narrow shelf below a crack, the shaded foot of a daylight shaft, a damp pocket between two fallen slabs. Small fronds spread almost horizontally. A little moss fills the joints behind them. Farther from the openings, green becomes a powdery film, then disappears. Most of the basin offers no leaf, root, or stem at all.

Nevertheless, the place is full of animals.

At first this is apparent only as sound. A dense, high chitter comes from above, threaded with dry clicks, scratches, and occasional sharper cries. The arches carry it across the basin and return it in softened layers. There is no single echo to count. Each call enters a sound already crowded with other calls.

Then the ceiling resolves.

The dark grain on the limestone is bats: close-packed bodies hanging from seams and rough patches, thousands within a single visible recess. Farther back, whole overhangs are occupied. Their folded wings make a pebbled, leathery surface over the rock. Here and there a pale ear or a small muzzle catches the dim light. An individual turns, scratches, pushes against its neighbor. The colony’s outline scarcely alters.

Other animals occupy the lower world.

Cave crickets stand beneath stone lips on legs so long and fine that their bodies seem suspended in little frameworks. Beetles work through the material below the roosts. Mites gather where the floor looks dusted with rust. In the closest view, that dust separates into living points. Flat-backed millipedes follow the sheltered contacts between slabs. Small spiders hang in the recesses that the crickets do not use.

Around each great roost, the floor has its own geography: a central deposit, dark and dry on top; a lower, damper margin; pale rubble beyond. Each band supports different inhabitants. The boundary between two textures of earth may also be a boundary between two almost identical beetles. A stone lifted from one side would have a different underside from a stone lifted a few feet away.

Between these crowded patches lie immense stretches of nearly bare ground.

You could cross a whole shelf seeing nothing move, with the animal noise loud enough to make conversation difficult. The emptiness is genuine. So is the abundance. Life gathers where food falls, where a seam holds moisture, where the ceiling offers a secure purchase. It does not spread evenly merely because there is room.

A few snakes rest in crevices near the roosts, their scales almost the same gray as the stone. Their presence is easiest to notice where a curve contradicts the straight fracture beside it. There are no large tracks crossing the basin, no browsed shrubs, no broad paths worn by heavy bodies. Nearly all the animal weight is overhead or divided among creatures small enough to disappear beneath a pebble.

Nothing here has been built. The level shelves are bedding planes; the apparent steps are broken strata. There is no soot on the low ceilings, no worked edge among the fragments, no arrangement of stones that needs a hand to explain it.

From a high ridge of rubble, the basin can be seen in sections: pale ground beneath a distant slit of sky, black arches, another pale slope beyond. The scale becomes clearer without becoming intimate. Most of it remains inaccessible to the eye.

The place gives little sense of an event approaching. The pools sit within their mineral rims. The fresh fractures stay sharp in the shelter of the cavern. The few plants hold their small allotments of light. Above the unoccupied acres of stone, the colonies maintain their dense, restless murmur.

There is movement everywhere one looks closely, but almost none in the landscape as a whole. A cricket shifts beneath a ledge. A bat opens one wing and folds it again. The noise continues at the same fullness, held beneath the limestone, while the great spaces between the living patches remain cool, dim, and bare.
