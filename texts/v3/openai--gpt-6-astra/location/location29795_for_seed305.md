The heights begin before the land rises. For several days’ travel across the lower plains, the horizon is already white with them: not snow peaks, but the exposed edges of a new continent, lifted into the upper air with its deep strata still showing. The cliffs have not had time to become slopes. They stand in long, clean breaks, dark green and silver, with shelves large enough to carry weather.

Above them lies a country so densely inhabited that, from a distance, it looks empty. Nothing moves quickly enough to distinguish itself from the ground.

The light is nearly unbearable. A pale sun hangs above a layer of cloud far below, and the cloud throws its light upward against the undersides of leaves, the bellies of animals, the overhanging lips of stone. There are few true shadows. What should be dark is filled instead with a cold, reflected brilliance. Fine mineral scales in the soil catch it; frost on the vegetation breaks it into colors too small to resolve. Looking across a valley gives the sensation of looking into something freshly fractured.

There is little loose earth. The rock is young, its corners intact, its fissures narrow and deep. Some cracks still fit together so precisely that their opposite walls seem like the halves of a mold. Pale roots enter them in bundles. As the plateau cools, the cracks open by fractions, and the roots thicken to occupy the new space.

Almost every exposed surface is growing something.

The vegetation stays low, but low does not mean sparse. There are forests here no higher than a person’s knee, with trunks as thick as wrists and crowns interlocked so closely that nothing can fall between them. Their leaves are fleshy, dark, and folded lengthwise around seams of white fur. Beneath these are smaller plants, and beneath those a nap of black filaments pressed against the stone. On sheltered faces, the growth rises into walls: overlapping fans, stiff combs, translucent bladders, branches bearing compact masses of green needles. The green is so deep it looks warm.

It is not warm. An unprotected hand placed among the leaves soon loses feeling.

Water arrives mostly as a brief condition of the air. At intervals, a cloud from below spills over an escarpment and passes through the vegetation. Every blade takes a skin of moisture. Hairs uncoil, pores open, and the entire low forest darkens. Then the cloud thins in the white light and everything closes again.

The water does not reach the ground unless there is more than the plants can use.

Where it does, it collects in narrow blue seams under clear ice. There are pools the size of a thumbnail that have lasted longer than the cliffs surrounding them, carried upward inside the rock and only recently opened to daylight. Around each one is a crowded assemblage of drinkers, filters, egg cases, and delicate grazing mouths. A larger pool, perhaps wide enough to step across, can support so many bodies that its surface is visible only as a wet gleam between them.

The animals are everywhere else as well.

What appears to be a stretch of pale gravel is a congregation of six-legged creatures with rounded mineral backs. They rest shoulder to shoulder, faces buried in the root felt. A ridge of black stone is lined with broad, flat animals holding their bodies upright to the light. Their blood, visible through the edges, moves in slow branching pulses. The hanging fringes beneath the vegetation are not roots but animals suspended by their mouths. Some are slender as thread; others have the weight and shape of filled sacks. All draw nourishment from the cold sap and return something to the plants through a second, smaller bite.

There is very little bare space in which to place a foot. Even the apparently vacant patches are occupied by transparent things.

Yet the plateau does not seethe. Its abundance is patient. A herd may take a season to cross a shallow depression. An animal lifting its head can remain between one posture and the next long enough for frost to form along the newly exposed skin. Predators lie among prey without causing alarm until a particular change of pressure or scent announces that feeding has begun. Then, within a few handspans, there is a brief violence. Beyond that small circle, nothing appreciably changes.

The loudness comes from deeper down.

At first it resembles machinery heard through the wall of a very large building: a steady low rotation, a knock repeated at uneven intervals, the rising strain of something under load. There are no buildings. No roads cross the plateau, and nothing has been assembled here.

Under the living mat are animals whose bodies extend through whole basins.

Most of each animal lies in the newly opened joints of the bedrock. Their organs follow faults; their insulated vessels climb through shafts that were once gas vents. At the surface, their exposed parts look like geology—a ring of hard plates around a hollow, a long ribbed bank, a smooth gray dome with frost gathered at its base.

They turn.

Not their whole bodies, but heavy internal organs, grown around axles of dense cartilage. The organs rotate continuously through baths of dark conductive fluid. Broad muscular bands maintain the motion. Where their tissues meet the mineral-rich rock, they separate metals from salts, compress gases, and drive warm fluids uphill. Their metabolism depends on throughput. A basin-sized body must process a basin’s worth of poor material to stay alive.

You can hear the individual stages if you remain still long enough. The coarse vibration of a crushing chamber. A sequence of valves opening beneath your feet. The remote, hollow impact of a piston reaching the end of its chamber. Some cycles recur every few breaths. Others take several days, and their completion is felt as a single deep blow through the plateau.

Around the exposed organs grow thick collars of vegetation. Animals crowd against their faint warmth. Entire colonies feed on the pale mineral meal discharged from pores along the flank. The largest grazers slowly scrape these deposits into their mouths with lower jaws like broad scoops. Their backs carry smaller grazers, and those carry still smaller organisms in the channels between their scales.

There is waste heat, but never enough. Within a short distance of a vent the frost returns.

On clear days, electrical charge gathers in the dry upper leaves. Fine blue light runs along the edges of the forests. It passes through the aligned animals and down into the bodies under the stone. Sometimes a whole slope discharges at once with a flat, enormous crack. For a moment, every translucent creature on it shows its nerves.

This is the nearest the country comes to sudden weather.

More often its weather is slow and local. A congregation of dark-backed animals absorbs sunlight for weeks, warming a shelf enough to draw mist over its rim. The mist feeds the vegetation. The vegetation thickens, shades the animals, and eventually drives them onward. Their departure exposes the ground to cold. The mist ceases. Frost advances through the abandoned feeding traces, and after several years the shelf is pale again.

Across a continent, these shifts make broad, almost motionless bands of color. Green districts widen. White districts contract. A dark front, composed of hundreds of millions of migrating bodies, advances perhaps a few lengths in a day.

The air above is crowded too, though not with the usual hurry of wings. Flat, translucent animals hang at different heights, supported by internal gas chambers and long fringes that hold the thin air. Their shadows drift over the plateau so slowly that plants have time to adjust their leaves as the light changes. Some trail threads down into the forest. Along those threads climb young animals, ascending toward a feeding place that may be a month away.

Farther out, beyond the nearest sharp ridges, the plateau continues under its suspended inhabitants until distance whitens everything. There are escarpments on that horizon too, and beyond them higher shelves. A deep mechanical note reaches you from somewhere in that brightness. Another answers from underfoot, close enough to rattle ice out of a leaf.

Along the lip of a fresh fissure, a crust of frost has split. Beneath it, the rock is clean and dark. A pale root is pressed into one corner. Beside the root, something jointed is easing its head into the opening, moving too slowly to see until you look away and look back.
