The hollow is just large enough for the shadow of one tree, though no tree grows in it.

It lies near the summit of a plateau, concealed behind a lip of stone worn so low and smooth that, from outside, it looks like another fold in the ground. You could pass within three paces and never see down into it. Beyond the lip, the land continues for miles in tawny, almost level shelves. This high country has been high for so long that little remains of its original steepness. Its stones have weathered through the shapes of cliffs, boulders, rubble, and finally these broad, shallow curves, comfortable-looking as places on a floor where animals have slept.

Inside the hollow, the air is warm enough to feel inhabited.

The floor is pale rock, slightly dished, crossed by narrow seams of dark soil. There are long bare intervals. A single fern occupies one seam; six feet away, a thick cushion of small, shining leaves spills from another. Along the western edge, a vine has gathered itself into a dense green fall no wider than a doorway. Its leaves overlap so tightly that you cannot see where it roots. The vegetation is rich wherever it occurs: fleshy stems, extravagant new growth, leaf undersides purple as a bruise. Between these green concentrations, the stone lies clean and open.

Almost nothing here grows toward the center.

At noon, ordinary sunlight fills the place. Small insects throw sharp shadows. The fern shows every vein. The surrounding rim cuts off the horizon, leaving a bowl of blue sky in which the occasional distant bird passes without seeming to move. There is no visible reason for the hollow to be different from the dry plateau around it.

Then a wet line appears on the rock.

It starts near the vine, a hairline of moisture darkening a groove. Slowly it lengthens uphill. The groove itself is old, rounded almost out of existence, and in several places the water must spread sideways to find it again. Dust becomes paste. A scale of mica lifts, turns once, and settles. Where the wet line reaches the fern, its lowest frond gives a small, abrupt shake.

That is the signal.

What seemed to be grit begins running.

There are copper-colored mites between the grains of sand, flat gray animals beneath the thin stone flakes, translucent jumpers clinging to the undersides of leaves. Beetles emerge from holes too small to have admitted them. A section of bare floor resolves into several hundred motionless, camouflaged wings, then becomes bare floor again as their owners take flight. The hollow fills with bodies at different speeds: drifting, darting, crawling, springing in short mechanical arcs.

The noise is astonishing.

Outside, there was only the scrape of wind. Here there is a dense, hard rattling, like rain striking a roof of seed pods, threaded with whistles so high they seem to touch the teeth. Something in the vine knocks three times, pauses, then knocks twice. Wing cases snap open. Buried chambers amplify the scrabbling of animals smaller than fingernails. The stone’s shallow curves throw these sounds back across one another until the whole little enclosure seems much deeper than it is.

You can still see plenty of empty rock. That is part of the difficulty of looking at the place: its abundance does not fill space evenly. It travels in fronts, pours through seams, vanishes under a leaf. One square inch may contain more animals than all the exposed floor around it.

A lizard the color of unglazed clay waits on the eastern slope. Its head turns in tiny increments. When it strikes, several insects leap toward its mouth rather than away; one fastens to the soft skin below its eye. The lizard rubs its face against the stone and resumes waiting. Above it, a small hovering predator with four transparent wings carries something almost its own size. Its burden continues singing.

There is very little water to support all this.

Rain reaches the hollow perhaps a few times in a season, usually as the ragged edge of a storm spending itself elsewhere. The rock drinks briefly. The soil seams swell. For an hour the place smells of crushed stalks and hot clay, and then the surface dries.

Most moisture arrives by other means.

Under the western vine stands a narrow upright object, black, waist-high, and smooth except for a shallow depression near its top. It might be the last portion of a gatepost. Its lower third has been engulfed by roots. Beside it, half buried, are three pieces of pale material that look like broken ceramic until an insect crosses one and appears beneath its surface, still walking.

The upright gathers dew in full sunlight.

Beads form on its warm face, hang for a while, and disappear without falling. A moment later the seams in the floor darken. The process makes no noise, but nearby wings falter whenever it happens. Hovering insects drop a finger’s breadth, recover, and go on. A loose hair laid beside the object slowly points toward it.

On the plateau, where the sun is equally strong, nothing condenses.

There are other remnants: two shallow sockets set opposite one another in the rim, a thread of silver substance running through an exposed root, a little square recess so precisely edged that weather seems unable to enter it. At certain hours, the recess contains the reflection of a leaf that is no longer there. The vine has grown several inches past the position shown.

Whatever was installed here still operates within a small, exact boundary. Moisture forms inside it. Outside it, the rock remains dry. Animals carried beyond the rim turn back, even species that could fly much farther. Roots spread freely through the hollow but thicken and branch when they reach its edges, making a loose, living basket beneath the stone.

The center stays warm after sunset.

During the day it is merely the emptiest part: a pale oval, worn smooth, with a few shallow pits. As the shadows lengthen, larvae emerge to drag husks and dead insects toward those pits. They work in teams that assemble and dissolve without warning. Larger scavengers steal from them. The fern uncurls a new tip over several minutes, visibly, its tissue almost transparent before it darkens into green.

Then the calls change.

The sharp daytime clatter gives way to a lower vibration from beneath the floor. Loose seed cases creep across the stone. A tiny pool in a root socket develops six stationary ripples, as though something held it lightly from below. Moths settle along the lip with their wings upright, spaced at remarkably regular intervals. Their antennae all incline toward the warm center.

For a little while, the hollow resembles a place arranged for a gathering.

There is no arrival. The warmth rises; the submerged humming deepens. Pale, jointed animals climb from the soil seams and stand on their hind legs, exposing the damp membranes of their bellies. The vine’s flowers open facing the rock. They are small and green, nearly invisible among the leaves, but their scent is enormous: fermented fruit, pepper, the mineral smell of a struck stone.

By full dark, the moths are feeding, the upright has begun another slow collection of dew, and the lizard has wedged itself into a crack with only its watchful eye exposed.

Beyond the rim, the plateau cools beneath the stars. Wind travels across miles of worn stone without finding much to disturb. Here, below the reach of that wind, wings brush leaves, jaws work, water advances another inch along its ancient groove. A fallen petal trembles on the bare floor until something underneath takes hold of it and pulls it away.
