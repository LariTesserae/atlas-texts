The ground has risen out of the sea, but not far enough to dry.

For several miles inland, water stands in the seams of the plain. It fills the shallow cups left by settling mud, lies under tilted slabs, beads along the undersides of stone ledges. There are no streams large enough to name. Instead, there are connections: a black slit under a shelf of chalk, a run of wet gravel, a pool that slowly empties while another, some distance away, slowly fills. The sea is seldom visible. You know where it is because the water in the seams becomes brackish in that direction, and because all the exposed edges of the land lean slightly away from it.

The plain is low, but it is not flat. Its surface has buckled into thousands of short ridges, some knee-high, others taller than a house. Between them lie narrow courts of mud and corridors floored with broken shell. A ridge that looks solid from one side proves, from the other, to be a stack of thin plates with darkness between every layer. Some plates are white, some smoke-gray, some almost blue. Their edges are fresh enough to cut a hand. Rain has only begun to round them.

From a height, the whole district would resemble a mass of shutters half opened beneath the water.

There is no height from which to see it that way. You see a few hundred yards at most: close ranks of pale edges, a broad pool holding the dim sky, a darker ridge beyond. The openness is overhead and in the knowledge that the country continues. On the ground, everything nearly touches.

Nothing grows on the stone. There are no grasses in the wet cracks, no moss where a ledge drips all day, no small tree making use of a pocket of soil. Even the mud is bare. Its surface carries rings of salt, tiny collapse holes, and the trails of things seldom seen. In places, a translucent skin lies over the shallows. It wrinkles when touched and takes several days to smooth again. Whether it is alive is difficult to establish by watching. Small animals sometimes eat it, though they also eat the soft outer layers of the rocks.

The air is cool enough that wet sleeves remain cold against the wrists. A low roof of cloud supplies a little light without any clear direction. Most surfaces are dull until you move; then a film of water flashes along an edge and disappears. Under the slabs, droplets gather with such regular spacing that the undersides look studded with glass. A drop may hang there for an hour. When it falls, it leaves a pale circular scar, which darkens again as the next drop forms.

The place is loud.

At first the noise seems to be surf, although there is no breaking water nearby. It has that depth and spread, that sense of something immense dragging over a rough surface. Beneath it are other sounds: a grinding low note, a long hollow whistle, sudden rattles that pass through a ridge and stop. The ground is full of chambers. Water moving almost imperceptibly through the lower seams displaces air in the upper ones, and the air escapes through narrow mouths in the stone.

One ridge sounds for most of a morning. Another answers after noon.

The notes do not rise cleanly or end cleanly. They thicken, split, lose a higher tone, continue at the edge of hearing. Sometimes a chamber fills far enough to seal its own opening. Its sound stops, and the absence is so abrupt that you turn toward it. Then you hear all the other ridges again.

Little appears to be moving. A pool has no ripples. A hanging flake of stone stays precisely where it is. Yet the roar presses against the chest, and a hand laid on a broad slab feels the faint vibration. The energy comes from differences in water level smaller than a finger’s width, working through miles of enclosed passages.

There are scattered structures among the ridges.

They are easy to miss because they use the same pale plates as the ground. Four or five slabs stand on edge around a patch of raised shell gravel, with another laid across them. Some are too low to enter except on hands and knees. Others would shelter two people sitting close together. They have no doors and no hearths. Their openings usually face a smooth, shallow basin, never the longest view.

Inside, the gravel is dry.

This seems a small distinction until you have walked for several hours. Everywhere else, moisture enters from above or below. Here a thin white crust lies unbroken between the stones. The inner walls feel almost warm because they are not wet. There is room to put down a bundle without choosing which side of it can be sacrificed to the water.

The shelters are also quieter, although not silent. Their roof slabs take up one note from the plain and hum with it. Each shelter has a different pitch. In one, the hum is low enough to feel through the seat of the body; in another, it makes the teeth ache. A few have fallen inward. The fallen slabs continue to hum, but with a loose, unpleasant chatter.

No road connects these places. Occasionally there is a line of flat stones across a muddy channel, or a series of shallow notches along a ledge where feet would otherwise slip. The marks are sparse and practical. Whoever made them did not try to impose straight routes on the plain.

There are reasons not to.

A dry passage may be roof rather than ground. Some chambers are covered by little more than a handspan of mineral crust. The safest surface is usually the roughest: loose shell, jagged rubble, ridges whose broken interiors are open to view. Smooth expanses can sound underfoot like a vessel struck with a knuckle. A broad, beautiful floor of gray stone may have nothing beneath it but cold water and trapped air.

The animals seem to know this.

Their tracks follow the rubble edges. Most are small paired impressions, with a fine line drawn between them by a tail. Once in a great while, something pale crosses an open patch: a low-bodied creature the length of a forearm, with short legs and a head that scarcely narrows from its shoulders. Its skin has the bluish translucence of soaked shell. It stops frequently, laying the underside of its jaw against the ground.

It waits through changes in the sound before moving on.

These creatures feed where the water leaves its translucent films. They scrape with the edges of their mouths, making clean little crescents in the cloudy surface. There are never many. A whole basin may contain the feeding marks of one animal, accumulated over weeks. If disturbed, it does not run far. It presses itself into a seam, and the seam, which appeared much too narrow, accepts it.

Toward the middle of the district, the ridges crowd more closely. Thin fins emerge between the larger plates. Some have only recently split free, and a thread of soft, wet material still joins their edges. Here the plain is making more of itself. Pale sediment, once held together under water, swells and separates in the air. What looks from a distance like a weathered ruin is often newly exposed stone.

A slab breaks perhaps once in several days.

The event is short: a crack like a heavy door slammed in an empty building, followed by the clatter of fragments. But its consequences spread slowly. A new opening releases air. A familiar note weakens. Water begins entering a hollow that has been dry for years. Far away, under another ridge, a pool rises until it touches a white mark on the wall.

By evening there is less light, though no sunset. The sheltered undersides become indistinguishable from the spaces beyond them. Pools seem wider because their edges disappear. You can still pick out the nearest white slabs, and farther off, a few wet faces suspended in the gray.

The sound continues without regard to the darkness.

If you stay in one of the little shelters, you may wake to a different pitch in the roof. Nothing inside has moved. Your bundle is dry; the gravel still carries the impression of your heel. Outside, in the basin the opening faces, the water has risen a little.

It is resting against the lowest stone of the threshold.
