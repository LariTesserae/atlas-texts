The height is most apparent at the edges, where the ground ends without revealing anything below.

There is no view down into foothills, no blue succession of lesser ranges. Beyond the rim lies a pale, unlit depth. Looking into it gives the same small muscular ache as looking upward for too long. A loose stone set near the edge will remain there, but its shadow sometimes falls outward, onto something much farther away than the ground.

The basin itself is only a few miles across. You can see most of it from the western saddle: a floor of gray shingle, three low ridges, a dark shelf along the north wall, and a shallow watercourse that passes beneath the ridges without cutting through them. Nothing crowds anything else. A boulder has a quarter-mile of gravel to itself. A patch of vegetation occupies the lee of a rock with the economy of a hand placed over a wound. There are long reaches where even the differences between stones seem to have been worn away.

Above this country, the mountain continues.

Its upper slopes are visible through the dimness, immensely steep but softened by age. Their shoulders have settled into broad curves. Old gullies terminate in blunt hollows. Here and there, a harder seam stands out from the surrounding rock, rounded along its upper edge like the spine of an animal that has lain in one position for a very long time. There is no summit in sight. The slopes simply become too faint to distinguish from the sky.

The sky has the color of ice seen through several thicknesses of cloth.

There is light enough to walk, light enough to tell wet stone from dry, but very little light to spare. Under a projecting ledge, darkness begins abruptly. The undersides of some stones are more visible than their upper surfaces. This is especially noticeable in the gravel beds, where every pebble seems to rest above a narrow seam of dusk-colored illumination. Turning one over does not explain it.

The cold is steady. It lives inside the materials rather than arriving on the wind. Touching a rock feels less like losing warmth than discovering how shallow warmth has always been.

There is almost no wind.

Nevertheless, the place is loud.

A broad, crushing sound fills the basin: water falling an enormous distance, or a mass of stones turning over beneath the earth. It has no obvious beginning and no interruptions. Close to the northern shelf it acquires a metallic harshness, though there is no metal there. On the open gravel it spreads into a low roar that seems to press against the ribs. In certain hollows, a higher tone stands within it, so precise that the teeth begin to hurt.

You can speak. Your companion’s lips move, and the sense of the words may reach you, but the voice scarcely does.

Putting your ear to the ground makes the sound quieter.

There are places where you can kneel, rest your forehead against the stone, and hear your own breathing clearly for the first time since entering the basin. People who have crossed here leave shallow, paired depressions in the grit at these listening places. Knees and hands. Nothing more elaborate.

The water is difficult to account for.

Most of the land is dry, its gravel loosely packed and pale with mineral dust. Yet dampness collects in precise, widely separated locations. A black oval halfway up a cliff. A line of moisture beneath a stone that has no visible crack. A shallow pool on a tilted slab, its surface maintaining the slope of the rock rather than finding a level of its own.

The stream in the basin is scarcely wider than a stride. Its bed is lined with smooth, dark stones, and a narrow fringe of white mineral deposit marks its banks. It looks as though it is running. Threads of brightness extend around the stones; little hollows gather behind them; the water leans against every obstacle with the familiar shape of a current.

But a pale leaf lies beneath the surface without shifting.

It is the only leaf you are likely to see.

The water is cold enough to numb a finger almost immediately. Withdraw your hand and droplets cling to the skin. One may remain there far longer than it should, preserving the distorted reflection of a ridge even after you have turned away from it.

Rain occupies part of the eastern basin. From a distance it resembles a fine gray curtain, perhaps two hundred paces wide. The shingle beneath it is dark. Outside it, the ground is dry within the width of a boot.

You can walk into the rain. You feel the small impacts on your face and hear, beneath the greater noise, an intimate ticking against your clothes. Yet from outside, no individual drop can be seen to descend. The curtain has the fixed texture of scratched glass. Its edge does not wander.

There are no clouds directly above it.

Vegetation survives where the rain ends, where the damp is present but not oppressive. The plants are low cushions of black-green leaves, so tight that their separate stems are hidden. Fine white roots penetrate cracks in the rock. Several cushions have dead centers, exposing gray, fibrous interiors, but the living rims show no new shoots. Their leaves are leathery and very small. Each holds a dull point of light.

Lichen occurs farther out, in faint yellow islands on the undersides of boulders. You have to crouch to find it. Once you do, you begin to notice that many of the stones are inhabited on their sheltered faces, carrying a little color where the landscape cannot ordinarily see it.

An animal stands on the third ridge.

It is about the size of a goat, pale at the shoulders and dark below the knees. Its coat hangs in long strips. The horns curve close to the skull, protecting the ears from a wind that does not blow. There is enough distance between it and the nearest rock that its outline is unmistakable.

You may spend a long time deciding whether it has moved.

Later, from another part of the basin, you see it behind a different stone. There was no crossing to witness. This does not feel like a sudden displacement. It feels as though both positions belonged to it all along, and your route has brought you into the reach of the second.

Its tracks occur in small groups. Four or five clear impressions, then nothing. They do not lead toward forage or shelter. In one damp patch, the prints are full of water; beside them lies a pellet of dung split open by frost, with a few green fibers inside. Such ordinary evidence is reassuring enough that you may look at it longer than you looked at the animal.

There are also buildings, though only three can be found from any one vantage.

They are low shelters of flat stone, spaced along the basin’s southern margin. Their walls lean into the slope. No mortar, timber, or worked fittings remain, if there ever were any. The roofs are enormous slabs resting on smaller stones, with dark entrances below.

Each shelter could hold two people lying close together.

Inside, the roar diminishes, but only if you face the back wall. Turn toward the entrance and it returns at full force. The effect is immediate and bodily, like putting your head beneath a waterfall and taking it out again.

The floors are bare. There are no hearths. A shallow groove runs around the inside of each wall, carrying beads of condensation to a little depression beside the doorway. The depressions are worn smooth, as if many hands have cupped water from them.

In the farthest shelter, a flat stone stands upright against the rear wall. On it are several marks, too simple to identify as writing: a long line, a small hollow, three shorter lines beneath. Their edges have weathered to the same softness as the stone’s natural fractures. If they were made deliberately, the act belongs to a time that has left no other obvious trace.

Outside, you begin to notice what the basin lacks.

There is no fresh scree below the cliffs. No patch of old snow shrinking at its margins. No fragile crust awaiting a footstep. Even the broken rocks seem finished with breaking. A split boulder rests with its two halves slightly apart, the gap between them dark and damp, and neither half has shifted enough to spill the gravel caught underneath.

The landscape bears abundant evidence of force. It has been cut, crushed, soaked, abraded. Water has polished the channels; ice has opened the joints. But the forces and their results have reached an accommodation here. The cliff does not need to fall farther. The stream does not need to arrive.

Perhaps this is what makes the noise so difficult to endure. It has all the violence of an event and none of the mercy of an ending.

You cross a dry reach toward the western saddle. There is room around you on every side. Your boots disturb the gravel with sounds you cannot hear. Behind you, the shallow marks of your passage are visible for a little way, then become difficult to distinguish from the settled ground.

At the saddle, the basin lies open again: the dim stream, the fixed rain, the shelters barely higher than stones. The pale animal is on its ridge. There is no sign of weather approaching, no warmer light anywhere along the slopes.

You can still feel the wet place on your hand.

Beyond the rim, in the depth where a lower world ought to be, the sound continues.
