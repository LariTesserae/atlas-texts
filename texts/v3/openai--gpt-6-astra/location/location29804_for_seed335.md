The hollow is easy to miss. From above, it is a split in the riverbank, half covered by bent sedge: a dark seam between the turf and a slab of blue-gray clay. Cold air comes out of it even when the wind is blowing in.

Inside, there is room to crouch, turn, and perhaps lie down, though nowhere dry enough to do so. The ceiling is the underside of the meadow. Roots hang from it, pale and sparsely branched, with water gathering on their tips. A few carry beads of clay down their length. The hollow has opened recently. Its walls still show the clean faces of the break, not yet rounded by seepage or written over with moss. Here and there a root stretches taut across the gap where the bank pulled apart.

Most of the space is empty.

There is the low roof, the shallow floor, and between them a cold interval through which a drop can fall a surprisingly long time. Everything living keeps close to a surface. Everything loose eventually goes toward the river.

The floor slopes into a pool connected to the river by a slit too low to see through. At its deepest, the water would cover a hand laid on edge. It looks much deeper because the clay beneath it is dark and the light arrives sideways. A pale stone near the middle gives the only reliable measure. Water climbs its lower half, pauses, then slips away, leaving a bright collar that slowly dulls.

Beyond the bank, somewhere out of sight, the river passes through turbines.

Nothing made has entered the hollow. There are no pipes, no scraps, no squared edges, but the machinery reaches it all the same. A heavy, continuous note comes through the water and the wet ground. Against the walls it is felt in the jaw more than heard. The air carries a rougher sound: rushing, grinding, a vast quantity of water being forced through a restricted place. At intervals another unit starts, and a second vibration searches through the first until the two settle into a deep, uneven beat.

The little pool responds before the river can be heard rising. Its surface trembles. Suspended clay lifts in fine veils. Then water presses through the slit and spreads across the floor, entering every footprint-sized depression, surrounding each fallen crumb of bank.

This is the hollow’s weather.

The releases do not come at quite the same hour each day. There are long quiet intervals when the pool clears enough to show the narrow tracks of snails, and busy afternoons when one pulse follows another. A change far downstream can reverse the slight current at the entrance. Leaves that have spent an hour drifting toward daylight come back into the dark.

Vegetation occupies one crooked shelf near the opening. There it is lavish: thick moss, small ferns with wet black stems, liverwort flattened over the clay in overlapping green lobes. The growth is only a hand’s breadth deep. Seen from outside, it conceals the cavity almost completely; seen from within, it is a green fringe around a large absence.

The ferns stay low. Their newest tips have been browned by frost. On cold mornings, needles of ice grow out of the bare clay beside them, lifting grains of soil into miniature colonnades. By afternoon the needles weaken. A section falls with a minute, dry sound, and a dark clot drops into the pool.

The bank is still deciding its shape. Water loosens it from within. Frost prises it apart from the exposed side. Once in a while a flake detaches from the ceiling and lands flat on the water with a slap far too loud for its size. The raw patch above it is lighter than the surrounding clay. For several minutes it shines.

Animals use almost none of the open volume, but the edges are busy. Freshwater shrimp flick backward beneath submerged leaves. A caddis larva drags its case over a pebble and leaves a delicate track through the silt. Along the waterline, springtails gather on a floating shred of bark; when it touches shore, the whole small congregation begins to disperse. Midges shelter under the overhang, their legs making faint stars against the clay.

A wren sometimes enters through the sedge. It appears abruptly, much too substantial for the place, and works along the root fringe with quick, violent attention. Each time it shakes a stem, droplets fall across the pool. Then it is gone, and the hollow seems larger in its absence.

There is food here because the river keeps bringing things in: softened leaf tissue, drowned insects, grains too small to distinguish from mud. There is shelter because the bank is broken. Neither condition is dependable. A strong release can scour the floor clean; another night of freezing can lower the roof.

Near dusk, the opening loses its green. The fern stems become a row of black hooks, and the water takes on the faint pewter light of the sky outside. The machinery continues beyond sight. A new current enters, cold enough to raise a thin mist where it meets the air.

The pale stone disappears under the rising pool. Shrimp retreat beneath the shelf. A snail that has been descending the wall stops, draws in its feelers, and begins, very slowly, to turn.
