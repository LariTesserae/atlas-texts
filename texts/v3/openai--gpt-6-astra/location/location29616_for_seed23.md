The town occupies a hollow no wider than a few hundred paces, high on a mountain whose summit has been worn away. There is no peak above it, only a rim of pale, rounded stone. From the highest roofs you can see other mountains standing clear of the cloud, but from the lanes there is usually nothing beyond the rim except white light.

Water enters on every side.

It comes through the rock in flat, trembling sheets, wells between paving stones, beads along the undersides of bridges. Even in the deepest cold it continues to arrive. It is bitter water, heavy with salts, and it freezes reluctantly: first into floating needles, then into soft grey skins that fold instead of breaking. Fresh snow vanishes where it touches it. The town smells faintly of wet wool, stone dust, and the underside of a shell.

Everything is bright enough to hurt. The cloud below reflects the sky; the hollow reflects the cloud; the water throws moving light beneath lintels and up the narrow shafts between buildings. Indoors, people hang dark felt over the walls. A room without it is almost as dazzling as the street.

The buildings are low but crowded, stacked up the sides of the hollow in close terraces. Their lower courses are made from the same rounded stone as the mountain, so that it is difficult to say where construction begins. Doorways have been shifted upward as floors sank. A blocked window may now admit a drain; an old chimney may serve as a passage between two houses. Most roofs are paved. People walk over kitchens, sleep beneath stairways, and reach their neighbors by crossing little bridges of bone-white timber, brought up from forests far below.

There is very little timber to spare. A straight beam is an inheritance. Broken ones are splinted and bound, and the bindings themselves acquire patches.

The town has no single street that remains usable all day.

Water takes one route in the morning and another by noon. A channel that has carried only a finger’s depth for years may suddenly fill from below, lifting its stone covers one after another. Elsewhere, a pool empties through a hole too small to put a hand into. The inhabitants move benches, door barriers, baskets, children. They lay narrow planks over new streams. They pull the planks away when the streams deepen.

The place is constantly rearranging itself, but its larger forms are old. Every threshold is hollowed by feet. Every spillway has a smooth lower lip. There are channels cut for floods no living person remembers, and when one of them begins to run, it runs cleanly, as if it had been used yesterday.

At the center lies a pool about the size of a courtyard. Buildings crowd so close around it that their upper stories almost meet. The pool is not level.

Its surface rises into low, clear hills, sometimes three or four at once. Between them, troughs open and travel, exposing the dark steps that descend from the houses. A person watching from a doorway may see the opposite doorway through a moving thickness of water. For a moment the other house looks enormously distant. Then the hill passes and it is near again.

Anything floating on the pool follows these changes: scraps of ice, shed feathers, small boats shaped like kneading troughs. The boatmen work with short paddles and hooked poles. They wait for a hollow to approach, push into it, and let its movement carry them across.

Occasionally two water hills meet. Their sides climb against one another, becoming a single upright pane. It stands higher than a person, trembling, full of suspended fish. Then it falls outward in several directions.

There is almost no sound.

The fallen water makes a close, papery sigh. Ice touches stone with the faint ticking of a cooling pot. Voices carry across a room but seldom across the pool. Beneath the town, where the channels converge, something takes the sound away with the water. A dropped bowl may shatter at your feet without a crash; much later, at a lower drain, someone hears a single clear note.

People knock on doors with their knuckles pressed against the wood. They call to one another by opening and closing pale cloths at the windows.

The animals are everywhere.

Small white birds stand shoulder to shoulder along roof edges, each with its feet tucked beneath its breast. Their eyes are black and unusually large. When a flood begins, they lift all at once, filling an alley from wall to wall, then settle on another roof without calling. Beneath them, dark little swimmers climb the wet masonry using broad, muscular lips. They graze on the films that grow where running water meets air.

There are thick-bodied fish in the street channels, transparent shrimp in household cisterns, and flat shelled creatures that fasten themselves under bridges in overlapping rows. Their shells are polished by the constant passage of water. Seen from below, a bridge looks tiled with wet fingernails.

The largest animals live in the central pool. They have smooth backs the color of old pewter and many short limbs crowded underneath. Most are no longer than a bench, though one is said to extend beneath three houses. They spend hours wedged in submerged doorways, faces turned into the flow. When the pool rises into hills, they loosen their grip and go drifting through the town.

A grey back appears beneath a kitchen hatch. A hand reaches down to push it gently aside. Elsewhere, half a dozen of the animals are packed into a flooded stairwell, slowly turning over one another until the current releases them.

Their young are kept out of the drinking jars with woven covers.

Almost nothing green grows here. In sheltered cracks there are stiff cushions of moss, black when wet and bronze when dry. On one south-facing wall, a plant with four tiny leaves has occupied the same handspan of mortar for longer than anyone can remember. The wall is repaired around it.

Most of the life feeds on what the mountain brings up. The water deposits a pale meal on anything left still: bowls, bones, submerged cord. By morning a hanging rope may be furred with it. The small swimmers rasp it away; shrimp gather in its loosened clouds; fish follow the shrimp. People lower rough pottery plates into the deepest channels and retrieve them coated. Some coatings are edible. Others are scraped into sealed pits outside the rim. Children learn the difference by touch before they are allowed to collect them alone.

The cold makes every task exact. Wet skin adheres to exposed stone. A mitten dropped into a channel is pursued at once. In the houses, lamps burn animal fat behind thin screens of scraped hide, providing warmth more urgently than light. Clothes dry above the lamps in rigid, salt-stiff shapes. At night, sleepers lie close together on raised platforms while water moves under the floor.

Night itself is brief and never quite dark. The hollow retains a milky shine after the sky has dimmed. Underwater things remain visible longer than things in air.

Toward evening the changes quicken. Little whirlpools travel uphill through the lanes. A dry well fills to its brim and spills for the first time in a season. Birds leave a roof just before the roof stones tilt. Two neighboring pools exchange their water through a wall, though the wall stays standing and no opening can be found afterward.

Everyone is busy. Hands knot ropes, lift baskets, catch a floating shoe. A woman braces a shutter with her shoulder while someone inside drives in a wooden peg. Three children herd fish out of a bedroom with a length of cloth. At the courtyard pool, an old man waits beside a boat, watching a rise in the water that has not yet reached him.

Above this small, crowded labor, the rim of the mountain remains still. Its rounded stones are dry on top, dazzling white, worn smooth by weather that has already taken everything loose.

Then a bead appears on the underside of one of them.

It swells, holds, and drops into the town.
