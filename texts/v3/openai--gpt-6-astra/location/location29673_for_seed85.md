The water ends at the edge of the square.

There is no shore. The paving continues for another handspan, then stops, and beyond it the water has a vertical face. Through that face, if you come close enough, you can see the mountains below: a few pale ridges, the black interval of a valley, snow that gives almost no light. The settlement stands higher than all of them. Its water is held around it in a volume roughly the size of a cathedral, deep enough to cover the roofs.

Nothing spills.

From outside, there might be a dark, square-ended mass on the summit. From here there is only the faint distortion of distant peaks through water, and the uncomfortable knowledge that the street ends in open air.

The summit itself is young. Beneath the paving, black rock breaks into blades and flat, glossy plates. Fracture lines meet at angles sharp enough to cut a glove. There is no gravel at their feet, no weather-softened margin, no accumulation of soil in the joints. In one unfinished courtyard, a stone flake remains attached to its parent slab by a narrow hinge of mineral. It looks as though the last blow of construction has just landed.

It has looked that way for a very long time.

The town is only six buildings, but it was planned as a town, not an outpost. There are separate entrances for goods and inhabitants, a recessed public basin, a raised crossing where two streets meet. Along one wall runs a row of shallow compartments at about shoulder height. Each has its own latch. Some practical distinction once required this many compartments; it is impossible to infer what it was.

The buildings stand well apart. Between them are generous rectangles of empty water.

They are made of a pale ceramic that appears gray until touched by a lamp. Then a little color comes back: the faint yellow of old ivory, blue in the thicker sections, a pink line where two pieces meet. No block is larger than a hand, but there is no mortar. The joints turn corners and continue through doorways with a precision that makes the structures seem assembled from a single folded surface.

Their roofs are steep and narrow. Underwater, they have nothing to shed.

Everything is flooded. Open windows lead into flooded rooms; stairs ascend through water to flooded landings. Inside the smallest building, three seats face a blank wall. They are set far enough apart that their occupants could not easily have touched. Beneath each seat is a recess for the heels, and beside each is a thin vertical slot whose interior cannot be illuminated. Light put into it does not return.

The water is cool, clear, and strangely easy to look through. There is no suspended silt to give your eyes a measure of distance. A doorway across the square can seem near enough to reach until the paving joints restore its proper size. Nothing grows on those joints. No weed, no film, no soft fur of organisms clouds the ceramic. The undersides of the eaves are as clean as their upper surfaces.

There is life, though very little.

Under the lip of the public basin are two animals like translucent ceramic washers. Each grips the stone with a ring of minute feet. Their internal organs are visible only as a slight thickening around a central hole. They do not wave, pulse, or turn toward a passing shadow. One is larger than the other. That difference is the strongest suggestion of a history anywhere in the square.

They may feed on something the water does not visibly carry. They may already have everything they need.

The basin contains the same water as the street, but a boundary can be felt at its rim. A hand lowered into it encounters a resistance softer than glass, firmer than a current. Below that boundary, a small white object rests on the bottom. It resembles a tooth, or the handle of an instrument whose working parts were never meant to occupy ordinary space.

There are other pieces of infrastructure, if that is what they are. Short posts mark the corners of the settlement. Each has a circular opening through which the building behind it appears slightly displaced. Narrow metallic strips run beneath the paving, visible where the summit rock interrupts the street. They do not converge on a central machine. They lead outward, toward the unsupported edges.

No machinery hums. There is no vibration underfoot.

Sound here is almost entirely something a visitor would bring. A buckle striking stone makes a close, hard click. There is no long underwater reverberation, no answer from the walls. Afterward the quiet resumes so completely that it becomes difficult to remember whether the noise was heard or merely felt through the hand.

Most of the illumination comes from the snow outside, far below. It enters through the vertical water boundary and lies weakly along the pavement. Upper windows disappear into blackness. At certain angles, a roof edge catches enough light to become a fine gray incision above the street.

There are lamps over the entrances, but none is lit. Their housings contain no bulb or lens, only a little hollow where the darkness seems unusually exact.

The place does not decay. Nor does it visibly repair itself. A chipped step stays chipped; an undamaged wall stays clean. The water neither circulates nor becomes stale. There is no warmer layer above, no cold settling into the doorways, no slow drift by which a loose thread might reveal a current. Whatever maintains the settlement has left no seam between maintenance and the ordinary condition of things.

At the last building, the street narrows to a passage barely wide enough for two people. It opens onto a small terrace cut directly into the summit. Here the water boundary is close to the rock, and the mountains can be seen more clearly. Their slopes fall away for miles.

Someone placed a seat there, facing outward.

Its edges are sharp. Its surface is empty. Above it, a shallow roof makes a patch of deeper darkness in the water, offering shelter from something that no longer happens.
