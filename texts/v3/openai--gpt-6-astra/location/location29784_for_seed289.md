Beneath the underside of a buried stone, in a space scarcely larger than a cupped hand, there is a white country.

It has no entrance. The stone above and the clay below fit together everywhere except here, where each has withheld a little of itself. Between them lies a shallow chamber, broad enough for a beetle to cross, low enough that the larger creatures touch both earth and ceiling. Nothing from the surface illuminates it. Nevertheless, the place is bright enough to show the translucent joints of a mite’s leg, the fine split in a seed coat, a bead of water lodged behind a grain of quartz.

The light belongs to the mineral. Every exposed surface gives off the same cool radiance, including the undersides of things. There are no dark hiding places. Concealment comes instead from how tightly everything fits: an animal may be visible in its entirety and still almost impossible to distinguish from the dozen presences fitted around it.

The ground is not quite rock. It has the pale, close texture of a broken tooth, but its ridges are softer in outline, like the first suggestion of mountains beneath a cloth. Crystals stand with only two or three faces completed. Some have an edge, a corner, and then a smooth, indecisive remainder. A seam of mica ends in something that is neither mica nor clay, a pearly thickness with no settled structure.

These are not ruins or deposits worn down to their beginnings. Nothing here has been worn. Even the smallest projecting point is intact.

In one corner, six grains of sand have begun to be a cliff. They are joined along their lower edges, with a little ledge halfway up and a vertical face about as high as a flea. Beneath this face is a hollow that has all the proportions of a valley. It contains a root hair, three pale bodies, and a narrow strip of dry ground. There is no spare space in the valley, but its shape is unmistakable.

Moisture occupies a few precise places. A wet collar surrounds the base of a mineral column. Two adjoining crystals hold a lens of water between them, magnifying the clay behind it into a mass of enormous, soft-edged plates. Along the ceiling hangs a droplet with a pointed lower end. It looks ready to fall, though readiness is simply its shape. It has no next moment.

Most of the chamber is dry. The dryness is clean rather than dusty; nothing has loosened enough to become dust. The whole place has the coolness of the interior of an apple taken from a cellar. There is no warmer patch near the ceiling, no colder water at the bottom.

Vegetation is sparse but individually elaborate. A single green filament lies along the valley floor, flattened where a translucent foot rests on it. Beside the column is a moss plant with four leaves. Each leaf is broad, glossy, and oddly thick, furnished on its underside with tiny white cups. A root penetrates the ceiling and ends in a blunt, cream-colored tip. It does not seek downward. Around it the stone has a socket so exact that the root and its opening seem to be two parts of one organism.

Animals fill nearly every other available interval.

There are springtails with folded forks beneath their bodies; mites round as seeds; a segmented creature wedged sideways into a crevice, its many feet set against opposite walls. A snail no larger than a pinhead occupies a mineral shelf. Its shell is transparent, and inside it the spiral continues farther than the shell ought to allow.

Other creatures are harder to name. One consists of a ring of jointed legs around an empty center. Another resembles a white scale until its paired mouths become apparent, one fitted to the clay below, one to the stone above. In the water lens, three narrow animals overlap so completely that their boundaries can only be followed by counting their eyes.

None moves.

They are not dead, nor do they have the slackness of sleep. Their bodies are full, their feet firmly placed, their mouths joined to food or water. A mite holds an egg between its forelegs. Within the egg, another mite is visible, smaller but equally complete. Both have the same taut, occupied appearance.

There is no waiting here. Waiting would require something absent.

The sound is enormous for the size of the chamber. A close, granular roar fills it, with a high, hard note through its center. It has the texture of grit dragged across glass, although no grit drags and no surface trembles. There are finer sounds within it: the dense crackle of many tiny feet, a wet clicking near the moss, a low wooden resonance from the buried root. They neither repeat nor subside. Each is continuously present, as steady and local as a color. Beside the water lens, the roar feels rounded; under the little cliff it has a sharp edge.

There are a few constructions. Between two mineral teeth, someone has fitted a roof of three seed husks. Beneath it rests a pale animal whose back almost touches the husks. A row of hollow eggshells makes a low retaining wall across a clay lip. Farther away, a hair passes through two natural holes and holds a flake upright. The flake divides one crowded space into two equally crowded spaces.

Nothing explains whether these were made. Their joints are intimate and convincing. They seem to express the same small preference for fit that shaped the snail’s shell and the socket around the root.

Near the chamber’s center is its largest open area: a bare oval, perhaps the size of a lentil. The surrounding bodies face it without apparent attention. In the oval, the pale ground rises very slightly. There is the beginning of a fold, a shallow crease, a patch of mineral with a different grain. Close inspection finds finer ridges within the crease, and within those, little enclosed flats.

This is where the country is most unfinished.

It will remain so. The ridge has never been lower; it will never acquire a summit. The droplet will keep its point, the egg its inhabitant, the green filament its one flattened place beneath the foot. Nothing needs replenishing, and nothing has room to accumulate.

Yet there is no sense of a scene arrested. Every contact is fully made. The roof rests securely on its supports. The animals fill their bodies. The water fills its narrow allotment. In the brilliant, uninterrupted noise, the small raw hills possess exactly as much shape as they have, and hold it.
