Under the lowest root of a drowned alder, there is a hollow scarcely large enough to hold two cupped hands. Its entrance is a slit in blue clay, hidden behind a beard of waterlogged moss. The lake above is cold, but the hollow is colder. Water passes its mouth without quite entering. A loose thread of algae can hang across that threshold for weeks, one end moving with the lake, the other perfectly still.

Inside, there is almost no open water. Everything grows into something else.

Black-green fronds lie folded between roots as tightly as wet pages. Clear filaments fill the intervals, each carrying minute beads of clay. There are low cushions of liverwort, pale branching growths too soft to be roots, and leaves so thin that their veins seem to exist without any surface between them. Some belong to plants rooted outside. Others have no visible stem. They continue behind a stone and emerge from a different stone, still the same leaf, with the same small tear along the edge.

The darkness is nearly complete. What little light enters does not illuminate the hollow evenly. It collects in a notch, or along one side of a root, leaving the space immediately beside it black. Once settled, a patch of light may remain after night has fallen over the lake. It grows dimmer over several days, allowing the nearest leaves a little more time.

The floor is fresh clay. Beneath its fine dusting of silt are ridges as sharp as the impressions left by a fingernail. Nothing here has been rounded by long use. Even the stones have tender-looking edges, clean fractures from which a faint milkiness continues to seep. The hollow is still being made.

A fold opens in the clay with extreme slowness. For much of a season it looks like a dark line. Then there is water within it, and a pale root tip, and eventually room for a small leaf to uncurl. No sediment is pushed aside. The floor does not rise elsewhere to compensate. There is simply a little more interior than there was before.

This is how the place deepens without becoming larger.

At the back, where the root curves downward, three tiny terraces have been pressed into the bank. Each is faced with flat flakes of bark. A splinter spans a gap between the upper two; its ends rest in shallow sockets, too regular to resemble breaks. There are no tool marks and no path leading to them. Moss has grown over the lower terrace but stops neatly at its edge.

Occasionally a grain of sand rests on one of these ledges. It will be gone the next time the same patch of light reveals the bark. Whether the ledges are being used, or whether they merely give falling things somewhere to pause, is difficult to tell.

There is little falling here, in any case. A particle released near the entrance sinks toward the floor. Farther in, it may settle against the underside of a root. Beneath the splinter bridge, silt gathers on a vertical face in small, stable drifts. The plants accommodate these local directions. Their stems bend at particular points, making abrupt elbows, and their finest root hairs all lean toward surfaces that seem, from the entrance, to be above them.

The water stays clear between the growths. Decay is slow in this cold, but not absent. An old leaf turns translucent, then loosens into threads. Those threads are caught before they can travel far. They become part of a cushion or a curtain, a soft obstruction against which the next leaf grows. Even disappearance takes up space.

There is usually one animal in the hollow.

It is a small white water louse, with an amber line visible through its back. It lives beneath a curl of bark near the entrance and spends long intervals holding still, all its legs fixed to the same dark fibers. When it moves, it travels only a few body lengths. It browses along a root, tests the edge of a leaf, then withdraws into a gap that seems too narrow to admit it.

Sometimes the hollow is found empty while the animal remains plainly visible beneath its bark. At those times its legs are slightly blurred, and the amber line has no definite end. The surrounding plants draw inward. They do not touch it.

Afterward there may be a new chamber behind the bark: no larger than a seed, already filled with cold water, already occupied by fine green threads. The animal enters it without hesitation. Nothing follows.

Most sounds come from outside. A stone shifted in the shallows sends a low knock through the clay. Rain arrives as a faint, continuous pressure rather than individual drops. In winter, ice forming on the lake makes long noises that the hollow receives in pieces, a small creak now, another much later.

Its own sounds are rarer. Once, a root fiber parts. Once, a bubble works free from beneath a leaf and touches a different leaf without rising. The contact makes a tiny dry tick, startling in so much water.

There are bubbles held deep in the vegetation, but no air to breathe. Some contain darkness a shade lighter than the water around them. Some show a bright, inverted root that is not nearby. They remain small and firm, caught in forks of stem. Over months, the stems grow around them.

Nothing in the hollow moves far enough to establish a current. A leaf turns, and its turning makes room for a neighboring frond to ease forward. A root thickens against a stone. Moist clay yields a fraction. These changes pass through the packed growth slowly, until, near the entrance, the free end of the hanging algae gives a single twitch.

Above, the drowned alder loses branches. Its exposed wood softens. Seasons alter the height of the lake, and silt begins to cover the outer roots.

But beneath the lowest one, the small entrance remains open. Fresh clay shows along its lip. Inside, beyond the moss, a leaf has been unfolding for a long time. There is just enough room for it now.
