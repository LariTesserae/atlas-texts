The basin lies high enough that clouds meet its rim from below. Beyond the lowest notch, a distant range shows through the white air: blue at its base, pale along its shoulders, almost indistinguishable from the sky at its summits. But the basin itself is bright. Light falls through a thin, unbroken ceiling of vapor and comes back from every surface. There is no comfortable direction to turn your eyes.

The rock is mostly white limestone, with seams of gray stone that remain dark even when dry. Water makes the difference difficult to judge. It lies so thinly over some faces that only a slight thickening at an edge reveals it; elsewhere, it fills hollows to their lips. There are broad, shallow pools with bottoms close enough to touch, narrow pools sunk between leaning slabs, and water in cracks that would scarcely admit a finger. The air is cool. A hand laid on the stone becomes cold slowly, then stays cold.

From the rim, the place appears to be a single pale mass, roughened rather than divided. Down inside it, there is almost too much terrain.

Ribs of limestone crowd across the floor. Each rib is fluted; each flute contains smaller channels, blind sockets, shelves, and rounded knobs. The larger ridges run for hundreds of paces before disappearing beneath heaps of fallen stone. Those heaps have settled so completely that their separate pieces seem less like debris than another kind of bedrock. Boulders fit into one another with deep, wet seams. Slabs overhang hollows already occupied by smaller slabs. A passage that looks clear from above narrows at knee height, bends behind a fin of rock, and ends in a pool.

Nothing has been left open except the sky.

Even the few level areas are crowded with low mineral formations: blunt cones, thick rims around seep holes, clusters of pale nodules fused at their bases. There is no soil between them. No grass softens a margin, no moss marks the wettest places, no lichen gives the dry stone a color of its own. What looks, at a distance, like a dark growth beneath a ledge resolves into a band of harder rock, finely pitted. The yellow stain around a spring is iron. The white fringe at a pool’s edge is calcite, dense and cold.

Water is present everywhere without becoming a lake. The basin cannot hold it for long enough. Beneath the crowded surface lies another terrain of slots and chambers, and the water goes down into that.

This is where the noise comes from.

It is not the intermittent sound of drops, nor the distinct rush of a visible fall. A continuous, enormous roar rises through the limestone. You hear it first in your jaw and behind your ears, then as a pressure occupying all the air. Each fissure gives out part of it. Narrow cracks hiss. Deep openings produce a low, rough note. Beneath broad slabs there is a heavier sound, as though an immense quantity of water were passing just out of reach.

Across the basin these sounds combine so thoroughly that no single source can be found. Closing your eyes does not help you locate anything. A person a few steps away could speak without being heard.

Yet the visible water scarcely moves.

A clear sheet covers a sloping rock face without a wrinkle. The pools remain flat. At their outlets, the flow is disclosed by a slight inward bend of the surface, a small polished depression where the water enters a hole. There are no leaves or loose stems to show its speed. Even grit is scarce; what can be carried away has long since gone.

The supply comes through the upper walls, where a massive body of porous stone holds water gathered far beyond the basin. It releases that water evenly through thousands of seams. No one spring is large. Together they keep the whole place wet, and feed the hidden descent that makes the air unbearable.

There is little sense of weather arriving. The cloud caught along the outer cliffs keeps its place. Higher up, a cold current passes steadily over the rim, but down among the stone the air is almost still. Fine moisture rests on the sheltered faces. In the brightest reaches, the wet limestone gleams with a flat, persistent whiteness. Under overhangs the light is blue.

The forms have the confidence of things that have finished settling. Sharp edges survive on the harder seams, but most exposed corners have become thick and rounded. Old fractures are widened just enough to take the flow they receive. Pools are filled precisely to the lowest point in their rims. There are few precarious stones. A block balanced above a crevice turns out to have a broad foot buried deep within it.

Looking across the basin for a long time reveals no large movement. Nothing crosses the sky. No shadow travels visibly over the floor. The white walls, the water levels, the cloud at the notch, and the immense sound remain in the same relation to one another. The roar eventually loses the character of an event. It becomes something the place possesses, like its altitude.

There are animals, though they are difficult to find.

In a pool beneath a low shelf, a translucent crustacean clings to the underside of a stone. Its body is scarcely longer than a fingernail. The legs are almost invisible until they move. It feeds along a faint gray film maintained by mineral-rich seepage, in water too cold and sheltered to receive much of the basin’s brightness. Several neighboring pools contain nothing apparent at all.

Farther down a crack, there may be another, larger animal, pale enough to be mistaken for a chip of limestone. It stays beyond the reach of direct light. For a long while, the only evidence that it is alive is that two fine appendages do not quite share the stillness of the rock.

Life here occupies narrow allowances. It adds no covering, makes no audible contribution, gathers into no visible abundance.

Near the center of the basin stands a broad limestone shoulder from which much of the region can be seen. The packed ridges continue toward three enclosing walls, becoming smaller and whiter with distance. Hundreds of wet surfaces return the same light. Between them, the dark fissures are too narrow to reveal their depth.

From this height, the pools resemble pieces of clear glass fitted into the stone. Nothing seems to enter or leave them. Only the sound tells how much is passing underneath—and even that sound holds steady, immense and unbroken, while the water rests bright at every brim.
