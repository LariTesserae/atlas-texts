The hollow lies just beneath the hillside, close enough to the surface that the roots of summer grasses reach its ceiling. Most stop there. Their pale tips hang an inch or two into the brightness, each carrying a bead of water that grows for several days before falling.

There is no obvious entrance. A fold in the slope becomes a shallow ditch, the ditch passes under a lip of white stone, and a person following it would find the sky quietly replaced by rock. No descent, no steepening darkness. Only a change in the air: the smell of warm dust gives way to something mineral and faintly sweet, like the inside of a broken stalk.

The chamber is about as wide as a small grove. Its ceiling rises in the middle and slopes down until it meets the floor, leaving no sharp corners. Everything has been rounded by old water. The lower walls have the smoothness of objects carried a long time in a pocket; higher up, shallow ribs record currents that have not flowed here for centuries. Along one side, a dry channel bends around a mound of harder stone. It is scarcely deep enough to hide a foot.

There is almost nothing in the open space.

A few fallen flakes lie beneath the ceiling. Three low patches of vegetation occupy the dampest part of the channel. Near the far wall is a pool no wider than a spread hand. The rest is pale floor, pale vault, and air.

Yet it is brighter here than outside.

The stone admits sunlight without admitting a view of anything. It is thin in some places, thick in others, but its thickness seems to matter less than its grain: light travels along the pale seams, bends around opaque nodules, and emerges from surfaces that ought to face away from the sun. The floor shines most strongly near midday. The ceiling is brightest in the late afternoon. A hand held out at waist height casts several weak shadows, all with soft edges, and one of them may point toward the place where the entrance should be.

The brightness is warm, almost edible in its richness. It turns the small green plants the color of old bottle glass. It reveals every floating speck, though there are few enough that a single mote can be watched all the way across the chamber.

Heat collects here. Even after rain has chilled the hillside, the floor remains comfortable against bare skin. Small cracks breathe air that smells of deeper earth. There is no perceptible draft, but a loose root fiber will sometimes lean toward one crack for an hour, then stand upright again.

Most of the hollow is dry. Moisture keeps to particular routes: a dark thumbprint below a root, a narrow wet line following a fault, the tiny pool resting in its polished socket. The water there is clear until touched. Then a white cloud lifts from the bottom, hangs for a long time, and settles so slowly that the pool seems reluctant to become transparent again.

Around it grows a loose fringe of jointed stems, none taller than a finger. They have swollen, translucent bases and two narrow leaves apiece. Farther away, where drops strike the floor, a flat plant spreads in separate green coins. Its edges curl inward during dry weeks. When a drop finally lands, the nearest coin uncurls over the course of an afternoon.

The third patch grows upside down from a seam in the ceiling. Its leaves are gray above and silver beneath. They turn very slowly to follow the brightest part of the chamber, which does not always agree with the position of the sun.

At first, the animals are difficult to distinguish from the stone.

Small six-legged creatures rest in the shallow wall ribs, their bodies the same chalk-white as their surroundings. Some are no larger than seeds; others would fill a palm. They have broad, flattened backs and dark feet tucked completely underneath. Once one moves, others become visible everywhere: a seam crowded with young, a row of adults along an old waterline, dozens fitted into depressions no deeper than a fingernail.

Their abundance does not fill the chamber. They occupy its edges. The air and the broad center remain empty.

They travel only a little at a time. One lifts itself, walks a body-length, and settles. Another turns to present its back to a brighter wall. Their shells gather a faint yellow tint while they bask, then lose it when they retreat into cracks. At night, those that fed longest on the light are visible as weak amber shapes beneath the stone.

Some graze the thin growth around damp places. Some wait beside root tips for the minute soft-bodied things that arrive with water from above. The largest carry small chips of dark stone beneath their front legs. They use these to scrape mineral crust from the floor, working with a patient rocking motion. A well-used chip becomes polished on one edge. When its owner abandons it, another may try it, turn it over, and carry it away.

There are no nests, no piled stores, no marks that last. Even their scraping leaves only a dull patch, and the next seep of water lays a fresh skin over it.

The hollow is mostly silent, but close attention gives the silence a fine structure. A foot catches against stone with a dry tick. A leaf loosens its curled edge. Somewhere behind the wall, a bubble moves through a flooded passage. The rare falling drop is startlingly loud. Its sound circles the chamber once, returning thinner from the low vault.

After rain, activity increases without becoming hurried. Animals gather below the hanging roots, leaving a little clear space where each drop will fall. The smallest drink from the splash. Larger ones press their mouths to the wet floor. Sometimes a drop hangs too long, trembling at a root tip, and the whole gathering waits beneath it.

There are occasional winged creatures as well: narrow, nearly transparent things that enter through the hillside fold. They cross the open chamber singly. In the diffuse brightness their wings disappear, leaving a dark thread apparently suspended in the air. They settle on the ceiling plants, remain there through the warmest hours, and leave before dusk.

Evening arrives unevenly. One wall dims while another stays bright. The shallow pool holds a square of gold although nothing above it is square. For a little while the floor is luminous enough to illuminate the underside of every resting animal.

Then the stored light begins to fail.

The hollow becomes a low, warm darkness with a few amber backs along its margins. Outside, wind moves over the grass. Down here, a root lengthens imperceptibly toward the floor. A bead of water gathers on its tip, holding the last brightness long after the stone around it has gone dark.
