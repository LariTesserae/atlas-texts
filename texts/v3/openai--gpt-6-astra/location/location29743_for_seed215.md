The land is still being lifted.

You would not know it by standing still. The pavement does not tremble; water in a drinking glass remains level. But the survey marks along the tramway acquire another finger’s width of exposed stem each month, and the clouds that used to touch the eastern roofs now pass below them. Far out on the plateau, a lake is slowly becoming two lakes. Crews have already moved the floating docks toward the deeper water.

It is cold enough that the shaded sides of buildings keep yesterday’s frost. In sunlight, the frost loosens and drops from the railings in bright grains. The air smells of wet stone, with an occasional green trace from the low vegetation. There is no forest smell, no depth of old leaf mold. Most of the ground has only recently become available to roots.

The plateau extends beyond what can be seen from any one height. Its weather arrives from different directions at once. To the south, rain hangs in separate gray shafts over a pale plain; to the north, snow obscures the lower halves of distant towers. Directly overhead the sky is blue, crossed by the thin white scrape of an aircraft too high to hear. Sunlight is ordinary and clear. It makes the puddles hard to look at.

Much of the rock has no rounded edge. Broad slabs meet along tight seams, their surfaces faintly granular, silver-gray when dry and almost black where water spreads across them. In places the ground has risen as a single gently tilted sheet several kilometers wide. Elsewhere it has come apart into shallow steps. There are no deep ravines yet, no long-established river valleys. Rain tests the new slopes in hundreds of small channels, most of which end at a crack or spill into a hollow without an outlet.

Water is close everywhere without quite dominating the view. It beads beneath overhangs, darkens the joints between paving stones, gathers in the footprints of heavy animals. Across the open country, shallow pools occupy depressions so slight that their shores shift in a breeze. A patch that looks like bare stone may hold a clear film deep enough to reflect a passing bird.

The city has been laid across this unfinished surface with extraordinary patience.

It is not a continuous mass of buildings. From a ridge you see a group of towers, then three kilometers of wet grass and stone, then low residential courts arranged around a station, then another interval so large that weather can conceal the next inhabited district. At dusk the illuminated windows form separate constellations. By day there are stretches where the only evidence of urban ground is a perfectly straight handrail disappearing over the horizon.

Everything is connected. Transit runs beneath the exposed slabs or on narrow guideways raised just above them. Warm conduits follow the routes, keeping the crossings clear of ice. Schools, workshops, clinics, and covered markets cluster around the stops. Between them, immense areas remain open to weather and animals.

A walk to the next district can take most of a day. The train takes four minutes.

The buildings are compact and thick-walled, with shallow roofs that catch meltwater. Their lower floors stand on adjustable footings enclosed in smooth gray housings. Once or twice a year, a staircase gains a step at the bottom. Flexible bridges settle almost imperceptibly between structures moving at different rates. Older residents recognize a particular soft click in the walls and know that the foundations are making their overnight corrections.

Beneath the plateau, the lifting machinery works at depths where pressure should prevent any recognizable mechanism from existing. Its surface installations are modest: sealed service courts, rows of heat exchangers, occasional dark pylons with no visible joints. The great mass of the country rises without the earthquakes it ought to produce. Instruments record the missing strain. Engineers account for it elsewhere, in figures no one needs to understand to catch a tram or mend a roof.

The heat exchangers are easier to appreciate. On cold mornings, deer stand beside them.

There are animals almost everywhere, though the first impression is of emptiness. Their colors belong to the ground: gray-brown backs, pale bellies, winter-white necks. A field of what looks like scattered rubble resolves, after a few minutes, into several hundred resting birds. Farther away, a herd crosses a wet terrace in a line so long that its leading animals are lost in rain while those at the rear remain sunlit.

The land carries little tall vegetation, but its shallow waters are productive. Algae slick the submerged stone. Minute crustaceans crowd the warmer seeps. Insect larvae occupy every pool that lasts through the short summer, and fish move through culverts linking the deeper basins. The earliest planted sedges now spread without help, making narrow green margins around water that was bare rock a decade ago.

Birds follow these margins. They feed beside station platforms and under the walkways, their legs reflected in the thin water. Migrating flocks use the whole plateau as a resting place, descending out of clear air in such numbers that a district can acquire a second, moving surface of wings and backs. They stay for two nights or twelve, according to weather far beyond the city.

Sound does not match their abundance. In the cold middle hours, most are feeding or asleep. You hear the small tearing noise of a grazing animal, a beak tapping stone, one brief exchange of calls across a pool. A flock may lift almost silently, turn together, and settle half a kilometer away.

Foxes work the drier seams. Small predators live beneath the transit embankments, where pipes keep the soil unfrozen. Out on the broad terraces, larger hunters travel behind the herds. They seldom enter the residential courts, but paths are closed when they pass near a school, and the morning notices may report a delayed tram because animals are resting across the guideway.

The restrictions are ordinary enough to have become part of the city’s manners. Do not drain an unmarked pool. Do not salt an outdoor step. Leave the green crossing lights undisturbed even when nothing appears to be crossing. During nesting season, certain roof gardens are reached only through screened passages.

Plants remain low. There are cushions of saxifrage in the joints, fine grasses bent flat by the wind, and dark mats of moss wherever a seep has kept the same course long enough. Trees grow mainly in sheltered courtyards. Their crowns rarely rise above the parapets, and people tend them with the seriousness given elsewhere to old buildings. Around their roots lies some of the deepest soil in the city, carried up in sealed freight containers before the first residents arrived.

Beyond these pockets, you can watch soil beginning. Dust lodges behind a stone. A feather catches there, then a little dead grass. Moss holds the mixture through a winter. On the next terrace, where runoff is stronger, the same process fails and begins again.

Nothing has had much time to wear away. Even the animal trails are shallow. Paths do not yet follow ancient passes or the curves of old rivers; they follow last season’s dry ground, the newest bridge, the places where the lifting has left an easy gradient. Maps are revised continually. Some changes are small enough to appear only as a new blue patch between familiar streets.

In late afternoon, cloud climbs the western edge of the plateau and moves through the city at walking height. Towers lose their upper floors. Moisture gathers on eyebrows and coat sleeves. The guideway lights come on, pale in the fog, and a train arrives carrying warm air and the smell of bread.

Outside, the pools begin to freeze along their sheltered edges. Birds crowd into the remaining open water. Far across the plain, beyond several districts hidden by cloud, sunlight still falls on a newly raised face of rock.

By morning, that face will be wet with condensation. Water will find its smallest irregularities, pause there, and begin to run.
