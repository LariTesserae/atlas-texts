The country lies low under a roof of cloud that seldom breaks. Even at midday there is only enough light to separate the nearer ridges from the ones behind them: long, blunt backs of limestone, dark gray above, pale where their sides descend into hollows. Between them the ground extends for miles in corrugations, shelves, shallow basins. Nothing grows high enough to interrupt these forms. There are no trees, no stalks, no roots holding the slopes together. The stone has kept its shape by wearing down slowly.

At first the hollows seem full of loose gravel.

Then a patch of gravel contracts. A ripple crosses another patch against the wind. What appeared to be a bank of flat shale lifts along one edge, exposing a thousand pale feet.

Almost every sheltered surface is occupied.

The most numerous animals are broad, low creatures with overlapping shells the color of wet stone. Some are smaller than a fingernail; others could cover a person lying down. They fit themselves into the worn irregularities of the limestone, crowding flank against flank, younger animals tucked beneath the rims of older ones. Their shells are pitted and chalky on top, polished underneath by the passage of other bodies. In the deepest hollows they lie several layers thick. You cannot see the ground there, but you can hear it being scraped.

That sound joins a much larger one.

Across the basin comes a continuous, almost solid roar: shell against shell, jaws milling mineral crust, air forced through breathing slits, feet striking hollow rock. Every shelf answers the shelf beside it. Low notes travel through the ground; sharp ones scatter between the ridges. Near a densely occupied slope, the noise is so powerful that drops of condensation tremble on the stone before they fall. There are intervals when you can hear your own breathing, but they last only as long as the passing shadow of a larger sound.

The animals do not call over this uproar. They call within it. A small alteration in pitch moves across a crowd, and tens of thousands turn together.

Water is scarce in the open. The upper ridges are dry and cold to the touch, their surfaces rough with ancient dissolution. Rain arrives as a fine slanting mist, wets one district, and leaves the next untouched. There are no broad rivers. Moisture collects in seams and under overhangs, gathering patiently into drops that fall into basins scarcely deeper than a hand.

These pools are black, edged with gray paste. Some remain for decades; others vanish between visits of the rain. Their margins are packed with mouths.

Below the limestone, water moves more generously. It passes through buried beds of sulfide and rises warm enough to smoke faintly in the cool air. Where it meets the surface, films form: ivory, rust-colored, sometimes a dark purple visible only when the cloud cover thins. The films have no leaves or stems. They spread across wet mineral grains and renew themselves as quickly as the grazers rasp them away. A fresh crack in the rock can become a feeding ground within a few days.

The richest seeps are hidden beneath the heaviest animals. Their shells hold in dampness; their slow movements distribute it. Smaller creatures travel underneath them, feeding on the film that develops where warm bodies meet cold stone. Others live in the grooves between shell plates, or cling around breathing openings to catch whatever the outgoing air carries.

Nothing here has a clean outline for long. A motionless animal acquires passengers. A discarded shell fills with bodies before it has cooled. Even the dry crest of a ridge, apparently bare, is threaded with hair-thin worms withdrawing into pores at the vibration of an approaching foot.

There are larger inhabitants too.

Along the basin floor move pale, jointed grazers with narrow heads and heavy bellies held close to the ground. They advance slowly through the shelled crowds, testing each foothold with several limbs before committing their weight. The smaller animals flatten under them or flow aside. Their skin is almost translucent, but the dimness prevents you from seeing much within it—only a shifting darkness, and occasionally the red contraction of something long.

Behind these grazers follow predators that look, from a distance, like breaks in the pale procession. They are smooth and black, with the patient movement of fluid creeping downhill. A predator may remain beside a large grazer for hours, touching it lightly with two feelers. Then the grazer stumbles, or stops to shed a damaged plate, and the black animal folds itself around an exposed joint.

The struggle disappears beneath a gathering crowd. The sound there deepens. For a while the surrounding stone is slick.

Higher up, beneath the lips of eroded shelves, membranous animals hang folded into creases. They emerge when the rain begins, not to escape the wet but to intercept it. Their wings are small for their bodies, translucent and heavily veined. They fly low, turning through the damp air in such numbers that the contours of the land seem to blur. Droplets strike their membranes with a soft rattling almost lost beneath the general noise.

When a gust carries rain toward a dry ridge, the whole airborne population follows. For several minutes an otherwise barren slope becomes the busiest place in the basin. Wings brush stone. Bodies collide. The damp disappears into mouths and skins before it can gather into runoff.

The weather is cool, but warmth persists in crowded places. If you put a hand into a gap among the shells, the air below is close and sour. A few feet away, wind pours through an empty fissure with the chill of deep rock. These little differences govern movement more strongly than daylight does. At certain hours the animals leave one side of a ridge and accumulate on the other, exposing limestone that has not felt open air for months. It gleams with moisture. Steam rises briefly from it, and then the surface dulls.

Over many years, these migrations have marked the terrain. Channels are rounded by bellies. Narrow passages have been widened by the scrape of generations of shells. Some slopes bear shallow parallel furrows that begin nowhere obvious and end at seeps now dry. The limestone is old enough to retain the habits of animals no longer present.

Darkness thickens early, though there was never much day. A few organisms show light along the edges of their bodies: a green seam, a blue point opening and closing, a row of dull amber beads beneath a lifted shell. They are too faint to illuminate anything. Instead they reveal distances. A moving point close by is answered by another far across the basin, then by dozens at different heights, until the black space between the ridges becomes visibly inhabited.

Occasionally, a pause travels through the region.

It begins beyond sight. The roar weakens on one side, then another. Scraping stops. Wings settle. The last few clicks seem enormous.

In that sudden quiet, water can be heard dripping far below the ground.

The animals remain still, their breathing slits open, feeling some change that has not yet reached the surface. A tremor, perhaps. A pressure shift in the buried water. The arrival of a new mineral taste.

Then one crowded shelf begins to rasp. Another answers. The sound spreads across the low country, and the darkness is once again full of movement.
