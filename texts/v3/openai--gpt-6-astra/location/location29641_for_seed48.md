The town begins at a line of drains.

They run out from beneath a raised road and empty onto a shallow coastal flat, each pipe wearing a dark beard of wet mineral crust. The water falls only a few inches. Even so, you can hear the separate trickles from well down the road, because there is almost nothing else to hear.

It is an hour before dawn. The air is hot enough that the concrete still feels warm through the soles of your shoes. Rain ended sometime in the night, leaving the road glossy and the gutters full, but it did not cool the place. Moisture beads on the steel handrails. In the weak amber light of a service lamp, every drop has a bright lower edge.

Beyond the drains lies an industrial district built almost entirely flat: settling ponds, salt pans, empty loading aprons, roads laid on low embankments. It extends for several miles along the back of a bay. There are buildings, but most are far apart, each with an expanse of bare ground around it. A pump house stands at the intersection of four canals. A long, windowless processing shed occupies the far side of a basin. Three cylindrical tanks rise behind a wall too low to hide their rusted feet.

Between these structures, the ground is open enough that a single maintenance truck, parked beside a gate, seems to belong to the whole horizon.

There are no trees. No grass grows through the paving. The margins of the roads are white with salt, and where the rain has washed that away, they are gray with fine estuarine mud. Even the abandoned lots remain bare. Saltwater comes up through their foundations; sun bakes the surface between rains; the next high tide seeps back in. Here and there a strip of rubber or a length of cable lies half embedded in the silt, with no leaves or stems to interrupt its shape.

The district was built for handling water, and water has spent decades revising the construction.

The basin walls have rounded shoulders. Stair treads descend into greenish darkness, their lower edges worn into shallow crescents. Along the canals, concrete has lost its smooth outer skin and exposed the small stones inside. Iron fastenings have swollen, split their sockets, and left orange trails down the walls. The roads are patched in broad, slightly different shades, each repair settling toward the same low places as the surface it replaced.

Nothing looks recently ruined. The damage has a routine. There are drains beneath the leaks, and newer pipes beside older pipes that were never removed. A wall that slumped years ago has been held in place by three heavy braces, all now equally weathered.

At first the ponds appear empty.

Then the light catches a movement along the nearest edge, and the edge resolves into crabs.

They occupy the waterline shoulder to shoulder in some places, though each is no larger than a thumb joint. Others cross the exposed mud with their bodies held high, leaving paired rows of punctures. Around every drain mouth, they gather where fresh water spreads over the brine. Some feed with a quick, repetitive motion of the claws. Some remain motionless until a neighbor touches them, then shift sideways just far enough to restore a small private interval.

Farther out, a whole patch of mud seems to tighten. Hundreds of crabs have stopped at once beneath the shadow of a passing bird. After it goes, feeding resumes from one side of the patch to the other.

The abundance is mostly this small, and mostly close to the surface. It does not fill the air or obstruct the view. You can look across an acre of basin and see only water, while immediately below that water shrimp move in such numbers that their bodies give it a faint, granular shimmer.

The old saltworks divides the bay’s water into different conditions. Some ponds are flushed on each tide. Others receive water through narrow sluices and lose most of it to the heat. In the freshest canals, small fish crowd the mouths of culverts. In stronger brine, there are no fish at all, only tiny crustaceans moving through rose-brown water. The driest beds carry a hard white crust, broken by shallow pools where insect larvae gather beneath the surface.

What looks like stained concrete is sometimes a thin microbial film. Grazers work along it in narrow tracks. Food arrives with the tide, grows invisibly in the warm water, washes through screens, settles in corners. Almost none of the life here casts a recognizable shadow from a distance.

Under a loose sheet of corrugated metal, cockroaches pack the cool side of a ridge. Small pale geckos wait beside the service lamps. A few moths beat against a lamp housing, and every so often one disappears with a movement too brief to follow. Along the seawall, mussels fill the shaded joints below the high-water mark. Their shells are layered so densely that the original masonry is visible only above them.

The place is busy without being loud.

A fish turns and makes one soft slap. Crab feet tick against a hollow pipe. Somewhere inside the processing shed, a loose piece of metal shifts as the roof contracts. Mosquitoes are audible only when they come close to your face.

Then a pump starts.

Its motor takes several seconds to settle into a low, even vibration. The handrail trembles lightly beneath your fingers. Across the basin, water begins to pour from a square outlet, first muddy, then clearer. The stream lifts a pale cloud from the bottom. Fish come toward it from the dark parts of the canal, holding their heads into the new current.

Most of the machinery is idle, but enough still works to keep the district arranged. Float switches start the drainage pumps after rain. Gates hold the tide out of the lowest yards. Warning lights burn above electrical cabinets. Beyond a fence, a conveyor remains under a long metal roof, its belt sagging between rollers, while a newer pipe runs beneath it and disappears toward the tanks.

There may be someone on duty in the lit room beside the pump house. From here, only a desk fan is visible through the glass.

Toward the bay, the embankments grow lower. The last service road ends at a broad apron where barges once loaded. Its outer edge has broken away in slabs, and the gaps fill and empty with the small movements of the tide. Water pushes into one opening with a hollow gulp, pauses, and slips back out carrying flakes of rust.

The sea beyond is barely distinguishable from the sky. A bank of low cloud holds the night’s heat over the coast. There is no surf in this sheltered water, only a slow lifting against the pilings and the occasional click of shells.

As daylight begins, the surfaces separate: pale salt beds, dark canals, the brown backs of roofs. Distances become easier to judge. The far processing shed is larger than it seemed, and farther away. Between it and the shore are empty yards, shallow ponds, another road, another line of drains.

A heron stands beside a sluice. It has been there long enough for crabs to resume moving around its feet. When it strikes, the sound is slight. It swallows, takes one careful step, and waits again.

Soon the exposed metal will become too hot to touch. The crabs will retreat beneath ledges and into their holes. Fish will collect in the deeper cuts under the gates. The small animals of the walls will withdraw into bolt sockets, fractured mortar, the shade behind signs.

For now, the whole district is feeding in the dimness. The tide rises against the outer gates. Rainwater continues to drain from the roads. On a concrete apron large enough to turn a freight convoy, a thin moving line of shrimp follows the shadow of a curb.
