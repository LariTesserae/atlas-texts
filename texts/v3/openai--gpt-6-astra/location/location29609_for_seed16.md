The city fits beneath a lifted floor tile.

Not a small version of a larger city: there are no tiny doors or streets awaiting tiny inhabitants. It is a settlement of earthen partitions, firing chambers, shallow courts, and covered passages, built into the handspan of space between the tile and the bedrock. From above, the floor appears undamaged. One corner sits slightly high. A dark line beneath it gives off heat.

Inside, most of the space is empty.

The ceilings are low, but the courts are broad in relation to them, and the thin pillars stand far apart. A fallen grain of grit may occupy the middle of a square by itself. Long, bare routes connect one little gathering of structures to the next. The builders seem to have valued separation: between hearth and storehouse, between planted bed and wall, between anything that might expand in the heat and anything that could not move.

Everything is the color of old firing. Dull ocher, brown-black, a red so dark it becomes visible only close to the entrance. There is no glaze. The walls were pinched up from soft mineral paste and hardened where they stood, leaving parallel thumb-shaped depressions. Here and there a partition has been mended with a sliver of stone pushed into a crack. The roofs are thin flakes laid across clay ribs. Nothing spans a distance that a small, simple beam could not bear.

Whatever made these things is absent. There are no tracks newer than the buildings, no sleeping places, no bones. No creature disturbs the loose powder in the passages.

Yet the place is loud.

Heat comes through the bedrock in uneven pulses. A chamber warms, expands, and gives a sharp report. A roof flake lifts almost imperceptibly, then settles with a flat clap magnified by the low ceiling. Farther in, grains of mineral fall through a shaft onto a taut stone membrane: a dry, sustained patter. The sound travels along the empty streets. It is difficult to tell which district is active until a second sound answers it, nearer, and dust jumps from the seams overhead.

The planted courts are green.

They are not full of foliage. Most have only one plant, anchored at the base of a wall or growing from a narrow trough. But each plant unfolds into an extravagance of thin, overlapping blades, green-black in shadow, olive where the dim light reaches them. Their edges curl outward in soft-looking frills. A single crown may cover a whole court without touching the floor except at its thick central foot.

There is no water in them, nor anywhere beneath the tile. No dampness under the roots, no vapor awaiting a cool surface, no buried reserve. Break one of the blades and it separates along dry layers, like mica. The green comes from a living mineral lattice that grows by taking gases from the hot air and salts from the stone. Its exposed surfaces work slowly; its folded interior remains hotter than the surrounding space.

The plants favor the constructed ground. Their roots enter the old clay joins and follow them, drawing particular minerals out of the walls. An abandoned trough supports a heavier crown than bare rock can. The oldest roots have hollowed the mortar without quite bringing the masonry down. Through these slender hollows, hot air moves from room to room.

In the narrow light below the raised corner, the growth is pale and compact. Deeper inside, where almost no light penetrates, the blades lengthen. They depend more on the temperature difference between floor and ceiling than on brightness. Their broad upper surfaces lose heat into the tile while the roots receive it from below. They turn that passage of heat into slow additions at their edges.

During a strong pulse, the whole crown changes shape.

First the blades stiffen. Then they open, one after another, revealing their dull copper undersides. A court that seemed to hold a low, lush mound becomes an arrangement of upright fans. The movement is gentle but not quiet. The blade edges scrape together with the sound of stiff paper being sorted. In adjoining courts, other crowns answer at different speeds. For a while the city is full of this busy rasping.

Beneath them, the empty lanes carry loosened dust.

There is an order to the lanes that becomes clear only after watching several heat cycles. Some are shallow channels, descending toward a sunken square near the center. Others rise slightly and end against perforated walls. Warm air travels outward through the high routes; cooler air returns close to the ground, sweeping grains inward. The channels turn at rounded corners. Tiny baffles prevent the currents from crossing too abruptly.

The system was made with clay, stone wedges, and patient adjustment. No part moves except what heat moves.

It still works, though not as precisely as it once did. One passage has widened where centuries of grit struck its outer bend. Another has partly closed beneath a root. The central square contains a low drift of sorted minerals, the lightest powder at its edges, dark and heavier grains near its heart. A few troughs receive fresh material whenever the currents strengthen. Others have gone hungry. Their plants remain small, with thick, tightly stacked blades and old dead layers gathered around their bases.

The city has not been ruined so much as slowly redistributed.

Corners have rounded. Thin walls have become thinner; ledges on the sheltered side have grown thick with deposited dust. The clay is polished at ground level and rough above it. In one room, the floor has been scoured into a shallow bowl. In another, an accumulation has reached the ceiling, sealing the space so completely that only the top of an arch reveals there was ever a room there.

Nothing suggests a sudden end. The handwork simply stops being repaired at some point in the layers, and the place continues.

At the far edge, beneath the portion of tile that lies closest to the ground, stands a row of roofless enclosures. Each contains a single black pedestal. Most are bare. On the last, a young plant has established itself in a hairline split. It has three blades, still smooth, held wide apart. Fine red grains cling to their margins.

When the floor warms, the split opens enough to feed them.

This is the principal danger here: not fire, but expansion. The builders left gaps around their doorways and slipped loose stones beneath their lintels. The plants shed the outermost parts of their roots before those roots become thick enough to lock two walls together. Even the old floor drifts have seams. Things that cannot yield eventually snap, and their fragments join the material circulating through the streets.

The loudest sound comes late in each cycle, after the fans have opened and the air has begun to slow. Somewhere under the central court, a buried slab releases its tension with a deep, blunt knock. It is much too large a sound for the visible place. Every roof returns a little of it. For a moment the cavity beneath the tile seems deeper than it is.

Then come the small settling sounds: a grain rolling down a drift, a blade closing against another blade, a roof returning to its clay support.

From outside, only a faint ticking can be heard. The raised corner is warm enough that a hand would withdraw from it quickly. Beneath its dark edge, one pale green fringe extends into view, coated with a fine red powder. During the next pulse of heat it draws inward again, slowly, until the line under the tile is empty.
