The ground is warm enough that an animal lying still upon it must eventually turn over.

It rises in low, crowded folds: black shelves, shallow basins, ridges with edges still sharp beneath their coverings of roots. Much of this land has only recently lifted out of the earth. In the deeper cracks, stone shows a clean blue-black fracture, untouched by weather. Elsewhere, a thin crust has split under the pressure of growing tubers. There is little loose soil. What looks like soil is mostly chewed leaf, insect casing, dung, and living things small enough to pass between your fingers.

Over all of it grows a forest that seems too abundant for the moisture available.

The trees are short, thick, and very close together. Their trunks fork near the ground and fork again at shoulder height, supporting a nearly continuous roof of fleshy leaves. Climbers fill the remaining space. Ferns grow out of the climbers. Pale cords of aerial root descend through everything, some ending in swollen bulbs, some entering the mouths of cracks. The plants hold their water inward: in waxed leaves, in spongy bark, in fist-sized knots buried where a little grit collects. A cut stem would bleed clear fluid for a long time.

There are no streams to follow. Water occurs as a local surprise.

A groove beneath a root contains three dark mouthfuls. A hollow in a stone holds a sheet so shallow that beetles stand on the bottom with their backs dry. High in the foliage, overlapping leaves form cups whose contents never reach the earth. At the coolest hour, condensation beads along the lower surfaces of the canopy. Small animals begin moving toward these beads before they are large enough to fall.

Almost none do fall.

The air is warm and close, smelling of bruised greenery, resin, mineral dust, and the sourness of crowded bodies. It has the damp touch of a room full of breathing animals, though the exposed rock is dry. Now and then a cooler draught moves through at ankle height. It comes from fissures in which roots have found moisture well below the surface.

There is very little light.

Above the forest, a heavy cloud layer covers the entire low country. Beneath the leaves, darkness accumulates in distinct depths. Nearby bark can sometimes be distinguished by its dull shine; beyond it, movement has no visible owner. Flowers open white and hang downward, but even these are usually found by scent. On a few stems, luminous sap marks the toothwork of feeding animals. The marks fade from green to a thin, dirty yellow over several hours.

Then something passes overhead and shakes a thousand weak lights loose.

They are insects, resting on the undersides of leaves. Their illumination begins only when they are disturbed. A climbing animal leaves a brief constellation behind it; a falling branch produces a whole collapsing volume of light. For a moment, the forest becomes visible in fragments: a hooked foot, polished bark, the ribbed back of a leaf, a long face looking straight down. Darkness closes before the relationships between these things become clear.

The sound never closes.

There is a continuous rasp from the leaf litter, a high electrical shrilling from the canopy, and a lower, almost solid drumming made by animals striking hollow trunks. Each basin has its own pitch. Calls cross between them, overlap, return altered. Close sounds are difficult to separate from distant ones. A creature calling beside your knee may seem to be on the next ridge; a chorus half a mile away can vibrate in the bones of your jaw.

At intervals, a deeper noise moves beneath all the others.

It begins as pressure through the feet. Loose fragments of stone chatter. Hanging roots touch one another. Somewhere far off, new rock parts with a noise like an enormous tooth breaking.

The animals respond before the fracture reaches the surface.

Climbers climb higher. Burrowing things erupt from narrow holes in such numbers that the ground appears to loosen and run. Broad-backed grazers, normally deliberate, force paths through the undergrowth, carrying mats of insects in their folded skin. Long-legged hunters follow them, stepping onto their backs when the traffic becomes too dense. Entire flights of winged animals launch into a canopy already full of wings.

For several minutes the landscape changes lanes.

A hollow that was full of bodies empties. A ridge becomes impassable with movement. Roots pull taut across an opening crack; some snap, releasing the wet, green smell of their interiors. Then the pressure subsides. Feeding resumes almost immediately. Freshly exposed surfaces are inspected, licked, probed, and seeded with eggs before they have cooled.

Across the wider country, these disturbances travel unevenly. From an open stone rise, if the cloud thins enough to allow a little grey light, the forest can be seen extending for miles: a low, rumpled mass, interrupted by fresh mineral ribs and shallow depressions filled with taller growth. One patch trembles violently while the surrounding canopy remains still. Another folds inward around a fallen tree. Farther away, a strip of vegetation lifts and settles as something immense passes beneath its interlocked branches.

The largest animals are seldom seen whole. There is not enough open space.

Their presence is measured in displaced foliage, in trunks pressed slowly apart, in the abrupt silence of smaller creatures immediately before their arrival. They browse without stopping, cropping the heavy leaves and crushing succulent stems against ridged palates. Water travels inside them from basin to basin. Their droppings create brief gardens of soft, vulnerable growth, each surrounded by competitors before the first shoots open.

The small animals are everywhere else.

They shelter in flowers, cling beneath jaws, occupy the cool seams between scales. Some spend their lives inside a single swollen root. Others move in dense processions along established routes, consuming whatever falls across them. A dead animal can disappear before the leaves it disturbed have stopped rocking. The bones persist longer, polished by tongues and mandibles, then dragged into fissures for the salts they contain.

Along the bare ridges live narrow-handed creatures that carry stones.

They have no shelters. At night they sleep wedged together in forks of living wood, their hammer stones held against their bellies. They use them to open the hard nodules growing on certain roots. Each feeding place is a small interruption in the forest’s excess: shell fragments, a bruised patch of bark, one smooth stone left warm on another. When the ground begins to tremble, they abandon everything except the stones they know best.

Occasional rain arrives with little warning. It batters the upper leaves loudly enough to change the character of the whole forest’s roar. Below, the ground stays mostly dry. Leaves swell and tilt; animals crowd beneath particular drip points; closed flowers unfold wet chambers full of waiting mouths. A few rivulets descend trunks, and their routes become fiercely contested territories.

Afterward, the stored water sustains weeks of growth and argument.

There is no quiet season here. Even in the pauses between upheavals, the place is busy repairing, consuming, and pressing into whatever room has just become available. A fallen trunk does not open a lasting clearing. The surrounding vegetation leans toward it while its leaves are still green. A fresh ridge acquires a covering of animals before it acquires moss.

Stand still long enough and something will use the shade beneath your foot. Something else will test the salt on your skin. Overhead, unseen bodies pass along branches bent almost to breaking, and beneath you the young ground gives another small, warm shudder.
