The hollow is small enough to call across, though a call will not cross it.

It lies just below a mountain’s highest ridge, cupped between five blades of dark, newly exposed stone. Their edges are clean enough to show the individual crystals broken along them. Nothing has rounded the corners. A flake resting at the foot of one blade fits the pale scar above it exactly; beneath the flake, even the smallest plants turn aside, leaving the place where it fell unoccupied.

Beyond the ridge is a dim white sky and the occasional black point of a much lower summit. There is no view of the slopes connecting this place to the country below. The hollow seems to occupy the uppermost portion of a mountain whose middle has been omitted.

Water stands almost level with the ground. Here and there it overtops the black mineral soil in shallow, irregular sheets. Elsewhere it shows through narrow fissures, so that a foot placed on apparently firm earth presses a cold brightness into the surrounding cracks. The air has the wet, metallic taste of a stone held beneath the tongue.

There are only seven substantial clumps of vegetation.

Each is extravagant. Ferns grow shoulder-high, their stems pale and thick, their overlapping fronds supporting smaller ferns, glossy liverworts, and cushions of plants with translucent leaves. Roots hang from roots. Flowers the color of old cream open underneath the foliage, facing the soil. At the heart of each clump stands a single tree, short and wide, carrying so much dark leaf that its trunk can be seen only through a few narrow passages.

Between these islands lie broad spaces of wet rock and shallow water. A single fallen leaf would be conspicuous there. None has fallen.

The plants have the gloss and weight of vegetation from a much warmer place, but the cold is unmistakable. It works into teeth and fingertips. Droplets on the fern tips are almost frozen, each with a white needle extending a little way into its center. The leaves themselves are warm. Put a hand beneath one and warmth gathers against the palm, together with a faint vibration.

The hollow has an industrial sound.

At first it seems to come from behind the stone blades: a deep, loaded hum, the steady note of enormous machinery working beyond sight. Closer to the ground, it separates into layers. There is a fine electrical whine among the roots. The exposed rock gives off a low pounding that cannot quite be counted. Beneath the pools is the sustained rush of a great volume of water passing through a narrow throat.

Nothing is concealed behind the ridge. There are no buildings, cables, shafts, or roads. The stone itself carries the load.

One of the smaller outcrops has split open along a vertical plane. Inside are repeated bands of black metal and milky crystal, curved around a central hollow. Fine copper-colored veins cross the bands at exact intervals. Their exposed ends carry beads of moisture. This structure continues into the unbroken rock; where a second, shallower chip has come away, the same arrangement appears at a different angle.

It is geological, down to the irregular impurities and the small fossils caught in the crystal. It is also unmistakably a motor.

Its center does not turn.

Nevertheless, the outcrop bears torque. You feel it through the soles of your feet as a persistent tendency to lean. A loose pebble on one side weighs heavily in the hand; carried around to the other, it strains against the fingers like a buoy held underwater. The ferns grow with their stems braced against this force. Their roots clasp the buried metal-rich seams, drawing heat from them.

There is no exhaust, but the cold air above each grove has the dense, saturated quality of air over a cooling pond.

Animals occupy almost every available niche within the vegetation. The open spaces remain empty, which makes their abundance difficult to grasp until you look into one of the groves.

Small dark mammals cling beneath branches, each wrapped in its own long tail. Wide-eyed birds sit among the ferns with their beaks parted. Transparent snails cover the lower trunks, exposing intricate blue organs. In a pool beneath the roots are hundreds of fish, silver from above and red underneath. Their bodies are arranged at different depths with such precision that there is room for every one of them.

A broad-backed animal, something between a deer and a hare, stands near the outermost leaves. Water has darkened its legs to the knees. One ear points toward the stone, the other toward an insect resting on its shoulder.

The insect’s wings are extended. Their tips blur.

Nothing here changes.

It takes time to understand this, partly because the place is so full of the evidence of activity. The animal is breathing. You can see the breath in its nostrils, feel its warmth if you bring your hand close. But its ribs never rise or fall. The fish exert themselves against a current that does not displace them. A bird’s throat holds a song, audible as several notes occupying slightly different places beneath its beak.

Move your head, and you hear the notes in order. Hold still, and one clear tone continues.

The hollow distributes into space what elsewhere would have happened in time.

Along a fern stem, an aphid is being eaten by a pale spider. From one side, the aphid’s legs are folded under its body. From the other, they are extended. There are not two aphids. Looking around the stem passes through the event, and looking back returns through it. The spider’s hunger and its nourishment are both present, held a fraction of an inch apart.

This is how the lushness survives. Every root is taking up water. Every leaf is making sugar. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is exhausted. The groves have reached an exact, vigorous condition from which there is no next condition.

The rock is equally fixed in its newness. Its sharp faces will never weather. Water occupies the beginning of every route erosion would take: a bead lodged in a hairline fracture, a shining film over a ledge, a little suspended lip where a channel should eventually deepen. The hollow contains all the preparations for becoming an old place.

Near its center, water drops from a shelf no higher than a knee.

The fall is loud. It has the white, compact violence of water under pressure, though there is only a hand’s breadth of it. Each droplet is distinct and motionless. Between them are smaller drops, and between those a mist that seems to go on dividing past the limit of sight. Touching it produces the continuous impact of falling water against the skin. Your hand comes away wet.

For a moment the gap left by your fingers remains visible. Then you notice that it was already there before you touched the fall, waiting at the proper angle.

The dimness is gentle and remarkably thorough. Light reaches the underside of stones and the interiors of folded leaves, but nowhere gathers into brightness. Even the exposed metal is dull. The seven groves cast shadows into their own centers, leaving the bare spaces between them pale and legible.

You can walk from one side of the hollow to the other in a few minutes. Your clothes grow damp. Your feet become cold. These ordinary changes continue to happen to you, and their small accumulating discomforts begin to feel indecent here.

At the far edge, beneath a projecting slab, a little animal sleeps on a bed of living moss. Its fur is dense, reddish at the roots and silver at the tips. One paw rests over its nose. The stone above it vibrates with the deep, tireless labor of the mountain.

There is room beside it for another animal of the same size.

The moss in that space is equally warm.
