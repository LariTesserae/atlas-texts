The water occupies a notch between two summits, higher than the clouds, and does not fall out.

On the eastern side there is no rock to hold it. The basin simply ends in a vertical face of water, slightly convex, forty paces across. Beyond it are white cloud tops and the blue distances between mountain ranges. Small waves travel down this face rather than across it. Occasionally a bead separates, hangs for a while in the cold air, and returns with a dimple that passes inward through the whole basin.

Inside, it is bright enough to see the joints in the stone.

The notch is small: a flooded chamber without a roof, perhaps the size of a modest cathedral, crowded almost solid with fresh black rock. The mountain has split here recently. Fracture surfaces still have their glassy edges, and pale seams, exposed for the first time, run uninterrupted from floor to rim. Nothing has had time to become a beach. There is no sand except a teaspoonful caught here and there in a crack. Where a slab has fallen, it lies among the others with all its corners intact.

Sunlight comes through the upper surface and the unsupported eastern wall. It meets itself in the water. Shadows have narrow, unusually dark centers and broad green margins; a fish passing between two rock blades throws three shadows, one of which seems to go ahead of it. At noon, the undersides of the ledges are better lit than their tops.

Almost every available space contains an animal.

They are not, for the most part, swimming. They are fitted into the place. Pale, many-jointed bodies occupy the vertical fissures. Thick disks press against the undersides of slabs. In the narrow lanes between rock fins, colonies of translucent bell-shaped creatures hang one above another, their rims almost touching. Through each bell you can see the next, and the next, until the overlapping organs darken the view to amber.

What initially looks like a coating on the stone resolves into thousands of small shells, each mounted on a soft stalk no longer than a fingernail. They lean in the same direction. When a current changes, it takes several minutes for the new inclination to pass across them.

There is no weed, no moss, no fringe of green at the bright margins. Even the shallowest shelves are bare mineral or animal. Their color comes from rust-red gills, bluish milk inside transparent bodies, white shell, and the yellow deposits accumulating around mouths that exhale warm water.

Warm is relative. Most of the basin is cold enough to ache in the teeth. A vent feels like a hand that has just come out of a glove.

The noise is astonishing.

From outside, the notch gives off a low note that can be heard across the snowfields. Within the water, it becomes a layered, bodily sound: continuous grinding, a deep repeated concussion, high metallic tremors that seem to originate inside the listener’s bones. Some cracks sing at one pitch for hours. Others produce a loose chattering like a great number of hard objects being sorted out of sight.

Nothing moves quickly enough to account for it.

A white animal lodged between two blocks takes most of an afternoon to contract. As it does, a dark band travels down its body, and the sound around it deepens. Its neighbors begin their own contractions a little later. The sequence passes through the fissure, around the basin floor, and up the opposite wall. It is a very slow wave, but hundreds of bodies are always somewhere within it.

Each draws water through a comb of mineral teeth. Each compresses it into chambers beneath its shell. Each releases it through an opening smaller than a pinhole.

At those openings, the water flashes.

The flashes are tiny and numerous, visible even in daylight: violet points along the seams, white sparks under ledges. The animals grow plates of two different metals on opposite sides of their bodies. Between the plates they maintain a bitter fluid. Thin, flexible whiskers reach from one animal to another, touching, withdrawing, touching again. Their electricity feeds through the crowded rock like a pulse through wet skin.

There is no single heart to it. A broken connection is bypassed. A dead body is slowly incorporated into the mineral seam on which its neighbors rest. New mouths open beside it.

The basin has the load and vibration of machinery running far below its rated speed. Pressure accumulates. Contacts close. A chamber discharges with a report that travels through every shell in the notch. Yet there is no worked surface anywhere, nothing assembled from outside, no object that can be picked up and distinguished as a tool. The hard parts have growth rings. The conducting threads branch into nerves.

Near the floor, the water is thick with minute grazers. They feed on the cloudy precipitate where vent water meets cold water, turning somersaults so slowly that a single rotation takes several breaths. Larger animals crop them from the current: long, nearly colorless tubes that unfold a circular mouth, wait, and close it without haste. Flat predators rest against the rock, betrayed by paired eyes and the occasional rearrangement of their edges.

Everything here has somewhere to cling. Even the free-swimming fish carry small gripping pads beneath their jaws. When the deep concussion comes, they attach themselves. Their fins continue waving while their bodies remain fixed, like flags in a wind too weak to lift them.

Along the eastern boundary, the animals are different.

They form a loose, living mesh a little way inside the vertical water face. Long silver bodies cross at patient angles. Their filaments extend sideways, meeting those of their neighbors. They do not quite touch the air. Between their backs and the outside world is a clear hand’s breadth of water, trembling with close-set ripples.

A fish can leave the mesh and another will slowly take its place. A whole row can loosen to admit a drifting sheet of ice. But when the electrical tremor in the basin falters, the exposed water bulges outward. The silver animals stiffen. Their bodies brighten from tail to head. A bass shudder passes through the rock, and the boundary draws tight again.

Beyond it, clouds continue moving over valleys thousands of feet below.

Sometimes a cloud rises high enough to meet the notch. For an hour the view disappears into white, and the distinction between the basin and the weather grows uncertain. Moisture gathers on the outer face. Tiny airborne things become trapped there, their dark bodies entering the water one fraction at a time. The silver animals turn toward them. Much later, deeper in the chamber, a mouth opens.

At evening the sun shines horizontally through the whole place. Every filament is visible. The crowded stone becomes a mass of fine connections, all gold at their edges, all trembling. The fresh fracture at the back of the notch glows red through a thickness of cold water.

Then the light leaves, although the noise does not.

In the dark, the little discharges continue along the seams. The upper surface takes the stars. A few animals unfasten themselves and move to new feeding places, crossing distances no greater than their own length. Against the eastern face, one large bead of water begins to swell.

It will be morning before it either falls or returns.
