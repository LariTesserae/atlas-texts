The country is high enough that weather sometimes passes underneath it. Through breaks along its margins you can see the backs of clouds moving across a lower, darker world. There are no peaks here. Whatever mountains lifted this place have worn down to broad shoulders and shallow saddles, to distances so gently curved that a river can spend centuries deciding which way to go.

Nothing resembling stone is visible. The ground is a close, dark weave of living stems.

They lie lengthwise underfoot, flattened and interlocked, each no thicker than a cord. Beneath them are larger stems, then trunks pressed almost beyond recognition, then a depth of vegetable material in which the distinction between growth and geology has become unhelpful. It is cold to the touch. Water comes up around any pressure placed on it, clear at first, then green with minute swimming things. A footprint takes several minutes to fill and several hours to close. While it is closing, fine root hairs comb the water for whatever the foot has left behind.

The openness is immense. Almost everything grows horizontally. There are miles of low, wet surface, pewter pools, shallow channels edged with white leaf tips. The occasional upright growth stands alone: a thick, blunt column with its crown bent away from the wind, or a rack of bare green ribs enclosing a little darkness. You can see such a thing from a long way off and walk toward it for most of a day without discovering anything else that rises above your knees.

Yet there is almost no unoccupied space. Even the pools have skins that part and knit together. What looks like mud is a colony of tiny, overlapping leaves, dark on top, pale underneath. When a gust turns them, whole acres change color.

The light seldom casts a shadow. It gathers in wet edges and in the bellies of low clouds. Snow falls often but does not stay white for long: it becomes threaded with green, then sinks intact beneath the surface, so that in places you can look down through a pool and see yesterday’s snowfall lying under a transparent mat of new growth. Much of the plateau’s water is held this way, in layers. Some winters are still stored far below.

The place has the noise of heavy industry, although nothing has been built.

A slow concussion travels through the ground. It reaches your feet before it reaches your ears. Far away, a stand of hollow stems exhales, and vapor pours sideways across the plain. Elsewhere there is a rapid clatter, a pause, and another clatter: hundreds of seed valves opening in sequence. Beneath the surface, great chambers contract around their water. The valves are living collars. The pistons are columns of tightly furled leaf. Fibrous cables pull taut between them, thickening under load.

All of it has grown into its work.

Across a region wider than a town, pressure is being raised, transferred, relieved. The wet ground trembles with it. A channel runs uphill for a while, stops, and runs back carrying shredded white tissue. Under a distant slope, something labors with a regular, double-ended beat. You might hear it for hours. Then a joint opens somewhere out of sight and the beat becomes a roar.

There is no central engine to find. Every part draws a little power from the parts around it: wind flexing the upper mat, water descending through old growth, chemical differences between buried cold and the faintly warmer skin. On rare clear days, sunlight sets off a frantic acceleration. Pools empty. Stems stand erect. The ground acquires a thin, continuous whistle. By evening the whole country is exhausted and dripping, with great flocks feeding along its newly opened seams.

The animals are most visible during these changes.

At rest, they resemble part of the surface. Flat-backed grazers lie in the channels with only their nostrils showing, their fur threaded with the same plants they eat. Six-legged white birds tuck themselves into the pale undersides of leaves. Tiny, translucent animals occupy the water between stems in such numbers that a lifted handful seems to thicken as you watch it.

When the pressure shifts, they move together.

A quiet basin suddenly releases thousands of birds. Their extra legs unfold behind them like loose stitches. Grazers heave out of water that is draining beneath their bodies. Long, dark predators follow the moving edge of a ground swell, catching creatures forced up through the weave. Above them wheel broad-winged scavengers, almost motionless in the cold air, waiting for the larger ruptures.

Some animals travel inside the plateau. Their passage makes a low ridge that advances through the vegetation, dividing at obstacles and joining again beyond them. Occasionally a pale back appears where the surface is thin. It is difficult to judge the size of the animal from this glimpse. The same back may surface again half a mile away while the first exposure is still visible.

Distance is least dependable near running water.

Two neighboring channels can carry reflections of different skies. One shows the dim overcast overhead; another shows a hard blue opening with a white sun low in it. Birds drink from either without hesitation, but they watch their feet. Where the waters meet, reflections spill briefly onto the banks. A patch of blue sky travels over the dark stems, folds at a rise, and disappears into a drain.

There are routes across the plateau that are longer in one direction than the other. This is not a subtle difference. Returning along your own tracks, you may come upon a basin that was not there on the outward journey, with mature growth around its edges and animals already feeding in it. Your footprints continue on the far side.

The oldest ground is the most liable to these disturbances. It has been used too many times.

Water has worn its passages until some run through the same place at different ages. Roots enter them and emerge carrying seeds from growth that has not yet appeared on the surface. During a violent pressure release, a slope may shed its present vegetation and expose an earlier covering beneath: small red cushions, perhaps, or a forest of inch-high black trees. The exposed plants are healthy. For a few minutes they bend in a wind from another direction. Then the modern mat creeps over them again.

Almost no loose material escapes. Broken stems are caught, dissolved, and taken back into growth. Banks collapse into channels that consume the collapse. The great flatness has been maintained through ages of upheaval too local and too frequent to leave a lasting hill. Even a rupture large enough to lift a lake into the air settles, eventually, into another shallow hollow.

Such an event begins with an absence of sound.

The clattering valves stop. The buried pulse hesitates. Animals lift their heads across miles of open ground.

Then the lake rises, held in a transparent bowl of stretched plant tissue. Its fish fall toward its new lowest point. Water streams off its sides, and the surrounding surface draws inward, exposing pale fibers under tremendous tension. For a moment there is a round, trembling height where there has been flat water longer than any creature remembers.

The bowl tears.

What follows is cold spray, a crash felt through the knees, birds everywhere. Slabs of living ground roll over and reveal their crowded undersides. Channels open in branching flashes. The buried machinery takes up its beat at different speeds until, one by one, the rhythms catch.

Hours later the basin is low and quiet again. A few stems still stand out of alignment. The grazers return before the water has cleared.

Toward evening, mist settles so evenly that the isolated columns seem to stand on separate shores. The ground continues moving beneath it. There is a suck and knock from the nearest pool, the rasp of feeding mouths, a distant discharge rolling through the fog. Frost gathers on the exposed leaves while water runs warm enough to steam a finger’s breadth below them.

At the plateau’s edge, a thin stream slips over a lip of roots into the clouds below. It falls only a short distance before separating into droplets. The droplets hang there, trembling in a vertical line.

New roots are growing down toward them.
