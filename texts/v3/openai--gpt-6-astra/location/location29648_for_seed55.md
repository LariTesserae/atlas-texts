The water is so dark that the terraces appear first as differences in darkness: a level band, a deeper band beneath it, then another level band extending beyond sight. There is no shore nearby. Above, somewhere, lies a flat surface under cloud. Below, the steps descend through cold brown water toward a plain that has not received daylight in a very long time.

Everything beneath the water was laid by hand.

The upper terraces are faced with thin stone slabs, fitted without mortar. Behind them lie courses of broken pottery, gravel, and clay. Where a facing has slipped, these fillings spill out in subdued fans, each fragment furred with the same brown film. No exposed bedrock interrupts the work. A sounding pole driven into the bottom would pass through silt, then softened brick, then another bedding of fragments, then older paving. Even the material called gravel is mostly masonry worn past recognition.

The construction continues for distances that make individual terraces seem incidental. Whole districts are no more than slight changes in depth. A rise that takes days to cross might amount to the height of a person. Long embankments divide the drowned plain into basins, and the basins contain smaller basins, channels, settling pools, low enclosures. There are no towers left to declare a center. If there was a center, the water does not distinguish it.

Near the surface, the vegetation is thick.

Pondweed grows from joints in the paving, its narrow leaves crowded along pale stems. Water moss covers the north-facing sides of blocks, though north makes little practical difference now. In sheltered reaches, hornwort forms loose, tangled masses several bodies deep. The plants almost meet across the old channels. Between them remain dark passages just wide enough for a slow current to pass, carrying an occasional loose leaf.

Most of this growth occupies a shallow upper layer. The water is stained by centuries of fallen vegetation, and light cannot travel far through it. At midday, under the broad mats of floating leaves, there is only a weak olive illumination. A little lower, green becomes black. Lower still, the plants cease to grow and their fallen stems begin to accumulate.

This gives the landscape two floors. There is the built floor, with its paving and channels, and above it a suspended, uneven floor of living vegetation. Between them is a low, dim space crossed by roots.

In places the roots have found the old work. They enter cracks between coping stones and spread along the clay beneath, fastening floating mats to submerged walls. Elsewhere they hang freely. A whole field of sedges may be attached to the bottom at only a few points, its woven underbody lifting and settling so slowly that the motion becomes apparent only beside a fixed stone. Beneath such a field, the water smells of peat and cold mud. Fine root hairs catch flakes of brick.

The surface is rarely visible from below. When a gap opens in the vegetation, it shows as a pale, trembling patch, without enough brightness to cast a shadow.

There are no fish moving through the stems. No shells gather at the foot of the walls. A leaf can remain balanced on a ledge until it softens and comes apart. The only disturbances are caused by water, by the weight of growing plants, and by the slow failure of things that were once carefully supported.

The cold helps preserve what has fallen.

In the deeper channels, lengths of timber lie darkened but recognizable. Some retain the rectangular sockets where crosspieces were fitted. Wooden pegs remain in a few of the joints. There are wicker panels under the mud, baskets collapsed into flat spirals, bundles of brushwood pinned beneath stone. The smallest details have sometimes survived better than the grand arrangement: a thumb hollow in a brick, the marks of a cord twisted around wet clay, a line of closely spaced blows along the edge of a slab.

The work was done with simple tools. Nothing suggests machinery beyond levers, rollers, ropes, and the repeated lifting of manageable weights. Large blocks are uncommon. The immense scale comes from small pieces laid beside other small pieces, then repaired, then raised by another course.

On some terrace faces, the repairs can still be read. A stretch of thin gray slabs gives way to rounded brown blocks. Above them is a cap of brick, and above that a final narrow curb made from mismatched fragments. The water level must have risen slowly enough for people to keep answering it.

Now it stands above all of them.

The old channels still influence its movement. Water entering a basin follows the shallow depressions beneath the weed, turns through gateways whose lintels have fallen, and leaves through narrow cuts in the far embankment. These currents are too weak to stir the bottom continuously. Their presence is marked by clean strips on the upper edges of stones, or by long stems all inclined in the same direction.

Farther out, broad open reaches separate the floating fields. Here the wind has room to work. A storm elsewhere can send a gentle displacement of water through the channels for hours, even when the local surface is still. The weed leans. Silt lifts from a sill. In a covered passage, a few grains travel along the floor and collect against the next obstruction.

That is often the largest event of the day.

Sound carries from a distance but has little to carry. Sometimes there is a soft rubbing where two pieces of timber touch. Sometimes a bubble escapes from buried vegetation and passes upward, striking the underside of a leaf mat. In the coldest season, ice forms over the open reaches. Its contractions produce long, low reports that arrive through the water without a clear direction.

Under the ice, the shallows become dimmer still. Growth pauses. The floating fields are held in place, and the dangling roots stand almost motionless between their ceiling and the drowned masonry. Snow gathers above them where no one below can see it.

Over much longer periods, the terraces continue to lose their edges. Clay washes from behind a wall one grain at a time. A slab settles into the space left beneath it. Its neighbor tilts after it. The resulting hollow catches silt and dead leaves, and eventually the break becomes difficult to find.

Nothing here remains sharp merely because it was made sharp. Steps have become ramps. Channels have broadened into swales. Courtyards are shallow dishes full of black sediment. The differences between a wall and an embankment, a road and a ridge, grow less certain with depth and distance.

Yet the old arrangement persists. Plants grow more densely along buried joints. Open water follows the former thoroughfares. The straight edge of a floating sedge bed may continue for hundreds of paces because its roots have fastened to a wall just beneath the surface.

From above, in the brief colorless light of winter, those lines would be almost all there is to see: dark water, dull vegetation, a few pale seams of ice, and the enormous, faint geometry of a place still holding the shape of its making.
