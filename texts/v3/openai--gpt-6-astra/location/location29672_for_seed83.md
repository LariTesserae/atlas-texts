The stair ends in warm water, two steps before the bottom.

It is only ankle-deep. Beneath it, the treads continue into a floor of small hexagonal tiles, their joints widened by generations of soft corrosion. You feel the pattern through your soles before you see it. There is almost no light down here: a red indicator above the stair door, a little of its reflection in the water, and farther off a pale seam where two metal panels do not quite meet.

The chamber is not large. A person could cross it in thirty paces if there were a clear way across. There is not. Its interior is crowded with condenser columns, tanks, valve housings, and brick-jacketed ducts, some so close together that the spaces between them admit a hand but not an arm. Overhead, pipes occupy the height that might otherwise make this a lofty room. They cross in layers, thick as tree trunks near the ceiling, narrowing into copper bundles as they descend. Some have sagged until they rest on other pipes. The whole weight has settled into arrangements no plan could have anticipated.

Nothing here is exposed earth. The floor, the pillars, the walls behind the machinery, even the shallow trough collecting water along the perimeter, were made. Yet their surfaces have acquired the variety of a much older terrain. Tile has become smooth in the walking channels and rough beneath persistent drips. The brick jackets have shed their square edges. Ridges of pale deposit descend from pipe joints and spread across brackets, binding separate parts together. In places it is difficult to tell whether a supporting foot was cast in that shape or slowly buried in it.

The heat is the first thing that makes distances uncertain. Beside one tank the air is merely close; half a step farther on, it presses against the face like the breath from an opened oven. There is no single hot source. Heat arrives through the ceiling, rises through the tiled floor, seeps from unmarked cylinders. A handrail remains too hot to hold long after the adjacent pipe has cooled. In the narrow passages, your clothes begin to cling before you have decided which way to go.

Most surfaces are dry. A touch leaves gray powder on the fingers, with the faint greasy feel of old insulation. The moisture keeps to particular routes: a sweating elbow joint, a chain, the underside of a sloping duct. Drops travel surprisingly far before falling. One gathers on a rivet, enlarges without apparent hurry, and lets go into a cup-shaped depression worn in the tile below. There may be several minutes between drops. Their impacts are small and distinct.

The water at the stair belongs to a shallow basin bounded by raised floor seams. Other basins lie farther in, separated by dry islands only inches higher. Some contain clear water over orange sediment. Others wear a skin so dark and still that they look like holes through the floor. There is no stream linking them. They fill when particular pipes cool, diminish when those pipes heat again, and occasionally spill across a saddle in the paving. A basin can overflow without a sound.

The machinery still operates, though not in any rhythm easy to recognize. Somewhere behind the rear wall, a motor runs for less than a minute and stops. Nothing immediately changes. Later, a column gives a soft inward knock. Later still, a patch of condensation withdraws up a panel, leaving a border like the edge of a receding tide.

Most of the time the chamber is quiet enough to hear a bead of water slide down sheet metal.

Near the pale seam, there are plants. Not many: a few thin runners rooted in the damp packing around a cable entry, carrying leaves the color of old celery. They have grown flat against the panel rather than outward into the room. Their newest tips disappear into the gap, following the light toward a space too narrow to see into. Fallen leaves collect below in a little curl of dust and mineral flakes. Nothing grows around them. The warmer wall on one side and the dry air on the other keep this small colony within the width of a hand.

A former inspection lamp hangs nearby. Its glass is opaque, its cage thickened with deposits. Around the hinge, where the heat is less severe, a rust-colored film develops after each wet interval. It dries to a fine cracking skin. When the lamp trembles, a few flakes fall, exposing the black metal underneath.

You might see an animal only once. A pale cricket, long-legged and almost translucent, stands on a dry gasket above the waterline. It does not call. When disturbed, it backs into the gasket’s split instead of jumping. The chamber offers many such shelters, but little food; most are empty. Beneath the light seam, a few bitten leaf edges provide the only other sign of it.

There are paths through the equipment. They survive as polished corners, bent handles, a sequence of footholds above the wetter floor. At the narrowest point, someone long ago removed a valve wheel to make room for a shoulder. The stem remains, wrapped in a hardened pad of cloth. Farther on, a low pipe has been rubbed bright on its underside by people stooping beneath it. No one has passed often enough in recent years to keep the bright patch free of tarnish.

At the back is a service recess, barely large enough to stand in. Its floor is dry and its wall unexpectedly cool. Through the metal comes a faint, continuous rushing, much quieter than breathing. Whatever moves there lies beyond several layers of construction. There is no hatch.

From this recess, the room seems deeper than it did at the stair. The tanks hide one another; a little reflected red light appears between them in a place where you would not expect to see the door. As your eyes adjust, more edges become visible without the spaces behind them growing clear. You can trace a pipe into darkness, find what seems to be its continuation, and then discover by touch that it is another pipe entirely.

After a while, warmth and stillness make the place feel inactive. Then water begins to tap into an empty basin near your foot.

The sound is different from the earlier drip: hollow at first, then flatter as the depression fills. A cold line develops along the underside of a pipe. The nearest tank stays hot. Nothing else moves.

By the time you return to the stair, one of the dry crossings has become wet. You step over it easily. Behind you, the new water spreads through the tile joints, advancing into the powder a fraction of an inch at a time.
