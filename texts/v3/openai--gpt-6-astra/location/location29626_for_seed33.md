The city occupies the upper edges of a mountain range. From a distance, its districts look like chips left on the rim of a broken bowl: pale, widely separated, almost too small to account for the shadows beneath them. Between one district and the next are kilometers of open air.

There is no bowl below. The mountains descend until the light gives out.

At this height, the sky is the color of the underside of a stone. A weak brightness travels through it over the course of the day, illuminating first one side of a tower, then another, without ever revealing a sun. Exposed surfaces are cool enough to numb bare skin. Sheltered courtyards retain a little warmth, though nothing in them seems to produce it. Dust collects in those places, an exceedingly fine gray dust that moves away from a foot before the foot touches down.

The ground is a dry, porous mineral, cream-colored where freshly broken and brown where old. Most of it is old. Corners have been worn into shoulders; flights of steps have become shallow inclines; pillars swell at their bases like ankles. In some streets, the abrasion of many ages has erased the distinction between paving and wall. You walk along the bottom of a long, rounded groove, with windows opening overhead.

There has never been rain here. The grooves were made by the daily passage of height.

Near the dimmest hour, elevation loosens from the mountain and moves uphill. It travels slowly, in broad bands. A low step becomes a high one. A courtyard rises through its surrounding houses, emerging above their roofs for an hour before settling back. Entire neighborhoods lean toward the summit, although their stones remain in place. This wears them down. You can see the effect on a doorway that has survived many thousands of these passages: the upper lintel polished thin, the threshold deeply scooped, everything between them drawn slightly upward.

The safest routes follow the grain of this wear.

They are recognizably streets. They meet at small squares. There are houses, arcades, passages between houses, communal halls with benches along their walls. Some districts have several layers of construction: a street laid across older roofs, beneath it another street full of settled dust, beneath that the faint outlines of rooms embedded in solid rock.

Yet there are no bricks, joints, or tool marks. No piece of a house has ever been separate from the mountain.

Architecture forms here under conditions that elsewhere produce crystals.

Give this stone sufficient exposure to emptiness and it develops an interior. First a shallow depression appears, then a recess, then a room. A room facing another room produces a lane between them. Lanes attract further rooms. Over centuries, the process organizes itself into districts, complete with sheltered meeting places and long, inconvenient approaches. A steep slope may grow a staircase before there is anywhere for the staircase to go.

The city is still forming, but so slowly that its freshest quarter consists of three niches on a remote cliff. Each niche is just deep enough to protect a face from the wind.

Elsewhere, whole districts are wearing back into mountain.

One of these stands on a ridge as narrow as a wall. Its houses have lost their rear halves to the gulf. From the remaining street, each open doorway frames the same depthless gray. Alcoves, shelves, and the rounded hollows of sleeping places persist in the side walls. A fine powder sifts from their edges. It falls a short distance, stops, and spreads into a horizontal seam.

There are thousands of such seams below the city. They mark old heights the dust has not yet learned to leave.

The atmosphere is thin, dry, and surprisingly busy. Air pours through the mountain gaps in transparent currents, visible only when they lift a thread of powder or distort the edge of a distant building. At some crossings, a current bends the view so sharply that the next district appears within arm’s reach. Its balconies tremble just beyond your fingertips. Then the current passes, and the district is again a pale cluster on a far summit.

You must never step toward a place merely because it looks near.

The few creatures that live here seem to understand this without hesitation. They are flat-bodied animals, each about the length of a hand, with six long legs and a fringe of stiff white hairs. Their bodies resemble thin flakes of the same mineral as the streets. Most of the time they are motionless beneath ledges, gripping the stone with hooked feet.

When the height begins to move, they emerge.

A dozen may cross a square in a scattered procession, placing their feet on patches of ground that have not yet risen. They pause at invisible boundaries. Their fringes lift. Then, all at once, they hurry forward. One takes a route straight up a wall and arrives on the far side of the square without crossing its middle.

They feed along the newest cracks, scraping away a dark film too thin to be seen except at a low angle. This film is almost all the living cover the city possesses. It makes no leaves or stems. In sheltered fissures it has a faint violet sheen; in exposed places it contracts into isolated black points. Its growth follows strain in the stone. A wall about to split may darken for several days beforehand.

Sometimes a much larger animal appears on an unoccupied ridge.

You first notice the interruption it makes in the skyline. It is tall, narrow, and jointed, with a head that hangs far below its shoulders. It walks slowly along the outer walls, lowering that head into courtyards. The small creatures retreat deep into cracks before it arrives.

Its feet make no audible contact with the ground. But after it has passed, the rooms beside its route are shallower. Shelves protrude farther. A passage that once admitted a body becomes too narrow to enter.

It may be eating the interiors.

There are only a few sounds. A loosened grain striking a step. A dry scrape inside a wall. Occasionally, from far below, a single low knock travels up through the rock. The knock is felt in the teeth before it is heard, and by the time its echo reaches the neighboring district, several minutes may have passed.

Wind here seldom whistles. There is too little air, and the openings have grown too smooth. It presses against the ears instead, leaving a sense of something large moving nearby.

During the brighter hours, the city appears abandoned. During the dimmer ones, abandonment becomes a less certain explanation.

Doorways shift their attention toward the streets. Rooms that were perfectly ordinary at midday become difficult to look into. Their floors remain visible, their walls remain close, but the space enclosed by those walls seems to continue a long way beyond them. From a small upper window you may see the mountain range outside; from the window immediately beneath it, you may see that same range from the other side.

The discrepancy lasts until the traveling brightness returns.

For all this, the city is not chaotic. Its changes have seasons. There are times when the western ridges acquire stairways, times when courts deepen, times when a whole avenue closes its doors and becomes an unbroken channel in the rock. Old districts are calmer than young ones. Their streets have found durable courses through the moving height. Their houses have settled upon shapes they can retain.

On one broad summit is a square that has apparently remained unchanged for a very long time. It contains no monument, well, or tree. Four low walls enclose it, with an opening in each. The paving has worn into a gentle hollow at the center.

From there, you can see most of the city: sparse ranks of buildings along the ridges, isolated halls on their spurs, a long stair descending into dimness and returning, impossibly, on a mountain farther away.

A little warmth gathers in the hollow.

For an hour or two, nothing near you rises or recedes. The animals rest under the wall. Fine dust travels overhead in a level sheet. Across the gulf, an entire neighborhood slowly turns its windows toward the brighter part of the sky.

Then a shallow line appears across the square.

By morning it will be a step.
