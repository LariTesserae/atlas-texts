The place is a shallow pocket left between two very thick walls. You could stand inside it, but not turn comfortably. It has no view, no doorframe, and no obvious purpose. The way in is sideways, through a break where several courses of brick stop short of meeting.

Inside, everything is white or nearly white.

The floor lies a little below the passage outside. It is made from broken roof tiles packed on edge, their curved profiles nested so closely that they resemble the exposed pages of a heavy book. Pale mortar fills the intervals. There is no loose piece to kick aside, no sand, no soil underneath a crack. Where a tile has worn through, another tile shows below it. The whole depth appears to have been filled by hand.

The tiles make a low, crowded terrain: overlapping ridges, shallow saddles, narrow hollows whose bottoms are already occupied by smaller fragments. Most of the ridges rise only as high as a fingernail. One reaches ankle height against the back wall, where the fragments become too densely nested to distinguish from a single ribbed block.

Nothing here has a sharp corner. The tile edges are rounded, the mortar worn into smooth fillets. Even the entrance has the broad, comfortable curve of something touched often and long ago. Whatever did the wearing is finished. There is no fresh dust beneath the walls.

The walls themselves are built from many kinds of leftover material. Thin bricks, a piece of fluted column, lengths of pale earthenware pipe, flattened lumps of lime: all have been fitted together and plastered over. The plaster has worn thin enough to disclose their shapes without quite exposing them. A circular pipe mouth is visible at knee height, but it has been filled flush with mortar. Above it, a row of thumbprints remains from the making. Their raised edges have softened until they look less like marks than a small variation in the wall’s grain.

There is no machinery, no fitting, nothing adjustable. The construction consists entirely of weight, adhesion, and careful wedging.

High overhead, the walls nearly touch. Between them is a crooked opening no wider than your palm. You cannot see the sky through it. Light enters by striking one white surface and then another, spreading into the pocket until even the undersides of the tile ridges are plainly visible. It is bright enough to make you narrow your eyes, yet there is no warm patch to put a hand in. The clay holds the coolness of an unheated interior. So does the air.

The sound is much larger than the space.

A dense, continuous rush fills it, loud enough that an ordinary speaking voice would be difficult to follow. It comes through the masonry and down the narrow opening overhead. Somewhere beyond these walls, dry air passes through a long constriction. Here there is no perceptible draft. Nothing stirs. The noise has neither gusts nor pauses; it is an even, coarse sound, like a great quantity of grain being poured without ever striking bottom.

At first it obscures the smallness of the enclosure. You might imagine a wide bright country just beyond the wall. Then you look down and find your foot occupying a substantial part of the landscape.

A little vegetation survives in the mortar seams. It grows in short, stiff fans, gray-green at their roots and chalk-white at their tips. Each fan has only three or four narrow leaves, thick as folded paper. They do not droop. A few have pushed out horizontally beneath the tile lips, taking the tiny spaces that the mason’s fingers could not fill.

There are no dead leaves nearby. The oldest leaves remain attached, gradually becoming paler and harder until it is difficult to tell them from the lime around them. In several seams, a plant seems to have completed this passage: its whole shape is present, but its surface has the dull whiteness of the wall. Whether these are still living is not something the pocket makes easy to decide.

There is no insect traffic among them, no web across the empty pipe’s shallow rim, no burrow or scratching. Nor is there any dampness. The hollows in the floor contain only light. Touching the mortar leaves nothing on your fingertip.

The longer you remain, the more of the making you notice. A crescent of red clay showing through the white. A smooth pebble set into plaster where a larger fragment would not fit. Two tiles facing opposite ways, their curves clasped together. Small decisions occupy every inch.

Yet none of it feels provisional. Nothing needs repairing, clearing, or finishing. The little plants have their seams; the tile fragments have their weight; the light reaches the deepest accessible recess. Even the enormous noise fits here, held between surfaces that never answer it with a rattle.

You could leave and return without finding a single displaced grain. The pocket would still be full: cool clay, crowded ridges, pale leaves, and that bright, steady roar.
