The country is high enough that clouds catch beneath its edges, but warm enough that water left in a leaf stays warm all night. From a distance it looks like a valley lifted out of the earth: a broad, shallow basin, several long ridges, a few isolated heights. There is no mountain underneath it. Along the southern rim, where the cloud thins, the underside is visible—pale ribs and hanging rootwork, all joined into a single immense construction.

Nothing here rests on stone.

The ground is living wood, though it has neither the grain nor the hardness of a fallen trunk. It gives slightly underfoot. Across the open flats it is covered with a short, fine nap, green when seen toward the light and silver when seen against it. Each filament ends in a clear bead. Walking leaves a dark footprint that closes behind you, not by springing upright but by drawing water back into the compressed tissue.

There is little to interrupt the view. A person standing in the center could see almost to both rims, several miles apart. Vegetation has not filled the basin with thickets; it has become the basin. What would elsewhere be soil, rock, paths, streambeds, and the sides of distant hills is here different work performed by the same continuous body.

Here and there, widely separated, structures rise from it.

One resembles an unfinished hall. Its columns are hollow green shafts, broader at their tops than at their bases. Between them hang the beginnings of arches: thick lips of tender material that almost meet. The roof consists of three broad leaves held horizontally, with open sky between them. Rain passes through those openings into shallow cups in the floor. You can stand inside and hear each cup sounding at a different depth.

Farther away is a stairway wide enough for a procession, climbing a low ridge. Every step has a soft, rolled edge. At the top there is no platform, only a smooth bud the size of a house, with a seam where it seems about to open.

It has always seemed about to open.

The whole country has this quality of recent making. Its ridges have the rounded, uncreased surfaces of things newly pushed into existence. Beneath overhangs, the membranes are thin enough for daylight to show the branching vessels inside. A split in a wall exposes white fibers still glossy with sap. Nothing is weathered gray. Nothing has acquired the settled irregularity of age.

Yet the place is not growing larger. The unfinished arches remain at the same narrow distance from completion. The stairway’s upper bud neither opens nor hardens. A visitor returning after many years would find the same crease in the same translucent parapet, with the same drop of amber liquid swelling beneath it. The drop falls, eventually. Another takes its place.

Water moves everywhere, and the shapes remain.

Along the higher slopes it emerges through thousands of pores, making the surface shine without quite forming a stream. Lower down, these wet surfaces fold into channels. The channels are lined with slick red tissue and carry clear water through the flats toward a chain of broad, shallow pools. There is no mud at their edges. The banks are thick green cuffs, warm to the touch, with delicate submerged roots combed flat by the current.

The pools are spaced far apart. Between them lie long stretches of empty, softly shining ground. Their water is transparent, but the bottom is difficult to judge because it is alive: a pale membrane with faint violet branching beneath it. Occasionally a slow contraction travels across that membrane. The water rises a finger’s breadth against the bank and subsides.

At the western end of the basin, the drainage disappears under a low vault. This is where much of the noise comes from.

The place is loud without seeming busy. Water runs through hollow members beneath the floor, falling through concealed chambers and striking taut partitions. The larger cavities produce a continuous, deep rushing note. Smaller ones knock and bubble. A distant ridge answers with a dry wooden booming, though its surface is wet and no visible branch moves.

Out on the flats, conversation is difficult. You have to turn your mouth toward another person’s ear. Inside certain alcoves, however, the sound drops away almost completely. Their walls are covered with soft downward-pointing scales. There you can hear water leaving your clothes, or the faint friction of your own hand against the floor.

There is no machinery below, no buried masonry, no original foundation that the plants have covered. The hollow channels, load-bearing ribs, thresholds, and cisterns are organs. Every surface has been laid down by a living seam. You can trace those seams with a fingertip: the neat join where a wall rose from a floor, the thicker collar where a bridge fastened itself to a bank.

What made the decisions is less clear.

Some passages fit a walking body remarkably well. Others narrow to the width of a wrist while retaining the proportions of a grand entrance. One bridge crosses a dry depression and ends halfway across, in a perfectly finished rounded edge. Beneath it, the depression is shaped like something removed rather than something worn away.

Animals are uncommon. Near the central pools, a few long-legged, cream-colored creatures stand with their feet spread over the wet banks. They have small heads and no visible mouths. For hours they face the same direction. Then one lowers its body until the soft underside touches the ground, remains there briefly, and rises again. A damp oval marks the contact.

Nothing pursues them. There are no busy clouds of insects, no continuous movement in the vegetation. Their occasional crossing of a flat is conspicuous at a great distance.

The light is ordinary daylight. It makes clear shadows beneath the open halls and brings out the yellow in the highest ridges. That ordinary light makes the place easier to see and harder to explain. There is no dimness in which to lose an awkward junction, no haze hiding the fact that a distant hillside is folded like the inside of a leaf.

Toward the rim, the ground becomes thinner. It sounds hollow beneath your steps. You pass scattered upright forms, each bearing one broad canopy on a fleshy stalk, with hundreds of feet of open space between them. Their shade smells faintly of cut melon. Water gathers along the canopy edges and falls in heavy, widely spaced drops.

At the last rise, the living floor curls upward into a low rail. Beyond it lies bright air, then cloud, then occasional glimpses of a far lower country.

The rail is tender. Press it with your thumb and a pale mark appears, then slowly fills with green. Beneath your hand, through all the rushing and booming, there is a smaller sound: a steady wet creak from the seam where the edge is being made.

It does not advance. It does not stop making.
