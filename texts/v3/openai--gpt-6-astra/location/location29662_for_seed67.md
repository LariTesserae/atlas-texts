The plain is pale enough that the animals show first.

At a distance they look like dark water caught in the joints: long stains between platforms, black rims around the shallow basins, a stippling across the undersides of bridges. Then one of the stains lifts and settles a little farther along, and its surface resolves into thousands of backs. The animals are crowded wherever a body can find shade. They hang beneath the walking decks, pack themselves into drainage mouths, occupy the spaces between pipes. Out on the illuminated flats, the larger ones lie with their broad bellies in a finger’s depth of water.

There is scarcely a place from which you cannot see something breathing.

The ground is made of fitted ceramic plates, each the size of a town square. They are a warm ivory, faintly pink where the light enters their edges. Their upper surfaces have been cast with ribs, dimples, shallow channels, and occasional patches of close-set studs. Nothing here has the irregularity of exposed rock. Even the rough surfaces repeat, although the interval can be so large that you may walk for hours before recognizing a particular arrangement of ridges.

Water occupies the lowest relief. It lies in the dimples, fills the channels, laps against the bases of service housings. Across long stretches it forms a continuous sheet that scarcely covers the ceramic. Beneath it, pale lines converge on drains as narrow as knife cuts.

The plates are sharp at their corners. Their surfaces have no weathered rind. A broken-looking escarpment in the middle distance proves, when approached, to be a line of intentionally staggered edges, clean as pieces removed from a mold. The whole plain has the freshness of a place opened yesterday. Yet its joints are fully inhabited, its animal populations immense, its maintenance markings familiar enough to have become routes.

There is no dust.

Warm moisture rests on everything. It gathers beneath handrails, beads on the insulated collars of pipes, slicks the flanks of animals moving through a culvert. Put a hand on the ground and it feels like the outside of a cup that has recently held hot water. The air is a little cooler. Clothes become damp at the cuffs and remain so.

Above, the light is ordinary daylight: broad, white, sufficient to show the small differences between cream ceramic, gray enamel, wet steel, and living skin. Its source is less ordinary. High above the plain is another made surface, so distant that atmospheric haze gives it the appearance of sky. Vast illuminated panels alternate with dull structural bands. Most of the bands are lost in brightness. Here and there one emerges as a straight gray line, too long to have an easily grasped scale.

The light does not move across the ground.

A narrow shadow on the side of a valve house is as much a geographical feature as the channel beside it. Animals adapted to that shadow live their entire lives within its boundaries. A band of small, translucent bodies follows its precise edge, each animal keeping its pale underside out of the brighter zone. Across the plain there are thousands of these permanent shores between light and shade.

The buildings are low and numerous. Pump halls, exchange chambers, feeder cabinets, pressure housings: they stand nearly shoulder to shoulder, leaving passages just wide enough for water, animals, and occasional access. Their roofs form a second broken plain a few body-lengths above the first. In places a third layer of walkways covers them. Under all of it are further floors visible through gratings, lit by the same steady white light.

The density is difficult to understand from any single viewpoint. What looks like an open basin has two occupied decks beneath it. What looks like a solid wall is a rack of flat conduits with finger-wide corridors between them. Look into one of these corridors and you see eyes, moist mouthparts, tiny feet braced against porcelain.

The plain continues beyond the distance at which structures can be distinguished. There are no mountains to announce its limits. Far away, concentrations of warm vapor make low white provinces, each with a darker base where the machinery is packed most tightly. Other districts remain clear for distances that would contain countries. From an elevated inspection bridge, the surface resembles an enormous, shallow estuary whose banks and islands have all been fabricated to tolerance.

Everything beneath it is fabricated too.

The deepest inspection shafts reveal no bedrock. They pass through tanks, cellular supports, broad dark reservoirs, load-bearing diaphragms, and further levels of ceramic ground. Their walls carry depth marks and part numbers. Somewhere far below, the materials change, but there is never a point at which construction gives way to earth.

Most of the animals are soft-bodied.

The commonest are palm-sized, flattened, and dark blue, with six short legs and a fringe of fine white filaments around the mouth. They graze the plates where nutrient fluid seeps through microscopic pores. Seen close up, their crowded feeding grounds are orderly. Each animal works a small patch, lifting its mouth fringe, setting it down, easing forward. Their feet have broad adhesive pads. Thousands can occupy a vertical face without one falling.

Among them move clear-bodied grazers through which the ceramic pattern remains visible, slightly enlarged. Narrow silver animals live inside the runoff channels and hold themselves against the current with paired hooks. The hooks fit grooves molded into the channel walls.

There are creatures the size of oxen, though much lower, with blunt heads and a continuous fleshy skirt hiding their limbs. They rest across the warm basins, their backs raised like dark, smooth stepping-stones. Water runs under them. Smaller animals cluster along their skirts, picking material from the folds. Each large body has several dry patches on its upper surface where a different community lives: chalk-white mites, little jointed scavengers, glossy worms no longer than an eyelash.

You could spend a day examining one of these backs without exhausting its inhabitants.

Predators are slender and comparatively sparse. They follow the channels with their heads held low, selecting animals by touch. Their jaws close sideways. There is little pursuit; prey is abundant, cover is dense, and every passage restricts speed. A predator takes something from beneath a pipe, swallows, and returns to a recess worn into nothing at all—the recess was made for that purpose.

This is the peculiarity that becomes harder to overlook the longer you remain: the fit is too exact.

The broad animals settle into basins that support their weight evenly. The grazers’ mouth fringes match the spacing of the nutrient pores. Narrow ledges beneath the light panels have the right width for the feet of the pale fliers roosting there in continuous ranks. Even the nests, where animals gather to shed skins or release their young, correspond to numbered depressions in the infrastructure.

In the service galleries, behind thick windows, industrial lines assemble replacement membranes, feeder plugs, pump vanes, and shells for the little maintenance crawlers. Farther along the same lines are warm vessels filled with developing bodies. These have no viewing lights. Their contents show only when an inspection panel catches the daylight from outside: pale folds, a comb of limbs, the slow contraction of something nearly ready to leave its mold.

The categories used by the machinery are size, demand, useful life, and recovery rate.

It is a quiet place.

The pumps are everywhere, but their masses are enormous and their bearings submerged. Their operation reaches you mostly through the soles of your feet. A handrail gives a faint, steady tremor. Against a tank wall you can hear a low note without a discernible beginning.

The water seldom falls far enough to splash. It passes over rounded lips or descends along cords. The animals make soft contact sounds: pads releasing from enamel, a wet flank easing against a basin, small jaws working close to the ground. Now and then a flier crosses a passage on broad gray wings. The wingbeat stirs the damp air against your face.

There are no calls carrying over the plain. Bodies find one another through touch, scent, vibration, and the permanent arrangement of the passages. A loud sound would travel a very long way here.

Nothing accumulates.

The water level remains at the same fine line on every basin wall. The feeder pores deliver exactly what is consumed. Dead bodies soften in the recovery channels and are drawn beneath screens; the emptied spaces fill without any visible surge in population. Ceramic surfaces retain their newness. The channels do not deepen. No colony grows into a blockage, no damp corner develops an unplanned skin of green.

Movement is abundant, but the place keeps its shape.

At first this stability resembles good maintenance. Later, it feels more fundamental. There is no district awaiting completion, no abandoned hall, no pipe whose purpose has lapsed. Condensation hangs under a bridge at the same density as under the next. A warm haze occupies one far basin and never advances across the adjoining clear ground. The balance extends beyond what can be watched or measured from any local station.

You can walk until your feet ache and find another low parapet, another expanse of pale flooded plates, another dense congregation of dark bodies under a pipe. The arrangement will differ. The fullness will not.

At the parapet, a large animal rests with one blunt forelimb over the edge. Its skin is warm and has the faint mineral smell of the water. Beneath the limb, three small grazers continue feeding in the protected strip of shade. A clear thread of runoff passes between them and disappears through a seam.

Beyond it are the bright flats, the close roofs, the vapor provinces, all of them occupied. The machinery holds its low note. The animal’s flank rises against the parapet and subsides. Water returns to the space it briefly displaced.
