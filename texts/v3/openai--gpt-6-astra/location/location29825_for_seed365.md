The town occupies a split in the mountain, not quite a valley and not quite a courtyard. You can cross it in a few minutes if the stairways are clear. On either side, pale rock rises in fresh, angular faces, so recently exposed that the corners still look breakable. Above them is a narrow strip of gray sky. Cloud passes across this opening without entering, and the light at the bottom has the cold, indirect quality of light beneath a door.

There are seven houses, a granary, and a long public building with no roof. This last structure is called the hall, although it is mostly steps: shallow tiers descending to a square of bare stone. All the doors in town face it. Their arrangement makes the settlement seem larger than it is, as though streets and districts have been compressed into a few carefully distinguished distances. The space between the granary and the nearest house is a market. The space beside the standing slab is a gate. Nothing closes it.

Construction is heavy at the bottom and delicate above. House walls are fitted from slabs pried out of the mountain, their outer faces left rough, their inner faces rubbed smooth with smaller stones. The roofs are split wood weighted with turf. Wooden pegs, rawhide bindings, a few small iron hooks: there is little else holding the upper town together. Along the walls run gutters of hollow stalks, each section overlapping the next. They lead to covered jars in recesses where the rock stays cold.

Water does not flow through the place. It arrives in beads.

Before dawn, the cliff faces darken along certain seams. Moisture gathers on the underside of leaves, along roof edges, on the whiskers of sleeping animals. By morning there may be enough in a household jar to make a shallow sound when it is lifted. Rain sometimes reaches the bottom of the cleft, but most of it is taken by the plants high overhead. No one here would leave a vessel standing in the open and expect it to fill.

Nevertheless, the vegetation is sumptuous.

It grows in separate, extravagant masses, with broad intervals of bare paving between them. A single rooted pocket may support a whole suspended garden: thick fern fronds, dark cushions of moss, trailing stems with leaves as broad and soft as folded cloth. Plants spill over parapets and stop just short of the ground. Some have translucent bladders at the bases of their leaves, faintly green and firm to the touch. Others curl pale hairs around any cold surface, including the water jars. The inhabitants gently unwind these hairs each morning.

Most roots never enter soil. They follow cracks into the cliff, or disappear beneath roofs, or wrap themselves around the old brackets of balconies. Under each garden is a dry brown fringe of discarded leaves. Nothing there smells rotten. The cold keeps decay slow, and whatever falls is soon carried away.

At first the town appears almost unoccupied.

There might be one person crouched beside a cistern, another crossing the hall with a bundle of trimmed stems. No smoke rises. The doors are dark. There is no conversation, no hammering, not even the usual small commotion of birds settling on roofs.

Then a strip of moss lifts its head.

It is an animal lying flat along a sill, with six small feet tucked beneath it and a green coat arranged in overlapping fans. It opens a mouth the color of unglazed clay, closes it, and moves on. Where it had been, the stone is covered with minute pale creatures, each carrying a grain of something between its forelegs.

Once you have seen those, you begin to see the rest.

The roof turf is crowded with low, blunt-eared grazers. They part the foliage with their noses and browse the undersides of leaves, leaving the upper surfaces intact. Along the gutters, narrow animals advance belly-up, their claws hooked over the rims. What looked like a cord securing a shutter is a chain of sleeping bodies, each one holding the next by the tail. The black specks in the wall joints have articulated legs. A long white thing hangs behind a curtain of fern, slowly transferring itself from one stalk to another.

There are animals everywhere, but they occupy the town with extraordinary exactness. Each holds to its ledge, route, thickness of shade. The open spaces remain open.

Across the hall, a procession of small shell-backed creatures is passing through a groove in the paving. There are so many that the groove seems to contain a moving rope. They travel beneath the lowest step, emerge beside the granary, and climb a wall in three evenly spaced lines. No one steps on them. A woman carrying an empty basket waits with one foot raised until a gap comes, then finishes her stride.

This is one of the local habits that takes time to understand: people are seldom still, but they often wait in the middle of movements.

A hand rests beside a latch until something withdraws from the hole. A bowl remains tilted until the little scavengers have left its rim. A doorway is crossed only after a shadow has finished passing over the lintel, though the animal casting it may be on the far side of the house. The town’s routines have been fitted around these passages so closely that there is no obvious boundary between tending the settlement and accommodating its wildlife.

The silence is not the silence of inactivity. It is a silence made by soft feet, wet mouths, wing membranes, and the careful use of wood.

The birds here—if they are birds—have no visible beaks. Their faces are buried in a fine nap of pale feathers, and they feed by pressing those faces against the flowers. They travel in short, buoyant flights, often sideways, steering between the hanging gardens. Hundreds may be aloft at once. Against the dim sky they look like scraps shaken from a blanket. Their wings make no more sound than a sleeve passing close to your ear.

Below them, predators move through the vegetation.

One waits beneath a leaf so thinly that its body is visible as a network of dark lines. Another resembles a length of root until it unfastens itself and reaches across an open gap. The grazers know about these things. They do not scatter; they fold their ears and slowly change places, turning their blunt shoulders outward. Sometimes a leaf jerks. Sometimes a space opens in a cluster of animals and closes again. There is almost never a cry.

The strangest movement belongs to the stone.

The cleft is still widening. Not quickly enough to watch directly, but quickly enough that a person who lives here must account for it. Doorframes are not mortared to the walls. Bridges across the narrowest fissures rest loose at one end. The roofless hall has been built in separate sections, and its steps do not quite line up.

Each morning, someone measures three cracks with a set of tapered wooden slips. The slips are hung afterward on hooks beside the granary, so everyone can see how far they entered. A wider crack may mean a new pocket of growing space. It may mean that a gutter must be lengthened. Occasionally it means an entire staircase will be dismantled before evening.

The fresh stone exposed in these seams is a faint pink, almost flesh-colored. Within hours, tiny animals gather on it. They stand head-down in dense rows, touching it with the fringes beneath their mouths. Behind them comes a sheen of green. By the next day the sheen has thickened; by the next week roots are following the fracture inward.

No one has seen what the animals eat there. The people call it grazing, just as they do on the roofs.

There are places where the stone has moved apart faster than the town can mend itself. Near the gate, a stair ends above a dark slit scarcely wider than a hand. A single slab has been laid across it, with a low rail of knotted rope. Looking down, you see several older crossings beneath the present one: wood, then stone, then a pale beam wrapped in roots. The slit goes deeper than the town’s foundations. Cool air rises from it in slow pulses.

When that air arrives, everything changes direction.

Leaves turn their dull undersides outward. The suspended white animals draw themselves into compact knots. The shell-backed procession pauses, and a second procession begins on the opposite edge of the groove. Roof grazers lift their heads. From beneath every eave, wings unfold.

For perhaps half a minute the whole settlement is moving at once. Plants tighten around their supports; animals transfer from stone to leaf, from leaf to roof, from roof into the dim air. The person at the cistern lays a palm over the mouth of the jar. Fine droplets form along the rope railing, each briefly catching what little light there is.

Then the breath from below weakens. The town settles into a different arrangement.

A patch of wall that was bare is now furred with resting bodies. A garden has drawn itself up enough to reveal a small window. Three pale flying creatures cling to the woman’s basket, and she carries them with her into the granary without looking down.

The only sound is a wooden gutter shifting on its peg.

Toward evening, the strip of sky pales rather than darkens. Light leaves the lower walls first, and the gardens lose their separate colors. Their fullness becomes a depth around the edges of the square. Small eyes remain visible in it, some reflecting the sky, others a muted green light from the open seams.

The inhabitants put flat stones over their jars. They bring in the measuring slips and brush the door sills with bundles of stiff grass. The brushing is gentle. It clears a space just wide enough for a door to close.

Afterward, the hall is empty. Its broad, shallow steps descend through the dusk, carefully swept and almost perfectly dry. Under the lowest one, the living rope continues to pass.
