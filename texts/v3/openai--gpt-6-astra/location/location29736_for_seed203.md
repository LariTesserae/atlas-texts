At first the brightness makes it difficult to understand that you are underground.

There is no dark roof overhead. There is a white distance, faintly ribbed, with brighter places too broad to look at directly. Light comes through it as through the flesh of a leaf held against the sun. It falls without warmth. Your breath clouds in front of you, and the cloud is one of the few things close enough to give the air a scale.

Underfoot, the ground is pale green, hard, and minutely grained. It takes a knife mark but not a bootprint. Where it has broken, the edges are sharp enough to cut leather, exposing layer after layer of compacted fiber. The lowest visible layers are gray. The uppermost are almost translucent. In some of the fractures, a bead of sap has frozen before it could fall.

There is no stone in sight.

The ground is a living rind stretched across a hollow beneath a larger hollow. The distant ridges are folds in it. Those high, straight cliffs, with their apparent strata and ledges, are the edges of leaves grown to a thickness of several hundred feet. Beyond them lie other folds, other basins, long bright expanses that disappear into the curvature of the chamber. A person can spend a lifetime traveling through this place without reaching a root that touches actual earth.

Most of it is empty.

This is what the eye keeps failing to accept. Everything solid is vegetable, but there is very little solid compared with the volume of cold air. A stem may rise alone out of a plain and continue upward until its upper branches become a faint discoloration in the white ceiling. Between that stem and the next are distances in which whole countries could fit. No canopy crowds the middle air. No tangle fills the floor. Growth has gone into surfaces, spars, partitions: the least material that will hold the greatest space.

Across those spaces, movement is almost always visible somewhere.

A ledge is slowly tilting. A far wall is whitening as its outer skin stretches. Above a ridge, something like a bank of cloud is unfolding, but it keeps the same hard creases as it expands. It is a new membrane, opening into the chamber.

The place is young in a way that has little to do with age. Its structures may be ancient, but most of its exposed surfaces have only recently come into existence. There are few rounded edges. A valley opens when two sheets pull apart; the walls retain matching teeth and torn strands. A plateau rises in a single cold season, lifted by growth underneath, and splits into angular slabs before anything can weather it smooth. The wind has not had time to wear these features down. There is scarcely enough loose grit for it to try.

Instead, the wind carries ribbons.

They are shed skins, thin enough to pass light and long enough to bridge ravines. They travel high in the chamber, twisting into cables, opening again, flashing white as they turn. Sometimes one catches on a solitary stalk. Over days, more collect there. The stalk acquires a streaming crown, leans, and eventually comes free of the floor.

From far away, its fall is silent. You watch it cross an enormous arc. A pale disturbance spreads where it lands. Much later, the ground gives a small movement under your feet.

Close sounds are slight and distinct: the tick of frost breaking inside a stem, a dry membrane dragging over another, the little tearing noise made by a root hair withdrawing into its socket. Loud events happen, but usually somewhere beyond the reach of their own sound. The scale makes a quiet country of a violent one.

Water is concentrated in particular places. Most surfaces are dry enough to squeak under a glove. Moisture escapes through narrow breathing seams when pressure rises inside the living mass. For a few hours a seam exhales mist. Frost builds along both lips, and droplets collect on anything held in the plume. Then the lips close, cutting through their own ice, and the air becomes clear again.

Below some seams are shallow cups worn less by water than by repeated thawing. They hold a finger’s depth of dark liquid beneath a skin of ice. Fine red rootlets line the cups. At the first touch of a shadow, the rootlets contract, drawing the water down. Travelers approach with their vessels already extended so that the vessel’s shadow falls elsewhere.

Rain is rarer. It forms after a large rupture exposes wet tissue to the chamber. For a day, perhaps two, vapor spreads beneath the bright roof. Cold currents gather it into a moving bank, and rain falls in a narrow, slanting territory. The drops are very small. They freeze on high ground, soak into lower ground, and leave almost nothing behind except a darker stripe and a brief increase in growth.

People follow reports of these rains. So do the animals.

The animals are few enough that their tracks become landmarks. A trail of paired punctures may run for miles along a ridge, made by a white, six-legged grazer whose feet can grip the slickest rind. It feeds on the tender lips of new fractures. Its mouthparts are black; the rest of it is easily lost against the landscape. You are more likely to find its droppings, little bundles of neatly clipped fiber, than the animal itself.

Near the wet seams live smaller creatures, flat and silver, which shelter between layers of peeling skin. When a seam begins to close, they emerge all at once and run downwind. Their appearance is a useful warning. Whatever opens behind them may do so violently.

The light is carried down from above through enormous upright bundles. In a broken bundle, each fiber shines at its cut end. Intact, the fibers branch into the roof and feed its luminous cells, spreading daylight across an area far larger than the opening that admitted it. Somewhere overhead there must be a surface forest gathering the sun, but nobody here has seen its shape.

There is a night, though it is never quite dark. The ceiling fades from white to a cold greenish silver. Stored light remains in the oldest tissues. The newest growth goes black first, so after dusk every fresh seam becomes visible, an intricate map of where the world is least secure.

The scattered buildings stand on those older tissues.

They are low shelters assembled from cut ribs and overlapping bark plates, usually no more than three or four together. Their weight is spread on broad runners rather than fixed foundations. A house must be dragged when the ground begins to curl. The largest possessions are sledges. Doorways are small, tools are short, and every household keeps wedges hammered from dense black heartwood.

Freshly cut material is dangerous to build with. It may thicken, bend toward light, or join itself to whatever it touches. Builders season their beams in racks suspended above the ground, scraping off buds until the wood has stopped trying. Even then, a roof occasionally produces a blade in spring, and someone must climb up to sever it before it opens.

Beside abandoned shelters, ladders lead to ledges that are no longer there. Pegs survive high on sheer faces, marking the level of a former floor. A line of carved direction signs may turn upward into a folded wall. Routes are recorded by the character of the tissue—its grain, its color after nightfall, the spacing of its breathing pores—because a route’s shape cannot be trusted.

The most settled places are not the flattest. They are the thick, dull islands where growth has nearly ceased. Their surfaces are scuffed by generations of sledges. Under a hand, they feel less cold than the young plains. People sleep there while the bright country moves around them.

Out on the new ground, change can come without much warning.

A pressure line travels beneath the surface, lifting a low ridge as it goes. Ahead of it, small flakes stand upright. A basin tightens and expels its air in a wind that knocks you down. Far overhead, a sheet detaches from the ceiling and begins a descent that may take hours. It billows across half the visible sky. Sunlight passes through it, turning the ground briefly green.

There is time to watch, sometimes time to leave. Not always time to understand which part is moving.

Afterward, the air fills with bright scraps. They settle on the sharp new terrain, trembling in the last currents. The wound exhales moisture. Ice grows along it. From sheltered crevices, the pale grazers come to feed.

And under the old island where the houses stand, a fresh line appears: hair-thin, green, scarcely visible in the abundant light.
