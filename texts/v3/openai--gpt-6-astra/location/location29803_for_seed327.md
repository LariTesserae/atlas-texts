The place is a hollow in the lip of a mountain, high enough that the weather usually arrives from below.

It is no larger than a small hamlet might have been: a curved floor of pale rock, a rear wall leaning slightly overhead, and an open side beyond which there is nothing close enough to judge distance by. Other summits stand across the gulf. On clear mornings their snowfields look almost within reach, until a bird crosses toward them and dwindles to a point long before it has crossed even a fraction of the intervening air.

Most of the hollow is empty. You can stand at its entrance and see every stone large enough to sit on. A shallow runnel crosses the floor. There is a low shelter against the back wall and, near the outer edge, a single upright slab with a hole worn through its upper corner. Between these things lies a broad, bare expanse of rock, smooth enough to carry the movement of clouds in its wet sheen.

Life occupies the margins.

Along the rear wall, where the mountain leaks, vegetation forms a deep green seam. Moss fills the joints in swollen rolls. Small ferns hang from cracks, their new fronds curled tightly against the cold. There are mats of glossy leaves, miniature willows with trunks as thick as a thumb, and flowering cushions so densely packed that rain stands on them in trembling beads rather than sinking through. Everything grows low. Even the oldest shrubs spread sideways, making little forests beneath which beetles travel through a brown, sheltered twilight.

From the middle of the hollow, this growth appears as a narrow border. Kneel beside it and it becomes an inhabited country.

Water is always passing through, although there is almost nowhere for it to gather. It emerges from the back wall in dark patches, swells into drops beneath overhangs, then runs through grooves whose edges have been rounded by generations of thaw. The stone is veined with harder material. Where the softer rock has worn away, those veins stand up like the ribs of a worn palm.

A few basins hold water no deeper than a hand. Their bottoms are crowded with reddish larvae and tiny pale snails. When the sun reaches them, insects begin to rise almost at once. Their wings catch the light, and the birds come down from the cliff.

The birds are the loudest living things here, but they do not have the place to themselves.

Small dark animals live beneath the root mats and in the rubble at either end of the ledge. They emerge with scraps of grass in their mouths, freeze with their ears turned toward the open air, and vanish so abruptly that the grass seems to have fallen into a crack. Their hay stores give the sheltered crevices a warm, dry smell. There are moths thick-bodied enough to resemble bits of fur, flies that walk across cold puddles, and spiders whose webs are built sideways under stones. A long-bodied brown hunter slips along the wall at intervals, inspecting each entrance. Its passage sends a wave of whistles through the vegetation.

Overhead, white-rumped birds wheel in groups. Some nest in the ceiling of the hollow; others occupy holes farther down the precipice. They arrive from below at full speed, rise past the rim, and fold themselves into the cliff. The air may look vacant for several minutes, then suddenly fill with bodies.

Their calls strike the back wall and return altered. A single cry becomes three. Wingbeats make a surprisingly heavy sound beneath the overhang, like cloth being snapped out.

And under all of this is the wind.

It does not blow steadily across the hollow. It climbs the mountain face, catches beneath the projecting lip, and rolls inward. Near the ground you may feel almost nothing while the taller flower stalks bend in one direction and the hanging ferns stream in another. At the outer edge, the wind pulls tears from your eyes. Two steps back, it merely lifts the hair at your wrists.

When a cloud presses against the cliff below, the hollow begins to hum. Air moves through narrow passages in the rock, through old fracture lines and the spaces behind loosened slabs. The deepest note is felt through the soles before it is heard. Higher tones wander over it, thin and wavering, sometimes disappearing into the birds’ cries. Conversation becomes difficult. A person a few paces away must turn their whole face toward you to make out words.

The little shelter has been built where that sound is least severe.

Its walls are dry-laid stone, shoulder-high, with a roof of flat slabs resting on short, crooked poles. The wood must have been carried a long way uphill. Each pole has been trimmed with a small blade, and several are bound where they split. Old fiber cord remains tucked beneath the lashings, replaced in places by strips of hide.

Inside are two sleeping hollows, a soot-blackened cooking stone, and a shallow wooden bowl turned upside down. The doorway faces sideways, away from both the falling water and the worst of the wind. At its threshold, hundreds of soles have worn a slight depression in the rock.

There is no village beyond it. The path enters through a cleft, skirts the moss, and ends at the perforated slab.

A rope once passed through that hole. Its movement polished the lower edge smooth, but the rope is gone. From the shelter there is no sign of what it served. Only at the brink can you see a succession of narrow footholds descending diagonally beneath the ledge, each briefly visible before the cliff folds them out of sight.

In sunshine, the place is bright and plainly colored. Pale stone, black wet seams, green leaves, the yellow interiors of flowers. The sky has a deep, clean blue. Shadows beneath the roof slabs are sharp enough to conceal small animals completely. Yet warmth remains shallow: a sunlit rock may warm your palm while the water running beside it numbs your fingers.

Afternoon often brings the cloud upward.

First the distant summits lose their bases. Then loose strands of mist rise beyond the rim, moving fast, like torn wool drawn past an opening. Droplets collect on the underside of every leaf. The birds drop out of sight. For a little while the hollow seems suspended over a luminous white surface.

Then the cloud enters.

The rear wall fades. The shelter becomes a dark patch. Water begins to fall from places that were dry all morning, and the upright slab sings through its empty hole.

At dusk the cloud may drain away again, revealing a gulf filled with copper light. Everything on the ledge is wet. The little animals come out to rearrange their spoiled hay. Birds crowd the nesting cracks, quarreling over space. The runnel carries torn petals toward the edge, where each one hesitates on the water’s lip before being lifted away.

Long after sunlight has left the hollow, it remains on the peaks opposite. You can sit in the shelter doorway and watch it climb them, leaving one ridge after another cold and blue, while beneath your feet the mountain goes on sounding.
