The opening is about sixty paces across, shallow as the impression left by a hand on a cushion. Nothing in it rises much above your ankle. The surrounding willows lean away from its center, so that the sky seems wider here than it does anywhere along the approach: a pale, ordinary sky, with enough sunlight to give every small leaf its own shadow.

There is no bare ground.

What looks at first like earth is a close skin of plants, dull olive and brown from a distance, intricately green when you crouch. Moss grows through prostrate heather. Fine grasses lie along the surface rather than standing upright. Between them, creeping stems have rooted at every joint, making a fabric too continuous to lift. Even the little depressions where an animal has stepped are lined with growth. Under that living layer lies more vegetable matter, compressed and darkened, then older peat, then peat so old and firm that a heel scarcely dents it.

The place is almost empty above the surface. You can see across it without finding anything for your eye to stop on. The vegetation has occupied the ground completely and left the air alone.

It is cool enough that your fingers become stiff if you stay still. Moisture gathers on the undersides of leaves, out of the light. The exposed moss has dry tips, but part it with a fingertip and the stems beneath are cold and wet. Rain comes often enough to keep this lower world alive, not often enough to leave standing water. There is one dark pocket near the willow margin where you could collect a palmful by pressing down. Once released, the moss slowly takes it back.

The opening occupies a slight hollow in a very old lowland. Whatever once gave the country its relief has been worn down almost to a level. There are no steep slopes nearby, no fresh banks, no stones with broken faces. The few changes in height are so gradual that you notice them through your calves before you see them. Here, a difference of a few inches decides which moss grows, where a vole keeps its tunnel, how long the morning damp remains.

The peat conceals what is left of the underlying landscape. Roots hold its upper layers together; low stems interrupt the wind. Nothing moves quickly enough to cut a channel. When rain is heavy, the water spreads through the plant skin and leaves without making an edge.

Yet the opening is loud.

Beyond the willows, out of sight, a mill runs continuously. You cannot tell how far away it is. Its fans make a broad rushing sound that fills the clearing from rim to rim, and underneath that comes the lower, slightly uneven note of machinery turning under load. Occasionally a pressure valve releases with a long exhalation. The noise has no visible source here. There are no buildings, wires, paths, or discarded objects in the opening; nothing to connect the clipped leaves and tiny moss heads with the working machinery. The sound arrives over the trees and stays in the air.

After a while you begin to hear the animals through it.

A wren sings from the willows with startling force. Another answers from almost exactly the opposite side. Starlings work over the short turf, each keeping a few body lengths from its neighbors, probing, pausing, taking three quick steps. A blackbird turns over a loose flap of dead heather and exposes a pale commotion beneath it. Everything there retreats into the interlaced stems.

Most of the animal life occupies the thickness of the ground cover. Small spiders hang in gaps no wider than a thumbnail. Beetles travel beneath the dry upper tips of the moss. Springtails gather where moisture films the darker stems. Voles have made passages through the vegetation without exposing the peat below, so that a moving animal is sometimes visible only as a brief lift in the surface.

There are many mouths, many feet, very little displacement.

At the margin, the willows are old but not tall. Their trunks have settled sideways, rooted where they touched down, and sent up fresh shoots. Some of the wood is hollow. Some has softened until roots from the same tree pass through it. It is difficult to decide where one individual ends. The ring is continually replacing small parts of itself while remaining much the same height, with the same gaps through which birds come and go.

The opening does not seem to be waiting to become woodland. Browsing animals nip the willow seedlings. The exposed center is colder at night than the sheltered rim, and its closely woven surface gives few new roots an easy entrance. A twig may take hold, survive several seasons, and disappear. The low plants close over the spot.

There is no single ancient tree to give the place an age. Its age is distributed through the worn hollow, the buried plant layers, the sideways growth of the willows. Most of what you can touch is young. Nearly everything occupies a position that has been occupied before.

By afternoon, a little of the moisture has left the upper leaves. Their color lightens. The starlings depart and others arrive. A vole clears a blocked passage under the heather. Above them the mill continues its broad, unvarying rush, loud enough to obscure your breathing.

You can stand in the center and turn all the way around. Low green surface, low tangled margin, open sky. Nothing monumental, nothing exposed. Beneath your shoes, living stems flex against the accumulated remains of other stems, and recover when you move on.
