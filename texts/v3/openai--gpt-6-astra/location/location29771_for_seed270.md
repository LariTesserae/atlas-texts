Behind the lowest bearing of the mill, where the iron housing stops just short of the wall, there is a pale hollow no wider than two hands.

You could pass it every day without noticing it. From the aisle it looks like a shadow under a machine, edged with spilled flour. But daylight enters through a low window opposite, and at certain hours it reaches almost to the back. Then the hollow shows its distances: a white foreground, a crowded middle ground of gray chips and amber husks, and a rear boundary where the limestone curves down to meet its own debris.

Nothing stands very high. The largest rise is a stone flake scarcely thicker than a thumbnail. Even this has a rounded summit and slopes softened by powder. The whole place is low, worn, close to its floor.

The hollow is older than the mill. It was already present in the block when the masons brought it here, a shallow weathering pocket in limestone that had spent a long time exposed on a bare upland. Its little ridges were once the edges of shells. Their projecting parts have gone; what remains is a subdued pattern of crescents and dimples, almost level with the surrounding stone. The mill has added scratches, an oil stain, a few grains of rust. These look recent even where they are a century old.

Inside, it is cool enough that the iron gathers the chill of the building overnight and holds it well into the working day. There is no damp smell. No moss grows at the back, no green touches the windowward edge. The flour stays loose except where animals have bound it with silk or pressed it into paths. A shed wing left on the floor keeps its shape until something eats the joints or the vibration rubs it apart.

At first the surface appears to be trembling.

Then individual movements become visible.

A beetle pushes through a drift, lifting a low wave of flour ahead of its head. Three smaller beetles follow the trough it leaves. A pale larva withdraws into a tube beneath a scale of stone. Mites cross the exposed limestone so thickly that their paths overlap in every direction; in the brightest patch they are almost invisible, disclosed chiefly by the tiny shadows running beside them.

Under every chip there are more.

They occupy the seams between flour grains, the curled undersides of moth wings, the hollow legs of dead insects. They ride on one another. A beetle pausing beside the iron foot has moving specks lodged around its leg sockets, passengers waiting for it to reach another deposit of food. Along its back, flour has settled into the grooves of the wing cases. It looks dusted and elderly, though it may have emerged only days ago.

The food arrives from above.

Some falls continuously, too fine to see until it catches the window light. Some comes in sudden deliveries when a conveyor jolts or a sack is dropped on the floor overhead. Then the hollow has weather: a brief white descent that buries the smallest tracks and changes the outlines of the slopes. Beetles come up through it. Larvae remain below. Mites disappear under the new layer and begin finding their way back to its surface.

There are lean places despite this abundance. Beneath the bearing’s overhang, the falling flour seldom reaches the ground. Here the stone is exposed, and the animals crossing it move quickly. A small spider has stretched threads between a rust blister and a broken grain husk. The web is low, scarcely a canopy at all. Its lower strands catch the legs of things walking beneath it.

Farther back, another spider lives inside the empty casing of a moth pupa. Only its forelegs usually show.

Nothing edible stays unclaimed for long. A dead beetle may remain recognizable for weeks, but its interior is soon occupied. The hard shell becomes a shelter, then a brittle obstacle, then a scattering of curved plates. There are places where several generations of these plates lie nested together. Moving through them produces a faint dry crackle, inaudible while the mill is running.

The mill is almost always running.

Sound enters the hollow through the stone before it enters through the air. A heavy, repeating knock comes up from the foundation. Over it lies the close growl of the bearing, and over that the hiss of belts and grain passing through machinery. There is a loose part somewhere beyond the wall that strikes twice, pauses, and strikes twice again. Every exposed husk shivers with it.

The noise has consequences. Fine material moves a little downhill with each cycle. Grains lodge against other grains, hold for an hour, then slide. Along the iron foot, vibration has sorted the debris into bands: coarse fragments nearest the metal, finer meal beyond, and at the outer edge a smooth apron of dust.

The animals know these bands through their bodies. A larva anchors itself with silk before feeding. A beetle wedges its feet against opposing grains when the heavier knock comes. The spiders keep to attachment points that move together; a web fastened across the wrong gap is pulled apart within a shift.

Occasionally the rollers stop.

The change is enormous. In the sudden quiet, the hollow seems briefly larger. You can hear a beetle scrape beneath a flake and the minute ticking of something chewing inside a husk. A settling grain strikes another grain. From outside comes the ordinary sound of a person walking across the room.

Some animals stop with the machinery. Others emerge almost at once. A narrow ant trail forms along the rear wall, where the smooth limestone gives good footing. The ants have a way out through a mortar crack beyond the hollow. They return carrying fragments of insects, flour-packed pellets, sometimes a living larva that bends and catches on every irregularity. The traffic crowds at the crack. Returning workers climb over those coming out.

When the machinery starts again, the trail breaks into isolated runners.

There are only a few constructed things within the hollow itself. A short length of copper wire lies half buried near the center. It has gone dark except on its upper curve, where passing bodies and shifting grit have kept a thin bright line. Beside it is a square flake of old red paint. A brass washer leans against the back wall, sunk almost to its hole in compacted meal.

The washer makes the deepest shelter in the place. Its underside is packed with larvae and shed skins. At its rim, a moth has spun a cocoon incorporating flour, fragments of legs, and a single red grain of paint. The cocoon resembles the surrounding debris so closely that its opening is easier to find than its outline.

A moth emerging there must crawl outward before it can spread its wings. Some reach the open aisle. Others turn toward the light and become caught beneath the bearing lip, where their wings brush powder from the iron. There is a thin cemetery along that edge, but even there the ground is busy. Scavengers enter from below. Predators wait where struggling bodies make the flour shake.

By afternoon, the illuminated strip has moved toward the rear. The limestone is plainly visible now: cream-colored, finely pitted, without a sharp edge anywhere. A small procession of beetles crosses an ancient shell impression. Beneath them, mites spill into the depressions and climb out again. Near the front, fresh flour advances over yesterday’s paths.

The bearing warms slightly. The ants retreat deeper into the wall. A larva cuts loose a piece of silk that has become choked with powder and begins another tube beside it.

At the mouth of the hollow, one flour grain balances on the rounded lip. Each knock of the mill turns it a little. Below it, in the narrow space between stone and iron, bodies continue to pass in both directions.
