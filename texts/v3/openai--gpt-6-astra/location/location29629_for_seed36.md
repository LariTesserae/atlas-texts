The way down is a split between two roots, broad enough to enter without turning your shoulders. Their exposed surfaces are pale and warm, with the faint grain of stems peeled before they have hardened. For the first few steps there is soil underfoot. Then the soil thins, becomes a brown dust caught in creases, and ends. Below it, everything is growing from everything else.

The hollow extends for miles.

You see it first from a ledge of folded tissue: a low country under the ground, with broad basins, close-set columns, ridges that overlap into the distance. The far side disappears in brightness. Overhead, the underside of the surface forms a continuous canopy of roots and translucent plates. These plates fit together loosely, like the overlapping scales of a bud. Daylight enters through them and spreads sideways through their flesh, so that the ceiling shines almost everywhere. The thickest parts are amber. The thin parts are a white-green too bright to look at for long.

There are shadows, but they are full of reflected light. Under a ledge, your hands turn green.

The heat is close and steady. It comes from the floor as much as from above. A knee pressed into the ground feels the warmth through clothing; a palm laid against a column finds the same temperature. There is very little movement in the air. Smells remain local: bruised leaf in one narrow passage, something like warmed figs beside a wall, a dry, peppery scent across a high saddle. You can step out of one scent into another.

From the ledge, the country looks wooded. On reaching the lower ground, that distinction becomes difficult to maintain. The apparent trunks broaden into buttresses, and the buttresses flatten into the floor. What looked like low vegetation is the finely divided surface of that floor: thousands of short, fleshy blades, each with a clear margin. Beneath them is a springy layer of tightly compressed stems. Farther down, visible where the ground parts at a seam, are folded sheets of yellow-white tissue.

There is no stone in reach.

The ridges have the rounded outlines of hills that have not yet been lifted into place. Some end abruptly in smooth lobes, their undersides suspended just above the neighboring basin. Others carry thin vertical fins, tender-looking and improbably tall. Nothing has chipped them. No sediment has accumulated at their feet. Their edges retain the delicate irregularity of something newly unfolded.

Yet they do not unfold.

A curl at the end of a ridge hangs in the same position year after year. The surfaces have the freshness of new growth without its haste. There are no dead leaves, no weathered husks, no succession of small plants struggling into the gaps left by larger ones. Even the narrowest spaces are occupied, and every occupant seems to have reached an exact accommodation with its neighbors.

The whole hollow has the appearance of a place a few days short of becoming a landscape. Those few days never arrive.

Water is present in small, definite holdings. In the basins, the living ground draws inward to form cups, some no larger than a footprint, some wide enough to lie down in. Their rims are firm and polished. The water inside is clear, warm, and shallow; beneath it, green ribs converge toward a dark central pore.

Between these pools are long stretches of dry footing. The surface there has a waxy bloom that leaves a pale mark on a fingertip. Thin leaves rise edge-on from it, reducing the area exposed to the luminous ceiling. In the damper hollows, leaves lie flat and overlap so densely that their boundaries resemble the scales of an enormous fish.

Along one descending seam, moisture beads on the underside of a root. The droplets are large and evenly spaced. Each trembles when you pass, disturbed by the small current your body makes, but none falls. Beneath them is a channel ready for water: a winding groove, gently graded, empty from its beginning to its end.

There are whole systems like this. A spillway waiting above a lower pool. A fan of folded fronds enclosing a space they would fill if they opened. Pale root tips poised a finger’s breadth from the floor. The country is full of preparations that have become permanent features.

Travel follows the spaces between them. These routes are often narrow at ground level, with foliage brushing both shoulders, then open without warning into courts of white light. From such a court you can look through successive ranks of pillars and see other bright clearings, other low ridges, the whole interior densely divided but continuous. Distance is measured in glimpses.

Here and there stand small enclosures.

They occupy the dry rises, usually alone. Each is made of several living uprights joined overhead, with thin sheets drawn between them. The openings are approximately the height of a person. Inside, the floor rises around the edges into smooth shelves. Some have a second, smaller chamber behind the first; one has a shallow basin beside its entrance.

They are convincing enough as shelters that you lower your voice when approaching them.

Up close, there are no bindings, cuts, or tool marks. The sheets join the uprights through the same fine branching veins that cross the surrounding leaves. Nothing suggests they were assembled. Still, the openings face the easiest approaches, and the shelves are at a comfortable sitting height. Their interiors smell faintly sweet and offer a little relief from the glare.

No paths are worn into their thresholds.

Animals are scarce. On the underside of a shelf, you may find a pale creature with six bent legs and a body like a closed seedpod. Its feet fit into tiny depressions in the tissue. It remains motionless while you watch. Elsewhere, a narrow-backed lizard rests in the crotch of two stems, its skin the dusty green of the dry floor. Its throat moves once, then becomes still again.

There is no calling, no wingbeat overhead, no rustle advancing through the growth. Your own passage supplies most of the sound: the soft compression under each step, clothing drawn across a leaf, breath made conspicuous by the heat. If you stop, a faint creak can sometimes be heard from deep within a column. It is the small sound of weight being held.

From a high fold near the middle of the hollow, the scattered enclosures are visible among the pale ridges. Warm pools shine in the low places. The ceiling has no single opening through which to locate the sun. Light lies over the entire country with the even force of noon.

You can sit there for a long time. Nothing droops in the heat. No shadow travels far enough to notice. The unfinished hills remain soft-edged and immaculate, each carrying its burden of leaves, each fitted closely against the next.

Beside your foot, a new leaf stands half released from its sheath. Its exposed tip is almost transparent. Through it you can see the tiny channels already branching, the thickening midrib, the folded green surface waiting within.

There is no room for it to open. There is no sign that this troubles the plant.
