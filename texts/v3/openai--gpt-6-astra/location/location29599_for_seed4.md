The place is underneath a fold of living bark, just above the waterline.

From outside, there is almost nothing to find: a seam in a swollen marsh root, a little black moisture at its lower edge. The root has lifted during the first hard cold, opening a space no larger than a loosely closed hand. The surrounding sedge leans over it. Water beads on the sedge, freezes at the tips, and remains liquid farther down, where the plants keep a small, stubborn warmth.

Inside the fold is a town.

Its ground is pale inner bark, although there is too little light to see the color. It rises in three shallow terraces around a central hollow. Nothing has had time to weather. The edges of the terraces are clean and curled, like the edges of something peeled; thin fibers still bridge the gaps where the surfaces have drawn apart. Here and there a fresh split gives off the green, bitter smell of broken stems.

There is no stone. What feels like stone underfoot is a hardened root node. What looks, in the rare light from the entrance, like a bank of clay is densely packed pith. Even the black bottom of the central pool is alive.

The town occupies the upper terrace and two sheltered corners. Most of the chamber remains open.

This is important. An empty stretch of bark may swell overnight. A wall may begin to draw inward. The inhabitants leave broad distances between their buildings, and lay no road that cannot be lifted in sections. Across the central hollow there are only three bridges, each made of paired grass fibers, knotted at one end and looped at the other so that it can slip without snapping.

The buildings are little collars woven around upright root hairs. Their roofs are overlapping flakes cut from the dry outer bark and carried inside. Some have a second story, hung below the first rather than raised above it: a basket room suspended over damp air. The largest structure is a storehouse with six such rooms descending toward the pool. Its supports creak as the root drinks.

Everything is tied, wedged, scraped, or patiently persuaded to grow in the required direction.

The inhabitants work with split thorns and the sharpened rims of seed cases. Their cord is stripped from the chamber itself. To make a doorway, they insert a forked sliver into a young crease and return each day to turn it. To close one, they remove the sliver and keep the edges wet. There are older doorways here that have sealed so neatly that only their thresholds remain.

The town is young, but some of its tools are not. A polished thorn can outlast several chambers.

Cold lies most heavily in the central hollow. There, condensed water gathers on the ceiling fibers until a drop grows large enough to pull them down. For a long time it hangs within reach of the pool, joined to the roof by a trembling neck. Then it falls.

The impact is the loudest ordinary event in the town.

A low, soft knock travels through the living floor. Bridge tenders feel it in their feet. Workers pause with their tools against the walls. Along the water’s edge, whole crowds withdraw into seams, and a few moments later emerge again.

The pool is narrow enough to cross with a jump, but nobody jumps it. Its surface is much harder to escape than it appears. Loose fibers lie across it in deliberate patterns, maintained as rescue lines. At one end, a comb of fine rootlets draws water into a channel cut along the lower terrace. The channel supplies three drinking places and turns beneath the storehouse, where its flow keeps the hanging rooms cold.

Excess water leaves through the entrance seam. When the marsh outside rises, the direction reverses.

There are flood marks on the walls, made not by sediment but by little rows of abandoned footholds.

For all its open space, the chamber contains an almost unmanageable quantity of animal life.

Small translucent grazers occupy the undersides of the bridges. Pale, many-legged bodies lie within the roof fibers, each fitted to a passage scarcely wider than itself. Eggs are fastened behind every loose flake. The eggs have other, smaller animals moving over them. In the pool, minute swimmers gather wherever a wall releases a trace of sweetness; seen in light, they would make the water appear faintly granular.

But here they are mostly known by touch. A railing shifts beneath the hand. A damp step yields and divides into moving bodies. A strand believed to be loose cord curls away when lifted.

The citizens have six limbs, flattened heads, and broad gripping feet. They wear no coverings except the occasional harness of fiber used to haul a roof panel or a winter store. Their antennae reach ahead through the dark, touching doorframes polished by generations of the same gesture, though the doorframes themselves are only weeks old.

Other animals vastly outnumber them.

Some are tolerated because they crop the films that would otherwise clog the drains. Some are driven from the stores with bitter sap. A slow, armored creature the size of a house is allowed to pass along the lowest terrace; it shaves away dead tissue without cutting into the living floor. Its route is kept clear. When it approaches a bridge anchorage, the bridge is unhooked and drawn aside.

Predators inhabit the ceiling.

They do not need many legs to move there. Their bodies shorten, grip, lengthen. A disturbance travels down a root hair, and one of them turns toward it with a precision that seems impossible in the dark. Beneath their usual stations the streets are roofed. The uncovered crossings are brief.

Almost none of this makes a sound audible beyond the chamber. Even within it, there are long intervals when one hears only the slight tightening of fibers, or water taking air into a drain. Crowds pass as a faint dry brushing. A struggle on the ceiling may announce itself by a single falling husk.

Yet the place never quite rests.

The walls thicken. The pool rises and shrinks. Tender new rootlets push through public spaces and are guided into racks before they harden. Grazers follow the rootlets. Hunters follow the grazers. Along one terrace, a work party is moving a row of dwellings away from a swelling knot; along another, empty houses are being lowered toward a fresh opening in the floor.

There is no fire. Warmth is stored by closing rooms, packing their outer baskets with dry fiber, and gathering bodies inside. Near the entrance, frost grows around the knots of the outermost bridge. Deeper in, it melts before reaching the lashings.

Once, near midday, the sedge outside shifts enough to admit a narrow trace of light. It touches the far wall and reveals an astonishing green: not forest green, but the translucent color inside an unopened shoot. For those few moments the floor, the columns, the hanging chambers, and the curved roof can all be understood as parts of the same plant.

Then the sedge settles.

In the darkness, work resumes at the new opening. It is still too small for a body to pass through, but cool wet air comes out of it, carrying the smell of fresh pith. A worker sets a forked thorn between its edges. Another brings cord.
