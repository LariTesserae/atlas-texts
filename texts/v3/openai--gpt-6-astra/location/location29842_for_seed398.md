Near the top of the mountain, above the last place where snow can remain attached, a seam in the rock opens into a warm, dark chamber. From outside it looks no wider than a split fingernail. The wind passes over it without whistling. A little salt has collected on its lower lip.

Inside, there is room to stand.

There is also a city, although at first you see only three pale ledges and a thread of light descending through empty air.

The chamber lies within the mountain’s highest tooth. Its ceiling is the underside of the summit: less than a meter of black, freshly formed stone separates this interior from the thin, violent weather outside. The altitude is sufficient that water exposed on the outer slope does not behave consistently as water. Here, under pressure maintained by something in the walls, it gathers in ordinary-looking drops. They cling to the ceiling, swell, and fall.

Each drop is accounted for.

The floor is a shallow basin of dark glass, about the size of a small room. It has cooled recently enough to preserve the delicate folds made when its surface was still viscous. Some folds have split, exposing red heat along their roots. Between them are flat plates, sharp as broken crockery, with scarcely a grain of loose sediment anywhere. Nothing has been walked smooth. Even the oldest surfaces retain the crisp edges of things cast yesterday.

Across this small terrain runs the city’s street system.

It is difficult to recognize because the streets are not meant to support weight. They appear as hairline incisions, most of them empty, a few holding a dim silver illumination. At their intersections, narrow rectangular openings descend into the glass. There are no towers, no roofscape, no crowded accumulation of objects. Most of the architecture is the careful provision of absence: a volume cleared beneath a ledge, a shaft that passes through stone without breaking its surface, a courtyard whose far side is farther away than the chamber could possibly contain.

Lean over one of these courtyards and the heat touches your face. Far below, perhaps only a hand’s breadth below, a row of blue doorways comes on in sequence.

The city occupies very little of its own space. Entire districts consist of a single threshold and the distance reserved in front of it. That distance is expensive. You can tell by the retaining structures: fine dark braces that emerge from the rock, cross nothing visible, and disappear again. Their shadows fall in directions unrelated to the light.

One district has failed. Its courtyard has become shallow enough to touch, and the doorways at the bottom are crushed flat beneath a transparent flake. A line of amber marks surrounds it. Elsewhere, construction continues.

At the basin’s upper end, new stone grows from a hot fissure. It advances in slow, exact increments, pushing out a ridge thinner than a knife blade. The ridge is glossy at first, then dulls as its surface rearranges. Without any tool passing over it, steps appear along one side. A drainage channel sinks into the other. For several minutes the whole formation trembles with heat. Then a small opening establishes itself at its base, and a previously dark street acquires another point of light.

The mountain and the city are still making accommodations for each other.

Heat comes through the floor in patches. Beside the entrance the stone is merely warm; half a pace farther in, it would burn exposed skin. Above the fissure, the air bends so strongly that the ceiling seems to roll. Yet the chamber is dim. Most hot surfaces radiate below the range of sight, and the civic lights are weak, intended for some sensitivity other than yours. You notice them best by looking away: violet at the edge of a platform, green deep within a drain, a dull copper glow behind what might be a sealed public building.

The only daylight enters through the seam. It draws a short, pale wedge across the floor and stops before reaching the city center. Occasionally something outside eclipses it—a gust carrying ice crystals, a fragment shed by a higher face—and the whole chamber becomes briefly easier to see.

There is no soil. No moss softens the wet places. The little white fans beneath the ceiling drops are salt, brittle and translucent, and the velvet-dark coating on the lower walls is a field of microscopic heat sinks. It changes its orientation as warmth moves through the room. Seen close, the coating resembles closely packed needles; from a distance, it resembles a stain drying in reverse.

Moisture arrives irregularly. Some comes through the mountain as vapor. Some condenses when the summit cools during its long passage through shadow. A bead forms overhead, gathers enough weight, and falls toward a square depression in the floor.

Usually it misses.

The depression moves to receive it.

This adjustment is nearly impossible to watch directly. The floor offers no ripple or sliding seam. A moment ago the catchment was beneath one fold of glass; now it is beneath another, and the drop has struck its center. There is a small, intimate sound, louder than anything else in the chamber.

Water travels outward through the illuminated streets. It passes reservoirs no larger than pinholes, cools a structure under strain, deposits a few crystals at a junction. Some of it rises again through shafts as invisible vapor. Some remains in deep storage. During a wet interval, the whole city brightens slightly and the dark braces lift away from the walls, holding their empty distances more securely.

There are dry intervals long enough for several streets to go out.

No inhabitants emerge. Nothing crawls toward the water, and nothing circles the lamps. Nevertheless, certain doorways close while others open. A broad platform tilts a fraction toward a neighboring district and stays that way for an hour. Small burdens move beneath the translucent floor, visible only where they briefly occlude the glow of a street. They stop at intersections. They wait for one another to pass.

The quiet is not complete. The hot ridge ticks as it cools. A drain makes a soft inhalation. Somewhere beyond a doorway comes a low, single note, felt more readily through the soles of the feet than heard. Afterward, several lights change.

Outside, wind is stripping the summit. Its pressure reaches the chamber as a faint flexing of the ceiling. Dust falls, and the city catches that too: grains diverted into narrow slots, sorted by color or weight or some property without a visible equivalent. One red speck remains on a ledge for a long time before the ledge opens beneath it.

The entrance offers no view of the lower world. There is only a piece of dark sky, unnaturally close, and occasionally the bright edge of a cloud far beneath the mountain’s tip.

Here at the highest point, enclosed by stone still young enough to give off its making-heat, the water begins to gather again. A drop hangs directly above the unlit district. It lengthens without falling. Below it, very slowly, the amber boundary withdraws from one flattened doorway.
