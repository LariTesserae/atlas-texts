The town is low enough that a spoonful of water spilled in the square takes most of the afternoon to decide which way to go.

It travels in a widening stain over the green pavement, catches in the faint depression left by a footstep, and eventually joins one of the narrow canals. There are no curbs. Street, bank, and canal bed are different expressions of the same living tissue: dry and densely felted where people walk, slick at the waterline, translucent underneath. In the shallows you can see pale fibers tightening around the places where small fish have bitten through.

Nothing here is made of timber. Timber would imply that something had been cut down.

The whole town grows from a single low mat of vegetation, a little wider than a village, floating or rooted in a basin too old and too thoroughly filled to make the distinction useful. Its outer edge is a ragged lip of yellow-green fronds. Beyond that lies warm gray water, then other low islands, visible on clear afternoons as strokes scarcely thicker than the horizon. No hill interrupts the view. Whatever mountains once fed this basin have been reduced to ingredients.

Underfoot, there may still be mineral earth. No one needs it.

Despite the continuous vegetation, the town feels open. Its buildings stand well apart, with broad wet courts between them. A bathhouse occupies one corner of a square large enough for a market that no longer meets there. Three dwellings face a canal. A public stair ascends six steps to a platform overlooking nothing higher than a stand of reeds. Across the water, a single shade tree spreads a roof of leaves over a bench grown from its own exposed root.

There is room to see each thing.

Most surfaces are close-cropped, olive or straw-colored, with tiny leaves pressed flat against one another. From a distance they resemble worn stone. Up close, each leaf has a swollen joint and a pore that opens when your shadow crosses it. Bare feet come away damp. The walls smell faintly of warm peas, with a darker sweetness near their foundations.

A house is a thickening of the mat, lifted around an interior pocket. Its roof may be no higher than a tall person can reach. Window openings have smooth muscular rims; they adjust slowly to the sun. Indoors, the walls bear fine white root hairs that retract from hands and clothing. There are places to sit, cool niches for food, shallow basins where drinking water beads out of the surface. Rooms can be encouraged to change, though older houses have preferences. A doorway repeatedly requested in one wall may keep returning to another.

The oldest buildings have almost no distinguishing features left. Their columns have broadened into their walls, their steps have sunk, and their decorative ridges have softened until they look like folds in a sleeping animal’s skin. Yet doors still admit people. Roofs still shed rain. Beneath every softened surface, the tissues renew themselves without restoring the original sharpness. The town has retained its use and worn away its emphasis.

By late morning the air is warm enough that you feel the moisture gathering under your collar. Light is ordinary and plentiful: a pale blue sky, passing white cloud, sunshine lying flat on the canals. A few copper-colored dragonflies patrol the open courts. Water birds with blunt toes walk over the pavement, listening for movement below. Small shelled creatures cluster around the drain mouths, and in the hollow walls something scratches industriously, pauses, then starts again.

There is much more animal life than the open spaces first suggest. Every building has a submerged underside. Fish browse its hanging roots. Eels pass through the plumbing. In the bathhouse, little transparent crustaceans clean the water until someone enters, then retreat into a seam behind the lowest step. Frogs live inside the shade tree’s roof and drop into the canal during rain with soft, widely separated plops.

The loudest sound, most of the day, comes from underneath.

It begins as a rolling knock, like heavy bowls being stacked in another room. Then the ground shivers. Water jumps against the canal banks. Across the square a row of pale bladders rises from the pavement, inflates to the size of melons, and bursts in succession. The sound is startlingly hard in the open air.

This is the town adjusting its position.

It does so often.

Channels that ran east at breakfast may run west by noon. A courtyard sinks the depth of a finger and becomes a pond full of frantic insects. Elsewhere, a pond lifts, sheds its water, and offers a dry path before the last fish have finished wriggling down its escape gutters. Houses move less frequently, but their movements are more troublesome. People leave long slack loops in the lines of laundry stretched between them.

The changes are organized, though too many things are being accommodated at once for the organization to look calm. A brood of fish requires warmer shallows. One household asks for a second room. A damaged root field needs to be brought near the surface. Rain is approaching from the west. Somewhere below the public square, a transport chamber has failed to close properly around an arrival.

The town responds to all of it. Its priorities can change halfway through a movement.

There are brief collisions. Roof edges buckle against neighboring walls and withdraw, leaving wet seams. Water takes routes that were footpaths a moment earlier. You learn to watch the birds: when they all step neatly off one stretch of pavement, you step off too.

At the center stands the oldest piece of infrastructure, a shallow oval basin beneath an arch of bare green fibers. It resembles a public washstand. Its water contains the reflection of the arch and, usually, the sky.

Sometimes it contains a different sky.

A person waiting beside it may see dusk while standing in morning sunlight, or clouds moving in opposing directions at different depths. Then the surface lowers without draining. Steps unfold down into a space that should be occupied by roots, mud, and several meters of water. Someone comes up carrying a bag. Behind them there is a momentary view of another landing: dry red pavement, perhaps, or snow against a balustrade. The steps close and the local sky returns.

Arrivals leave the basin cold. For an hour afterward, its rim sweats heavily in the warm air, and fish gather beneath it.

This is why the town remains a town, despite its few houses and wide empty spaces. It has connections out of proportion to its size. Behind an unremarkable wall lies storage that no local survey has found the far end of. One small chamber holds the town’s water level steady by exchanging weight with something elsewhere. The inhabitants check its health by placing a reed across two supports. If the reed bends upward, they leave the chamber alone and notify the gardener.

Most of the machinery flowers once a year.

Its flowers are modest, greenish, easily overlooked. They must not be pollinated by the wrong insects. During that week, children are paid to sit near the infrastructure with soft brushes, shooing away beetles.

In the afternoon, rain arrives abruptly. The horizon disappears behind it; then the square turns silver. Roofs lower their edges. Drinking pores close, storm drains open, and the whole town begins to gulp.

The turbulence becomes almost festive. Canal water rushes around corners. Frogs call from cavities in the walls. Broad temporary leaves push up through the pavement to catch floating debris, each leaf shaking under the force of the rain. A footbridge untwists itself, lies flat, and becomes a spillway. People shelter in doorways, speaking loudly over the hammering on the roofs.

For a few minutes, the oldest square is a brown, churning lake.

Then the rain moves on. Sun catches the water streaming from the arch. The square rises slowly beneath the flood, and everything loose is carried toward its edges: seed husks, fallen petals, a sandal, several indignant crabs. Pale shoots emerge in the scoured patches and flatten into fresh pavement.

By evening there are open distances again, low houses reflected in quiet channels, the solitary tree dripping over its bench. A bird resumes hunting beside a drain that has moved several paces since morning. From beneath the bathhouse comes one final hollow knock.

The town settles for the night with water close under every surface. Its outlines look much as they did before the storm, and much as they did long before anyone living here was born. Only the ways through it have changed.
