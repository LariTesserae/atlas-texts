The sea is high enough that clouds pass beneath its floor.

You can see them sometimes through the shallower stone: pale weather moving far below, lightning spreading silently inside it. The rock between is worn thin and translucent, a greenish mineral with the greasy sheen of old jade. Elsewhere it is opaque, thick enough to support forests and whole provinces of warm water. No one could say whether this was once a mountain range. Nothing retains a summit. Everything that rises has been rounded, undercut, and rounded again, until the land resembles the exposed joints of something buried at continental scale.

Water covers almost all of it. It fills the saddles between the worn heights, lies in deep lenses on the broad plateaus, and travels uphill through channels polished too smooth to hold sediment. At the edges of the country it does not fall. It curls underneath, following the stone, so that a swimmer approaching the rim would find the bottom bending away and the faint light changing sides.

Down is a local condition here. Roots are a more reliable guide than bubbles.

The warmth comes from below, though below is not always where the clouds are. Put a hand against the exposed rock and it feels like a place where someone has just been sleeping. In hollows, the water is almost uncomfortably warm. Pale films gather there, fold over themselves, and lift away in sheets when the currents strengthen. Cooler streams move through them without mixing, visible by the way they draw the suspended silt into slender, trembling walls.

There are many such walls. Some are only a handspan thick; others divide regions that would take a lifetime to cross. On one side, a forest leans west. On the other, the same species grows downward into a blue depth. Between them hangs a narrow seam of perfectly still water, crowded with spores that cannot decide which current to enter.

The forests have little wood.

Their trunks are bundles of living cord, loosely twisted around channels of clearer water. They swell in the warmth and slacken during the long dim intervals. Leaves grow in shelves and ruffles, broad enough to roof a clearing, thin enough that you can see the branching shadows of leaves behind them. They are mostly olive, wine-brown, and a dull copper that brightens when turned toward the light. The brightest green occurs underneath, on the surfaces facing the stone.

On the old ridges, vegetation grows so densely that there is no open water between the canopy and the ground. There are leaves behind leaves, root curtains, floating seed veils, soft colonies spreading over dead stems. A passage that seems empty from a distance becomes, on approach, a succession of membranes. Each must be pushed aside. They close slowly behind you, trembling long after you have gone.

Yet this growth does not cover everything. Wide stone barrens interrupt the forest. Their surfaces are swept clean by patient currents, and across them lie grains too heavy or too old to be carried farther: black sand, rounded crystals, little red nodules polished to the shape of eggs. Here the size of the landscape becomes apparent. A pale rise visible beyond the nearest forest may be another ridge, or a shelf of rock several hundred miles away. The water offers no haze by which to judge. Small things sometimes seem impossibly distant. Distant things occasionally cast a shadow across your hands.

Light arrives without a visible sun. It filters down through the upper water in long, uneven seasons, gathering first along leaf edges and the lips of hollows. For a while the entire country glows faintly, as though its shapes have been traced beneath the skin of the sea. Then the light thickens overhead but never becomes bright. Looking upward, you see overlapping, slowly moving darknesses. They may be floating vegetation. They may be the undersides of other plateaus, carrying other seas.

There is rain here, though everything is submerged.

It begins with a grainy feeling against the face. Then warmer droplets fall through the surrounding water, each held intact by a delicate skin. They descend in their millions, making the forest shiver. Where they strike leaves they flatten into silver disks; where they reach the bottom they roll into hollows and gather there. For days afterward, these pools refuse to merge with the sea around them. Roots enter them cautiously. Some roots blacken and withdraw. Others branch so rapidly that a bare basin becomes a thicket before the weather changes.

The storms are nearly silent. A low pressure gathers in the ears. Leaves turn over in great districts, copper giving way to green. Loose stones creep across the barrens. Occasionally a whole bank of vegetation detaches and goes traveling, roots combing the rock, its shadow passing over valleys where nothing else has changed in centuries.

The oldest channels no longer lead anywhere. They are broad, shallow grooves through which the water continues to move because it has moved there for so long. At certain crossings, a current takes several routes at once. A drifting leaf divides without tearing, follows each channel, and arrives downstream as a single leaf bearing several different patterns of decay. The plants nearest these crossings grow thick, tangled crowns. Some of their branches are newly opened; others have already rotted back to the trunk.

Erosion continues, but almost nothing is sharp enough to lose an edge. The sea wears away differences now: a faint depression, a change in texture, the last suggestion that one shelf once stood higher than another. Silt settles on a surface, becomes part of it, and much later is loosened again. In some places the accumulated sediment is older than the stone beneath it.

There are buildings, though you would pass most without recognizing them.

A low enclosure of fitted blocks stands in a clearing of ribbon leaves. Its doorway has become wider than its walls. Inside, the floor slopes toward a shallow basin, and the basin holds a different darkness from the water above it. No roof remains. Nothing suggests that there ever was one.

Farther along the ridge are seven upright stones, worn almost flat against the ground. Their spacing is too regular to be accidental. Roots avoid the narrow intervals between them. On calm days, a little current goes from stone to stone, stopping briefly at each.

These traces are uncommon. For immense distances there is nothing arranged except by growth, pressure, and the habits of water.

Animals are rarer still.

Once in a long while, a white creature moves beneath the forest, parting root fibers with the slow opening and closing of its body. It has no evident face. Its passage leaves small clear patches on the stone where it has fed, and these are soon colonized by green threads. In deeper basins there are shells large enough to shelter a person, most empty, some closed. You could watch one for years and mistake its breathing for the gradual settling of the seabed.

The absence of calls is striking. Nothing sings from the canopy. There is no clicking multitude hidden among the stems. Most of the time the only sound is a soft, close friction as one leaf rubs another. Occasionally a mineral seam opens in the rock with a single dry tick, startlingly distinct in all that water.

Still, the place is busy.

Forests migrate by growing on one side and dissolving on the other. Warm fronts climb the ridges. Curtains of spores descend into the basins and rise again somewhere else. Root mats tighten around loose shelves, holding them together through seasons of pressure, then release them when the water turns. Over long intervals a valley may cross beneath a forest without disturbing it.

Near the outer rim, where the stone thins and the lower clouds become visible, the vegetation grows short and close. Each leaf lies against the next. The water there is clear, warm, and moving steadily toward the edge.

Lie flat on the bottom and look through it. Far beneath your chest, a storm is assembling over a dark land. Its clouds swell against the underside of the sea, brightening from within. The stone carries no thunder. Only the fine roots around your wrists begin to tighten, and all along the rim the leaves slowly turn to face downward.
