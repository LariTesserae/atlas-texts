The floor rises in shallow steps, but the edges have worn away so completely that they feel like slopes beneath the silt. A hand laid against one finds a slight hollow, then another: the places where individual blocks have softened around their joints. Everything is underwater. Even the ceiling, where it dips between its supports, has water pressed against it.

The chamber is about the size of a small temple. Six thick pillars divide it into narrow aisles, and the aisles are crowded with lesser structures: low walls, perforated jars, stone trays stacked on short legs, rows of clay pipes with mouths large enough to admit a fist. There is scarcely a straight passage through. Moving from one end to the other requires going sideways between a pillar and a partition, passing over a rounded sill, then descending beneath an arch whose underside is polished by the backs of animals.

The water is warm. Not hot enough to announce a source, but warm all the way through, with none of the cold waiting near the floor that a swimmer might expect.

At the far end, high in the wall, a slit admits a little gray light. It illuminates the thickness of the masonry more clearly than anything inside. Sometimes a pale drifting shape passes beyond it. These are clouds.

The chamber stands near the crown of an artificial height, a mountain of retaining walls and packed rubble whose lower terraces disappear into weather. Far below are other roofs, other reservoirs, the folded backs of ancient embankments. From here they would look almost level with the land, if there were enough light to see them. The flooded room is one of the last enclosed spaces at the summit. Its water is held high above the country by stone laid on stone.

No single block is remarkable. Most are small enough for two people to carry. They were shaped with pecking stones and fitted with wedges, their irregularities packed with fired clay. The builders knew the useful curve of an arch, the weight a sloping wall could bear, and how a channel could divide a flow without any moving part. There are no surviving gears or blades. The elaborate thing here is repetition: another course, another buttress, another little conduit passing through the thickness of a wall.

Time has made the workmanship difficult to recognize.

Mineral deposits bridge the joints. Thin partitions have swollen into blunt fins. Some jars have become joined to their shelves by pale crusts, while others have dissolved until only their thicker rims remain, resting like bracelets on the floor. A broken screen has lost every sharp edge without losing its pattern of holes. Through it, the darkness looks studded and deep.

There is no exposed earth, no bedrock, no bank from which something might take root. Beneath the silt lies paving; beneath the paving, a foundation of deliberately placed stones. Even the sand consists mostly of ground mortar, shell fragments, and grains shed from the fired clay. Nothing grows toward the slit of light.

Yet the room is full of animals.

Small, nearly transparent shrimp gather beneath the trays, packed so closely that their antennae form a trembling mesh. When one moves, its neighbors make room a few body lengths away, and the disturbance spreads along the underside of a shelf without breaking the stillness above it. White snails occupy the jar rims. Their shells are low and heavy, stained amber where the water has deposited iron.

In the pipes lie fish with flattened heads and eyes reduced to pale points. Some face the incoming current; others face the room. Their fins move just enough to keep silt from settling on them. A larger fish, the color of unglazed pottery, rests inside a cracked vessel with its tail protruding through the break. It has occupied that vessel long enough for the exposed portion of its body to be darker than the rest.

The smallest creatures are everywhere. They work through the sediment, crowd the pores of the masonry, and graze the almost invisible bacterial films around the inlets. There are no leaves, no weed, no waving growth to reveal the water’s motion. A current becomes visible only when a shrimp releases its hold and travels slowly across an aisle, or when a thread of waste lifts from the floor.

Water enters through three clay mouths near the ceiling. It comes from covered basins farther up the crown, where warm rain collects and seeps through beds of porous stone. One conduit also carries a faint mineral taste from deeper within the structure. Around that mouth the pale films are thicker, and the animals arrange themselves in close, overlapping rings.

The builders gave the water several paths. One crosses the upper chamber and leaves quickly through an overflow. Another descends behind the pillars, passes through the stacked trays, and takes much longer to reach the outlet. The narrowest channels exchange their contents so slowly that a little cloud of disturbed silt can remain between two walls for most of a day.

Each route has its inhabitants. The shrimp favor the quiet shelves. Small hard-shelled grazers crowd the mineral inlet. The fish wait where passages constrict, taking what the water brings or what ventures out of cover. Larger snails rasp the mortar itself, leaving fresh, pale tracks on surfaces otherwise darkened by age.

Their feeding changes the room. It does so imperceptibly, until a partition becomes thin enough to fail.

One has failed near the center. Its collapse must have been a great event in this small volume: a brief rush, a thick obscurity, animals driven from their recesses. Now the fragments lie in a gentle heap. Each has its own crust and its own population. The opening has made a sheltered eddy behind the third pillar, and a cluster of long-bodied fish breeds there. Their eggs are attached individually beneath the fallen slabs, out of reach of the snails.

Silence here has substance. There is no surf, and the rain outside falls onto so many layers of stone before reaching the room that it arrives only as pressure. The usual sound is a faint contact somewhere nearby: shell against ceramic, a grain dislodged from a ledge. Occasionally a fish closes its jaws with a tiny click. The sound seems disproportionately intimate in the dark.

The chamber has almost no surface water open to air. There is only a pocket beneath the highest part of the vault, above a shelf worn into a shallow bowl. A swimmer could lift a face there, though the air would be warm, stale, and mineral-scented. From that pocket the overflow can be heard.

It is a soft, continuous slipping sound.

On the other side of the wall, water passes over a broad stone lip and follows the outside masonry downward. It does not fall freely at first. It clings to the rounded face, spreading into a skin, dividing at old seams, gathering again beneath protruding blocks. Farther down, somewhere below the clouds, it leaves the wall.

The lip has been replaced many times. Older lips remain underneath it, each slightly lower and farther out, making a fan of worn stone along the exterior. Some are scarcely more than curved ridges now. The present one was installed so long ago that its tool marks survive only in a sheltered notch.

No one tends it. Still, the chamber keeps nearly the same level. Rain raises the water by the width of a finger; the overflow takes the excess. In a dry interval the inlets weaken, and the room slowly gives up a little of its stored warmth. Animals move closer to the conduits. Sediment settles into the fresh tracks they leave.

Near the light slit, the masonry is thinner and the water a shade cooler. Small fish sometimes gather there with their heads pointing outward. Beyond them lies a narrow brightness, then mist, then an immense depth of air.

They remain just inside the wall, scarcely moving, suspended in the last handspan of warm water before the drop.
