The sea lies high above the clouds.

Through gaps in its floor, you can see them far below: a white overcast pressed against the lower world, with the blunt tips of mountains showing through. There is no shore at that distance, no visible country. Only cloud, occasional stone, and the blue depth of air between them and the underside of the sea.

The water does not fall. Across each opening, its lower surface is slightly convex, as clear and firm-looking as the lens of an enormous eye. The clouds are magnified in it. Around the edges, roots hold the water in place.

Everything that seems to be land is part of a plant.

The broad submerged shelves are wood, though wood of a kind that makes the word feel small: pale, compressed, densely grained material, worn into long terraces and shallow basins. In places it has the dull finish of old bone. Elsewhere a skin of living green covers it so completely that no grain shows. The ridges between basins are roots. The distant escarpments are the exposed sides of roots. Farther away, where the water gives objects a blue cast and makes their distances uncertain, an entire mountain flank resolves into the curve of a stem.

There are not many stems. That is one of the first things the place makes you understand.

Between one and the next lie expanses of clear water large enough to contain an ordinary sea. A single leaf may roof such an expanse, but beneath it there is room: unobstructed depths, long views, pale light passing through water without encountering anything. The vegetation occupies almost everything solid while leaving immense intervals empty. Its abundance is a matter of extent rather than crowding.

The leaves are held horizontally at different heights. Their upper surfaces lie beneath a few metres of water, receiving the full daylight. Their undersides hang over deeper basins like green continental shelves. Along their edges, the tissue thickens into rounded lips, with old splits that have healed into blunt, dark seams.

There is no fresh break anywhere.

The place has the forms of something that has endured immense weather. Grooves run down the stems, several hundred metres deep. Root buttresses have been rounded off. The oldest exposed wood bears hollows within hollows, each softened until its edges agree with the next. Yet there is no loose sediment. No pale dust gathers beneath the scarps. Whatever could be worn away has been worn away; whatever could settle has found its place.

The water is cold enough that an exposed hand would soon ache. It has none of the milky darkness of a fertile pond. You can look down through it for an unreasonable distance, past one shelf, past a second, to where the great root surfaces become narrow green lines. The sunlight is ordinary daylight, white and generous. It illuminates a world whose distances are not ordinary at all.

And everywhere, there is the sound.

It is a deep, continuous mechanical roar. Close to the large stems, you feel it in the jaw before you distinguish it with the ear. Farther out, it becomes a broad vibration occupying the water from floor to roof. Higher notes lie within it: a smooth whine, a low electrical hum, something like the sustained breath of a furnace.

There is no hammering, no irregular rattle, no starting or stopping. The sound has the completeness of a vast works already running at its final capacity.

Nothing has been built.

Along the undersides of the leaves are bands of thick, dark tissue arranged in close parallel folds. They resemble heat exchangers. At the stem junctions, chambers with translucent walls reveal bundles of white fibres, each bundle sheathed separately, each descending into the root mass. Broad conductive veins shine dully through the green. Some are copper-coloured, some black, some a soft silver that disappears when viewed from an angle.

The resemblance to machinery becomes less comfortable the longer you look. These are not plants that happen to suggest engines. The sea’s living mass performs work on an industrial scale. It separates salts. It maintains electrical potentials across membranes wider than valleys. It bears the weight of water above open air. Its tissues include pressure vessels, filters, insulated trunks and vast areas of exchange surface, all grown into a single anatomy.

There are no service passages or controls. There is no distinction between the mechanism and the landscape it sustains.

In the deeper parts, the roots enter dark green swellings that rest against one another without quite closing the spaces between them. The gaps are narrow from far away, but each would admit a mountain. Here the roar is lowest. It seems to originate in the wood itself, though no surface visibly trembles.

Animals occupy these spaces.

Long, silver-sided fish hang beside the root walls, aligned with one another at intervals too wide to resemble a school. Their eyes are large and clear. Small crustaceans stipple the undersides of the leaf rims, their transparent legs laid precisely against the veins. On the pale terraces rest colonies of soft-bodied grazers, almost indistinguishable from the olive growth beneath them except for the dark buttons of their mouths.

There are larger creatures in the open water. One has a body like a flattened ribbon, with a row of small fins along either side and a blunt head turned toward the light. Another is visible only as an immense, faintly patterned flank beyond a stem. Neither breaks the spaciousness. They belong to distances in which even very large animals can seem occasional.

All are alive.

None changes position.

A fish holds its tail bent at the beginning of a stroke. Fine feelers project from a crustacean’s head without wavering. Beneath a root arch, a cluster of translucent eggs contains embryos at several stages of formation, each arrested in its own small completeness. There is no next heartbeat you can wait long enough to hear.

At first, this seems like a brief stillness. Then it seems like a silence of movement imposed upon an otherwise active world. Eventually even that understanding fails, because it assumes the stillness began.

There are no beginnings here.

The sea is not caught in the instant before something happens. It has no stored storm, no strain approaching release. Its great leaves will not grow larger. Their healed scars will not fade. The animals are neither sleeping nor waiting. The daylight does not advance across the terraces. A suspended fleck beside a root is as much a feature of the place as the root itself.

Even the immense sound has no sequence. You cannot find the point at which one vibration gives way to another. It is simply present, dense and continuous, as green is present in a leaf. Listening longer reveals no repetition because nothing has recurred.

Far out across the high water, the nearest stem gives way to a view of others: immense vertical forms, widely separated, their tops passing through pale layers of foliage. The intervals between them are blue. Some leaves are close enough to show their fine branching veins; others are no more than green horizons. Through a gap below, the cloud country remains visible, bright against the darker roots.

There is no ruin here, though everything has the authority of age. No wilderness is reclaiming anything. No machine will wear out. The living floor, the clear cold water, the widely stationed animals and the enormous labouring sound have reached an agreement so exact that nothing further is required of them.

Under one leaf, near its rounded edge, a small fish faces the open sea. Sunlight passes through the thin web of its fin. Beyond it there is room for thousands of kilometres of water, and beyond that, another green shelf, equally lit.
