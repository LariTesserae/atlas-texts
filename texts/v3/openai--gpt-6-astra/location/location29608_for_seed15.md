Behind the bathhouse stair, beneath a square opening in the courtyard, is a basin scarcely larger than a bed. From above it looks like a patch of white light interrupted by the stair’s shadow. Its depth is difficult to judge. The water is clear, the lining pale, and sunlight moves so vigorously over the bottom that the floor seems closer than it is.

The basin is new. The corners have not yet softened under deposits, and the narrow steps descending into it still carry the straight marks of a mason’s chisel. Even the lowest step has a crisp outer edge. A little pale grit rests where the wall meets the floor: not sediment brought from elsewhere, but the basin’s own first loosenings.

Nothing here is unworked ground. Beneath the water are fired tiles, lime mortar, shaped stone. The walls rise to a rim of plastered brick, above which the underside of the stair closes off most of the view. A wooden lintel holds the remaining opening. Through it comes a hard rectangle of sun.

By midday the whole enclosure shines. Light strikes the opposite wall, returns through the water, and gathers under the steps in a softer, greenish brightness. Small irregularities in the hand-glazed tiles become conspicuous: a swollen lip, a shallow thumbprint, a patch where the white glaze has thinned to the color of baked dough. On the bottom, a single tile is faintly blue. It was probably taken from another batch. There is nothing arranged around it to make it important, but in such an empty place the eye keeps returning to it.

Water enters through a clay spout just below the rim.

The spout has been fitted slightly crooked. Its stream strikes the surface at an angle, opening a slanted pocket of air that repeatedly collapses. This makes more noise than the size of the basin seems to permit. There is the slap of falling water, a hollow knocking at the mouth of the pipe, and a deeper, uneven gulp from the overflow beneath the stair. The close walls throw these sounds back across one another. Outside, someone could pass along the courtyard and hear only an ordinary pouring noise. Down at water level it fills the place.

The supply comes along a shallow channel exposed to the sun. By the time it reaches this sheltered recess, it is warm. The tiles are warm too, especially near the surface. Only beneath the lowest step is there a cooler seam, where fresh water takes a slower route through the basin before finding the outlet.

The currents are visible without carrying much. A grain of mortar shivers, travels the width of a finger, and stops. A bubble rolls under the projecting edge of a tile and stays there, silver against white. When enough smaller bubbles join it, it slips free and rises. The broad middle of the basin remains empty.

Growth has begun in three places.

A green film follows the damp lower lip of the inlet, thin enough that the clay shows through. Two dark threads of algae bend from a mortar joint near the overflow. Higher up, on a ledge that is splashed but never quite submerged, a small liverwort spreads over the plaster. Its flattened lobes hold droplets after the surrounding surface has begun to dry. There is no soil beneath it, only a rough patch where the plasterer’s tool failed to close the lime completely.

Most of the basin is still too clean and too recently made to look inhabited. No fish turn in the open water. No snails interrupt the walls. Even the algae are easy to miss when the sun is moving across them.

The water level is governed by a notch cut into the far rim. When the incoming flow strengthens, the basin does not grow much deeper; instead, a wider sheet pours through that notch into a covered drain. Its edge trembles against the cut stone. When the supply weakens, the sheet narrows to a cord and finally separates into heavy drops. Then the room’s sound changes. The continuous wash recedes, and each drop can be heard striking the drain below.

A wooden plug lies on the uppermost step, beyond the usual waterline. It is dark at one end and pale at the other, with a short loop of coarse cord passed through it. Someone has pared it by hand to fit the inlet. Beside it are a shallow earthenware bowl and a brush made of bundled fibers. These are the only loose objects. Their placement leaves almost the entire ledge bare.

Late in the day, the shadow of the stair crosses the basin one tread at a time. The deepest water loses its glare first. Tool marks become easier to see; so do the slight disagreements between adjacent tiles. The inlet remains bright a little longer, throwing sparks of light onto the underside of the wood.

The stored warmth outlasts the sun. Water continues to arrive, slide along the new walls, and leave through the notch. Under the lowest step, the pale grit slowly sorts itself into a narrow line. At the mouth of the spout, the green film bends in the current.
