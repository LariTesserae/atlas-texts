The clouds are below the ridge for part of the day. When they rise, they do not arrive as weather seen approaching across a distance; the distance simply disappears. One moment there is a shallow valley, a dark shoulder of mountain, another shoulder beyond it. The next, there are thirty paces of wet stone and a pale, moving edge.

This is a high volcanic basin, newly opened to the sky. Snow survives in its sheltered folds, but the broad floor is bare: charcoal-gray crust, broken along seams that still have sharp corners. There are no deep gullies, no rounded boulders, no comfortable accumulation of soil. A stone loosened here has not necessarily traveled anywhere. It may lie beside the exact hollow it came out of, its edges fitting the edges of the absence.

The basin extends several kilometers between low ridges that would look modest anywhere else. Here, with whole ranges occasionally visible beneath the cloud shelf, their height becomes apparent. The air is thin enough that distances are difficult to judge. A bird crossing the valley seems to make no progress until it turns and shows the sudden smallness of its body.

Water is everywhere without much of it being deep.

It beads on the windward faces of stones. It fills the narrow separations between overlapping plates of lava. Beneath a skin of ice, it creeps across almost level ground, taking a different route each afternoon. There has not been time for a proper streambed to form. A trickle encounters a lip of rock, spreads, freezes overnight, and is diverted the next day by its own obstruction. Across one flat stretch, hundreds of these provisional channels shine faintly through the mist.

Some disappear into the ground with a sound too small to hear unless you kneel.

Farther out, that same ground exhales.

The vents are inconspicuous: an opening under a slab, a gravel patch that will not hold snow, a pale yellow rim around a crack. Steam flattens in the wind. On still mornings it rises straight for a few meters before fraying into the cold air. The largest vent makes a low, continuous pressure noise, almost below hearing. You notice it most when a gust changes direction and the noise stops reaching you.

There is something industrial in the scale and coordination of these processes, although nothing has been built. Water enters the fractured upper slopes, descends through hot rock, and returns under pressure. Minerals separate out along the cooling margins. Ice seals an outlet; pressure finds another. A flat stone lifts slightly, settles, lifts again. Beneath acres of apparently inert surface, heat is being transferred and fluids driven through narrow passages.

Yet the basin is mostly quiet. There is no great roar to match the amount of work taking place. A little hissing. The brittle tick of cooling rock. Water dropping beneath a hollow crust. Long intervals in which the only sound is wind rubbing against the ear.

Most of the land is empty even of vegetation. You can look across a whole slope and find no green at all. Then, at the foot of a warmer outcrop, there is a cushion plant scarcely wider than a hand, its leaves packed tightly enough to keep a milder climate inside them. A few wiry grasses occupy a strip of older ash. Their roots follow moist cracks rather than spreading through soil. Around a seep, moss forms a dark border that looks black until you get close.

These small patches hold an improbable amount of life.

Lift the edge of a loose stone beside the moss and tiny bodies move away from the light. Springtails gather in damp recesses. Midge larvae twist in water shallow enough to freeze solid at the margins. Thin microbial films stain the warm runnels rust-red, green, or dull cream; a change of only a few degrees produces a visible boundary.

The animals are abundant, but their abundance is concentrated. Almost all the space between them remains vacant.

At the basin’s far end, meltwater gathers in a broad, shallow tarn. Its center is lead-colored under the cloud, its edges divided into clear water, slush, and cloudy shelves of ice. Where warm groundwater enters, insects emerge in brief, dense hatches. They stay low, hovering just above the water in the slight protection of the banks.

Small shorebirds run through these swarms. From across the basin they resemble scraps blowing along the margin, except that they stop together and bend their heads. There may be hundreds around the tarn, spread so thinly along its circumference that no part looks crowded. Their calls carry sharply, then cease. The birds feed with urgency during the few hours when the shallows are open.

Higher up, dark birds work the updrafts along the rim. They appear out of cloud without a wingbeat, tilt over the warm slope, and vanish into whiteness again. On the rougher ground, little burrowing mammals occupy the few banks deep enough to hold a tunnel. Their paths connect islands of vegetation across expanses of bare grit. A fox follows one of these routes at dusk, pausing at each patch as though checking a series of widely separated rooms.

The weather moves faster than the land can acquire a settled appearance.

By noon, weak sunlight may soften the ice along the channels. The water darkens the dust, and the basin briefly becomes a map of drainage. By late afternoon, fine snow crosses it almost horizontally. It collects on cold ground but melts over the buried heat, revealing the warm seams as black lines through white. Later, freezing water prises a flake from a stone. The flake falls only a few centimeters. That is the beginning of a slope.

Nothing here is wholly still, but little has yet become established. Water is testing its routes. Plants are occupying the first usable shelter. Animals arrive for a hatch, a thaw, a brief exposure of food, then withdraw when the temperature falls. Even the tarn’s shoreline changes as fresh gravel settles beneath it.

Toward evening the cloud lowers until the opposite ridge is gone. The open water remains visible a little longer than the land, a dim reflection without an apparent source of light. Birds keep feeding along its edge. Somewhere beyond them, steam rises from a fissure newly cleared of ice.

By morning, the opening will have a delicate white mineral rim. It will be one of the youngest features in a place where almost everything still has its original edge.
