The city stands on land that has not yet learned to crumble.

Beyond the last buildings, the plain breaks into broad, shallow plates of olive-black stone. Their edges are thin and surprisingly sharp. Some rise a hand’s breadth above their neighbors; others have lifted enough to expose a dark slit beneath, where roots enter and disappear. There are no rounded pebbles. Very little dust. A dropped piece of masonry lies where it fell with all its corners intact, and the few paths across the unbuilt ground have been made by laying down material rather than wearing anything away.

The country is low. From an upper terrace you can see across several districts to the faint rim of another settlement, all of it under the same dim, warm sky. Nothing rises high enough to become a mountain. What look at first like distant hills are reservoirs, roofed over with living green.

There is more city here than the number of buildings would suggest.

Streets continue through long intervals of open ground. They have curbs, drainage mouths, strips of soft illumination, places where one surface yields to another at a crossing. Then, after half a kilometer of empty pavement, there is a cluster of tall, narrow residences, a shaded public court, a station. Utilities reach everywhere. Small service doors are set into stone far beyond the nearest occupied block. A handrail follows an unbuilt embankment out of sight.

The spaces between neighborhoods are not ruins or abandoned lots. Their lamps work. Their drains are clear. At dusk, which differs only slightly from the rest of the day, the crossings change color for traffic that may not arrive.

Vegetation gathers lavishly around each inhabited structure. The buildings stand inside their own loose green envelopes: broad leaves on long jointed stalks, curtains of climbing stems, heavy rosettes rooted in the seams between balconies. Fernlike growth spills from the undersides of walkways. At street level, beds of fleshy, translucent plants fill the narrow shade beside foundations, their inner veins visible as branching amber threads.

Between these concentrations are wide stretches of bare stone.

The change is abrupt. You can step from a garden dense enough to conceal a doorway onto a plain where nothing grows for two hundred meters. There are no struggling seedlings along the boundary. The roots stop at a line beneath the pavement, although leaves lean across it and open eagerly into the vacant air.

Water explains some of this, but not all.

Rain comes infrequently, in warm, short falls that darken one district while leaving the next dry. The clouds are difficult to distinguish from the sky until their undersides loosen into narrow hanging veils. Often the rain evaporates before reaching the street. You feel it first as a change against your face, then smell the mineral surface of the buildings.

The city also condenses water.

At certain hours the facades lose their reflections. A haze appears on the dark panels, gathers into beads, and runs upward in thin, hesitant tracks. The tracks converge beneath the eaves. There the water enters transparent channels and travels toward the gardens, accumulating in swollen joints that look almost botanical. A building watering itself makes a subdued ticking sound, like a cooling pan in another room.

The plants wait for this. Their leaves change angle before the first beads appear. Some uncurl soft collecting surfaces; others close, protecting pale flowers that open only in dry air. When the water arrives, a whole courtyard can shift without wind, each stem taking up the small weight passed to it.

Most of the moisture is kept above ground. Under the vegetation, the pavement is often dry.

There are public pools, but they are small and deep, set in courts below street level. Their rims are warm enough to sit on. In the dimness their surfaces look solid until a drop strikes them. No fountains throw water into the air. Instead, a thin horizontal sheet occasionally passes across a pool, removing fallen leaves without disturbing their reflections. The leaves remain briefly visible in the water after they have been carried away.

The oldest buildings are recognizable by this kind of delay.

Their windows hold the color of a sky that has already changed. Their stairways sometimes deliver the sound of footsteps to the landing before the person arrives. Beside one station, the shadow of a demolished canopy still falls across the paving every afternoon. Maintenance crews have outlined it with a low rail so that no one installs anything there.

Most of the city’s stranger workings are treated with similar practicality. There are warning strips, access panels, posted hours.

At the center of each district stands a narrow tower with an open room near its top. Through these rooms you can see the landscape, though not always the landscape behind the tower. One frames an expanse of yellow reeds moving in bright sunlight. Another shows the far side of the city from an impossible height. A third is dark except for occasional drifting sparks.

They are part of the ground system.

Several times a day the open rooms cloud over, and a pressure passes through the plain. It is too slow to be a tremor. Water inclines in cups. Hanging stems lean toward the nearest tower. A shallow depression beyond the southern district may rise by a few centimeters, bringing a new seam of stone into view. A street that had ended at a retaining wall acquires another short section of pavement.

This is why the exposed ground is so fresh. The city is still obtaining it.

From where is not apparent. The new stone is warm and faintly flexible for its first few hours. Work crews keep it shaded until it stiffens; children are kept behind temporary fences. In the evening, narrow white lines mark the places where another increment is expected. Gardens are planted back from those lines. There have been mistakes.

Farther out, where the towers are less closely spaced, the process produces folds and incomplete joins. Long slots open between plates, too narrow to enter but deep enough to swallow lamplight. Warm air rises from them carrying a smell like cut stems and electrical insulation. Occasionally a root emerges from below, thick, pale, already branching. The city’s gardeners do not cut these. They build little shelters over them and wait to see what the roots will support.

A few have grown into trees.

These stand apart from the cultivated greenery. Their trunks divide near the ground and reunite higher up, making tall enclosed spaces among the wood. The bark is smooth, dull red, and marked with tiny rectangular pores. Their crowns spread widely without becoming dense. Under them, the light is greener and objects are difficult to count: a row of six benches may seem to contain seven until you walk around the trunk.

People use the shade nonetheless. There are tables, charging rails, polished places where hands have rested.

The inhabitants are not often visible at a distance. A figure crosses a balcony. Two people wait beneath a station roof that hangs without columns. A maintenance vehicle moves along a utility strip, its wheels turning slightly above the road. The transit cars are slender, separated by long intervals, and almost silent. You notice their passage because the hanging leaves beside the track draw inward, then relax.

At the larger intersections, the city feels capable of receiving crowds. There are broad stairways, many entrances, signs visible from several approaches. Yet footsteps remain individual sounds. Somewhere behind a green wall, a chair scrapes across a floor. A door closes four streets away.

Animal life is scarce enough to attract attention.

Small, flat-bodied creatures shelter beneath the rims of the condensation channels. When disturbed they do not flee immediately; they tighten against the surface until their edges are nearly invisible. At night, a few emerge to graze on the film left by the water. Their movement produces a faint dry whisper.

There are also birds, or something sufficiently like birds to occupy the same part of the eye. They cross the long spaces between districts alone. Their wings are narrow and translucent, their bodies dark. Each chooses a single tall stem in the roof gardens and remains there for hours, turning occasionally toward one of the open towers. They are never seen flocking. When one calls, another may answer from so far away that the interval feels accidental.

Insects are almost absent. The flowers are serviced by the gardens themselves. Fine strands extend between neighboring blooms overnight, touching the inner surfaces, retracting before the next condensation cycle. On dry mornings, these strands sometimes break and drift through the streets. They catch on railings and eyelashes, soft as spider silk.

Warmth accumulates everywhere, but rarely becomes oppressive. The stone releases it slowly after a shower. Building entrances breathe cooler air across your ankles. In the open avenues, a mild current usually moves toward the newest ground, stirring only the highest leaves.

The sky gives little indication of direction. Its light is a diffuse bronze-gray, brighter behind thin cloud and darker where the atmosphere seems unusually deep. No sharp sunlight reaches the city. Colors reveal themselves at close range: violet undersides of leaves, blue seals around doors, rust-red pollen caught in a drain. Seen across the plain, everything settles into green and charcoal, with the pale infrastructure lines threading between.

After the evening water cycle, the gardens become the most luminous part of the landscape. Not because they glow, but because their wet surfaces collect the city’s low lighting. Each leaf carries a narrow reflection. The residential blocks appear porous, their outlines broken by hundreds of small green flashes.

Then the towers begin another adjustment.

For a minute or two, all the channels stop ticking. The pools grow perfectly level. A transit car waits in the middle of an empty avenue while its destination sign continues to scroll.

Out beyond the last maintained street, a dark seam opens across the plain. It opens slowly enough that you can watch a whole leaf detach, turn in the air, and land before the gap is as wide as your thumb. There is no crash of breaking rock. Only a low, fibrous sound, almost the sound of a thick stalk being pulled apart.

By morning there will be another strip of land there: clean-edged, warm, not yet bearing a footprint. The city has already installed a drain at one end.
