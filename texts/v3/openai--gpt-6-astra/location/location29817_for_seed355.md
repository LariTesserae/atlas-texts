The city occupies a shallow basin that is almost, but never quite, a lake.

Water lies across its avenues in broad, hot sheets. A few streets stand above it, their crowns exposed like the backs of buried walls; others are visible only where rain dimples them differently from the deeper squares. There is no shore to arrive at. The approach is a long passage through increasingly wet ground, from sweating clay to ankle-deep water, with the first buildings appearing so far apart that each initially seems to be the last.

It is very dark. The cloud cover has thickness but no visible underside. Occasionally a pale stain develops within it, too weak to cast shadows, and for a minute the city can be seen: low roofs, immense gaps, a row of doorways standing out in water. Then the stain closes. The eye continues supplying the buildings for a while after they have ceased to be visible.

The heat remains steady through these changes. It comes from the ground as much as from the air. Water gathers on every surface, including the undersides of lintels and the sheltered inner walls of rooms. A hand laid against stone comes away wet and warmer than before. In some districts, the water is hot enough that the air above it has a dry, mineral taste, though nothing here is truly dry.

There are no lamps. No wheels, vessels, cables, drains with fitted mouths, or other signs that anyone has ever brought an implement into this place. Yet it is unmistakably a city. There are courts within courts, residential lanes, long public buildings raised on plinths, divisions between districts that persist across empty ground. Construction is layered upon construction. A doorway has been partly enclosed by a later wall; an older staircase enters the belly of a newer foundation. The arrangements imply ownership, access, ceremony, inconvenience.

The material does not imply masonry.

Walls have no courses. Their substance resembles a dark limestone with the texture of kneaded clay, and they rise from the basin floor without a joint. Where a corner has worn away, there is no rubble beneath it. The missing material has spread into the adjoining pavement in a shallow fan. Even the most elaborate buildings seem to have thickened into existence, carrying their rooms inside them from the beginning.

Most stand only one or two stories high. Their mass is concentrated close to the water. Here and there an upper chamber rests on a narrow waist of stone, but its reflection shows a broad foundation descending below the reflected street. These inverted foundations are often easier to see than the buildings themselves. They remain distinct in complete darkness, not luminous, simply available to sight in a way the air above them is not.

The empty spaces are extensive. Between a colonnaded hall and the next cluster of houses there may be half a mile of flooded paving. Broad terraces face nothing but steam. A civic stair descends over fifty shallow steps into water no deeper than a palm.

The steps are all submerged to the same depth.

This is one of the first difficulties the place presents, and one of the easiest to live with. Water keeps its own levels here. It can occupy a sloping street without running down it. It can lie flat across a staircase while faithfully covering every tread. At thresholds it sometimes rises into a thin vertical sheet, trembling in the opening, with a room full of ordinary air beyond. Crossing such a threshold leaves the front of the body wet and the back untouched until the next breath.

The city has accommodated these habits for a long time. There are deep, rounded grooves where standing water has worried at stone. Pillars are narrow at heights where floods used to remain. Whole facades have softened until windows and doors are recognizable chiefly by their spacing. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even the impossible parts have weathered edges.

Rain comes frequently, but much of it never falls.

It forms in the streets as a coarse suspension of droplets, shoulder-high, motionless at first. The droplets enlarge. They touch and draw together into threads. Then, throughout a district, the threads descend at once. For several moments there is the close, restrained sound of water entering water, and a warm pressure passes between the buildings.

Elsewhere the process is reversed. A square begins to bead. Little columns lift from its surface, narrow, separate, breaking into drops as they rise. Roofs receive this upward rain on their undersides. Their chambers slowly fill from the ceiling down.

These weather systems travel along the avenues. At a junction they divide, following routes too regular to be wind. One neighborhood may be losing water while the next is filling. A distant facade darkens as moisture climbs it; nearby, a doorway clears with the slow withdrawal of a transparent curtain. The whole basin is active, though very little of this activity asks to be heard.

The loudest sound is usually a drop falling somewhere indoors.

It may repeat for hours. Its interval is nearly regular, but never regular enough to disappear from attention. There are also soft settling noises, a grit-like whisper along the foot of a wall, and, at great distances, the low exhalation of a courtyard emptying beneath its own paving. No wings cross the streets. Nothing splashes away at an approach. There are no insect voices in the heat, no small feet inside the walls.

Life holds to a few specific places.

In cracks above the old flood marks grows a sparse, colorless plant with short, folded blades. Its roots lie exposed against the stone, thick as wet thread. It grows where the wall gives off a certain bitter moisture, not where there is more water or better shelter. Long stretches of apparently identical surface remain bare.

The plants open during the upward rain. Their folded blades separate into flat little cups, facing down. A few hours later they close again, and a faint rust-colored deposit appears around their roots. Over many seasons these deposits build narrow ledges. New plants take hold along the ledges, making thin, widely separated horizontal gardens around otherwise empty buildings.

There are no fallen leaves beneath them. Dead growth becomes translucent, loses its edges, and joins the dampness on the wall.

Farther into the basin, the city becomes less willing to keep its spaces separate. Two streets may share a single line of doorways. From one side, the doors open into small rooms with low ceilings. From the other, they open into those same rooms from above. Water moves between these arrangements without disturbance.

Some courts accumulate distance. They look modest from the entrance, perhaps twenty paces across, with a low arcade at the far end. Walking into them takes progressively longer. The columns do not retreat; their size remains unchanged. It is the paving immediately underfoot that grows more abundant. Old courts have become so extensive that weather develops inside them while the street outside remains still.

Other spaces have worn thin. A ruined house may contain only the narrow interval between entering and leaving. There is a threshold, the sensation of warmth held under a roof, and then another street. Its interior has been used away as thoroughly as the centers of the ancient steps.

Toward the middle of the city is a district of large, roofless enclosures. They are separated by low embankments and connected by openings wide enough for processions, though there is no evidence of one ever passing. The water here is black and almost free of minerals. It feels cooler at the surface. Below that first thin layer, the heat increases sharply.

During certain rains, the enclosures begin to exchange their contents. Water passes through the wide openings in slow, thick folds. The current brings no sediment, no branch, no drifting body by which its progress could be measured. Only the reflections move.

An arcade passes beneath an open square. A set of upper windows slides under a blank wall. For a while the reflected city has a different plan from the one standing above it.

Then foundations begin to loosen.

There is no collapse. A building eases sideways with the deliberation of something settling into mud. Its steps detach from one avenue and meet another. A wall lengthens across a vacant plot, thinning as it goes. The change may take most of a night, if these dark intervals are nights. By the time the water stills, the new junctions are already rounded and stained.

Only the plants preserve a brief evidence of the adjustment. Their mineral ledges end abruptly in the middle of a wall, or continue into open air for the breadth of a finger before softening and falling.

Beyond these central enclosures, more neighborhoods extend into the heat: widely spaced roofs, drowned courts, the faint wet outlines of streets. There is no tower from which to understand their arrangement. No district is high enough to overlook the rest.

The city continues at the level of the body, doorway after doorway, across a basin whose edges can no longer be seen. Water gathers on the inner surfaces of the empty rooms. Somewhere ahead, a whole avenue begins quietly to rise as rain.
