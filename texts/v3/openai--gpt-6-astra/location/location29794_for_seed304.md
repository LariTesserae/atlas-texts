The town occupies a hollow scarcely wider than a long stone’s throw. From its outer wall you can see almost every roof, though you cannot see a single street all the way through. Houses crowd around houses; steps descend beneath doorways; covered passages turn where a buttress, a cistern, or an older wall has taken the space. The buildings are only two or three stories high. Their closeness makes them seem taller.

Beyond them lies a flat, pale marsh. Nothing on the horizon rises enough to shelter the town from wind.

The ground here is new. A shelf of blue-gray clay lifted out of the marsh within living memory, carrying slabs of white stone upright through its surface. The slabs still have edges sharp enough to cut a wet palm. There are no rounded hills, no deep gullies, no old soil. Along the settlement’s southern edge, the clay ends in a small, crisp escarpment, knee-high in one place and higher than a person in another. Water comes out beneath it. Fallen pieces lie with their corners intact in the shallow pools.

The first houses were fitted between those standing stones. Later houses incorporated them. Now a single pale slab may be the back wall of a kitchen, the side of a stair, and the partition of a storage room reached from another lane. Its upper edge emerges through three different roofs. Nobody owns the whole of such a stone. Agreements concerning it are scratched beside the doors.

There is water everywhere, but little open water within the walls.

It occupies narrow stone gutters, bowls sunk into thresholds, the black seams between paving blocks. It hangs in clear beads from the undersides of stairs. In some passages, you walk on split logs laid across a channel barely wide enough to admit your foot. Through the gaps you see a slow, bright movement under the town.

The water is cold enough to ache in the fingers. It has a chalky taste at the western wells and an iron taste beneath the meeting house, although the wells are only twenty paces apart. Rainwater is kept separately in covered jars. The jar lids are weighted with little stones that fit into hollows worn by generations of hands; these stones, unlike the town’s foundations, were brought from elsewhere.

Most roofs are shallow pitches of reed thatch held down with clay battens. Some are tiled with thin flakes split from the white rock. After rain, the thatch remains dark for hours while the stone roofs dry in pale patches, each patch spreading from a raised corner. The light is ordinary daylight, gray beneath cloud and almost painfully clear when the sky opens. At noon the upper walls shine. Below, the lanes remain cool and blue.

A few plants live where construction has failed to exclude them. A tuft of stiff grass bends out of a roof joint. Tiny leaves grow around a leaking jar stand, their stems translucent from lack of sun. On the marsh side of the wall, reeds gather in separate, untidy clumps, leaving most of the mud bare. There is too little loose earth for gardens. Herbs are grown in cracked cooking pots and carried upstairs when the lower rooms become damp.

Nothing is abandoned simply because it is wet.

A workshop may have water moving beneath its bench all winter. Its owner stands on a wooden block and keeps the tools on pegs overhead: stone adzes, bone awls, one short copper blade carefully wrapped when not in use. Clay is kneaded on woven mats. Timber is split with wedges. The drains are cleared with crooked sticks whose polished handles pass from one worker to the next.

This work continues almost constantly. Someone is lifting a paving slab. Someone is pressing fresh clay around a wall foot. Someone carries a basket of sharp rubble through a passage too narrow to turn around in.

Yet the town is mostly quiet.

There are no wheeled streets and few animals. People carry loads on their backs or slide them on small wooden trays over wet boards. The thick walls swallow voices. A mallet striking a wedge can be heard distinctly, but only for a moment before a corner closes around the sound. More persistent is the faint ticking of drips into covered vessels. From inside a house, it is possible to know which neighbor has emptied a cistern by the change in that rhythm.

The unusual thing happens after several days without rain.

Water begins to rise through the white stone.

First the slabs darken at their bases. Then dampness climbs them in branching lines, crossing rooms, disappearing behind shelves, emerging again on the upper floors. By evening, their exposed tops are beaded with water. A slab projecting above a roof may weep steadily into a gutter while the paving beneath it stays dry.

The townspeople prepare for this as they prepare for weather. Bedding is moved away from certain walls. Flour baskets are hung from rafters. Little removable troughs are fitted into grooves cut across the stones. Children are taught not to lick the shining surfaces, and not to sleep with a cheek against them.

The rising water brings a fine white grit. It settles in the troughs and hardens there, sometimes so firmly that a wooden scraper will no longer shift it. Fresh crusts form along the channels. Doorframes slowly narrow. A drain left untended for a season becomes a stone tube with scarcely an opening through its middle.

Much of the town’s maintenance consists of cutting room back out of this growth.

Near the central cistern, the steps have been recut so often that their oldest outlines remain as faint ledges above the present treads. At the foot of one wall, a discarded basket has become a pale, rigid bowl, preserving each strand of its weave. It is still used to catch drips.

There are small lives in the wet seams: translucent snails no bigger than a seed, dark larvae that retreat when a slab is lifted, a few narrow fish in the deeper sump beneath the outer gate. Once or twice a day, a marsh bird lands on the wall and stands with its feet together, looking down into the crowded roofs. It seldom stays.

Toward evening, cooking smoke settles low in the hollow. Doors open briefly to let it pass. The last workers wash clay from their wrists, standing sideways so others can get by. Above them, laundry is drawn in along cords stretched from house to house.

Then the passages empty, though they never feel vacant. Jars, braces, stacked fuel, projecting stairheads, and the shoulders of the raw stone fill every turn. Behind the walls, people shift benches and lay out their bedding. Beneath the floorboards, water continues through the channels.

In the morning, someone will find a threshold a little higher, or a gutter running the wrong way. A bowl will have overflowed in a room that stayed dry all winter. Before breakfast, a neighbor will arrive with a wedge and a wooden hammer, and the two of them will kneel in the doorway to make space for it.
