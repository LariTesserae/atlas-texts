The first slope is so gradual that you climb for days before discovering you have left the lowlands. Water tells you. The streams become narrower, slower, warmer; instead of cutting beds, they spread into transparent sheets across the roots. Eventually there are no streams at all, only places where the ground shines.

By then you are high enough that clouds pass beneath the occasional opening in the vegetation. Through these openings, very far down, you can see the pale backs of storms.

The upland continues beyond sight.

It was once a mountain range. Almost nothing of that shape remains. The peaks have worn down into broad, shallow rises, and the valleys have filled until they are distinguishable chiefly by the kinds of leaves growing over them. The last exposed stone lies in the bottoms of certain pools: smooth, dark pieces no larger than a sleeping animal. Around each one, fine roots assemble in layers, pressing against it, taking from it whatever can still be taken. Some stones have become so thin that light passes through their centers.

Above them, the country is mostly alive.

What seems to be soil is a felt of roots, fallen leaves, new roots, and wood softened without quite dying. A trunk descending into this material does not end. It spreads, joins other trunks, rises again somewhere else. There are ridges made from generations of interlaced branches, hollows held open by the tension of living fibers, long level expanses supported over water by a raft thicker than any forest is tall.

A heavy animal can cross such a place without making it tremble. But if it stops and drinks, its weight slowly changes the angle of the surface, and the pool may begin moving toward its feet.

The warmth comes from below and from the vegetation itself. Even in the dimmest hours, the air inside a root hollow is soft against the face. Water collects on the ceiling, swells, and falls at long intervals. Some drops are clear. Others have the faint green color of a leaf held against the sky. Where old growth is breaking down, a little heat rises through the walking surface; these places smell of wet bark and opened fruit.

There is seldom enough light to cast a distinct shadow.

The upper leaves overlap loosely, each broad enough to roof a small clearing. Above them are other leaves, turned at different angles, and above those a cloud cover that the upland largely makes for itself. Moisture ascends through the plants, leaves them as vapor, and returns as a rain so fine that it is often visible only on hair or fur. At midday the whole depth of the place brightens slightly. Leaf veins appear overhead. The undersides of branches become separate from the darkness behind them.

Then the light diminishes again, without anything resembling sunset.

For all its fullness, the landscape is not an uninterrupted thicket. There are immense interiors. Beneath a canopy supported by widely spaced columns of braided wood, you may walk for half a day through an open chamber floored with small, yielding leaves. The columns lean almost imperceptibly. High overhead, their branches carry gardens that have never touched the ground: shallow marshes, stands of pale reeds, mats of flowering creepers. Water spills from one suspended garden into another, usually along the bark, quietly. Only where a root has recently broken does it fall free.

Such a fall is a landmark. Animals arrange their journeys around the sound.

Most of them move slowly enough to be mistaken for features of the place. Broad-backed grazers stand in the wet hollows, their coats bearing short stems and blunt white flowers. They eat the soft growth along pool margins, taking one mouthful and waiting before taking another. Small, translucent creatures live in the vegetation on their backs. When a grazer wades into deep water, these climb toward its spine.

There are long-bodied animals in the branchwork whose many feet grip without scraping. Their young cling beneath them like unripe fruit. In the pools, flat swimmers rest against the underside of floating leaves, showing only the delicate movements of their breathing. The insects are numerous but rarely gather in loud clouds. Most walk. Some sail between stems on membranes so light that a falling drop can carry them down several levels of the forest.

Predators wait at crossings, beside the narrow dry ribs between pools. They have short faces and soft, spreading feet. A hunt may consist of remaining in one place until the grazing animals, over several days, have eaten their way close enough.

Sound does not travel cleanly here. The layered growth takes the edge from it. A call made nearby seems to arrive from farther away, while a single knock inside a hollow trunk may emerge through several connected chambers. You hear water entering water, the occasional folding of a large leaf, a slow exhalation from something you have not seen.

Sometimes a low wooden note passes beneath your feet.

It comes from a root under strain. Somewhere across the upland, a water-filled basin has deepened, or a great branch has settled, and the adjustment travels through the connected wood. The note is felt before it is heard. Animals pause at it, then resume what they were doing.

There are structures, though they are easy to miss.

At the edges of certain open chambers stand low enclosures with rounded doorways. Their walls are woven from roots still growing, their roofs from overlapping sheets of bark. Inside, the floors are dry. A raised rim follows the wall at a comfortable height for sitting or sleeping, and a narrow channel carries condensation outside.

No cuts are visible. No fastenings remain. Perhaps the roots were guided when they were young; perhaps a creature occupied the hollow for so long that the growth accommodated its body. The oldest enclosures have several doorways, each opening onto a different level of ground. Some of those levels have since risen above the roof.

In one, a tree grows through the center of the room without touching the ceiling. The roof has opened around it, leaving an even gap through which leaves fall into a pool. Along the raised sleeping rim are shallow depressions, polished smooth. Small animals shelter there now. When approached, they retreat into holes that fit them exactly.

Farther inland, these shelters occur beside wide paths that hold no standing water. The paths cannot be followed indefinitely. After a few hours, a root may cross at knee height; farther on, foliage fills the space, though the dry footing continues beneath it. Elsewhere a path opens into a clearing with no visible entrance. Whatever kept these routes open moved differently from the animals that use them now, or moved here when the surface lay several body-lengths lower.

Nothing decays quickly enough to erase all trace, and nothing stays separate long enough to remain an artifact.

The upland has weather on a scale too large to watch. During the wet part of its cycle, basins fill in the far interior. Their weight depresses the living raft, lifting its margins slightly. Water begins to move outward through passages inside the roots. By the time the outer forests start to drip more heavily, the center may already be drying.

A creature born on one rise might grow old before that rise reaches its lowest position.

This is how the ground changes here: through thirst, weight, growth, and the patient yielding of old wood. Leaves turn toward openings that will not become bright for years. Roots thicken beneath pools they will eventually lift and empty. A grove can move across a hollow by dying on one side and extending on the other, leaving its tallest trunks standing in water behind it.

Near the rim, the vegetation grows shorter. Warm mist streams out between low, thick leaves. The footing becomes springy, and through gaps you glimpse the vast root curtain descending into cloud. Some roots reach the buried mountain. Others end in the air, feathered with tiny growths that drink from passing weather.

Here there is a little more light.

You can look across the upper surface and see the long, nearly level swells of the country, dark green beneath a low sky. Beyond the nearest swells are more of them, softened by vapor. What looks like a distant hill may be a canopy rising above its neighbors; what looks like a cloud may be the pale flowering of an entire basin.

Behind you, a grazer steps onto the path. It stands with water shining around its feet, then lowers its head.

The surface accepts its weight so slowly that you notice only after the nearest pool has changed shape.
